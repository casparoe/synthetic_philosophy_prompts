In a popular book on the history of physics I've been reading, there is a chapter on Newton's bucket that I'd like a reality check on, because parts of it struck me as too tidy. The chapter goes roughly like this: Newton hung a bucket of water from a rope, twisted the rope, and let go. At first the water stays level even as the bucket spins around it, and the relative motion between water and bucket is at its maximum; later, friction drags the water around with the bucket, the relative motion between them falls to zero, and it is exactly then that the surface becomes concave. Newton concluded that the concavity cannot be due to any motion of the water relative to the bucket, or to any other body, and must reveal its rotation with respect to absolute space itself, so the experiment is a direct empirical proof that space exists as a container-like thing in its own right. Leibniz objected that space is nothing over and above the system of distances between bodies, so the water must really be responding to its motion relative to the fixed stars. Mach later showed that Newton's argument fails, since in a universe containing nothing but the bucket, the water would not climb the walls at all. Einstein sided with Mach, and general relativity finally vindicated the relational view: space is no longer a fixed stage, geometry is determined by the distribution of matter, and rotation is just motion relative to the distant masses. The chapter ends by saying the debate is therefore closed and the bucket is remembered as a beautiful mistake.

I don't know enough about the history or the physics to tell which parts of this are solid and which are the author taking liberties. Please go through the chapter's account part by part and tell me what a philosopher of physics or a historian of science would accept, what they would want to qualify, and what they would simply reject. In particular I'd like to know whether the bucket really works as a proof of absolute space, what Leibniz's actual objection was, whether Mach demonstrated anything or merely conjectured it, and what general relativity actually settled here, since I have read in other places that Einstein's theory did not straightforwardly declare a winner. Finally, does the chapter's closing verdict, that the debate is closed and Newton simply lost, follow from whatever is true in the summary? Please write your answer as continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as in a long letter.
