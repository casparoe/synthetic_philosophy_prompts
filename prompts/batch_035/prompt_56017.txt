I've been following the argument between illusionists about phenomenal consciousness and their realist opponents for years, and it has settled into a fixed pattern I can recite from memory. The illusionist denies that phenomenal properties exist and treats introspection as a kind of misrepresentation that fabricates them; the realist replies that an illusion of pain is a pain, so the position is either self-refuting or changes the subject; the illusionist rejoins that the residue the realist insists on can be characterised in entirely functional and physical terms, and that only a sentimental attachment to a certain way of thinking blocks this. Both sides apparently agree on all the scientific facts about the machinery of experience; the fight is only about whether something is left over. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the disagreement is not substantive metaphysics but a quantifier-style verbal dispute — structurally like the argument over whether fictional characters exist, where the parties agree on every ordinary fact and differ only about what their existential quantifiers are to range over. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is: verbal, a clash over the epistemology of introspective reports, a difference about which intuitions count as data, or a genuine substantive dispute in disguise. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually claims rather than in a survey of the literature, and say what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a reformulated question, or nothing at all. State how confident you are in the diagnosis overall, and single out the part of it you are least sure of. Please answer in British English.
