For a graduate seminar on historical explanation, I am trying to straighten out my own views on path dependence — on whether history is "locked in," and whether, as my dissertation adviser liked to put it, the same outcomes would have arrived anyway. My economist colleague presses on me a threefold distinction he swears by: dependence on the path that is efficient regardless; dependence that is inefficient but was unremediable given what anyone could have known at the time; and dependence that is inefficient, remediable, and persists nonetheless. I find the distinction useful but insufficient for what I actually care about, which is less efficiency than modal structure: which counterfactual histories were live, and whether the path merely selects among fixed alternatives or reshapes the space of alternatives itself.

Here are my verdicts on cases, offered as gut calls, some of which I suspect are inconsistent:

1. QWERTY. Genuinely locked in. If Dvorak is better, that has mattered not at all for a century and a half, and nothing about the layout's original rationale — mechanical jam avoidance, I am told — survives in the typing it now governs.
2. The standard railway gauge (four feet eight and a half inches, inherited, so the story goes, from wagon ruts). Locked in, but I feel differently about it than about QWERTY: some standard gauge or other was inevitable; the path picked the token, not the type.
3. Alternating current over direct current for long-distance transmission. Barely path dependence at all, in my view: the physics of transmission losses meant the same outcome would have arrived, give or take a decade.
4. English as the global lingua franca. Here I want to say the opposite of what I said about gauge: token-contingent, certainly, but genuinely and interestingly path-dependent — a world with French or Spanish in that role would be importantly different, not just differently labeled.
5. Packet switching over circuit switching in the design of the Internet. Contingent in its historical sequence, but I believe it would have won on the merits regardless; the appearance of path dependence strikes me as an illusion of narrative.
6. Left-hand versus right-hand driving. Pure token contingency, but so trivial that I am not sure it deserves the name of path dependence at all — it is just a convention that, once set, is not worth the trouble to change.
7. Internal combustion over electric propulsion circa 1900. My most unstable verdict. On the one hand, lock-in of the textbook kind: network effects, fueling infrastructure, compounding R&D. On the other hand, the re-switching now underway suggests the lock was only ever a long detour, from which a sufficiently patient observer would conclude that the same outcome has arrived anyway, merely slowly. But by that standard QWERTY's lock might also be redescribed as a very long detour — swipe input and autocorrect are wearing at it — and I do not want to say that.

Is there a single principle — presumably one that separates the cases along modal lines: which alternatives were live, whether the path selected among them or constituted them, whether the selected state is stable under perturbation, whether it feeds back into the very standards by which the alternatives are judged — that captures all seven verdicts? Or must some verdicts go?
