I'm a PhD student in statistics, and I've been made the person in our consulting group who signs off on forecasts we deliver to clients — regional utilities, hospital systems, public agencies — so the question of whether to extrapolate a trend lands on my desk a few times a month. My training gives me tools for quantifying uncertainty within a fitted model, but almost nothing for the prior question: when is it legitimate to treat an observed regularity as holding beyond the observations at all. I've been reading about Goodman's new riddle of induction — the observation that the same data support a predicate like grue that flips at some future time, so something beyond the data must decide which regularities we project — and the notion of projectibility. Please turn whatever the philosophy of induction actually has to say about this into a procedure a junior analyst could run in about an hour on a proposed extrapolation: the questions to ask in order (could the client's hypothesis be cheaply re-described so that the identical data support the opposite continuation? has anything about the underlying process or population changed across the observation window, or is the regularity just a run of points? is there a candidate mechanism, or only fit?), what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure must stop at a no, and where it can only flag that the question outstrips the data and hand off to someone with domain knowledge. Then run it on two cases: extending a hospital system's admission trend five years out to justify a staffing contract, and extrapolating a bridge-cable fatigue curve beyond the stress range that was actually tested. Please be candid about where the procedure will misfire — especially whether it can ever distinguish a genuine regime change from a grue-like re-description of the same evidence, and whether it degenerates into refusing every extrapolation the client actually pays for.
