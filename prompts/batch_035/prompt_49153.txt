I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on virtue ethics — sixteen students, one 90-minute session, plus twenty minutes of the previous week's session that I can use for setup — and we are about to reach the situationist critique: the empirical claim that trivial situational variables like time pressure, mood, framing, and the presence of bystanders predict morally relevant behavior about as well as any stable trait does, together with the argument about what that is supposed to do to virtue ethics. I am tired of teaching this from published studies, and I want my students to run into the phenomenon themselves. Design a staged activity along roughly these lines, and improve on the sketch as you see fit: in the setup session, students privately write trait attributions for two or three named classmates (helpful, honest, patient, and so on), each paired with a concrete prediction of how that person will behave in a specific helping or truth-telling task to come; in the main session, students go through several rounds of small-stakes tasks in which exactly the situational parameters the situationists say matter are varied across rounds — hurry versus leisure, diffused versus individual responsibility, a trivial mood induction, different framings of who is depending on whom — and only at the end do we lay out how well the trait attributions and the situational variables each predicted what actually happened. The idea should show up in what they do, not in anything I announce beforehand; the debrief, not a preamble, is where it gets named. You will need to solve the ethical problem of covertly manipulating classmates: tell me whether the thing can be run with consent given in advance at the right level of abstraction, and if you judge that it can't, design the nearest honest alternative and be explicit about what it loses pedagogically. The debrief itself should do three things: map particular moments in the activity onto the parts of the philosophical argument, treating cross-situational consistency and the criterion problem as the central targets — and note that the students' own struggle to code ambiguous behavior into trait vocabulary is a live instance of the criterion problem, so use that; give the standard virtue-ethical replies their due, especially the ones claiming the situationist data leave untouched a conception of virtues as deliberative or socially intelligent achievements; and say plainly which parts of the debate the activity cannot reproduce at all — real stakes, longitudinal stability, anything needing population base rates, and anything else you think of — so that I know what I must supply by lecture. Close with what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and what I should do about it. Two requests on the response itself. First, write it as continuous prose, like a long letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Second, push back on me where warranted. If you think my design rigs the game in the situationist's favor — say, because the trait attributions were made too hastily to be a fair test — or that the prediction element is too noisy at sixteen students to show anything and will mislead rather than illustrate, say so plainly and adjust the design rather than complying politely.
