I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a European current-affairs weekly, and I'm at the drafting stage of a long-form essay on the ethics of the bystander. The piece grew out of reporting I did last year on two very different stories: a trial in Germany, where someone was prosecuted for walking past a person in distress (German criminal law, unlike the common law, imposes a duty to render assistance in emergencies), and a series of conversations with donors who describe their giving in explicitly arithmetical terms. The juxtaposition set me thinking about Peter Singer's old thought experiment — the shallow pond, the drowning child, the ruined trousers — and about why it still provokes such discomfort more than fifty years after his famine essay appeared.

The thesis I'm developing is this: the standard objections to Singer's argument, and the legal arrangements we actually have, both track a single gradient — the visibility of the moral arithmetic — rather than any considered view about how much morality may demand. We routinely accept cost-imposing safety regulations that save statistical, unidentifiable lives at modest expense to ourselves, and no one calls this tyrannically demanding; we impose legal duties to rescue exactly where the victim is present, identifiable, and the rescue immediate; and we bridle at Singer's pond precisely because it compresses a tradeoff we normally make diffusely and invisibly into a single deliberating agent at a single moment. My draft claims, in other words, that the famous demandingness objection is best read not as a rejection of the content of Singer's principle but as a complaint about the tempo and granularity at which it is presented to us — and that the law, with its emergency-only duties, quietly registers the same complaint. To make this stick I engage three critics: Liam Murphy's argument, in his book on moral demands in nonideal theory, that an individual's share of remedial duties should be calculated as if everyone else had complied rather than as a license for the conscientious to be exploited; Peter Unger's envelope-based variations on Singer's case, which purport to show that our intuitions flip with morally irrelevant factors like salience and administrative framing; and the proximity objection in its several forms, from bare distance to special obligations to those near us. I end, somewhat uneasily, with a speculative flourish about donation platforms and disaster alerts that re-render distant need as vivid and immediate, and whether technology might eventually dissolve the very gradient the essay describes.

Before I go further, I'd like comments on three specific points. First, accuracy: have I represented Murphy's compliance-cost point and Unger's experimental strategy fairly, and is my characterization of the common-law position — no general duty of easy rescue, duties only where special relationships or assumed responsibilities create them — defensible as a generalization? Second, and most important to me: is the visibility/tempo thesis genuinely a new angle, or is it just Unger's salience point wearing different clothes? If it is a repackaging, I'd rather know now — and if there's a way to sharpen it into something distinct, I'd like to hear how. Third, the ending: does the technology flourish earn its place, or is it doing speculative work the essay's core argument should be doing on its own? I'd welcome blunt judgment on all three, along with any passages or transitions you think are weak, but please focus your comments on those three questions rather than giving me a general line edit.
