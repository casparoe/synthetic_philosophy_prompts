I've started going to a lettering evening class after years of just being a typography nerd online, and the instructor leans constantly on one analogy: every letter has a skeleton, the bare geometric core of strokes that makes it that letter, and everything else -- the thick and thin, the contrast, the serifs, the terminals -- is just clothing you dress that skeleton in. The practical upshot she draws is that letter identity is purely structural: strip the flesh off any 'a' and the same essential 'a' is still there underneath, so when we learn to draw letters we should drill skeletons first, and when we judge whether a weird experimental typeface "still reads" we're really asking whether its skeletons survived the styling. She even uses it philosophically, saying style is inessensial to a letter the way a coat is inessensial to a person, so there's no fact of the matter about a letter's "essence" beyond its skeleton.

I mostly buy it but a couple of things nag at me. The obvious one: a double-storey 'a' and a single-storey 'a' in the same typeface family don't seem to share a skeleton at all -- they look like different stick figures, yet both are unmistakably 'a'. And in blackletter the 'a' seems to have some third structure entirely. So it looks like at least some letters have a family of skeletons rather than one, which starts to smell like Wittgensteinian family resemblance rather than essence, and I don't know if her "strip the flesh" test survives that. Or maybe the skeleton itself quietly changes with style, which would mean the analogy is smuggling in the conclusion rather than supporting it.
