I'd be grateful for a consistency check on a set of views I hold about moral testimony — that is, about whether one can properly come to believe moral truths on someone else's say-so.

(1) Testimony can transmit true moral belief, but not moral understanding: someone who believes exploitation is wrong merely because a trusted teacher said so, and who could not say anything about what makes it wrong, is missing something that matters for action, not merely for philosophy seminars.

(2) Nevertheless, I hold that most people rightly acquire most of their morality through trust in parents, teachers and inherited traditions rather than through private reasoning, and that this is a good thing, since individual moral reasoning is far less reliable than a decent inherited code.

(3) Accordingly, I think demands that citizens reason their way to their moral and political convictions from first principles are misguided, and faintly elitist.

(4) Yet I also hold that a functionary who participates in an atrocity because he sincerely defers to superiors he regards as morally wiser is fully blameworthy: "I was only following orders" does not excuse him, and neither, it seems to me, does "I deferred in conscience to someone better placed to judge".

(5) Finally, I don't think that taking the facts of some historical atrocity from historians, and condemning it on that basis, involves moral testimony at all: the historians supply facts, and my condemnation issues from values I already hold.

Do these hang together? Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look as though they are, and what would be the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious, and be specific about which commitment would have to give way in each case.
