I'm preparing to write a term paper on the paradox of fiction, and before I drown in the literature on make-believe, thought, and simulation, I want the specification first. Please take the role of a philosophy of mind scholar who thinks hard about method before theories. Brainstorm before you commit to the list: what phenomena are genuinely in the theory's remit, and what is adjacent clutter? Then write out the desiderata that any adequate theory of our emotional and evaluative responses to fiction must satisfy -- the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must respect, the empirical results about affect, imagination, and belief it cannot contradict. I'll seed you with the cases I care most about, though you should add or refuse cases as you judge fit: weeping at a novel one has read before and whose ending one remembers; jump-scares and dread in horror films, where one knows the protagonist survives the sequel; suspense persisting on rewatching despite knowing the outcome; the different phenomenology of learning that the "based on a true story" frame at the start of a film was false after one has already been moved by it; and fiction that depicts the suffering of a real, living person. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and be explicit about which ones you would treat as non-negotiable versus which are artifacts of one camp's theory -- for instance, whether it is a genuine requirement that our fictional emotions be rational in the way real ones can be, or whether that demand just smuggles in a cognitive theory of the emotions.

The two parts I most want to see clearly marked: first, whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable -- I have a suspicion that a requirement to distinguish sharply between fear of the fiction and fear for oneself, a requirement to explain rewatched suspense, and a requirement that the responses be genuinely emotional rather than mere imaginings may not be jointly satisfiable, but I want your view, not mine; second, which single desideratum does the most to exclude the leading contenders, and whether that speaks against the desideratum or against the contenders. You need not score the contenders at any length -- a sentence each at the end, if you like, is plenty.

One epistemic note: throughout, calibrate your confidence. Flag where a desideratum rests on a contested empirical assumption or on your own intuition about a case rather than on anything principled, and say explicitly what would change your mind about the list -- which case, thought experiment, or empirical finding would force you to drop or add a requirement, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to go wrong. If you find that two of your desiderata genuinely conflict, do not quietly weaken one to save the set; state the conflict and say which you would keep.
