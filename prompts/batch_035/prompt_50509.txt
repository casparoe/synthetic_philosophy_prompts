I'm in a bioethics reading group (we meet thursdays, it's mostly snacks and yelling) and I'm putting together a parody zine for our next session on how death is actually defined in medicine. The thing that hooked me is that "dead" isn't one thing: neurological criteria (the whole-brain checklist, brainstem reflexes, the apnea test) and circulatory criteria (heart stops, then you wait a few minutes to make sure it stays stopped) are two different gates, they can come apart in edge cases, and the rules apparently vary by country and even by US state. And then there's the whole organ transplantation angle, where the timing question gets genuinely uncomfortable — the dead-donor rule says you can't take organs before death, but organs degrade after it, so the clock is pressing in both directions.

Could you write a deadpan corporate FAQ page, around 600 words, for a fictional hospital's new "Demise Verification Desk" (or "End-of-Life Confirmation Services," your call) — that cheerful, slightly-too-helpful customer-service voice, Q&A format, with sections like "Which death is right for me?", "How do I know if my loved one is dead?", "Can the determination be reversed or transferred to another facility?", and some fine-print policy clauses at the end. The humor has to come from getting the actual bioethics right rather than from generic hospital jokes: the two criteria and how they can disagree, the whole-brain vs. higher-brain debate about whether the person is gone when cognition is gone or only when the whole organ is, the apnea test, the dead-donor rule and the timing pressure around retrieval, jurisdictional weirdness, religious accommodations, families asking "so when exactly did she die" and there not being a clean answer.

The target of the satire is the practice of drawing bright lines through a process, not the doctors or any real institution — so exaggerate real features (the checklist-ification, the brochure optimism, the way the same body can be dead in one jurisdiction and merely critically ill in another) rather than inventing silly ones. A bioethicist should read it and feel seen rather than insulted. Keep it deadpan throughout, no winking, but it should still be readable to someone who knows nothing about the topic — by the end they should have absorbed the real point, which is that "the moment of death" is a decision we make as much as a fact we discover. A typo or two in the brochure itself is fine and even welcome (it's a zine after all). Don't cite any real hospitals, laws by name, or actual guidelines — invented specifics only.
