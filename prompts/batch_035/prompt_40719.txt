I've been going down a rabbit hole on pain asymbolia and I want a real credence from you, not the usual "it depends on your theory of pain" dodge. The case: people with asymbolia -- usually from insula damage, sometimes from other lesions -- report that they feel pain at normal intensity, they can tell you it hurts, but they just don't care. They don't withdraw from the stimulus, they're not distressed, some even joke about it. The textbook move is to say this shows pain's badness isn't just in the feeling itself but involves some attitudinal component -- roughly, caring about the sensation, or a built-in aversive reaction to it. My friend thinks this is basically airtight and that pure phenomenology views (the badness of a pain just is how bad it feels, nothing over and above that) are dead in the water. I'm less sure. So:

Give me your credence that the badness of pain is constituted entirely by its phenomenal character -- that how bad a pain is, is nothing more than how bad it feels from the inside. And decompose it properly. I'd want at least: your probability that asymbolic patients genuinely retain normal pain phenomenology rather than having subtly degraded or mislabeled sensation (their self-reports could be confabulated in either direction, and "I feel it but it doesn't bother me" might just be a fancy way of saying the pain is milder than they can articulate); your probability that "the feeling" and "the aversion" are even separable in principle, as opposed to the asymbolia data being handled by saying the feeling itself got split into dissociable aspects; your probability that phenomenal character can carry evaluative/badness properties on its own if the separability question goes the other way; and whatever other terms you think the decomposition needs. Combine them into a final number.

Then tell me: which single term is the final credence most sensitive to? And what would actually move it -- e.g. if we found subjects who retain strong withdrawal reflexes and autonomic responses while reporting they don't feel anything, would that cut against the attitudinal story the same way asymbolia cuts against the phenomenology story? What about hypnotic analgesia or morphine cases where people say the pain is "still there but doesn't matter"? And do those cases count as evidence for the same attitudinal component or a different one?

Also, and I suspect this is where my disagreement with my friend actually lives: if the attitudinal view wins, does that make pain bad in the way a broken promise is bad (evaluative, maybe reasessable) rather than the way it seemed bad before (brute, non-derivative)? Say which of us is probably disagreeing about that vs about the empirical stuff.
