I teach a second-year course on moral status — the question of who counts, and for how much, working down the supposed gradient from apes through insects to, at the very bottom, things like thermostats that merely track a variable and respond. I want to bring a film in for the last week of term and I keep coming back to WALL-E, because it's maybe the most widely-seen piece of popular culture that seems to actually run the experiment: we get a trash-compacting robot with what the film presents as a rich inner life (700 years alone, replaying Hello Dolly on VHS, collecting sporks and rubik's cubes), a cockroach called Hal who is treated as a beloved pet but purely as comic relief and never as a subject, an autopilot called AUTO which is nothing but a control system executing a stored directive and yet which the film needs us to treat as a villain, and a human race rendered as passive blobs on hover-chairs who get arguably less sympathetic screen time than any of the machines.

Here's my working hypothesis and I'd like a proper reading of the film through it: the film's sympathy gradient doesn't actually track anything like a capacity for suffering — it tracks expressiveness and cuteness. WALL-E and EVE have binocular eyes and body language and squeaky voices, so we're moved when he's crushed at the end (even though we're told a machine can be repaired with parts, which ought to change everything and doesn't change our feelings at all); Hal the cockroach is invulnerable and never shown suffering, so he stays a joke; the humans lose our interest precisely insofar as they've stopped behaving, like objects; and AUTO, the closest thing in the film to an actual thermostat, is only granted agency in the form of malice. So my suspicion is that the film quietly dramatises the confound that worries people in the moral status literature: that our intuitions about degrees of concern are really tracking perceived resemblance to human expressiveness, not any graded capacity.

But I want the reading to be honest in both directions, so tell me where the film resists my hypothesis. The captain stands up (literally, for the first time) and fights AUTO and turns the ship around, which looks like the film insisting that agency can wake up rather than being fixed — and there's the moment where WALL-E shields Hal from the dust storm, which could be read as the film taking the cockroach's welfare seriously after all. Which scenes would you point students to, where does my reading distort the film to fit the thesis, and does WALL-E add anything to the debate on graded moral status that the arguments by themselves don't? I'm half-convinced the answer to that last one is yes — the film makes you feel concern for a machine before you've decided anything, and that pull seems like genuine data about our intuitions — but I'd like you to actually make the case rather than just agree with me.
