I teach an upper-level ancient philosophy seminar and I'm writing the final exam. I want one essay question, answerable in about 60 minutes, on the exchange argument in Book V, chapter 5 of the Nicomachean Ethics — the passage with the builder and the doctor. But I'm tired of students getting full credit by reciting the one-liner that money makes incommensurable goods commensurable by measuring need, and then gesturing at proportional justice. The question should force them into the parts of the text where the standard gloss stops doing work. In particular, I want the question constructed so that a good answer has to confront at least three of the following tensions:

(1) The proportionality puzzle. Aristotle insists exchanges must be equalized, and he says the relevant comparison involves the builder and the doctor as persons, not merely the house and the medical service — yet the arithmetic he sketches pairs quantities of products, and it is far from obvious whose ratio to whom the proportion is supposed to be: between the goods, between the persons in their productive roles, or between each person and each product. Students should have to say which reading they accept and what in the text supports it, not just name "geometric proportion."

(2) The pre-money constraint. Aristotle apparently holds that the demand for equality in exchange pre-dates and would survive the absence of money, since he presents barter exchange as the base case that money later facilitates. So what exactly is money doing — creating the equality, or merely representing an equality that must already hold for other reasons?

(3) The status of need. The claim that all goods are commensurable by need (chreia) is doing enormous work, but need is not a stable measure in the way a currency unit is: whose need, at what time, and why would the resulting equality count as a form of justice rather than as a fact about who wants what? A strong answer should say whether need functions as a measure, as a mere demand-side fact, or as something else, and should notice that Aristotle himself flags the apparent circularity when he observes that money can lose its purchasing power even while the conventional unit remains the same.

(4) The placement puzzle. The chapter appears in a discussion of particular justice, but many readers suspect Aristotle is reporting and refining a popular view about reciprocity rather than endorsing a distinct form of justice at all. The question should make students take a stance on whether the chapter is an analysis of justice in exchange or an economic analysis that the framework of the justice discussion does not obviously accommodate.

Please write the essay question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme totalling 20 marks built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth. For each must-hit point, add a one-line note on how students typically miss it. Finally, include a short paragraph naming the specific difference between an A answer and a B answer on this question — I want the line between them to be something a grader can actually apply, not a difference in polish. I do not want the question to reward regurgitation of secondary literature: if any interpretive controversy is needed to make the question work, state the competing readings plainly in the question itself, so students who have only read the primary text are not disadvantaged.
