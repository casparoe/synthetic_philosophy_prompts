I sit on my university's Committee on Naming and Memorialization (the one that got dragged into existance after the whole mess with the Culver statue last spring, long story). We have a live case: Whitlock Hall, our second-oldest building, named for Asa P. Whitlock, a founding trustee whose money basically built this campus in the 1890s. A grad student in history has recently documented that Whitlock's fortune came substantially from a company that, among other things, ran vaccine trials on children placed in the state orphanage where he also happened to sit on the board. The student paper ran it as a front-pager, there's a petition with 3,000 signatures, and the Whitlock family (still donors, there's a named scholarship) has sent a letter through their lawyer saying that removing the name would erase history.

Our options as laid out in the committee's charge: (1) retain the name with a permanent interpretive exhibit in the lobby, (2) remove the name, move the archival material to the library's special collections, and rename through an open nomination process, or (3) a "transition" option where the name comes off in five years regardless of how the rename debate goes. I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting recommending one of these with reasons, and I want it to actually move people, not just do the standard "we honor complexity" thing that reads like a press release.

Here's my angle, and tell me if you think it's a good one or if I'm overcomplicating: I took a course a while back on Nietzsche's Untimely Meditations, and the second essay -- on the uses and disadvantages of history for life -- has been rattling around in my head the entire time I've served on this committee. As I remember it he splits our relationship to the past into monumental, antiquarian, and critical history: monumental pulls heroes out of the past as fuel for present action, antiquarian preserves and reveres, and critical drags the past before a tribunal and destroys what we can't live with. Each of our options seems to map onto one of the modes (the exhibit is arguably antiquarian history dressed up as critical, outright removal is critical, the transition option is... some muddle of the two), and Nietzsche has a warning about each: monumental history can falsify by making the past grander and cleaner than it was, antiquarian reverence can harden into blindness toward anything new, and critical history wounds -- he's clear that you need genuine strength to bear having destroyed part of your own past without the wound festering. And hanging over all of it is his basic demand, that history is only justified insofar as it serves life going forward, not knowledge for its own sake.

So: draft the memo. It should recommend one option concretely, with maybe a time frame and a rough idea of who does what, and it should use the three-modes framework for real rather than as decoration -- including taking seriously whichever mode ends up on the losing side, and being honest about whether the family's "erasing history" objection, which on its face is an antiquarian reflex, deserves more than the usual dismissal (after all, Nietzsche himself thought the antiquarian impulse had real value, not just a vice). But don't let it turn into a philosophy seminar paper: my colleagues are smart but only one of them will have read any Nietzsche, and a memo that opens with German terminology is dead on arrival. Maybe a page and a half, and it has to be something the committee could actually vote on Thursday.
