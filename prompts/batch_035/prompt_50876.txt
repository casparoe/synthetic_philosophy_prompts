Some years ago I wrote a master's dissertation on the species problem, and I have since drifted into biodiversity informatics, where the question refuses to stay academic: every time my colleagues build a database, they have to decide whether a rank in one checklist means the same kind of thing as a rank in another, and the old philosophical fight breaks out in the issue tracker. The debate as I received it ran roughly thus: Mayr's biological species concept (species as groups of actually or potentially interbreeding populations) sat uneasily alongside phylogenetic species concepts of various stripes (some keyed to diagnosability, some to monophyly), and by the 1990s many had concluded that no single concept could cover sexual animals, asexual microbes, and hybridising plants alike — whereupon the field split into pluralists, who said that was fine and that several equally legitimate species concepts should coexist, and monists, who insisted that a single, unified account must be available. De Queiroz then offered the general lineage concept, which was meant to dissolve the whole dispute by identifying the species concept itself (species as separately evolving lineages) and treating every other 'concept' as a mere criterion for identifying the lineage in a particular case.

The puzzle I want your help with is this: that dissolution was proposed decades ago, everyone cites it approvingly, and yet the arguments continue essentially unchanged — taxonomists still take sides, the zoological and botanical codes of nomenclature still operate on different assumptions, and the pluralism-versus-monism dispute has not moved appreciably in my professional lifetime. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a verbal dispute about the word 'species' — in which case de Queiroz should have ended it and did not, which itself needs explaining? Is it a hidden empirical disagreement about the structure of the living world, roughly: whether the tree of life is full of discrete, objectively separate lineages or grades into a continuum that our vocabulary carves at somewhat arbitrary joints? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading — one side asking what taxonomy is for (storing and retrieving information about organisms, which pluralism serves well) and the other asking what kind of thing exists in nature (which perhaps only monism even addresses)? Note that the paradigm cases do not discriminate: ring species, purely asexual lineages, and plant hybrid swamps are familiar to all parties and have been for half a century, so the persistence of the dispute through cases everyone classifies the same way is precisely what I find diagnostic and want you to explain.

Please give me a diagnosis rather than a survey of the concepts, and defend it against the obvious rivals: if you say the dispute is verbal, say why the participants — intelligent people, by and large — have not noticed, and whether the fact that many of them now say 'it is only a word, get on with classifying' supports the verbal reading or instead conceals a real disagreement about the aims of taxonomy that has been relocated rather than resolved. And then tell me honestly whether anything would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a change of question, some future empirical finding about the discreteness or continuousness of lineages — or whether, given your diagnosis, we should expect no progress at all, and why that expectation would not itself be one more move in the same stalled game.
