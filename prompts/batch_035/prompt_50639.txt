I'm teaching a seminar this term on aggregation in ethics, and we spend a good chunk of it on expected value maximization and the question of whether you should follow it all the way down -- the fanaticism problem, where you're obligated to take a zillion-to-one shot at an astronomical payoff over a sure thing of ordinary size. The trouble is my students sort themselves into camps based on vibes. The ones who want to sound tough say they'd bite every bullet, the ones who want to sound humane say obviously not, and almost nobody has actually located where their intuitions break. So I want a diagnostic quiz, and I want it to do real work rather than just relabel people.

Here's what I need: eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased entirely without jargon -- no "are you a fanatic?", no "do you accept aggregation?", just cases people can react to. Some of these have to be in there: the classic enormous-number-of-tiny-sufferings versus one big suffering trade; a mugging-style case where someone offers astronomical value at absurdly low probability (you can decide how to set it up so it doesn't read as a Wikipedia rerun); a case where a small risk of total ruin for a few people buys a certain small benefit for an enormous number; a gradual spectrum case, where you slide from one person enduring a long pain to a slightly shorter pain each spread over more and more people, and the reader says where on the slide they'd stop trading (this one is the heart of the quiz, so take some care with how you phrase the steps); and at least one case involving something like beauty or knowledge versus pain, to catch the people whose resistance isn't about probability at all but about whether everything can be put on one scale.

Then a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions, not just labels -- the full-on expected value fanatic, the person with a bounded or concave valuation who absorbs most of the weird cases without giving up aggregation, the threshold or lexical view where sufficient suffering just dominates, the non-aggregative or person-affecting holdout, and whatever else falls out of the logical space when you cross the questions. Please build the space from the cases first and then place the positions in it, rather than starting from a list of names. Two more things: flag any pair of positions the quiz genuinely can't seperate, like bounded utility versus mere risk aversion, and note which answer combinations come out empty, with a sentence on whether nobody lands there because the view is incoherent, just ugly, or simply unexplored. I'll be reading these aloud and handing them out, so write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd explain it in an email -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs. A couple of my students are non-native speakers so keep the cases vivid but don't get cute with the wording. And don't worry about making the quiz fair to every position; it should make people sweat.
