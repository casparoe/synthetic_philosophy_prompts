**Title: The Symmetry Test — What Asymbolia Teaches (and Doesn't) About the Badness of Pain**

Pain asymbolia, first described by Schilder and Stengel in the late 1920s and early 1930s, presents a puzzle that has still not been properly absorbed by value theory. Asymbolic patients report that they feel pain — they can locate it, rate its intensity, describe its quality — yet they display no aversion to it. They do not withdraw, grimace, or become distressed; some joke about the pain or calmly allow noxious stimulation to continue. The standard lesson drawn is modest: the sensory-discriminative and affective-motivational components of pain can come apart. I want to draw a less modest lesson, and then show why the essay cannot quite get away with it.

Call a consideration about X's badness **intrinsic** if X's phenomenal character alone makes X bad for the one who undergoes it, and **relational** if X is bad only in virtue of how it hooks into the subject's motivational and evaluative architecture. Asymbolia looks like a clean argument for the relational view: if two states can be phenomenally identical in their sensory character while differing in badness, then badness was never written into the feel in the first place.

But the argument is only as good as the dissociation it rests on, and here I propose what I call the **symmetry test**. If the relational view is right, we should expect both halves of the dissociation to be realizable: pain-without-badness (asymbolia) and badness-without-pain. The second half is plausibly supplied by dread, nausea, and certain forms of grief — states that are non-nociceptive yet intensely bad. If both halves exist, then pain's phenomenal character contains its badness neither necessarily nor sufficiently, and the relational view follows.

The obvious rejoinder is one I want to take seriously rather than dodge. Perhaps asymbolia is not the preservation of pain with the removal of badness, but a breakdown in the *registration* or *expression* of badness that persists intact beneath the calm surface. The analogy I want to press is anosognosia: a patient who denies her paralysis has not thereby lost the use of the limb's underlying deficit. A patient who smiles at her pain may be in a state that is bad for her while her evaluative systems are simply disconnected from the ones that express and report badness. If this calibration objection is right, asymbolia is evidence about the *manifestation* of pain's badness, not its presence.

I respond with a dilemma. Either badness is phenomenally manifest or it is not. If it is manifest, then a subject with full phenomenal access to her pain's character and full introspective competence, who sincerely reports no badness, gives us excellent evidence there is none; asymbolics are such subjects. If it is not manifest, then the intrinsic view concedes that pain's badness is invisible from the inside, which is an enormous cost — it makes pain's badness more like a hidden relational property than an intrinsic phenomenal one, which is the relational view in all but name. Either way, the intrinsic theorist loses.

I close with two further observations meant to strengthen the relational view's credentials. First, there is what I call *acquired asymbolia*: some long-term chronic pain patients report unreduced intensity alongside sharply diminished distress — a spontaneous approximation of the asymbolic profile that suggests aversiveness is a parameter the system can turn down without altering the feel. Second, if badness is relational, then the question of whether a non-biological system could be in a genuinely bad state turns not on whether it instantiates pain qualia but on whether its states have the right motivational integration — a possibility the intrinsic view forecloses far too quickly.

I conclude that the badness of pain is not an ingredient of painfulness. It is, rather, a fact about how the feel is *used*.
