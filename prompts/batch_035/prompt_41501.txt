I'd like you to construct a thought experiment on the nonidentity problem to an unusual specification: it must reverse the usual dialectical role that the nonidentity fact plays.

In the familiar cases — a depletion policy, a woman who conceives at a time when a drug she has taken will affect the child — the nonidentity fact is invoked to dissolve a complaint. The affected person owes their existence to the act, their life is worth living, and so, the argument runs, they cannot claim to have been made worse off, and the act cannot have wronged them. Your case must run the other way. It must be one in which noticing the nonidentity fact makes the act seem more clearly wrong, not less: the wrong is visible precisely because the decision-maker chose which person to bring about, rather than imposing an outcome on someone who would have existed anyway.

The shape I have in mind is this. A decision-maker selects between bringing about Person A — whose life will be comfortably worth living but marked by a foreseeable, serious disadvantage — and Person B, whose life would be comfortably worth living without that disadvantage. The decision-maker chooses A, on the strength of a reason R that would plainly not justify imposing that disadvantage on a person who already existed. The complaint available to A, once adult, is not that A is worse off than A would otherwise have been; A's only alternative, had the decision gone the other way, is never existing at all. Rather, the complaint is that the decision-maker treated A's not otherwise existing as a licence to count A's interests for less than B's — a licence the decision-maker would never claim over an existing person.

Constraints on the case:

1. Realistic and contemporary. An ordinary institutional setting — a clinic, a licensing body, a local authority. No science fiction, and no technology that does not already exist.
2. One specific, identifiable complainant. A must reach adulthood and be able to raise the complaint in their own voice; no anonymous future generations.
3. The wrong, if there is one, must arise from the choice structure alone. No deception, no broken promises, no risk imposed on third parties, no harm to anyone else. If the case needs any of those, it has failed.
4. The disadvantage must be one about which there is no serious dispute that it is a disadvantage — a painful chronic condition, a markedly shorter life, substantially narrowed opportunities. Do not let the case trade on contested intuitions about the worth of any kind of life, and keep it well clear of the wrongful-life debate: stipulate that A's life is comfortably above the worth-living threshold, so that the case is not about whether A's life is worth living at all.
5. Specify the alternative. Had the decision gone the other way, A would never have existed and B would have existed instead, somewhat better off. Both lives are comfortably worth living.

After the scenario, I want four things. First, a walkthrough: state what a narrow person-affecting consequentialist must say (the comparative test — no one is worse off than they would otherwise have been — and hence no wrong), what an impersonal welfarist must say, and which of the two verdicts matches the complaint A is actually in a position to raise. Second, the contrast test: show that if A had existed independently of the decision, R would obviously not justify choosing A's disadvantage, so that it is the nonidentity fact that allows the decision-maker to offer R with a straight face. Third, tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the case if that feature is removed. Fourth, give the strongest reply available to the theory that comes out badly — argued on its own terms, not by appealing to the bad consequences of letting such reasoning circulate, and not by retreating to rule-consequentialist considerations.

The case is for teaching upper-level undergraduates who have met the depletion-style cases and concluded that the lesson of the nonidentity problem is that if the resulting life is worth living, no complaint is available. Your case should show them that the same structure can be turned round. One rule against cheating: do not simply stipulate within the scenario that the act is wrong. The wrongness must emerge from the theories' verdicts and the comparison between them, and you must demonstrate that each theory's verdict really does follow from that theory.
