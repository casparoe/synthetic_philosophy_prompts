Something about UBI pilots has been bugging me for a while and I want to know if I'm onto something or just being pedantic. Every time someone actually tries a basic income in the real world — Finland's experiment, the Stockton one, GiveDirectly's long-run study in Kenya — it gets set up basically like a drug trial: pick a few hundred or few thousand people, randomize, the money gets payed out for a couple of years (sometimes longer), then you measure employment, health, stress, school attendance, whatever, against a control group.

But that's not how the stronger advocates pitch UBI in the first place. The pitch is about redesigning the floor for everybody: it's supposed to change bargaining power, let people say no to garbage jobs, shift what counts as a decent offer in the labor market, change the norm around quitting and moving and retraining. That's a claim about the whole system, not about what a pill does to an individual patient.

So heres my question: what does the pilot-and-evaluate practice implicitly presuppose about what a basic income IS? My hunch is that it presupposes something like — UBI is a treatment applied to individuals, whose effects show up at the individual level and on a few-year timescale. Which would quietly rule out the redesign picture, since you can't observe a changed labor market in a three-year window with two thousand participants, and the design ends up treating community-level effects (the whole village gets it, so the village changes) as spillage to be controlled away rather than as, possibly, the main effect. There also seems to be a buried presupposition about what the policy is FOR — namely that its value can be read off a list of outcome variables, which is a stance, not a neutral measurement choice.

So, three things. First, reconstruct the implicit theory of the policy that the piloting practice rests on, as best you can from the shape of the practice itself. Second, tell me whether it's genuinely in tension with the redesign theory, or whether the drug-trial frame is still defensible — one obvious defense would be that pilots only need to rule out obvious individual-level harms before scaling up, they don't need to detect the big structural effects, and detecting those was never their job. Is that defense any good, or does it concede too much? Third, if you built a pilot around the redesign theory instead, what would it even look like — what would you measure, how long would it have to run, how many people would it need? And if the answer is that it's not remotely feasible, does that just show the drug-trial frame is the only game in town, or does it show that the redesign theory of UBI is untestable in any practical sense and therefore kind of empty?
