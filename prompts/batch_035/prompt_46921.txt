I'm a philosophy of mind person, and next week our department is hosting a day-long colloquium on consciousness. The guest of honor is a senior philosopher who's spent the better part of forty years defending the multiple drafts model -- the picture Dennett made famous, on which there's no single place in the brain where contents all arrive for an inner audience; there are just parallel, temporally sloppy drafts being edited on the fly, and "consciousness" of something is a matter of what's available when a probe (a report, a memory, a judgment) comes in. He's given more or less the same talk for decades, and the usual Q&A with him mostly produces canned answers, so I've been roped into moderating a proper discussion this time. The grad students are already grumbling that these sessions never get past the opening statements, so I want a sequence of eight to ten questions that actually presses on the model instead of inviting him to restate it.

A few things I want in there. At least one question should be about the color phi phenomenon and the old Orwellian/Stalinesque debate: his standard move is to say it's ill-posed to ask whether the subject experienced the intermediate color before the later probe, versus whether the memory was rewritten afterward, since there's no fact of the matter about when the content "became conscious" -- no arrival time, because there's no arrival place. I want a follow-up that presses on whether that's an explanation of the empirical datum or a decision to stop explaining. At least one question should be about the Block-style charge that the model only ever addresses access consciousness and stays silent about the phenomenal kind -- his usual reply is that the idea of phenomenal consciousness as something over and above discriminating, reporting, reacting patterns is a confusion we'd be better off dropping. And I'd like a question somewhere about the cutaneous rabbit, or about whether the multiple drafts picture actually constrains any neurobiology, or whether it's compatible with roughly any finding about timing, pathways, and post-editing you could imagine.

One more thing: he has a favorite deflection, which is to say that any objection to the model only seems pressing because the objector is still secretly committed to a Cartesian theater -- a place where it all comes together for somebody. I want the questions ordered so that this deflection gets progressively harder for him to use, and I want each question keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, with the follow-up ready, since I'll be thinking on my feet up there. End the sequence with one question about what empirical finding, if any, could count against multiple drafts -- something about actual neurophysiology rather than intuitions. Keep the register conversational, these are questions for speaking aloud in a seminar room, not for publication. Don't worry about citations or attributing exact words to anyone; paraphrase the positions as you understand them. And if you think one of my requested angles is a dead end, say so and propose something sharper instead.
