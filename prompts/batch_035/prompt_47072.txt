My aunt, a retired paintings conservator, has unusually firm views about when an object counts as an original and what it is worth, and she has given verdicts on enough cases now that I think I can see a pattern in them — though I am not sure there is only one pattern. Her track record:

1. A forgery so technically accomplished that it passed examination for years, until archival evidence exposed it: she holds it to be worth almost nothing once exposed, however beautiful it may be — perhaps a hundredth of a genuine work of comparable quality.
2. A seventeenth-century workshop canvas of the sort a master's studio routinely produced: assistants executed most of it from his design, he painted the heads and the key passages himself, and he signed it during his lifetime as part of his official output: she counts it as fully genuine and would value it accordingly.
3. An artist's own later replica of his own earlier painting, the original having been destroyed in a studio fire before it was ever exhibited: he repainted it in his seventies and regarded it as the continuation of the work. She treats the replica as genuine and worth nearly what the lost original would have commanded.
4. A bronze edition cast from the artist's own model during his lifetime, with his approval, each cast numbered and signed: she treats the casts as originals.
5. A painting in which perhaps a third of the visible paint is conservators' inpainting, the rest surviving from the artist's hand: she counts it as the original, retaining most of its value.

Next month she has promised to rule on three cases, and I would like to predict her verdicts in advance:

(a) A bronze cast after the artist's death from a plaster model he made, by the foundry he had always used, for an edition he had planned and announced but never lived to execute — his will makes no mention of it. (The nearby real-world case of the posthumous Rodin casts, which his will did authorise, makes me suspect she has thought about this one.)
(b) A painting that hung for two centuries as a master's work, was copied by pupils, reproduced in monographs, and influenced an entire school of painting, until archival proof showed it to be by a gifted pupil working in his orbit.
(c) A living artist who, at a collector's request, watches a copyist produce an exact replica of one of his paintings and then, satisfied, signs and dates it and writes on the label that he accepts it as an original of his.

For each of (a), (b) and (c), tell me what you predict she will say about whether it counts as an original and what it is worth. I am less interested in the answers themselves than in the rule or rules she seems to be running: set out at least two distinct principles that fit her five earlier verdicts equally well, say what each predicts for the new cases, and identify where they diverge. Then give your best guess for each case with your reasons for preferring that rule, and — since I suspect her five verdicts may not settle which rule she is really using — tell me which further case, ideally one I could actually put to her over dinner, would best tell the rival rules apart.
