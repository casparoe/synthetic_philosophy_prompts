I am three months from delivering a novel to my publisher and deep in revision, and I have become convinced that the hardest decisions in front of me are not decisions about prose at all but about the distribution of moral attention: which characters receive interiority, which are seen only from the outside, whose suffering is dwelt upon and whose is dispatched in a subordinate clause. A friend who teaches philosophy tells me the relevant body of thought is care ethics, and I have gathered enough to attach the usual names to it — Gilligan and the different voice, Noddings on the thick caring-for of particular persons versus the thinner caring-about, Ruddick on maternal thinking, Held on seeing persons as relational rather than as isolated units who meet to bargain. My friend also warns me that care ethics distrusts codification, holding that turning responsiveness into a rulebook is precisely the mistake the justice tradition makes. So I am asking for something its own advocates might call a category error, and I would like you to be candid about that tension rather than dissolve it.

The concrete situation is this. My novel follows an adult daughter who becomes her mother's caregiver, the home health aide she hires and gradually displaces, and the mother, who has dementia and is frequently cruel to both women. As the draft stands, the daughter receives full interiority, the mother's mind is rendered in fragments, and the aide exists largely as a function — competent, warm, interchangeable. In revision I must decide, scene by scene: who is granted a point of view; whose needs are narrated and whose merely implied; when tedious bodily labor — bathing, repeating, reassuring — deserves pages and when it should be compressed in the name of pacing; and how to render the mother's cruelty without either excusing it through her condition or reducing her to an emblem of decline.

Please write a procedure I can run on each scene during these three months: the questions to ask in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure should tell me to accept a cost to pacing or to the reader's sympathy rather than evade it, and where it must hand the decision back to me because it turns on what kind of novel this is, not on care. Then run it on three cases: the bathing scenes I am inclined to cut as repetitive; the aide, whom I suspect of functioning as furniture; and a scene in which the mother says something unforgivable to the daughter. I anticipate that the procedure will return at least one answer I will not like — most likely that the tedium I want cut is exactly what deserves the reader's time, or that the aide must become particular even at the cost of the daughter's primacy — and I would rather hear that than have the procedure defer to my preferences. Please state plainly what the procedure cannot settle, and where following it risks turning the novel into an illustration of a theory rather than a work of fiction.
