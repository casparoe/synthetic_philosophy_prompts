I've been in a running argument with my brother about whether our dad "still knows how to sail" even though a stroke took the fine motor control and he hasn't been on the water in a decade, and I realize the whole thing is happening one case at a time with no theory in sight. So before I read another word on knowing-how vs knowing-that, I want the spec. What does any adequate account of knowledge-how have to deliver? Write the desiderata list.

Cases and stuff it has to handle, at minimum, and you'll see why I picked these: the ski instructor who teaches flawless technique but literally cannot ski herself; my dad; the pianist with amnesia who can still play (does she know how to play, or just retain the ability without knowing anything?); the chicken-sexer who can't explain what she's tracking; the improviser who can produce a move she's never rehearsed in a situation she's never faced; my cousin who memorized every rule of German grammar and every phrasebook line and still freezes at the bakery counter; and the chess app on my phone that will tell you, in fluent prose, exactly why your knight belongs on e5, and then lose to a mediocre club player when it actually plays. That last one my brother thinks settles the whole debate, and I half agree and half think it's a trick case.

Also want the distinctions the theory can't be allowed to collapse: knowing how to do something vs merely being able to do it (the sleepwalker can walk, the drunk can drive us home); knowing how vs knowing that the way is how; skill you can articulate vs skill you can only exercise; and whether "knowing how to do A on command" and "knowing how to do A when it comes up" are one thing or two. For each requirement say what case or consideration forces it, and be honest about which are real constraints and which are just camp flags — like, is "explains why knowledge-how is a competence that can be possessed without being exercised" a genuine desideratum or is that already Ryle's thumb on the scale? Same question for "preserves the intuition that knowledge-how is propositional and therefore teachable in one shot."

The purpose to fix adequacy against: deciding when an attribution of know-how is true, for everything from praising my dad to hiring to arguing about machines. Not metaphysics of mind for its own sake.

Two things I especially want flagged: (1) whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — e.g., if you demand it handle the chicken-sexer AND demand know-how be fully propositional, is that already too tight? That's the part I most want to see. (2) Which single requirement does the most work excluding candidate theories.

Then, briefly, score the two obvious contenders against your own list: the intellectualist line (knowing how to do A is just knowing a proposition about the way to do A) and the ability/anti-intellectualist line (it's a complex of dispositions, not knowledge of facts at all). Keep that part short — the scoring is for me, the criteria are where I want you to actually think.

Throughout, tell me how confident you are in each requirement being a real one vs a prejudice, and single out the point in your answer you're least sure of. I'd rather have honest uncertainty than a clean-looking list that pretends the middle cases aren't a mess.
