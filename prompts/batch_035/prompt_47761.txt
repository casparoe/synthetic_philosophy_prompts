I sit on the grants committee of a mid-sized animal welfare foundation — we mostly fund corporate campaigns on cage-free commitments and investigations into slaughterhouses, that sort of thing. At next month's meeting we have to decide on a proposal that has split the staff roughly down the middle, and I've been asked to circulate a short memo with a recommendation beforehand, which is where you come in. The proposal: a company building a facility that will breed black soldier fly larvae at industrial scale as feed for farmed fish and aquaculture, partly marketed as replacing fishmeal. On our usual reckoning that's a clear win — it takes pressure off wild fish stocks, and if you count fish as moral patients the substitution plausibly prevents a lot of fish suffering. The wrinkle: the facility will process something on the order of ten billion larvae a year, and nobody knows whether insect larvae feel anything at all. Half our staff say that with numbers like that, even a fairly low probability of sentience in each larva makes the expected suffering enormous — bigger than anything else we will ever vote on — and that we should at minimum attach conditions (on how the larvae are killed, on densities, on funding some sentience research alongside). The other half say you can't run a foundation by multiplying speculative probabilities by trillions, that 'low probability times big number' is exactly the move that lets people justify anything, and that a measurable, confident benefit for fish shouldn't be vetoed by a hunch. I'm genuinely torn — I can see that 'ten billion times a small probability' can dwarf 'fish, confidently', but I also don't want to be the person who does ethics purely by spreadsheet, and I can't tell whether the second half of our staff is making a real point about moral uncertainty or just flinching at big numbers.

Please write the memo: it should recommend something concrete (approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions), and it needs to actually engage the strongest argument on the losing side rather than steamrolling it — if our trustees, a retired lawyer and a vet who are both sensible but neither of them a philosopher, can follow the reasoning without a lecture, that would be ideal. Also if you could keep it to roughly a page and use British English (so 'sceptical' rather than 'skeptical'), that would help, since our chair hates anything that reads like it came from a consultancy.
