I'm a PhD student in health psychology and I keep bumping into care ethics in my reading on family caregiving, and I've realized I don't actually know what I think "care" is, morally speaking. Like everyone cites Gilligan and Noddings and the "different voice" stuff but when I try to apply it to actual cases my gut verdicts feel all over the place. So here they are, and I want to know if there's some principle behind them or if I'm just inconsistent.

My cases and verdicts:

1. I drive two hours every weekend to sort out my mom's medications because she can't manage them alone. This is paradigm care, obviously, and if I stopped doing it to free up weekends I'd be failing her, full stop.

2. A hospice nurse I interviewed for my dissertation is scrupulously attentive to her patients but insists she doesn't "get attached" -- she'd say her job is competence plus respect, not emotional involvement. I think what she does IS care, genuine care, even though the caring feeling isn't there.

3. My friend cut off her brother, who has a serious mental illness and constantly demands rescue. Everyone in her family says abandoning family is the opposite of care. I think she's allowed to do it, maybe even that continuing as things were would have been worse for both of them.

4. An aunt who does endless favors for everyone but keeps a mental ledger and brings up everything she's done for you at every holiday. Her behavior is exactly what the care checklists say to do, but something feels off -- I don't want to say it's not care, but it's care that's gone sour somehow.

5. Donating to a charity that pays for strangers' home care, versus doing the same amount of hands-on helping for a neighbor I barely know. My gut says the neighbor one is "more caring" even though the charity helps more people, and I'm a little embarrassed by that verdict but I can't shake it.

6. The caseworker at the county benefits office who processes my mom's paperwork slowly but completely by the book, with no warmth at all. I want to say the system displays something like care-in-the-aggregate even though no individual act in the chain feels caring.

7. The weird one: my grandmother talks to the AI assistant on her tablet like it's a companion, and honestly it "listens" better than I do some weeks. Something in me says this is not care at all, it's a fake of care, but I can't easily say why, given that the nurse in case 2 also doesn't feel anything.

The tension I can already see: cases 1, 4, and 7 seem to make the relationship or motivation essential to care, while cases 2 and 6 seem to make skilled responsive practice enough, with the feelings irrelevant. And case 5 pulls toward partiality while case 3 shows partiality/attachment can license withdrawal. Can anyone knit this into one principle, or a couple of candidate principles? Tell me which of my verdicts has to get thrown out if they can't all be saved -- I suspect it's 2 or 7 that has to go, but I'd like to see the argument. And then tell me what the principle says about a case I didn't list: my mom has started not recognizing me, and I'm trying to figure out in advance whether continuing the weekend drives still counts as caring for HER, or whether at some point it becomes caring for my memory of her, and whether there's even a difference. That last bit is the part I actually need for my dissertation, so don't skip it.
