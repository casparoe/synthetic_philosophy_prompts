I teach a 300-level epistemology and rational choice seminar — 14 students, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel the problem with transformative choices: the ones where you can't know what the experience will be like until you've had it, and having it changes what you value, so it's not even clear how to model the decision, let alone solve it. I want them to hit the wall themselves, not be told about it: ideally they each make a real small-stakes decision on behalf of their future self, some transformation intervenes, and they have to live with the earlier choice afterwards. Rules, materials, and a payoff scheme in tokens (no cash), and then a debrief that separates the two distinct problems — the epistemic one, where you can't simulate the experience in advance, and the utility one, where your current preferences can't rank futures in which your preferences themselves are different — and maps specific moments in the game onto each. Include a second round where they know the transformation is coming and know its type, but not its content, so they can see whether knowing the category of change helps at all. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the author or book title until the debrief is done. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about the main thing the activity fakes — in the game the future self's preferences presumably arrive as something readable, which is precisely what real transformative experience denies you.
