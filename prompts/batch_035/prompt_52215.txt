At a reception after a regional wetlands conference, I spent an hour listening to two taxonomists argue about a listing decision, and I could not tell who won. I'm the planner responsible for the revision of our state's wildlife action plan, and I have to put a single number in that document, but I don't have a side in the fight and wouldn't know how to pick one. The case involves a small songbird that occurs in two forms: a coastal form tied to salt marsh, and a common interior form. The two meet along a river valley, where they interbreed; the hybrids are fully fertile; but the hybrid zone is only about twenty-five kilometers wide and, so far as anyone can tell from eighty years of museum records, it has not moved. The forms differ in plumage, song, and bill shape, and in genome-wide data each comes out as a separate lineage at the overwhelming majority of loci, with a small number of genes evidently exchanged. If the two forms are counted as separate species, the coastal one becomes eligible for protection its habitat loss would amply justify; if they are lumped, the populous interior form absorbs it and nothing gets listed.

The exchange ran as follows. (1) Ruth, a field ornithologist, opened by invoking the classic concept descended from Mayr: species are groups of actually or potentially interbreeding populations, reproductively isolated from other such groups. These two forms interbreed and produce fertile offspring in nature, she said, so they are one species, and splitting them is a preference dressed up as a finding. (2) Marcus, a phylogenomicist, replied that her criterion delivers verdicts that flip with the sampling grid: stand in the valley and the birds interbreed; stand anywhere else and they do not. What actually matters, he said, is that two diagnosably distinct lineages have held their identity for centuries despite full contact — continued distinctness under contact is precisely the phenomenon species boundaries exist to explain, and it is present here. (3) Ruth answered that his standard proves far too much: regional human populations were morphologically diagnosable and maintained their distinctness for millennia despite contact and migration, and no taxonomist proposes carving our species up on that basis; so diagnosability plus stability cannot be sufficient, and only something like the interbreeding criterion blocks that road. (4) Marcus said the block is illusory: gene flow is a continuum, accepted species pairs hybridize routinely — wolves with coyotes is the stock example — so her criterion has a cutoff only because no one has looked for one; and worse, it cannot apply at all to the majority of life, since bacteria and many plants never interbreed, so a concept that covers a minority of organisms is in no position to define the rank. (5) Ruth, pressed, fell back on pluralism: the word species legitimately tracks different standards on different branches of life, one criterion for outcrossing organisms, another for clonal ones, because the name marks comparable stages in the divergence of lineages rather than a single thing. (6) Marcus closed by pulling the rug out entirely: if species means eight different things, then counting species, comparing diversity across groups, and hanging legal protection on the word are all category mistakes; perhaps the honest view is that there are only lineages, and the species rank is like the genus rank — real lineages, arbitrarily chosen level — in which case the question they had been fighting over, one species or two, was the wrong question from the start.

Neither would budge, and the plan goes to print in the spring. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six steps, say whether the speaker is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, and justify each verdict briefly. Then tell me who, if anything, is more right overall. Finally, address what I suspect both of them are missing: does (6) actually follow from (5), and if Marcus's "wrong question" conclusion were correct, would that leave my committee with anything legitimate to decide about the coastal form, or would it merely relocate the same dispute one level down?
