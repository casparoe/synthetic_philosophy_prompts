I've been going in circles in a long-running argument with a friend about Newcomb's problem — he two-boxes and thinks anyone who doesn't is confused about causation, I'm not so sure — and what strikes me is that every discussion we can find jumps straight to scoring the theories: causal decision theory two-boxes, evidential decision theory one-boxes but tells you to decline a pleasurable habit on the grounds that enjoying it would be evidence of a lesion, the updateless or functional-decision-theoretic crowd one-box and pay out in counterfactual muggings. Nobody stops to say what a decision theory is *for*. So before we argue about the contenders any further, I want the specification, not the scorecard.

Here's the kind of thing the specification has to cover, at least as I see it. A general theory of choice under uncertainty has to say something sensible about Newcomb's problem; about the Death in Damascus case (you meet what you take to be Death in the Damascus marketplace, he seems surprised to see you, you infer he was expecting you in Aleppo, and if he's a perfect predictor of your choice then whatever your reasoning, you end up in the city he's in); about counterfactual mugging, where you're asked to pay $100 on learning of a fair coin's past outcome that was used to decide whether to offer you a windfall; about medical-Newcomb and smoking-lesion cases; and about whether an agent should be able to profit by precommitting, and whether its future self should endorse that precommitment. Beyond the cases, there are structural demands people put on theories — that the agent never pay to avoid free information, that it not be exploitable by agents who can model it, that its counterfactuals be well-defined, that it be something a bounded agent could actually implement. Which of these are genuine requirements and which are just the shibboleths of particular camps, I honestly don't know.

So: write out the desiderata for an adequate decision theory — the cases it must get right, the structural conditions it must satisfy, the distinctions it must respect — with the motivating consideration behind each, and mark which you take to be non-negotiable versus which are matters of taste. Then the part I most want: tell me whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. My suspicion is that the demands of getting Newcomb and Death in Damascus right, of never paying to avoid costless information, and of the agent endorsing its own precommitments cannot all be met by one theory — but I'd like to see it argued either way rather than assumed. Keep the treatment of the existing contenders brief and put the weight on the criteria; we'll do the scoring ourselves once we know what we're scoring against.
