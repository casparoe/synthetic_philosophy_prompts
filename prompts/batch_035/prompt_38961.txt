My friend is a paid participant in a multi-session study on choices under risk, and she's been telling me her answers between sessions. Every choice is between two gambles, each described on its own screen as a list of prizes with probabilities — never as a table aligning states across the two options, and never as a shared ticket. Her record so far:

1. $1,000,000 for sure vs. (89%: $1M, 10%: $5M, 1%: $0) — she took the sure million.
2. (11%: $1M, 89%: $0) vs. (10%: $5M, 90%: $0) — she took the second: the Allais pattern.
3. $3,000 for sure vs. (80%: $4,000, 20%: $0) — she took the sure $3,000.
4. (25%: $3,000, 75%: $0) vs. (20%: $4,000, 80%: $0) — she took the second: the common-ratio pattern.

The next session includes the pair you get from her Allais choices by replacing the common 89% branch that paid $1M for sure with an independent 50/50 flip between $1M and $0:

E — an 11% chance of $1M, otherwise an independent coin flip paying $1M or $0 (overall: 55.5% $1M, 44.5% $0);
F — a 1% chance of $0, a 10% chance of $5M, otherwise the same coin flip (overall: 10% $5M, 44.5% $1M, 45.5% $0).

Will she choose E or F? I have three candidate rules that each seem to rationalize her four choices so far, and I can't tell them apart: (i) a certainty effect, on which she is an expected-utility maximizer except that an option paying a prize with probability 1 gets an extra pull; (ii) Gul-style disappointment aversion, where each gamble is penalized by the probability-weighted shortfall of its outcomes below the gamble's own certainty equivalent; (iii) an inverse-S probability weighting of the cumulative prospect theory kind, with the value function left free. Please work out what each rule predicts for E vs. F, given what her four choices pin down — and fail to pin down — about her utilities and parameters; give your best guess with reasons; and specify the one further pair, with exact prizes and probabilities, that would most cleanly separate the three rules. Throughout, please keep clear the line between what is settled (what the independence axiom actually forbids, the fixed functional forms) and what is open or parameter-dependent, and don't present any rule's prediction as more determinate than your working-out makes it; if the record so far leaves a rule's prediction indeterminate, say so plainly.
