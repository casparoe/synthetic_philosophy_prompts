My flatmate reckons she's solved the lottery paradox, and worse, she thinks the solution proves something huge. The argument itself:

(1) It's rational to believe a claim when its probability is high enough — 999,999 out of a million counts as high enough. (2) In a fair million-ticket lottery with exactly one winner, the probability that ticket #1 loses is that high, and the same holds for each ticket taken on its own. (3) So it's rational to believe of each ticket, individually, that it will lose. (4) If it's rational to believe p and rational to believe q, then it's rational to believe p-and-q — and you can keep doing this through the whole million tickets. (5) So it's rational to believe that every ticket loses. (6) But it's also rational to believe that some ticket wins — that one has probability 1. (7) So rational belief can be jointly inconsistent, which is absurd; therefore (1) is false, and no probability threshold is ever good enough for rational belief.

And here's the bit where she looses me. She says the same argument works for everything, not just lotteries: my belief that my passport is in the drawer, that I locked the door this morning, that Wellington is the capital of New Zealand — each is some shade below probability 1, so if any threshold short of certainty licenses belief, then my entire belief system is rationally inconsistent; and if no threshold short of certainty licenses belief, then no one has ever rationally believed anything, including her own premise (2). She's also concluded that no scientific theory can be rationally believed, since every theory is a giant conjunction of individually-less-than-certain claims, and she's started refusing to say "I think" before sentences on the grounds that she hasn't earned it.

I'm fairly sure the argument doesn't license that last paragraph — or at least not all of it — but I can't point to where it stops licensing things. Is the problem at (4), where closure gets applied a million times? Is it that (6) and (7) quietly move between "rational to believe" and "ought to believe"? Or was the trap already set at (1), in the word "enough"? And she keeps dragging in the preface thing too — she says she rationally believes each individual sentence of her thesis, yet also rationally believes that at least one sentence in it is false (she's read enough theses to trust that), and she claims this shows the paradox was never really about lotteries at all, so a "lotteries are special because the structure is adversarial" answer won't cut it.

Which step would you reject, and what do the standard escape routes — dropping closure, dropping the threshold picture, accepting inconsistent belief sets, whatever else people actually defend — each have to pay for it? She wants to keep whichever premise makes her thesis defensible; I just want to know whether she has to go back to saying "I think."
