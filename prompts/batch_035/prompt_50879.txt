I'm taking a philosophy of mind course in which our two instructors have argued past each other all semester about the paradox of phenomenal judgment. One is a committed epiphenomenalist: phenomenal properties are nonphysical and make no causal difference to the physical world, so our thoughts and reports about our experiences cannot be caused by those experiences. The other is a physicalist who holds that our judgments about experience are caused by the experiential states they are about, and who takes the epiphenomenalist to owe us an explanation of why our reports track our phenomenology at all. Write the joint statement they should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration: a numbered section of claims both can sign (about the reality of phenomenal consciousness, the explanatory gap, the plain fact that we make and communicate judgments about our experiences, and whatever they can agree the paradox actually consists in); a table of their exact residual disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording of its own view; and the cruxes—subquestions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. As one candidate case, consider subjects under opioid analgesia who report that the pain is still present in full intensity but that it no longer bothers them: both sides should be able to say in advance whether this divergence between intensity reports and unpleasantness reports is evidence about how reports relate to phenomenal character, and what each would expect such cases to show. Finally, say whether any part of their dispute is verbal—for instance, over whether causal relevance requires being an intervening cause or only standing in law-governed counterfactual dependence—and if so, which part and why.
