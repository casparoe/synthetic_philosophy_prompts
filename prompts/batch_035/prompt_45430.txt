Been in a semester-long argument with my distributive justice reading group and I finally wrote down where I actually stand so they can tear into it. Before I send it round, I want a proper consistency check. Here's the package:

1. Resources, not welfare. The currency of justice is some kind of resource bundle (Rawlsian primary goods, Dworkin's equality of resources, whatever), and reported satisfaction should not enter the metric. Someone who's cheap to please doesn't thereby deserve less, and someone with cultivated expensive tastes doesn't thereby deserve more. I think the expensive tastes argument against welfare equality basically works.

2. That said, disability clearly matters, and not just as "needing a wheelchair is like having expensive tastes." Someone born paraplegic needs more resources to reach the same level of functioning as an able-bodied peer, and justice has to cover that gap. This is why I've always found Sen's complaint about primary goods convincing — Rawls' bundle is too coarse to handle differing conversion factors between resources and actual lives people can lead.

3. Inequalities should track choice. My slogan is Dworkin's: the distribution should be ambition-sensitive and endowment-insensitive. If you chose it, you absorb it; if you didn't, you don't.

4. But desert has no place in justice. Nobody deserves their income because they worked hard, natural talents are morally arbitrary, and Rawls was right that the distribution of abilities is neither just nor unjust, it's just a fact that institutions have to deal with.

5. Adaptive preferences are a real problem. The fact that people who've been dealt a bad hand report being content doesn't mean justice has been done, and a theory that reads off the metric from what people happen to feel about their lives is broken in exactly that way.

6. And yet I'm suspicious of the thick capability list approach — the state drawing up a catalogue of central human functionings and making sure everyone can achieve them feels like it writes a particular conception of the good life into the theory, and I want the state to stay neutral between conceptions of the good. I'm a liberal about this.

There's also a case that's been bugging me and I don't know where my own view puts it: someone with a mild but treatment-resistant depression who needs, say, double the resources — therapy, meds, accommodations — to function at the level everyone else gets by default. On my (1) this looks like an expensive taste of the mind, and it feels grotesque to say the rest of us owe her nothing extra. On (2) it looks like a disability, which is what my gut says. I honestly can't tell if my package even delivers a verdict here.

So: audit this. Which of the six commitments actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they're in tension but aren't, and where does the depression case fall out? For each genuine tension, tell me how severe it is and what the cheapest fix would be and what that fix costs me elsewhere, then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad.

And look, if you think I've misremembered one of these positions, or that the whole "currency of justice" framing is confused, or that my two instincts about the depression case are just incompatible and no clever distinction saves me — say that plainly instead of diplomatically making everything fit. I'd rather find out now than in front of the reading group.
