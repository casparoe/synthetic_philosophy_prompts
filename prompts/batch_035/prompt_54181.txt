I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course in social epistemology, and a few weeks ago we covered the distinction between epistemic bubbles and echo chambers: in a bubble, relevant outside voices are merely absent, so exposure to those voices can pop it, whereas an echo chamber pre-emptively discredits outsiders, so that the very same exposure comes back as further confirmation. The reading we did argued that echo chambers are much the worse of the two, and that made intuitive sense to me at the time. Since then, though, I've been keeping a list of actual communities I know about or belong to and recording my gut verdicts about how epistemically damaged each one is, and I've found that my verdicts don't line up with the structural distinction at all — which makes me suspect the structure of a community is not really what my judgments are tracking. I'd like your help doing something like reflective equilibrium on my list: tell me whether there is a single principle behind these verdicts, or whether some of them simply have to go.

Here are the cases with my verdicts. A conspiracy forum I lurked on for a seminar paper teaches members that anyone who disagrees publicly is either a paid shill or hopelessly brainwashed — this strikes me as the most epistemically wrecked community I've ever seen, and the pre-emptive discrediting is obviously central to why. My grandmother, by contrast, reads only one newspaper and has never encountered the contrary views it omits; she seems to me to be in a bubble, and mildly damaged, but the problem looks fixable by mere exposure. A third case already starts to wobble: working climate scientists as a community rarely engage with blog posts by amateurs disputing basic results, and I don't think they're epistemically damaged at all — in fact I think individual scientists who spend their time reading denialist blogs are, if anything, wasting time they could spend on evidence. Worse still for the structural story: my department's number theorists tell grad students not to read unsolicited proofs of famous open conjectures, and I think that's perfectly sensible — it is wholesale discrediting of outsiders, but calibrated to a track record. And then there's a support community for survivors of abuse that I know through a friend, which warns new members that their families will try to minimize what happened to them; structurally this looks like textbook pre-emptive discrediting, and yet I think the warning is usually accurate and the community is on balance epistemically valuable, even good for its members. The case I keep going back and forth on is my own climbing gym's nutrition group, where the moderators tell people to ignore dietitians who warn about a supplement the group loves, on the grounds that dietitians are captured by the food industry — I can't tell whether this is the number theorists' calibrated discount or the conspiracy forum's self-sealing one, and I have the uncomfortable feeling that my uncertainty is exactly what an insider to a real echo chamber would report.

So here is my question for you. My tentative principle is something like: pre-emptively discrediting outsiders is epistemically fine when the discredit tracks a genuine, evidence-responsive track record — the number theorists would revise their policy if amateur proofs started turning out correct, and the survivors' warning is grounded in repeated experience — and epistemically corrosive when the discrediting is instead doing protective work for a doctrine. But I can see two problems, and I'd like you to address both. First, the conspiracy forum's members would sincerely say their shill-detection is evidence-responsive too, so from the inside the two principles may be indistinguishable; is there a non-circular way to draw the line, or am I just labeling communities bad when I already believe they're wrong? Second, my principle seems to imply that an insulated community's being epistemically damaged is at least partly independent of whether its beliefs are true — so a cult that has, by dumb luck, mostly true beliefs would still count as damaged, while a lucky, accurate, tightly closed group of cranks would not, and I'm not sure I accept that. Please state the principle you think best fits my verdicts as precisely as you can, flagging which clause each case motivates; tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save and whether the cost of saving them is worth it; and then apply it to two cases I haven't listed: a mutual-improvement startup whose recruits are told that friends who call it a pyramid scheme simply don't understand the business model, and a secessionist village that has been factually right about every local prediction for a decade while teaching its children that the national press lies about everything. I want your actual adjudication, not a survey of the literature — no citations needed, just the reasoning.
