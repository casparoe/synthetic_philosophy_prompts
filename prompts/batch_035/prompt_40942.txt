I've ended up on the student committee that advises our university on which certifications to require across its procurement — coffee, tea, cotton for the merchandise — and the fair trade question has split us down the middle, partly because I'm taking a course that spent half a term on Marx and now I can't stop seeing the syllabus everywhere. The scheme we're being asked to endorse is the familiar one: certified producers, organised as smallholder cooperatives, receive a guaranteed minimum price plus a social premium; audits check for forced and child labour and impose basic wage floors on any hired labour; and a consumer-facing label marks out this chain as the fair one. The puzzle I keep circling is what theory of exploitation the whole practice presupposes. As far as I can tell, it treats exploitation as a defect of exchange — prices too low, intermediaries too powerful — remediable by fixing the terms of trade, and it treats smallholder ownership of the means of production as the cure. Yet in the same breath the scheme certifies plantations employing wage labourers, so long as a wage floor is met, which seems to presuppose that wage labour as such is not the problem, only underpaid wage labour — exactly the claim a Marxist account would deny. And the label logic seems to presuppose that exploitation is a property of particular commodity chains, one that a consumer can identify and exit from, rather than a feature of the system as a whole — which is presumably why half my classmates call the whole thing a marketing exercise and the other half call it the best instrument available.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory of exploitation for me, say whether the scheme's various elements — the price floor, the cooperative governance requirement, the wage floor on hired labour, the consumer label — presuppose one consistent theory or several stitched uneasily together, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that I can see which theories of exploitation would actually license it rather than merely which ones it offends. In particular I want to know whether any recognisably Marxist view could endorse the scheme as it stands, or whether its commitments point only to fairness-in-exchange theories of the sort that treat a low price as the disease and a floor as the cure. Then tell me what would have to change in the certification rules if we followed the presupposed theory to its conclusion — would a certification genuinely built on a structural view of exploitation resemble a wage floor at all, or would it mandate things like profit-sharing and worker control of the enterprise? Bring in alienation where it genuinely helps: our committee was told that the cooperative structure restores dignity to producers, and I suspect that claim smuggles in a view about what dignity at work consists in that none of the audits ever checks — if so, say what that view is and whether the practice could coherently test for it. Please write in British English.
