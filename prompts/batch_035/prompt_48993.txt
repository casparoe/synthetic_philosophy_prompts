I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rational choice theory, meeting for one 80-minute session with 15 students who have already covered expected utility theory and the basics of preference axioms. Next week's topic is diachronic rationality, and I want to open it with the money pump: the idea that an agent whose preferences cycle (A over B, B over C, and C over A) can be relieved of their entire endowment by a sequence of individually acceptable trades, each costing a small fee. Design an in-class trading game that makes the students feel this rather than just see it on the board. My thought is that each student gets an endowment and a preference profile, some with cyclic preferences and some with transitive ones, while one or two students play profit-seeking traders who may propose any pairwise trade with a small fee attached. But please work out the details yourself: the materials, the exact mechanics of offers and acceptance, a payoff scheme using something other than real money (candy, tokens exchangeable for grade credit, whatever works), a timeline with a fixed number of trading rounds, and how you'd prevent the transitive-preference students from being bored while the cyclic ones get drained.

One thing I want built in deliberately: at some point the students with cyclic preferences should start to notice the pattern and refuse further trades, and I want the design to allow that rather than block it — because the moment an agent refuses a trade they'd otherwise accept, purely because they foresee where the sequence leads, we've stumbled into the sophisticated-choice defense against money pumps and the question of whether the argument even works against a forward-looking agent. The debrief should map onto that: which assumptions the pump needed (repeated opportunities, no transaction costs beyond the fees, the agent evaluating each trade in isolation), what exactly the refusal of the savvy students shows, and whether refusing makes them irrational in a different way (they're now passing up trades that are, considered one by one, still advantageous to them).

Please include the standard debrief material — mapping specific moments in the game to specific steps of the formal argument — plus two things facilitators always need: a frank list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (confusion about rules, traders who can't find the cyclic students in time, students who trade randomly out of boredom) with suggested fixes, and an honest note on which part of the philosophical issue the game cannot reproduce no matter how it's staged — what I'd have to supply by lecture or discussion afterward. The point of the session is not "cyclic preferences lose you candy"; it's whether diachronic consistency is a requirement of rationality or just a prudential hedge against predatory counterparties, and I need to know where the game stops and the argument begins.
