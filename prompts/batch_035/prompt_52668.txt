You are a philosopher of biology with deep familiarity with the species problem — biological, morphological, phylogenetic, and evolutionary species concepts and their known failure cases. Please think carefully and reason step by step; begin by briefly brainstorming the candidate concepts before committing.

I'm an invertebrate taxonomist, and I've come to suspect my species judgments follow no coherent principle. Here they are. I treat the Ensatina salamander complex around the Californian ring as one species, even though the populations at the ends of the ring, where the arms meet, do not interbreed where they co-occur. I treat the cichlids of Lake Victoria as many separate species, even though they hybridize at a substantial rate and some forms may have arisen by hybridization within the lake. I treat bdelloid rotifers — entirely asexual for millions of years — as perfectly ordinary species, sorted by morphology. I treat the wolves, coyotes, and (arguably) golden jackals of North America as separate species despite extensive, ongoing, evolutionarily consequential gene flow among them — yet I'm uneasy about that one. And in my own group, I have two beetle "species" that are morphologically distinct and broadly sympatric, though I have never checked whether they interbreed, and I confess I would keep calling them species even if they turned out to interbreed freely, because their genitalia and microhabitat differ so cleanly.

My tentative principle is something like: a species is the largest lineage that shares a distinctive evolutionary fate. Test it against my verdicts, and repair or replace it as needed. Where two of my verdicts genuinely conflict — and I suspect the ring species and the cichlids do — tell me which one to give up and why. Then tell me what the repaired principle says about two cases I haven't decided: whether the two forms of a mosquito that exploit different human hosts in the same cities count as one species or two, and whether I should recognize species among clonal lineages in a fossil bed where I can see morphology drift continuously through time. Finish by stating plainly which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, and what that costs it.
