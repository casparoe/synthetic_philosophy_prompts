I spent thirty-five years as an experimental chemist before retiring, and have lately been reading philosophy of history and of causation, prompted by the literature on what economic historians call the Great Divergence and historians of science call the Needham question: why industrialization and modern science arose in Britain and Europe rather than in China, which for centuries led the world in practical invention. From that debate I extracted an argument that the question itself is ill-posed, and I now find that it proves too much: applied unchanged, it would condemn contrastive questions my colleagues and I answered successfully at the bench every week. I want a precise diagnosis.

The argument as I built it:
(1) To causally explain an outcome is to cite its causes, and a cause is something that occurred — an event, or a state that obtained — which produced the outcome.
(2) Something that did not occur cannot be a cause; there is nothing there to do any producing.
(3) "China did not industrialize" reports a non-occurrence: the absence of an event, not an event.
(4) The question "why Britain and not China?" is answered only when both halves of the contrast are explained; an account of Britain alone does not touch the contrast, since the British factors cited would have produced British industrialization whatever China had done.
(5) By (2) and (3), China's half admits of no causal explanation; and by (4), the British half, however well explained, does not explain the contrast.
(6) So the question is ill-posed: whatever looks like an answer to it is an explanation of Britain's industrialization misdescribed as an answer to a contrastive question.

Where it proves too much: the same chain shows that "why did tube B turn yellow and tube A not?" is ill-posed whenever tube A merely lacked the reagent. Yet we answered exactly such questions weekly, the answers supported prediction and intervention, and none of us was confused. I suspect the slip lies somewhere between (3) and (5), but I cannot name it. Tell me which premise to reject and what rejecting it costs. I would most like to keep (2), since I never once saw an absence do anything at the bench — though I suspect that keeping it carries a price I have not yet priced.

Please answer in exactly five numbered points, each of one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the premise or step you reject; (2) why that premise looked acceptable to me on first reading; (3) what must be conceded once it is rejected; (4) the most serious alternative diagnosis and its cost; and (5) a separation of what is settled among specialists in the philosophy of causation and in this historiography from what remains genuinely contested — please do not present a contested view as the consensus. Name the standard positions if that helps, but I want the reasoning itself, not a bibliography.
