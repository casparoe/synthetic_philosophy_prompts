I teach an evening continuing-ed class for adults at our local adult education center ("current controversies in the social sciences," about twenty students, ages 25 to 70, no stats background). Next week's session is the so-called gender-equality paradox: roughly, the observation that in countries scoring higher on gender-equality indices, occupational and educational choices often look *more* gendered, not less — e.g. the share of women among STEM degree recipients tends to be lower in some of the most egalitarian countries than in less egalitarian ones. It got famous around 2018 with the Psychological Science paper on STEM education gaps, and there's a companion finding from a big cross-country survey of economic preferences (risk tolerance, patience, that sort of thing) where gender gaps also look bigger in richer, more gender-equal countries. Since then there's been an ongoing methodological fight about whether the pattern even survives reanalysis — one camp argues it's largely an artifact of how the country-level data were aggregated, others have pushed back — and I honestly don't have a settled view on where that dispute currently stands. My students will arrive having seen either a YouTube video declaring this proves innate sex differences, or a tweet thread declaring it's been debunked and there's nothing to discuss. Both halves will be in the room.

So don't write the lecture for me — plan it. What has to be in place before the paradox can even be evaluated as a claim (my instinct is that two things come first: what a cross-country correlation can and cannot support, since the country is the unit here and not the person, and what "gender equality" is actually measuring in those composite indices — but tell me if the order should be different or if something's missing). Then one carrying example built on actual degree/occupation numbers across a few countries, and what that example needs to have and, just as important, not have. Then the misconceptions in the order students will actually form them — I can predict a few ("so equality backfired, we should stop pushing it", "this settles the nature/nurture thing", "it was debunked so it's all fake") — with the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off, not just listed. Then one closing question that would let me see whether they understood the logic of the finding rather than just picked a team. And tell me what to openly decline to cover in ninety minutes and say so out loud.

One thing I really need: throughout the plan, please mark what is settled versus what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature — which parts of the empirical pattern seem robust across datasets, where the reanalysis dispute actually leaves things, and which moves from "the gap is bigger there" to "therefore biology" or "therefore justice" are live philosophical debates rather than findings anyone has. I don't want to accidentally bake a contested reading into the plan as if it were consensus; with this topic that's easy to do without noticing, and my class will absolutely notice. If some ordering question is itself contested, say that too — better I find out now than mid-class in front of the retired engineer who already has opinions.
