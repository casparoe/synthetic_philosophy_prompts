I spent thirty-seven years as a freshwater ecologist, mostly concerned with nutrient loading and thermal stratification, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy — Hume's puzzles about causation, and the modern arguments over what it is for one thing to make another happen. A dispute at my lake association has now put that reading to a practical test, and I would appreciate advice on how to reason about it.

Our lake suffered algal blooms for years. Three summers ago, two things changed at once: the association, at considerable expense, installed circulation fans that mix the water column, and that same spring the township banned phosphorus fertilizers on lawns in the watershed, having been pressed to act after a bloom closed the beach. The next two summers the blooms were markedly milder. The board credits the fans and is weighing funding them for a neighboring lake; the township credits the ban; the local paper hedges. My neighbor, an engineer by training, says the whole matter reduces to one question: would the blooms have been as bad had the fans not run? He answers it by pointing to the earlier, bad summers. But I notice his comparison is not doing what he thinks: those summers differed from the recent ones in more ways than the fans, since the ban had not yet been passed; and, more subtly, in a hypothetical summer in which the fans never ran, I cannot say what else should be imagined to have gone differently along with them — perhaps a board frugal enough to decline the fans is also a board that lobbies less effectively for the ban, so the two changes are not independent even in the counterfactual. Each choice about what else to hold fixed seems to settle the answer to his question, which makes me doubt the question, as he poses it, has a single determinate answer.

Yet I also suspect we are not really asking his question at all. When the board debates the neighboring lake, we are not asking what would have been true of our own lake's past under some alternative course of events; we are asking what happens if one now reaches in and changes one thing about a system while leaving the rest of its setting alone — and the neighboring lake, incidentally, has almost no lawns around it, so there the fans and any fertilizer effects cannot be entangled the way they are here. In my working life we would have settled such questions with controls and replication, but history cannot be rerun. So I would like your help on three connected points. First, is my neighbor's question — what would have happened otherwise — and this other question — what happens if we deliberately change one thing while holding the rest fixed — one question in two moods, or two questions that only sometimes share an answer? Second, where does the responsibility lie for choosing what to hold fixed in a "would have" claim, and can that choice be made principled rather than rhetorical? And third, in practical terms, what should I tell the board about apportioning the credit between the fans and the ban, and about the kind of evidence we should demand before paying for fans on the second lake?
