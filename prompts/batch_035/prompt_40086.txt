Every spring my metaontology seminar reaches the week on quantifier variance, and every spring we stall on the same missing piece: we have all read verdicts declaring some dispute "merely verbal," but never a worked-out account of what makes one so. This year I want the specification before we read anyone's application of it. Write the desiderata that any adequate criterion for identifying verbal disputes must satisfy: the cases it must classify correctly — a dispute over whether an amateur cyclist's caffeine use counts as doping when the rulebook is silent; a dispute over whether a tomato is a fruit; the dispute, before chemistry settled it, over whether water is H2O or some other stuff could have been water; a dispute over whether mereological sums exist in addition to ordinary objects. Then the distinctions it must respect — merely verbal versus verbal-looking but substantive underneath; disputes dissolved by stipulation versus those dissolved only by discovery; disagreement between two parties versus confusion within one. And the constraints it must honor, chief among them neutrality: the criterion should not settle, in passing, the very first-order metaphysics it is meant to adjudicate. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, mark which are non-negotiable and which reflect the prejudices of one camp, and tell me where you suspect the list is jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect in particular that a criterion a non-metaphysician referee could apply without doing any metaontology, and a criterion that does not quietly take sides on whether quantifiers mean one thing across all competent speakers, cannot both be had. Close with only a brief scorecard of how Chalmers's method of elimination, Thomasson's easy ontology, and Sider's appeal to a joint-carving quantifier fare against the list; the criteria, not the scores, are what I want the seminar arguing about.
