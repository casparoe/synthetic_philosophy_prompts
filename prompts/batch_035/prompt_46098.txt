As a journalist who covers physics and the foundations of science for a general readership, I'm preparing a long-form essay on what it actually means to call something a "law of nature," and the essay turns on the dispute between Humeans and anti-Humeans about laws. The Humeans, following David Lewis's best-system analysis, hold that the laws are nothing over and above a particularly concise, powerful, and predictive summary of the total distribution of events in the universe — laws describe, they do not govern. The anti-Humeans, in the tradition of philosophers like David Armstrong, hold instead that laws are something in the world that constrains or produces events: a law of nature makes things happen, and could have been otherwise only if the world itself had been different. My essay is aimed at scientifically literate lay readers, not philosophers, and my editor has asked me to close with a section on what hangs on this for the public understanding of science.

Please describe the most important implications of this dispute for how a science journalist should frame claims in ordinary reporting. In particular, I'd like you to address the following, though I welcome further implications you think matter more:

1. Wording choices in routine copy. When a new result is announced, should a Humean journalist avoid saying that the universe "obeys" laws or that an experiment "violated" a law, given that on the best-system view nothing in the world enforces anything? What phrases would a Humean and an anti-Humean respectively reach for, and where would they mislead readers?

2. Anomalous results. When a measurement disagrees with an established law — as occasionally happens in particle physics or astronomy — the two views arguably counsel different coverage. On the governing view, a genuine anomaly is a violation in the world awaiting explanation; on the best-system view, it is a datum that may eventually force a re-carving of the summary system, or may simply be noise. How should a reporter convey this distinction without flattening it into either "the law is broken" or "nothing to see here"?

3. Laws outside physics. The special sciences — biology, ecology, economics — are full of generalizations hedged with ceteris paribus clauses that never quite hold. Does the Humean/anti-Humean dispute change how a journalist should treat claims of a "law of ecology" or an "economic law," and is one view friendlier to such claims than the other?

4. Discovery versus compression. On the best-system view, discovering a law looks partly like discovering that a certain compression of the data is unusually elegant — which can make lawhood seem partly relative to our interests and capacities as summarizers. If that is right, is there a sense in which a journalist should say that laws are partly about us, and if so, how can that be stated without sliding into the misleading idea that physical laws are human inventions or social conventions?

5. What the reader should take away. Finally, does anything of practical consequence for the public actually hang on this dispute — for instance, for how people reason about induction, about whether the future will resemble the past, or about whether nature has necessities that science uncovers rather than imposes? If the honest answer is that the dispute changes almost nothing in practice, say so plainly and explain why it nonetheless deserves a magazine essay.
