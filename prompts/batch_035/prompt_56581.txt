My metaphysics colleague has an unusually consistent-looking track record on constitution and persistence cases, and next week our reading group takes up several she has not yet ruled on. Her verdicts so far:

1. A statue and the piece of bronze constituting it — formed at a single moment from a shapeless ingot and destroyed, together with the bronze, at a single moment later, so that their entire careers coincide — she says there are two objects on the pedestal, not one.
2. A ship whose planks are replaced one at a time over a decade: the same ship before, during, and after the replacement.
3. The discarded planks, reassembled elsewhere into a ship: a new ship, a replica, not the original.
4. A gold ring melted into a bar: the gold survives, the ring does not.
5. A clenched fist: not an additional object alongside the hand; there is one thing there, the hand, in a state.
6. A cat, and she herself: no distinct object coincides with the cat or with her; she is one thing with her living body, and she denies that there is a separate person, or a separate lump of flesh, sharing her exact location.

The new cases are: an oak tree and the living wood composing it — one object or two?; a cat that has just died — given that no flesh-object coincided with the living cat, did a distinct matter-object come into existence at the moment of death?; and a chess club that has existed for fifty years while every founding member has left — is there a club plus a distinct aggregate-of-members, or just the club?

Predict her verdicts on the three new cases. Then identify the rule she seems to be running — worth weighing are a coincidence theory on which an artifact and its matter are distinct objects with different persistence conditions, a sortal-relative view on which identity claims are sortal-covered, and a broadly Aristotelian view on which organisms are unities of matter and form while artifacts and heaps are not — though consider other candidates too. Pay particular attention to the tension in her record: she counts two objects for statue-and-bronze but one for cat-and-flesh. Say which rules fit her verdicts equally well, where those rules diverge on the three new cases, which of the three would best discriminate between them, and, in case the tree, the corpse, and the club do not settle it, design a further case that would. Reason it through; you may attach names to positions if it helps, but do not rely on citations — only the argument matters.
