I'm in a philosophy of mind course and this week we read Dennett's thing on counterfeit people (the Atlantic piece from 2023, he also talked about it in interviews around then). The core analogy as I understood it: a really good counterfeit twenty is still counterfeit — passing doesn't make it real money, and counterfeiting currency is a crime precisely because good fakes circulate. He runs the same move on AI systems that talk like people: they can pass, that doesn't make them persons, and the frame we should have is counterfeiting people, which should alarm us roughly the way counterfeiting currency does. He even wants watermarking of the real ones, like the patterns they print on banknotes so machines can detect fakes.

In section it kind of fell apart though. One guy pushed back that the analogy backfires: for all practical purposes a perfect counterfeit bill just IS money, its value is entirely in how it functions in exchange, so if the AI functions as a person the analogy proves Dennett's opponent's point. Someone else said people aren't currency at all — there's no central mint that authorizes the genuine ones, every person is an original, so there's literally nothing to counterfeit, and if you made an exact copy of a person it would just be another person, not a fake one. And I couldn't tell whether those were real objections or just quibbles, since the analogy is obviously doing like three different jobs (metaphysics of what AI systems are, moral status, and then a policy point about regulating them).

And please take a definite position: does the counterfeit-people frame survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was it carrying the argument the whole time? If it doesn't survive, say what analogy would do the work better — I have a hunch the artwork forgery case is closer, but I can't tell if that actually helps him or just relocates the problem to "is an undetectable forgery of a painting still art."
