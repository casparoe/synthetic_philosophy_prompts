I'm the standards editor at a mid-sized regional news outlet, and the fight I keep losing is over the word "knew." Every week someone wants a headline or a lede like "Company knew pipes were leaching lead for a decade" or "Officials knew the dam report was flawed," the lawyers wince because "knew" reads as an accusation of intent, and the reporters push back that everyone talks like that and the documents obviously show the company was aware. What I need is a decision procedure the copy desk can actually run in ten minutes per headline: an ordered list of questions to ask about the subject's evidence and what was at stake for them, then questions about us — because I suspect the truth of a "knew" attribution isn't just about the subject, it's also about the context we're publishing it in — and for each outcome a safe fallback like "was aware," "was told," "had reason to suspect." It should say where it hands the call back to me because it's a values question and not a fact question, and it should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

For grounding: I took exactly one epistemology course a long time ago, so I half-remember this stuff. My recollection is that there are two distinct ideas that everyone runs together. One is that whether *you* know something can depend on the practical stakes for you — the famous thought experiments have someone deciding whether to stop at the bank on a Friday, where the same evidence counts as knowledge when the stakes are low and not when everything hinges on it. The other is that whether a "knows" claim comes out *true* can depend on the situation of the person doing the attributing — the standards for "knows" shift with the conversational context, so the same subject can be truly said to know by one speaker and not by another. If I have that right, they pull in different directions for us: one says look at the governor's stakes, the other says look at our headline's context, and a lot of our newsroom fights are people talking past each other because one side is running each theory. Please bake the distinction into the procedure rather than glossing over it, and tell me if I've misremembered.

Then run it on three cases so I can see it work. First: internal memos from 2011 where a company scientist flagged one small, ambiguous study linking our chemical to tumors, and the reporter wants "Company knew chemical was carcinogenic since 2011." Second: an aide mentioned bridge inspection worries to the governor in passing at a fundraiser, and the desk wants "Governor knew bridge was unsafe." Third, the one where I expect to be annoyed: a sympathetic whistleblower whose story everyone wants to tell as "she knew the product was dangerous," when what she actually had was one dataset and a strong hunch — if the procedure tells me to soften *that* one too, I want to see exactly why, and whether that's the procedure working or a place where it misfires.
