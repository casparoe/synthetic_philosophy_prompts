After a career in physical chemistry — thirty-odd years of reaction kinetics, mostly — I have drifted into retirement reading philosophy of science, and my reading group has lately been arguing about causation. One member, a convinced interventionist, keeps deploying the same image: to ask whether X causes Y is to ask what would happen if you reached in and turned the knob for X while leaving the rest of the panel alone; nature, on this picture, is an enormous apparatus with a knob for every variable, and causal claims are claims about which way the readouts move. He uses the picture to do real work. It is meant to explain why randomized experiments are the gold standard for causal inference, and to argue that where no knob could exist — the Big Bang, the electron's charge, the flowers dying because the gardener failed to water them — causal talk is at best a derivative shorthand and at worst a confusion. Another member, loyal to Lewis's counterfactual analysis with its possible worlds, thinks the picture can be repaired by idealizing the knob-turner into something superhuman rather than abandoning it. I trained as an experimentalist, so I want to believe the knob story; but I kept honest lab notebooks for three decades, and I remember how few of my systems had anything you could set from outside without wrecking the very structure you wanted to probe.

Please audit the analogy properly. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the knob, the hand, the panel of held-fixed background conditions, the readout — and then the places the mapping breaks, ranked by how much damage they do to my colleague's conclusion rather than by how colorful they are. I have in mind at least these candidate breakdowns, though I would welcome others: variables for which no intervention is even coherently definable (the value of the electron's charge, the expansion of the metric); causation by omission, where the knob appears to be a person's absence; and systems like thermoregulation in a living body, where "setting" a variable from outside destroys the feedback structure that made it the variable it was. Distinguish carefully between the breakdowns the interventionist has a standard answer to — the point that the manipulator need not be human, or even physically real — and those for which no answer is on offer. Then tell me plainly: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that non-manipulable causation is second-class survive, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if there is a better analogy available for what interventionism gets right, I would like to hear it — something from the laboratory, perhaps, that an old kineticist might recognize. Please hold the whole audit to 550 words or fewer; I would rather have a tight one than a sprawling one.
