Last night a friend and I spent close to four hours arguing about what emotions fundamentally are, and we ended exactly where we began: she was pressing something like William James's old line that an emotion just is a surge of bodily feeling — fear is the pounding heart and the trembling, nothing over and above it — while I kept pushing back that emotions carry evaluations, that anger is in some way a verdict about being wronged. We traded slogans until neither of us could tell whether we still disagreed, so I want to try something more disciplined. Below are my honest gut verdicts on a series of cases; I'd like to know what account of emotion, if any, they add up to. Please commit to a verdict of your own rather than surveying the judgment, perception, and feeling theories and leaving the final choice to me — say which of us was closer to right, and take the position under fire.

The cases: (1) I stand on a glass-floored skywalk; I've read the engineering reports and sincerely believe I'm safe, yet my palms sweat and my legs lock. I'd say I am genuinely afraid, full stop — not having fear-adjacent sensations while believing otherwise. (2) My anger at a friend for blanking my messages evaporated the instant I learned his phone had died; it didn't linger and require suppression, it simply went — which makes me suspect that anger was somehow made of the perceived slight. (3) I judge that eating insects is perfectly fine, even good for the planet, and I'd happily eat them; yet when I watched a chef batter-fry tarantulas I felt what I can only call real disgust, not a pale imitation of it. (4) Watching a film, I teared up at the father's deathbed scene; at the time I'd say I was genuinely sad, though I believed no one had died — and yet afterwards I wouldn't say I had grieved for him. (5) A trained actor who summons tears on cue for the tenth take: I do not say he is sad, however convincing his body. (6) Some mornings I wake up anxious and can find no object to point at — nothing is wrong that I can name — and I still count that as an emotion, not merely a mood or a symptom.

So here is my request. Tell me whether one account — emotions as judgments, as perceptions of value, or as feelings — captures all six verdicts; and if none does, say plainly which of my verdicts you would overturn and at what cost. Then tell me what your preferred account predicts about two cases I deliberately left out: whether jealousy that persists in someone who is fully convinced of her partner's fidelity counts as real jealousy, and whether a wave of doom brought on by a strong coffee should be called anxiety. I'd rather get a definite answer that costs me a verdict or two than a map of the options, so please don't hedge.
