I am taking a metaethics seminar, and we have just finished a unit on moral testimony — the question of whether one can come to know, or be justified in believing, moral propositions purely on the say-so of others. I want to practice defending optimism about moral testimony against a serious opponent, so I would like you to play a committed pessimist. Here is the position I want you to hold, and to hold well: moral deference is defective in a distinctive way, because the state that makes a moral judgment worth having is not bare true belief but moral understanding — a first-personal grasp of why the act is wrong — and understanding, unlike information, is not the kind of thing that can be handed over by assertion. Testimony can put me in a position to believe, but it cannot make the reasons for that belief my own. Two further constraints on your character, so that the debate is a real contest: you are a moral realist, so I cannot dismiss your pessimism as disguised relativism, and you accept without reservation that testimony transmits knowledge in non-moral domains — pharmacology, history, mathematics — so I cannot accuse you of despising deference as such. Be sharp, concede small points when I earn them, never concede the main thesis, and keep each reply to a single paragraph. Stay in character until I say stop.

My opening move: when my doctor tells me a drug is safe, I form a justified, even knowledgeable belief with no grasp whatsoever of the underlying pharmacology, and nobody thinks that belief is defective — deferring is simply what a rational patient does. If testimony-based belief in the absence of understanding is perfectly respectable there, you owe me an account of what is different in kind about the moral case, not just the insistence that it is different. And please do not answer that moral understanding is practically indispensable for right action: I can do the right thing for borrowed reasons, just as I swallow the pill on borrowed authority. Go.
