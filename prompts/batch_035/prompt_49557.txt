So, full disclosure: I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm writing this on a train, so forgive the typos. I'm about three quarters of the way through a book and I've got myself tangled up in terminology, and I suspect the tangle is actually interesting rather than just embarrassing.

The book is about a woman, Dora, who grows up in a small millenarian community out in the fens and eventually leaves. A lot of the novel is about what leaving does to her sense of what she knows — which of her beliefs were load-bearing, which ones were just furniture. My editor keeps saying Dora's community is "an echo chamber". My agent said it's more of an "epistemic bubble". I'd assumed these were just two fashionable names for the same internet-era thing — the filter bubble stuff everyone bangs on about — but a friend who actually reads philosophy tells me there's a genuine distinction, roughly: in an epistemic bubble you simply don't encounter the contrary voices, they're absent, like a hole in the map; whereas in an echo chamber you do encounter them, but you've been pre-loaded with a story that discredits them in advance — the outsider is "one of them", the fact-checker is "part of the machinery", the apostate who left is just "bitter". So on his telling the difference is omission versus active discrediting. Fine. But when I pushed him on it he got noticeably vague, which makes me think the distinction is shakier than the confident tone of these words suggests.

So here's what I want from you, and please actually argue it out rather than giving me a polite both-sides:

First, compare the two concepts properly. Are echo chambers and epistemic bubbles two different kinds of thing, or one phenomenon at different intensities? Is the bubble supposed to pop on contact with the missing information while the echo chamber doesn't — and if so, why exactly? A bubble that never meets the missing voice and an echo chamber that's already innoculated against it can look behaviourally identical from the outside, so what actually separates them, and does the seperation survive a case where a community does both at once (mine, for instance, both limits contact with outsiders AND trains people to distrust the outsiders they do meet)?

Second — and this is the bit I'd really like you to engage with rather than gently ignoring — I've got a novelist's wrinkle. In my book, Dora doesn't leave because she hears a debunking voice. There are plenty of apostates shouting at the fence, and they never get through. What breaks is something else: the community's story about her own life stops fitting. Her brother does something the doctrine can't metabolise, and the elders start improvising explanations that visibly strain — you can hear the narrative maintenance happening, like a fridge humming. Nobody argues her out of anything. It's more that the community's account of the world becomes effortful and audible as an account, rather than just being the world.

So: does that count as escaping an echo chamber? Is it even an epistemic process, or something more like a loss of trust in the teller? I have a suspicion the whole echo-chamber / bubble vocabulary is too much about information — what voices get in, what voices get kept out — and not enough about narrative, credibility, and who you can bear to believe. But I might just be projecting my job onto everyone else's. Tell me whether the pair of concepts is genuinely up to describing a character like mine, or whether you'd need a third term — and if so, sketch what it would have to capture that the other two miss. Concrete examples welcome, and if you can use Dora's situation as a test case, all the better.
