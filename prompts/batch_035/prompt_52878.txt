I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though I've read my way through the standard popular treatments of Gödel's incompleteness theorems and can follow the shape of the arguments. At the company where I work I help run a fortnightly technical reading group: about fifteen engineers, ninety minutes a session, and nobody is shy about invoking incompleteness whenever the discussion turns to the limits of automated reasoning. Over the past year the theorem has been cited, in apparent sincerity, to show that a large language model can never be trusted because it cannot certify itself; that any legal or regulatory code is guaranteed to contain loopholes; and that our static analysis tooling can never be both sound and complete. Each of these slides from what the theorems actually establish about formal systems that are consistent and strong enough to encode arithmetic into claims the theorems say nothing about, and I want the group to feel that slide happen rather than be lectured about it.

Please design a ninety-minute activity for the fifteen of us. My instinct, which you should correct if it's wrongheaded, is that the activity should have participants act as formal systems of different strengths: in one round they operate as checkers of proofs in a deliberately weak system where the self-referential manoeuvre cannot get a grip, because the system simply cannot say enough about its own sentences; in a later round they operate as a stronger system where it can. I want them to discover, rather than be told, that incompleteness tracks what a system can encode, not what it is about — so that when the debrief turns to language models and legal codes, the group can locate precisely which premise has gone missing from each of those analogies. Please give the rules, the materials (no specialist software; we have paper, cards, and a whiteboard), a timeline, a scoring scheme if one genuinely helps, and a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto the actual conditions of the first and second theorems — including the difference between a sentence being unprovable in a given system and its being unknowable in any sense. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how I should handle it, and be frank about which part of the real theorem no such game can reproduce and I will have to supply by hand at the board.

One request on form: please write the whole thing as continuous prose, as if a well-informed colleague were writing me a detailed letter — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — and please use British English throughout.
