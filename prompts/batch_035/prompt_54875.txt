I've noticed two things about the word "know" that I suspect philosophers have already carved apart, but I don't know the terminology, and I can't even settle in my own mind whether they're two faces of one phenomenon or two wholly different things. Does either have an established name, and if so, are they treated as the same phenomenon or as distinct ones?

The first pattern: how much is riding on a claim seems to change whether "know" is available to me. My regular pharmacy has been open until eight for years, and on an ordinary Tuesday I'll say without hesitation that I know it's open until eight, even though my evidence is nothing more than that track record. If my child's prescription must be filled tonight and the pharmacy closes at eight, the same track record suddenly feels insufficient — I find myself saying I believe it's open but don't know it, and I want to call ahead. Nothing about my evidence changed; the only thing that changed is what hangs on being wrong. There's a cousin of this that may be something else entirely: we seem willing to say an engineer knew the bridge was safe as long as it held, and to say he never really knew once it fell, as though whether he knew depends on how badly things could have gone — though this one feels more like hindsight than risk, and I can't tell whether it belongs with the pharmacy case or not.

The second pattern: the propriety of saying "I know" also shifts with facts about the conversation itself. I am exactly as confident on every flight I board that the plane will not crash. Speaking to a frightened first-time flyer, "I know it won't crash" sounds presumptuous — the natural thing is "I'm quite confident it won't" — while describing the very same flight to a colleague a minute later, "oh, I know the flight will be fine" is perfectly natural. No new danger appeared; what changed is who I was talking to and what the exchange could bear.

So my questions. Do these patterns have names in the literature, and are they the same thing? If they're distinct, what exactly distinguishes them — and in particular, is there a settled view about whether the first pattern is a matter of knowledge itself shifting with what's at risk, versus merely a matter of what we're entitled to claim or how much support we need before claiming it? A bare label won't satisfy me: what I want is where the boundary runs between what belongs to the situation itself and what belongs merely to the etiquette of saying "know." For instance, can a change in what's at risk make it literally false that I knew something I would otherwise have known, or does it only make the claim improper? And does the conversation-sensitivity of the second pattern cut at the same joint, or does it attach to the word rather than to the state? If no established distinction matches the carving I'm gesturing at, say so plainly and propose one, with the edge cases that would test it.
