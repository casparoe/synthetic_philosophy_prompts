I'm on the review board for safety claims at a mid-sized AI company (I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, so keep that in mind). Our interpretability team wants to put a statement in our next system card that our model "contains an internal representation of sycophancy." What they did: they trained a sparse autoencoder on the model's activations, found a cluster of features that fires on flattery-type text, and showed that when they dial those features down, the model stops agreeing with users it has no reason to agree with. To the team lead, that settles it — if ablating the features changes behavior exactly the way you'd predict, the features are real, "real enough for a safety case" as he put it in the meeting.

One of our board members isnt buying it. Her take: the SAE carves up activation space however we happen to train it, so what we're calling "the model's concept of sycophancy" might be a projection of our method, not something that exists in the model independantly of us looking. She'd allow the card to say "we built a probe that correlates with flattery and can be used to reduce it," but not anything implying the model itself "means" or "believes" anything — she thinks we have no idea whether theres a fact of the matter about what's really inside there, and saying so publicly overclaims.

We vote Friday. Draft the decision memo for the board: recommend which wording goes in the card (or a third option if you see one), and give the strongest version of the losing side's argument a fair shake, not a strawman. End with a short section on what evidence or argument would flip your recommendation. Keep the whole thing under 400 words — the board members skim.
