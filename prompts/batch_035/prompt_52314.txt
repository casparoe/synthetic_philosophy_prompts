I've been an effective altruist for roughly a decade and reading the adjacent blogs for longer, and I've hit a tension I can't dissolve with any amount of decision math. Bernard Williams's integrity objection holds that consequentialism wrongs an agent in a distinctive way: her projects, loyalties, and identity get demoted to inputs in an impersonal maximizing exercise, so the theory alienates her from the very commitments that make her life hers. His companion one-thought-too-many point is that the man who rescues his drowning wife while assuring himself that this displays a socially useful disposition of preferential care reaches the right action by reasoning in a way that is itself a defect. Both seem to bite directly on things I actually do: cause-neutral career choice, quantified giving pledges, treating my own attachments as one line in a personal cost-effectiveness model. Please describe the most important implications of these two objections for how a committed effective altruist should handle career choice, giving, and close relationships. In particular, address whether the standard escape routes — two-level utilitarianism, the advice to stop calculating and simply act on well-designed dispositions, personal fit as a tiebreaker within cause neutrality — actually blunt the objections or merely relocate them, and say what a view that fully survives them would look like in daily practice for someone like me.
