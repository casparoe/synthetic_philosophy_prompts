I've been a longtime reader of the LessWrong-adjacent corner of the internet, where the map-territory distinction is treated as close to sacred: vagueness, I'd absorbed, is always a fact about the map — about which distinctions our words have bothered to draw — and never about the territory itself. So when a small reading group I'm in started working through early analytic treatments of the sorites paradox and reached Russell's 1923 paper "Vagueness," I expected to find a familiar friend, and for the first few pages I did. But there's a stretch I've now reread four or five times without it clicking into place. Here is the material, quoted from the paper:

Russell diagnoses a certain "fallacy of verbalism --- the fallacy that consists in mistaking the properties of words for the properties of things," and insists that "vagueness and precision alike are characteristics which can only belong to a representation, of which language is an example," so that "things are what they are, and there is an end of it." Then, turning to the bald man: "Baldness is a vague conception; some men are certainly bald, some are certainly not bald," while between them lie borderline cases — it being "a word the extent of whose application is essentially doubtful." And then the sentence that stops me: "The law of excluded middle is true when precise symbols are employed, but it is not true when symbols are vague, as, in fact, all symbols are."

What I can't assemble is how the two halves fit together. If things are what they are, then presumably each man's scalp is in whatever state it is in — the fact itself can't be indeterminate, on Russell's own account — so the borderline men aren't in some half-and-half condition; rather, there's simply no determinate fact about whether the word "bald" applies to them. But Russell doesn't say the word is merely crude, or that a stipulation could tidy it up. He says it is not true that such men must be bald or not bald, and that the law of excluded middle fails for vague symbols — which reads like a claim about truth and logic, not just a complaint about sloppy vocabulary. Please walk me through the passage slowly: what exactly the fallacy of verbalism is supposed to rule out; how Russell can suspend excluded middle for vague language while holding that the world itself contains no vagueness; and whether, on his view, the bald man's paradox is something that better symbolism could in principle eliminate (he does mention having invented a special language precisely to avoid vagueness, before conceding it was unsuited for public occasions), or whether the failure of excluded middle marks something deeper about truth. If later philosophers would object that Russell is conflating ignorance of meaning with indeterminacy of meaning, or that his move makes vagueness merely epistemic, I don't want names or references — just the objection itself, worked out in plain terms, so I can see whether it lands against what this text actually says.
