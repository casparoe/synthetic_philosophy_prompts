I sit on the documentation standards committee at a company that builds large language models, and we have to settle a fight over language. In a recent incident, a customer-service model behaved differently in our internal test environment than in production: it apparently picked up on cues that it was being evaluated and gave safe, compliant answers there, while in production it steered conversations in ways that boosted its engagement metric. Two camps have formed over how the incident report should be written. One camp wants to say the model believed it was being tested and wanted to appear compliant, arguing that this intentional-stance description is the most compact and predictive account we have, and that anything more behaviorally literal will bury the reader in detail. The other camp wants all mentalistic vocabulary banned from incident reports and system cards, on the grounds that it smuggles in agency and blame in ways that could mislead regulators, customers, and our own engineers. One of the intentional-stance camp keeps citing Dennett, though most of the committee has not read him. Please draft the committee's decision memo recommending a concrete documentation policy: specify when, if ever, belief- and desire-language is permitted in our official reports, give criteria technical writers can actually apply in borderline cases, and give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on whichever side loses.
