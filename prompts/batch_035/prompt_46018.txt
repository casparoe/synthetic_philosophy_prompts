Write me a short story, roughly 2000-2500 words, about a small agency of time-travelling "prevention auditors." Their whole job is to go back to minor historical disasters -- a granary fire, a flood that wipes out one village, a famine in a single province, that kind of scale, nothing famous -- figure out which single cause to remove so the disaster never happens, and then leave history otherwise untouched. The bureaucratic constraint that makes the story interesting: the agency's charter (and their budget office) requires that each incident be assigned exactly one root cause for the paperwork. One cause, one removal, done.

The disaster your auditors are working on should be overdetermined: several causes, each of which would have been sufficient on its own. Every time they remove one -- the negligent official, the unusually wet spring, the delayed shipment of grain, the guy who ignored the warning letter -- they come back and find the disaster happened anyway, through the other routes. So they keep removing causes one at a time, and the event keeps reasserting itself, and the case file keeps getting thicker.

The philosophical meat should come from the auditors arguing with each other, so most of the story can be dialogue around a table in a rented room in the past. I want them to genuinely disagree about things like: whether there's a difference between what caused the disaster and what explains it, or what a historian two hundred years later will say caused it; whether "the disaster would have happened anyway" is even a coherent thing to say after you've changed one of its causes, since the disaster that happens through the alternate route isn't quite the same disaster (different victims? same victims, different week?); and whether their instinct that SOME removal has to work -- that if they pruned the whole branch of causes the event would finally have to not happen -- is real or just them demanding that the world be narratable. Maybe one auditor becomes convinced that the single-cause requirement isn't just paperwork but is actually load-bearing: that the future they're protecting needs history to have identifiable culprits in order to learn anything from it, and that a purely redundant, web-shaped history is one nobody can act on. Another thinks that's sentimental nonsense and the only honest case file is a list of maybe a hundred sufficient causes with a note saying "no root cause exists, close the file."

Please don't make the ending tidy -- no lecture, no narrator explaining who was right. Let it end on the auditors making some decision that a reader could defend either way (e.g. they pick the most narratively satisfying cause and falsify the file, or they file the honest hundred-cause list and brace for consequences, or something better than these that you come up with). If a story-within-the-story fits naturally, like the auditors reading a popular history book from their home time that confidently names The Cause of the disaster, use it, but dont force it.

Tone-wise I'm fine with dark comedy or melancholy or both. Just make sure the story shows the philosophy instead of stating it -- the arguments should feel like things tired coworkers would actually say at 11pm, not like position papers wearing a hat.
