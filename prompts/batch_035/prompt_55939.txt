I sit on the restitution and acquisitions panel of a mid-sized university museum, and we have a claim to decide at next month's meeting. I'd like your help thinking it through as though you were an experienced adviser to such panels — someone equally at home with the legal side (title, provenance, duress doctrine) and with the ethics of colonial-era collecting. Please begin with a brainstorming pass before committing to any answer: set out the distinct principles that could rationalize our past decisions, test each candidate against every precedent in turn, and only then make a recommendation. I want reasoning, not reading lists or citations.

Our precedents, in brief. First, we returned a group of Benin plaques donated in 1902 by the family of a soldier who took part in the 1897 punitive expedition — straightforward war loot. Second, we returned to a collector's heirs a painting he sold in 1938 under threat; our museum had bought it in good faith at auction in 1955, and we ruled that a transfer born in duress defeats a later good-faith purchase. Third, we declined a claim on a landscape bought at auction in 1971 with clean provenance back to the 1920s, the claimants being unable to point to any coercion, wartime or otherwise. Fourth, we declined a claim on a set of household curios bought in the 1900s from a community that our records show sold them willingly at negotiated prices; that claim rested only on the assertion that nothing acquired during the colonial era can be legitimate, and we rejected that as a principle. (We have a separate policy for ancestral remains, so set human remains aside entirely.)

The new claim: between 1904 and 1906, a district officer bought, in three separately documented transactions, a set of carved ceremonial objects from a community in what was then a protectorate. The prices recorded are a small fraction of what comparable objects traded for locally; the officer's own ledger notes the sellers' reluctance; and the archival record shows the community had been made to pay a punitive fine to the administration the year before, which is plausibly why they sold at all. On their face the sales are formally valid — written instruments, agreed consideration, no theft, no state expropriation — and the claimant today is the community's recognized traditional council, which says the originals are still wanted for ceremonies now carried out with replicas. For added complexity: the objects anchor one of our most-visited displays, and, with the broader debate having moved toward shared-stewardship arrangements of the Parthenon Marbles sort, two panelists are floating a middle option of negotiated shared ownership rather than outright transfer or outright refusal. So: what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which precedent is the closest analog, and do our precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer — and if so, what principle would we be adopting with each of the live options (return, refusal, shared stewardship)? Finally, identify the kind of case that, if it came to us next, would most cleanly separate the principles you've identified, since I suspect we will need it sooner than we think.
