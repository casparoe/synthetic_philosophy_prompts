Something I keep chewing on: insurers (and the NHS) will pay for antidepressants if you're diagnosed with depression, but not if you just want to be a bit cheerier than baseline. They'll cover growth hormone for a kid with a diagnosed deficiency but not for a short kid with no deficiency, glasses but not laser eye surgery to get better-than-20/20, and cochlear implants but (usually) not some implant that gives normal-hearing people superhuman range. Yet vaccines get covered without a second thought, and they don't treat anything — you're not sick, they just make your immune system better than it otherwise would be. So what view of health and disease does this pattern presuppose? Is it something like "treatment = restoring normal species functioning", and if so what fixes "normal" — statistics, design/evolution, what we'd choose behind a veil of ignorance? And are vaccines the counterexample that shows the practice isn't really tracking the therapy/enhancement line at all, maybe just medical convention or risk reduction instead? Reconstruct the implicit theory for me, distinguish what the coverage rules strictly presuppose from what they merely sit comfortably with (ie could several rival theories of disease license the same rules?), and say what coverage would look like if we ditched the implicit theory for an explicit welfarist rule — cover whatever buys the most wellbeing per pound. I suspect the therapy/enhancement line might be cost control dressed up as philosophy but im not sure, so tell me if thats unfair. Answer in British English please.
