you're a professional philosopher who works on causation, and I need something practical out of you. I chair the incident review committee at a mid-sized hospital. Our reports keep ending up with sentences like "the cause was the failure to escalate to the on-call registrar" — causes that are things nobody did. Half my committee thinks an omission can't literally cause anything (nothing happened, how can nothing do something?), and the other half wants to list every missed opportunity as a cause, which would mean the nurse on the previous shift caused it, and so did everyone who never happened to walk past the room.

Please turn the philosophy on causation by omission into a procedure the committee can actually run in a ninety-minute meeting. Ground it in what that literature actually says — the "but for" counterfactual test and how it explodes with omissions (there are always infinitely many non-doers), the difference between an omission and a mere background condition that was never going to obtain, the idea that which omission we pick as "the" cause is partly normative, tracking who had a duty or the ability to intervene. But make it usable by clinicians with no philosophy: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "contributing factor, not cause," and where it explicitly hands the call to a human because the question is about duty or foreseeability, not causation. Think carefully about which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which we can drop entirely — tell me what you dropped and why.

Then run it on three cases from our last quarter: (1) a nurse distracted during handover didn't register a steadily rising heart rate and the patient arrested; (2) an off-duty doctor who lived ten minutes away and, in hindsight, plausibly could have caught the deterioration before anyone on duty did; (3) a medication was charted but the ordering system never generated the pharmacy request — no individual did anything wrong, the whole harm just... didn't not happen. I expect case (2) to give us an answer I won't like, and I want the procedure to give it anyway. Finish by saying where this procedure will misfire — the kinds of cases it will get wrong no matter how we run it. No reading lists, nobody on the committee will open them.
