I run a small natural history blog with a mostly lay-but-curious audience (birders, native plant people, a few grad students) and I want to do a "which species concept do you actually hold?" quiz, similar in spirit to those "which philosopher are you" things but hopefully less useless. The problem with every version of this I've seen is that the questions basically just ask you which definition you already like — "do you think species are defined by reproductive isolation?" — so the quiz just echoes back your own label. I want the opposite: questions about concrete cases where people's intuitions genuinely split, so a birder who's never heard of Mayr can take it and find out where they land.

Please write me the quiz. Requirements:

1. Eight to ten questions. Each one is a concrete scenario, phrased in plain language with no jargon in the question itself (you can add a one-sentence setup of the biology where needed). Answer options should be 2-3 per question, and phrased as what to say about the case, not as which concept to endorse.

2. The cases should be drawn from at least these situations, since they're where the interesting splits happen: a ring species where the ends of the ring can't interbreed but everything along the chain can; two populations that interbreed freely and produce fertile offspring but never interbreed in the wild because of different breeding seasons; an asexual lineage (like bdelloid rotifers) that's been on its own for tens of millions of years; two populations that are physically indistinguishable to a human but have different songs/calls and don't recognize each other as mates; a lineage known only from fossils; a case where two "species" arose by hybridization, so the new species has one parent from each of them; and a bacterial population where lateral gene transfer means individuals across the "species boundary" share more genes with each other than with closer relatives.

3. Also include at least one more abstract question to catch the species-as-individuals vs species-as-classes issue — something like whether it makes sense to say a species is born, lives, and dies the way an organism does, versus being a set of similar things the way "the elements heavier than iron" is.

4. The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least: the biological species concept, the phylogenetic/clade-based concept, a phenetic or morphological concept, the general lineage concept, pluralism (species means different things in different branches of the tree of life), and the eliminativist/nominalist option that species aren't a natural kind at all. If a respondent's answers don't cohere into any of these, the key should say what mixed position they hold rather than forcing them into a bucket.

5. After the key, add a short section on which pairs of positions the quiz can't actually distinguish, and why — e.g. can any of my cases really separate a pluralist from someone with one concept plus exceptions? I'd rather know the instrument's blind spots than pretend it doesn't have them.

6. One epistemic note: in the answer key commentary and any wrap-up text, please be careful to mark what's settled among people who work on this (e.g. that no single concept handles all the cases — I'm fairly confident that part is just true) versus what's genuinely contested (like whether pluralism is a respectable resting point or a cop-out, or whether species-as-individuals is more than a metaphor). Don't present any of the contested stuff as if it were the consensus view, and if a claim in the key leans on a particular contested reading, flag it inline.

Casual tone throughout is fine — this is a blog quiz, not a paper. Don't include any citations or further-reading lists; my readers can't check them and neither can I half the time. If you want to name-drop the standard concepts (biological species concept etc.) in the key, that's fine, but keep the names out of the questions themselves.
