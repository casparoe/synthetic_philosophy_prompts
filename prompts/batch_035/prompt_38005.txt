I write long-form pieces on science and ideas, and I am preparing a feature on what has become of the ordinary indicative conditional — the 'if' of forecasts, bets and promises rather than of fantasy. The occasion is David Lewis's triviality results, which appear to show that no conditional with truth conditions can obey the equation tying a conditional's probability to the corresponding conditional probability: in other words, that some of the things we demand of a theory of 'if' cannot be had together. Before I assess any candidate theory in the piece, I need the specification it is being measured against, and I would like you to write it.

Please set out the desiderata an adequate theory of the indicative conditional must meet — I am envisaging between eight and twelve — each stated with the concrete case or consideration that motivates it. They should cover, at least: what the theory must say about betting on conditionals, where a bookmaker prices a bet on 'if A, B' as if at the conditional probability, and what happens when the antecedent fails and the bet is called off; which inferences it must validate and which it must block — modus ponens plainly, but also whether it must reject strengthening of the antecedent, as suggested by the match that lights if struck, yet plainly not if struck while soaked; the contrast between an indicative and its counterfactual twin, on the model of the well-known pair about whoever shot Kennedy, where the indicative about someone else having done it invites assent and the corresponding counterfactual invites denial; embedding under negation, disjunction and probability operators, where the material conditional notoriously lets too much through; iterated conditionals of the form 'if A, then if B, C', and whether the theory should validate importing the first antecedent into the second; and conditionals with impossible antecedents, where intuitions are least firm. For each desideratum, say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp, and explain the case behind it in a way an educated reader with no logic could follow — plainly, but without watering anything down.

The part I most want is the conflict. Identify which subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable given the triviality results, explain in plain terms why no truth-conditional semantics can deliver all of them at once, and tell me which single desideratum a reasonable theory should give up, together with what accepting that loss costs elsewhere in the list. Then, and only briefly, run the material conditional, the suppositional view on which conditionals lack truth values and express acceptance rates, and a Stalnaker-style possible-worlds semantics against your list, marking where each falls short. Please do not supply references or a bibliography; I want the reasoning self-contained. Write the answer in British English throughout.
