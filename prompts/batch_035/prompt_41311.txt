My book club picked a popular book on the philosophy of death, and its chapter on Epicurus left me unsure how much of it to trust. The author quotes the famous line from Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus — "seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not" — and then glosses the argument like this: since death leaves no subject to experience anything, and nothing can harm you unless you experience it, death cannot be bad for the one who dies. The book continues that the same reasoning shows the process of dying can't be bad either, since it ends in a state that involves no sensation at all; that Epicurus's symmetry observation — nobody regards the vast stretch of time before their birth as a misfortune, so the time after death can't be one either — establishes that nonexistence is never bad for anyone under any circumstances; that the same argument makes grieving for the dead irrational, since the deceased are beyond harm; and, finally, that Thomas Nagel's objection (death deprives you of future goods, and a deprivation doesn't have to be experienced in order to harm you) has pretty much settled the issue against Epicurus, which is why the argument is now chiefly of historical interest.

Parts of this hang together well, but a couple of things nag at me. I vaguely remember reading that the Epicurean view still has serious defenders in contemporary philosophy, so the confident "settled by Nagel" framing seems too quick, and I also can't shake the feeling that the moves from death to dying, and from death being bad for the deceased to grief being irrational, are sliding between different questions. On the other hand, the author has credentials, and the quoted line from Epicurus does seem to support the core reasoning, so I may be misremembering.

Could you go through the book's characterization claim by claim? For each one, tell me whether a philosopher working on death would accept it as stated, whether it's a defensible simplification that loses something important, or whether it's a genuine misrepresentation — and in each case, what an Epicurean (ancient or modern) would say in reply. Along the way, I'd appreciate it if you kept clear about what is actually settled in this literature versus what is genuinely contested among people who know it well — I don't want to leave with the impression that a live debate has been resolved, or vice versa, and I especially want to know whether the book's final conclusion about the argument being of merely historical interest follows from any part of its own summary that survives scrutiny.
