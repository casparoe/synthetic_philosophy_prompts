I'm taking a historiography seminar this semester and we just spent three weeks on Braudel's Mediterranean stuff, and I've become mildly obsessed with his three levels of time -- the event (l'événement), the medium-term conjuncture, and the longue durée of geography and social structures. My professor keeps saying the fall of Constantinople in 1453 is one of the most over-told "great events" in history, and that Braudel would basically shrug at it. That got me wondering what a proper Braudelian treatment would actually look like, and whether it would hold up.

So here's my ask: take the fall of Constantinople and run it carefully through the three temporal layers. The event layer is obvious enough -- Mehmed II, the Hungarian cannon-founder and his huge bombards, the chain across the Golden Horn, Constantine XI's choices, the ships hauled overland. But then work out what belongs in the conjuncture (Ottoman expansion in the decades before, the state of Byzantine finances, what Venice and Genoa were and weren't willing to do, the gunpowder revolution as a medium-term shift) and what belongs in the longue durée -- the geography of the straits, the Black Sea grain routes the city sat on, the Theodosian walls and their very long career, the deeper logic of who controls that chokepoint. I want a disciplined mapping: which facts about 1453 each layer actually explains, and which facts none of them touches.

And then be honest about where the model breaks. A couple of things that bug me: the city had been a rump, surrounded and strangled, for two centuries, and it had even survived a siege in 1422 -- so the structures and conjunctures seem to predict the fall well before 1453, and don't obviously explain the timing, which looks uncomfortably like the event layer (Mehmed's obsession, Orban's cannon) doing real work. Braudel isn't supposed to care about individuals. So does the case support his framework, strain it, or is it a case he'd rather not talk about -- an "event" whose consequences (shifts in the grain trade, the Italian city-states' position, the push that some historians connect to the later search for ocean routes) rippled for centuries and almost behave like a conjuncture of their own? I'd also like your view on whether saying "the fall was geographically inevitable" is even coherent given that the same geography let the city endure a thousand years of sieges before Mehmed. Sorry for the length, I've been arguing about this with my roommate all week and she's sick of it.
