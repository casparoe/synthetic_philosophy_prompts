I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a myrmecologist called before a research ethics committee to argue that the colony in her laboratory is itself a patient — not the ants, the colony. The idea took root in me years ago, when I read the dialogue in Gödel, Escher, Bach in which an Anteater converses with, and dines upon, the personified colony Aunt Hillary. As I read the Anteater, his view is that the colony's intelligence lives in its organisation rather than in any individual ant, much as my capacity to write this letter lives in patterns of activity rather than in any single neuron; the ants, individually witless, are like the neurons — and eating a few of them no more diminishes Aunt Hillary's mind than losing a few cells diminishes mine. I want my novel's climactic scene to turn on a thought experiment put to the committee, and I would like you to construct it to specification.

Set two positions against each other. On one side, a colony-level realist: someone who holds that a colony can literally have beliefs and desires — that the colony is a mind constituted by, but not identical to, its ants, with thoughts occurring at a level of description the ants themselves do not occupy. On the other, a deflationist: someone who holds that talk of what the colony knows or wants is a predictive shorthand of precisely the sort we extend to thermostats and markets, warranted only in so far as it compresses an individual-level story, and never amounting to genuine mentality.

The specification is as follows. Construct a scenario of a single paragraph, grounded in real myrmecology — the house-hunting emigrations of Temnothorax rock-dwelling ants, their tandem runs, their quorum thresholds, their pheromone trails — with no science-fictional augmentation and no individual ant cleverer than an actual ant is. Within that scenario, the two positions must come to different verdicts on a single question, and the verdicts must follow from the positions themselves rather than being smuggled in by the way the scenario is written. In particular, the case must be one in which the realist is committed to saying that the colony holds a belief that is false, while no individual ant holds any false belief at all: every ant's internal state must be accurate, every ant's behaviour appropriate to what it has encountered, and the error must exist only at the level of the organisation. The deflationist, meanwhile, must be able to deny that there is any belief present — only ants and trails, and the observer's habit of reading purpose into them.

Once the scenario is down, do the rest of the work. Walk through each position's reasoning to its verdict, showing the inference rather than asserting the conclusion. Identify precisely which feature of the case is doing the work. Tell me what happens to the case if that feature is removed. Then give the strongest line of attack a deflationist could mount against the case as constructed, and the strongest reply the realist could make to it.

Two further requests. First, be candid about the construction's weakest joint: tell me which step a critic would target first, and what would have to be true — or what consideration would have to arise — for you to concede that the case does not in fact force the realist's verdict. Second, wherever you attribute a claim to Hofstadter or to his Anteater, flag whether the attribution is settled or a contested reading, as I do not want the novel to rest on shaky exegesis. Throughout, please write in British English.
