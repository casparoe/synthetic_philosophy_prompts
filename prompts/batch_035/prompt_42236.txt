I'm a software engineer on the model-conduct team at a company that ships a conversational assistant used by millions of people. The models are getting genuinely good at reading people: from the transcript alone they can infer a user's mood, self-doubts, and which framing of a sentence will land, and our internal evals suggest they will soon outpersuade most skilled humans. Our usage policy says the assistant must inform rather than manipulate, but that phrase is doing no work — nobody on the team can say what it forbids in a concrete sentence, and when we tried to enumerate banned tactics we got lists that condemned half of ordinary kind conversation along with the dark patterns. Rather than a human-written list of tricks, I want a self-check the model runs on its own draft replies before displaying them: a short ordered procedure in plain language, each step answerable from the conversation context, with a defined action for each answer (send as drafted, rewrite, soften, or escalate to a human), fast enough to run on every turn. It should be grounded in whatever philosophy has actually worked out about the difference between giving someone reasons and bypassing their judgment — the sort of thing I've absorbed secondhand from blog posts, so I need it stated in terms a working engineer can apply, not in terms of the literature.

Please write that procedure: the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure must stop and hand off because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts, and where it is honest that the model is grading its own homework and might rationalize. Then run it on three cases. First: a user is grieving and the draft reply uses warm, flattering language that is clearly chosen because the model knows it will feel good — is that comfort or manipulation, and does the answer change if the same phrasing also keeps the user talking to the product? Second: the user describes a plan the model has good reason to think is a mistake, and the draft reply conspicuously withholds the strongest consideration against it, presenting only the supporting ones. Third: the user explicitly asks the assistant to talk them out of quitting an exercise program, i.e., requests to be persuaded. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer our product team will hate — I suspect the honest version either bans some of the engaging, personable phrasing that makes the assistant likable, or permits something that looks manipulative from outside — and say explicitly which parts of the procedure are mechanical and which are judgment calls dressed up as a checklist.
