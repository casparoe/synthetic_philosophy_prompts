The frame problem debate seems to have died around the late 90s and I genuinely can't tell whether it got resolved or just abandoned. On one side you had the logic people treating it as a technical problem: how does a system keep its beliefs updated without re-deriving every consequence, the Yale shooting anomaly, clever nonmonotonic logics, all that. On the other side the philosophers (Dennett calling it the problem of relevance, the Dreyfus crowd saying its really the deep question of what an embodied agent cares about, which no logic will ever touch). Then machine learning ate the field and everyone stopped talking, as if the thing got solved by gradient descent, which I doubt. My hypothesis is that the two camps were never arguing about the same thing at all -- one about tractable inference in a formal system, the other about how relevance gets determined in the first place -- so the "debate" was really two monologues that happened to share a term coined by McCarthy and Hayes back in 1969. Is that diagnosis right, or was this a genuine technical dispute that got settled or obsoleted? And the part I most want answered: now that LLMs apparently do common-sense updating with no frame axioms in sight, does that count as progress on the frame problem, evidence it was never a real problem, or just an invitation to restate it?
