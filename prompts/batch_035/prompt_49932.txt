I teach a graduate seminar on rational choice theory and I'm writing the take-home final. I want one essay question on whether money-pump arguments establish that rational preferences must be acyclic, answerable in about ninety minutes. The course has covered the standard responses to the classic pump, so I don't want a question students can answer by reciting the literature; the question should be built around a concrete case they haven't seen, so they have to reason rather than retrieve. Here is the case to embed in the question. An agent holds, stably, the pairwise preferences A over B, B over C, and C over A, each defensible on its own terms — say the options differ along two dimensions, and in each pairwise comparison the difference on one dimension swamps the difference on the other. A trader approaches with three sequential offers, each exchanging the agent's current option for the one they prefer plus a small fee; assume the fees are tiny relative to the strength of each pairwise preference. In the first condition, the agent chooses naively at each node. In the second, the agent is sophisticated: they see the whole sequence in advance and evaluate complete strategies rather than node-by-node. In the third, before the sequence begins, the agent may pay a small fee for the power to bind themselves to refuse all trades. The question should ask students to work out, for each condition, whether the agent actually loses money, and then to adjudicate the deeper issue: does the pump show that the preferences themselves are irrational, or only that some pattern of choosing given those preferences is, or neither? Students should have to confront the possibility that the argument proves too much — it would condemn agents whose nontransitive preferences arise from defensible tradeoffs between incommensurable dimensions — and the possibility that it proves too little, since an agent who can bind themselves never actually loses anything, and the commitment fee looks like an ordinary insurance premium. Please write the question exactly as it will appear on the exam, then a model answer of about seven hundred words, then a marking scheme out of twenty marks that names the specific moves separating an A answer from a B answer and notes, for each, how students typically fumble it. Write everything as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the sort of prose one finds in a well-written exam booklet.
