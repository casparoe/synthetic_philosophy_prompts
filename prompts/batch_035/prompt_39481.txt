I'm a PhD student in political science, and most of my work is on turnout, so I spend a lot of time with the paradox of voting. At last week's departmental reading group on the rational-choice literature, a colleague from the formal theory side shut down a discussion about whether citizens have any consequentialist reason to vote by saying, as if it settled the matter, that no single vote has ever decided an election. Everyone nodded, and I nodded too, but on the walk home it occurred to me that I have no real idea what precise claim that one-liner is making, and I'm not sure the colleague knows either. Notably, this is a man who votes in every election, including the municipal ones, so I'd like to know whether his slogan and his behavior are consistent.

Part of my uncertainty is empirical and part is conceptual, and I can't tell where the line falls. On the empirical side: in the 2017 Virginia House of Delegates race in the 94th district, the recount left David Yancey and Shelly Simonds tied at 11,608 votes each, the seat was awarded by drawing a name out of a bowl, and that drawing determined whether the chamber would be 51–49 or split 50–50. So in that race no single vote decided the outcome — yet exactly one additional vote for either candidate would have. That case made me realize the slogan's key phrase is ambiguous on its face: "decided by one vote" could mean the margin of victory was exactly one, or it could mean the outcome was within one vote of going the other way, and these are different claims with different frequencies. On the conceptual side, the sentence could be a claim about token elections that have actually occurred, or about the ex ante probability that a given vote in a given election is pivotal, or about the expected causal effect of casting one vote, or about what follows normatively for whether an individual ought to turn out. I suspect these come apart, but I can't sort out which differences matter.

What I'd like is a structured list I can circulate to the reading group before we reconvene. Take the sentence "no single vote has ever decided an election" and lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing — statistical, causal, decision-theoretic, and normative readings, ordered from the near-truism to the genuinely contentious. For each, say whether it is a platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, and whether it is outright false. Then, most importantly, say for each reading whether accepting it would actually support the use my colleague put it to, namely dismissing consequentialist reasons for individual turnout. And since the slogan was deployed as if it ends the argument rather than begins one, please also identify which reading he would need for that conclusion, and whether that reading survives cases like the Virginia tie. I would rather have the full menu than the one item he served.
