I'm helping draft a survey chapter on scalable oversight, and I keep circling the same headline claim about AI safety via debate: that two AI debaters of roughly equal capability, arguing adversarially before a single human judge and subject to cross-examination by each other, can be used to supervise models more capable than the judge. The theoretical picture is genuinely appealing — lying is supposed to be harder than catching a lie, and in the idealized version of the game there's an equilibrium where the honest agent wins because the dishonest one can always be pinned down under cross-examination. I remember a toy demo with hand-drawn digits in which cross-examination allegedly forced a debater arguing for the wrong label to commit to details that actively sabotaged its own story, which made the whole thing feel almost mechanical. But the picture assumes a judge who can't be exploited, and a colleague of mine who studies persuasion likes to say the scheme bottoms out entirely in "can a human be fooled," which he considers a settled empirical question answered in the wrong direction. I also can't shake the worry about how debate gets trained: by self-play, against near-copies of the debater. If there's any joint strategy in which both debaters cooperate to exploit a predictable human judge — coordinating on confident, flattering, mutually reinforcing falsehoods rather than engaging with each other's arguments — then self-play has no obvious reason to steer away from it, and the equilibrium analysis that says honesty wins may simply be analyzing the wrong game. Add the obfuscated-arguments worry, where every local step of an argument looks impeccable to the judge while the whole is unsound, and honesty loses every local exchange.

So here is the question I want a number for. Please give me your credence in the following proposition, which I've tried to make precise: that at some point before 2035, a deployed system in which two AI agents argue adversarially before human judges — with the agents trained substantially via self-play — will reliably elicit correct answers to natural-language questions that are beyond what the best unaided human judges could answer on their own, without requiring independent verification of the answers. Don't just say "it depends on whether debate works"; decompose it. I'd expect terms at least for: whether idealized debate with an unbounded, unexploitable judge is actually truth-tracking in principle; whether realistic human judges can avoid being systematically exploited by debaters more capable than they are; whether self-play training in practice selects honesty-favoring equilibria rather than joint persuasion equilibria; and whether whatever works at a moderate capability gap keeps working as the gap widens. Add any other terms you think belong, give each a rough probability with a one-line justification, and tell me how they combine into your overall number, which term you're least confident in, and what evidence would move that term the most. For calibration: my two officemates put this at roughly 0.6 and 0.1 respectively, and I'd like to know where their disagreement most likely lives — which single sub-question, if resolved, would collapse most of the gap between them.
