I sit on the academic advisory council of a European museum of the First World War, and our last substantive task before opening is a single wall panel, roughly one hundred and fifty words, answering the question visitors ask most insistently: who was responsible for this? The panel must do three things at once — state a defensible position, register the century of controversy behind that position, and stay honest about what kind of responsibility is being assigned. Our curatorial staff are split. One faction wants the panel to say that Germany's leadership bears primary responsibility, in the tradition opened by Fritz Fischer's 1961 study of Imperial Germany's war aims, Griff nach der Weltmacht, and sustained by later work on the July Crisis and the September Program. The other faction wants a shared-responsibility framing, in the spirit of the more recent histories that present the powers as sleepwalking into catastrophe — each locked into mobilization timetables, alliance commitments, and domestic fragility, none singly in control. A third, quieter faction notes that both framings quietly relocate the object of blame: the first concentrates it on a government, the second diffuses it across a system, and neither addresses what it even means to hold a nation, rather than a handful of ministers and generals, responsible a century on, when none of the living were parties to it — whether responsibility can attach to a continuing political collective, and what it would be responsibility *for* (starting the war, failing to prevent it, or conducting it once started).

Please draft the advisory council's decision memo. It should recommend concrete guidance for the panel's wording — not the wording itself, but the guidance: what claim the panel should license, how it should mark historiographical uncertainty without dissolving into mush, and how it should handle the distinction between a state's causal contribution and its moral responsibility. The memo must give the strongest losing side its due — if you recommend against the shared-responsibility framing, say what the sleepwalker view gets right about structural causes; if you recommend against the German-responsibility framing, say what Fischer and his successors actually established. It should also address the two failure modes the council has already rejected in earlier drafts: a panel so hedged that visitors leave knowing nothing, and a panel so confident that it simply re-runs the Versailles war-guilt clause in exhibit form.
