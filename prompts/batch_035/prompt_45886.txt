I'm taking a graduate seminar on decision theory, and we've spent the last two weeks on cases where your action in the actual world cannot causally affect your payoff, but where your decision procedure or policy seems like it still should matter. I've mostly made peace with counterfactual mugging: I understand the argument that a decision theory that pays the mugger does better across the possible worlds in which you might have found yourself, and I can see why updateless or functional decision theories endorse paying. I've also studied XOR blackmail, and I understand the standard line that refusing is rational there because refusing is the policy that wins once you consider the correlation between your policy and whether the blackmail arrives at all. But I can't articulate the asymmetry cleanly. In both cases, causally speaking, what I do now is irrelevant to my bottom line; in both cases, there's a predictor whose behavior depended on predicting my policy. So the paying/refusing distinction must be hiding somewhere in the details — something about how the predictor's action screens off the benefit, or about the difference between a policy that wins in counterfactual branches and one that merely reshapes which worlds you're in — and I keep losing the thread when I try to spell it out.

To make things worse, I've started worrying about a hybrid case. Imagine a mugger and a blackmailer who draw on the same source of randomness — say both condition on the same fair coin flipped long ago, in such a way that the counterfactual mugging occurs in the worlds where the blackmail doesn't, and vice versa. Now my policy toward one of them seems to leak into my expected payoff from the other: if I'm disposed to pay the mugger, that disposition affects what a predictor of mine would have done in the blackmailer's setup, and vice versa. I suspect this correlation structure is exactly where the asymmetry either gets sharpened or falls apart, but I honestly can't tell which. One of my classmates claims the hybrid just reduces to ordinary counterfactual mugging and I should pay; another says the correlation makes both offers "blackmail-flavored" and I should refuse both; a third says the question is ill-formed because the two agents' predictions of me aren't the kind of thing that can be glued together by a shared coin.

Write an essay, in American English, that weighs the main arguments about whether rational agents should pay in counterfactual mugging and refuse in XOR blackmail, and that gets into the weeds on exactly where the asymmetry between the two cases lies — or whether there is in fact no principled asymmetry. Then address the hybrid case I described: does the shared-coin construction preserve the pay/refuse split, collapse it, or reveal that my classmates' disagreement rests on a confusion? Don't end on a non-committal note; defend a verdict, and if your verdict depends on assumptions about decision theories (evidential, causal, updateless, functional), state those assumptions explicitly rather than gesturing at all of them at once.
