Two classmates in my philosophy of biology seminar spent an hour on a case our instructor invented to avoid textbook baggage: a cave-dwelling fish species descended from surface ancestors. In the ancestors, a skin gland secreted a bitter compound, selected because it made the fish unpalatable to predators. In the cave species, the same secretion now coats the egg masses, and it is the coating's stickiness, not its taste, that keeps the eggs from drifting off the reef; cave predators are scarce and seem indifferent to the taste. The instructor asked what the function of the secretion is, and they split.

(1) Ana said the function is what the trait was selected for: making the fish unpalatable. Coating the eggs is a new use. If "function" meant whatever a part does that benefits the organism, the word would no longer explain why the gland exists, which is its whole point.

(2) Ben said the function is keeping the eggs attached. A function is the causal role a part plays in the activities of a containing system, and the secretion currently makes the difference to egg survival; the unpalatability story explains where the gland came from, which is history, not function.

(3) Ana replied that without the selection requirement, functions multiply without limit: the secretion also tints the egg mass a color that happens to match the surrounding reef, marginally reducing light damage to the eggs, and Ben should then have to call camouflage a further function, which sounds wrong — functions should mark what a part is for, not every benefit it happens to confer.

(4) Ben conceded he must privilege the role that does most of the explanatory work in the system he is analyzing, and that which role counts as "most" is relative to the researcher's question — but he said that relativity is a feature for physiology, where "the function of X" always tacitly means "X's role in some capacity Y."

(5) Ana then granted a partial point: once generations of selection in the caves had favored better-coating secretions, adhesion would become a second genuine function even on a selection-based view. But she pressed a normativity issue: only selection grounds claims that the gland is supposed to coat the eggs, such that a too-thin secretion counts as a malfunction rather than a mere variant — Ben's view, she said, has no non-arbitrary source of "supposed to."

(6) Ben answered that during precisely those transitional generations, Ana's function claims point backwards: the secretion is doing nearly all of its fitness work as glue and almost none as bad taste, yet on her view its function is the one it is barely performing; and when a physiologist asks what a compound does in a newly discovered pathway, they want the role it plays now, not its archaeology.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate each numbered move, then give an overall verdict. Respond with a single JSON object of this shape: "ratings", an array with one entry per move, each an object with "move" (integer 1–6), "speaker" ("Ana" or "Ben"), "claim" (one line summarizing the claim being rated), "rating" (one of "clearly mistaken", "partly right", "clearly right"), and "justification" (two or three sentences); "crux", the move at which the dispute is really decided, and why; "overall", who is more right, about which specific points; and "missed_by_both", the most important consideration neither of them raised.
