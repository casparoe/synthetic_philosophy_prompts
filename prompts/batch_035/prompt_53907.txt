The three-century debate over Molyneux's problem appears to have reached an odd equilibrium: an empirical literature now exists, the results are broadly in, and yet the philosophical camps have not converged -- if anything, both the yes-side and the no-side cite the very same studies as vindication. I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this now is, and whether anything could count as progress.

Some background, so the diagnosis can engage the actual positions. Molyneux asked whether a man born blind, who knows a cube and a sphere by touch, could, upon being made to see and before touching anything, tell which is which by sight alone. Molyneux and Locke answered no; Leibniz, allowing the newly sighted man rational reflection, answered a qualified yes. The debate was long treated as armchair material, but there is now a body of data: historical cases of cataract removal collected in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and a 2011 study of Indian children operated on for dense congenital cataracts, tested within days of gaining sight. The children were asked to match a shape they had just felt to one of two shapes presented visually. They did poorly on the first trials -- well below the performance of normally sighted controls, though some of them performed slightly above chance in some blocks -- and improved to near-ceiling within a week. Since then, and this is the part I find telling, no-sayers such as those following Berkeley have read the initial failure as confirmation that visual and tactual ideas are heterogeneous, while yes-sayers have read the rapid learning, and the marginal above-chance performance, as confirming that some common spatial content is accessible across modalities, needing only a moment of calibration or rational inference rather than fresh learning. Same data, opposite verdicts, and a literature that keeps generating commentary without anyone budging.

Here is my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis only. I suspect the residual dispute is verbal in a specific and non-obvious way: it turns on an unacknowledged distinction between automatic perceptual matching and deliberate multimodal inference. The no-camp takes Molyneux to be asking whether the newly sighted person can, at a glance and without reasoning, pair the visual presentation with the tactual one -- a feat of perceptual recognition. The yes-camp, following Leibniz, allows the subject a slower, explicit route: the visual field presents something extended with a flat face and sharp corners, the hand had presented something extended with a flat face and sharp corners, and one can conclude, by reasoning rather than by recognition, that they are the same shape. On this reading, a subject who succeeds only through conscious inference satisfies one camp's Molyneux task and fails the other's, so the empirical findings -- initial failure with fast improvement, marginal above-chance responding -- get absorbed differently by the two camps without either being wrong about anything. But I am not sure this is the whole story, for two reasons.

First, the empirical situation is messier than the philosophical uses made of it. Nearly all human subjects, historical and modern, gained sight late, after years of atypical development: a visual system that matured without input, in some cases with deprivation-related pathology, tested on objects they never handled preoperatively. Whether such subjects tell us what a normally developing cross-modal system would do at first sight is a standing confound, and I am unsure whether the camps disagree about how much weight this carries or simply never confront it.

Second, there is a deeper question of whether the dispute is verbal at all, or whether it is a substantive disagreement about what vision makes available -- about whether the bare visual presentation of a cube, prior to any tactual-visual correlation, contains anything that could serve as the premise of an inference, or whether spatial content only shows up in vision after learning has bound the modalities together. That question does not look merely verbal, and the empirical findings do not obviously decide it.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, in the way I sketched, or substantive, or two research programs answering different questions under one inherited heading? Please give the diagnosis with reasons drawn from what each side actually maintains, assess my hypothesis honestly rather than adopting it, say what -- a conceptual distinction, a better-designed study, a reformulation of the question -- would count as real progress given your diagnosis, or argue that none should be expected. I would rather have a diagnosis I can act on than a survey of positions I have already read.
