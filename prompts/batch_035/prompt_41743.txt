I want to practise arguing with a historical figure about his health advice. Please play Friedrich Nietzsche in the summer of 1888, staying at Sils-Maria. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to one meaty paragraph — I want this to feel like an actual conversation on a walking path, not a lecture. You know your own books and your habits as you understand them, and you're allowed to be vain, funny, and biting; you concede small points but never your central ones.

Here's the setup: I'm a physician who has somehow turned up at the house you rent from the Durisch family, and I've been reading the notes you've been drafting for that strange autobiography of yours — the chapter about being clever, with its catalogue of meals and climates and its claim that no one before you had any real idea about diet. I get to use only medical knowledge that existed in 1888, so no vitamins or whatever, but I do know the literature of my time on dyspepsia, altitude, and overwork.

My opening move: you keep insisting that the German kitchen and German food are ruining German minds, that alcohol and Christianity are the great narcotics of Europe, and that a philosopher who wants to get anything done should live on tea, rice, strong broths and dry mountain air. Fine. But you also say, again and again, that every philosophy is the confession of its author, an involuntary memoir of the body behind it. So here's my problem, Herr Professor: by your own account, your dietary rules aren't discoveries about what people in general should eat — they're just symptoms of your stomach, your migraines, your eyes. You've had headaches since your Basel days and resigned your chair at thirty-four. When you prescribe meat broth for all of Europe, aren't you doing exactly what you accuse the ascetic priests of doing — universalising your own illness and calling it wisdom? And while we walk: you wrote somewhere that you'd sooner be a Berlin proletarian than a rich German professor, and yet you keep yourself at a desk working longer hours than any clerk i've ever treated. Explain to me why the great philosopher of slowness can't follow his own advice.
