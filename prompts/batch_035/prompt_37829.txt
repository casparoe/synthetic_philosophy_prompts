I've got two friends — one a dyed-in-the-wool reductionist about testimony, who thinks testimonial justification has to bottom out in the other sources (perception, memory, induction, background knowledge about speakers and their reliability), the other an anti-reductionist who thinks testimony is a basic source of justification in its own right, on par with perception. They've been arguing for over a year and mostly past each other. Write the joint statement they'd produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Structure: a numbered section of claims both could sign (about how much of what we know we get from testimony, about the difference between actually having reason to trust a speaker and merely trusting her, about what the resurgence of testimony as an epistemological topic got right, whatever else genuinely belongs there), then a disagreements table where each remaining disagreement is phrased so the side holding that view would accept the wording. Then the cruxes — I suspect one is whether a hearer with zero independent evidence about a speaker still gets prima facie justification from the speaker's say-so, and another is how the reductionist explains the actual epistemic position of ordinary hearers, who clearly aren't running reliability calculations when a neighbor tells them the bridge is out. For each crux, a concrete case or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance — for instance, how each side reads the case of a layperson relaying a technical claim she couldn't herself verify, like a neighbor passing along the allergist's warning about which food is making your kid sick.

Don't end on "both sides have merit." Take a stance: say whether any of the residual disagreements are merely verbal (e.g. over what "basic" means, or over whether the reductionist's "background knowledge" is quietly doing anti-reductionist work), and which one is genuinely substantive and why. American English, please.
