I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am at work on a long-read essay about what laboratory experiments genuinely settle — and fail to settle — in the interpretation of quantum mechanics. The episode I have chosen is the family of delayed-choice experiments, and I would like your help analysing it through the lens of the three main interpretations: Copenhagen, Everettian many-worlds, and Bohmian mechanics. My editor insists the essay go beyond the familiar claim that quantum mechanics is somehow weird; my aim is a disciplined mapping between experiment and interpretation, and I would be grateful for a correspondingly disciplined reply.

To keep us working from the same page, here are the facts as I plan to present them to readers, which I ask you to take as given. In Wheeler's delayed-choice set-up, a single photon passes through an interferometer; the experimenter decides whether to leave the second beamsplitter out, recording which path the photon took, or to insert it at the last moment, in which case the photon contributes to an interference pattern as though it had travelled both paths. Crucially, that decision is made only after the photon has already passed the first beamsplitter — and in the quasar version Wheeler liked to press on his audiences, the paths in question are routes around a gravitational lens separated by billions of light-years, with the astronomer's choice of apparatus apparently deciding retroactively whether the photon took one route or both. Later, the delayed-choice quantum eraser went further: using entangled photon pairs, a signal photon is detected first, and only afterwards do experimenters measure its partner in one of two ways — one of which makes the which-path information recoverable, the other of which erases it. When the signal detections are sorted by the partner's outcome, interference appears in one subset and not the other. Yet the statistics of the signal detections, taken alone and unsorted, never show interference, and no signal ever travels backwards in time. The popular framing, which Wheeler did much to encourage, is that the photon's past behaviour is not a definite fact until a present choice is made about what to measure.

Here is what I want from you. First, give me the mapping interpretation by interpretation. For Copenhagen, tell me where the formalism locates the phenomenon: what the wavefunction is doing before the beamsplitter decision, what role measurement plays, and what decoherence adds or fails to add to the story. For Everett, say what it is that branches, when, and how the account explains the subset-wise interference without any special status for measurement. For Bohmian mechanics, say what the particle is doing all along, why the interference pattern appears and disappears, and what the guiding equation does with the eraser arrangement. In each case, state plainly which features of the episode the interpretation illuminates, which it must strain to accommodate, and where it is simply silent rather than explanatory — I want you to resist the temptation to make any of the three speak where it has nothing to say. Second, tell me what the episode shows about the interpretation debate itself: whether it favours any of the three, whether it is an anomaly for any of them, or whether it reveals that the debate was never really about cases like this, since all three predict the same unsorted statistics and the differences surface only in the gloss. Third, address the retrocausality question directly: is the popular backwards-in-time reading a substantive claim any interpretation must answer for, or an artefact of describing a photon that never had a definite path as though it had one — and does the fact that the eraser requires coincidence counting to reveal anything at all bear on this? Finally, since my essay must tell readers what follows for science journalism: when reporting on such experiments, what is the honest thing to say about what has been demonstrated?

Please write your response in American English.
