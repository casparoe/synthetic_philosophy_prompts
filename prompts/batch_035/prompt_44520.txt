In my study group we've assembled what looks like a reductio of Marx's account of exploitation, and I can't find where it goes wrong. (1) On that account, the wage worker is exploited even though she is paid the full value of what she sells: the capitalist buys her labor power at its value, yet in production that labor power yields more value than it cost, and the capitalist keeps the difference. (2) Now suppose a smallholder sells a sack of seed at its full value to a neighboring smallholder, who plants and tends it and ends up with a harvest worth several times what he paid for the seed. (3) Here too the buyer got more value out of the commodity he bought than he paid for it, while the seller received only that commodity's value — exactly the situation Marx says exploits the wage worker. (4) So the seed seller is exploited in the very sense in which the wage worker is. (5) By the same reasoning, every sale of a means of production — tools, raw materials, machines — exploits the seller, including sales between independent producers and even between worker cooperatives. (6) That conclusion is absurd. (7) So Marx's account of exploitation collapses. Which step should I reject, why did that step look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost? If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) than grant (7), but I want to know what I am paying either way — including whether the only way to stop the slide is a premise about which commodities create new value, and whether the account can instead be re-grounded somewhere else entirely.
