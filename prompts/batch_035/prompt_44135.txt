As a PhD student in the history of economic thought, I've settled on a fairly specific version of the anti-socialist conclusion to the calculation debate, and I'd like a serious stress test of it. My view is that Mises's original argument, framed as a problem of computing equilibrium prices for a complex economy, was in principle answerable by the formal machinery socialists later invoked — Lange's trial-and-error procedure, and more convincingly the linear programming tradition that Kantorovich founded — so that the debate was never really settled on computational grounds. What settles it instead, I think, is the line running from Hayek's essays on knowledge through Lavoie's reinterpretation of the debate: the problem is not solving a given optimization problem but the discovery of ends, techniques, and tacit know-how that no one has yet articulated, which prices aggregate and signal and no planner, however powerful their algorithms or however much data they harvest, can draw out of people in usable form. What would count against this position? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and include evidence from actually existing planned economies that a critic could cite, formal or theoretical results that cut against the tacit-knowledge framing, and the strongest argument that my distinction between static computation and dynamic discovery is doing rhetorical work it cannot cash out — that "tacit" and "not yet articulated" are simply labels for the unsolved part rather than a diagnosis. I'd also like you to identify where a defender of my view would be most tempted to insulate it, for instance by stretching the notion of tacit knowledge whenever a new technique for eliciting or inferring dispersed information appears, and to say whether that kind of insulation is disqualifying for a claim of this sort or an acceptable feature of a broad social-theoretic thesis. Please answer in continuous prose only, as in a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
