I'm trying to get clear on why inferring preferences from behavior is harder than it first sounds, and I keep running into three problems that I can't quite keep apart. Please explain them, at the conceptual level rather than the mathematical one, and explain how they relate to one another:

1. Non-identifiability under full rationality: even if a human is a perfect expected-utility maximizer, the same behavior can be compatible with multiple reward functions. A constant reward makes every policy optimal, and some transformations of a reward function — scaling it by a positive constant, or adding a potential-based shaping term — leave the optimal policies unchanged. So inference needs structure beyond "this person is rational."

2. The rationality confound: once you drop perfect rationality, the problem appears to get worse without bound. For any observed behavior and any candidate reward, there is some model of human error under which that behavior is approximately optimal for that reward. The standard fix is to model the human as noisy but mostly rational — Boltzmann-rational, say — but this seems merely to convert the problem into a choice of error model, and it's not obvious what licenses that choice. Why privilege "has ordinary preferences and occasionally slips" over "has strange preferences" or "is mostly a creature of habit, and the habits track nothing"? These look like competing explanations at the same level, not signal versus noise.

3. Evidential feedback in interactive settings: if the assistant's actions influence the human's subsequent behavior, then the data available for inference depends on what the assistant does, so the choice of data-collection policy is itself part of the inference procedure. Worse, some hypotheses about the human may be testable only by treating the human in ways that are appropriate only if the hypothesis is false — for instance, only by overriding stated preferences long enough to see whether behavior reveals different ones.

On top of these, I'd like your take on a more philosophical worry: should the reward function being inferred be understood realistically — as a hidden fact about the human that correct inference would recover — or instrumentally, as a useful construct we attribute to people in order to predict and assist them, the way we attribute a center of mass to a rolling body, where different assistants could in principle legitimately attribute different "preferences" to the same person? Does the rationality confound in (2) push in one of these directions? In particular, if the error model is underdetermined in the way I described, does that suggest that the "true preferences" being recovered are partly an artifact of the priors doing the recovering?

Please lead with your bottom line — one sentence stating how the three problems are related — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my questions before answering them. Assume I already know what an MDP is; skip the standard history of the field.
