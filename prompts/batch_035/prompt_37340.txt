I've been following the argument about whether culture evolves in a Darwinian way for a while now and its completely stalled, and honestly I can't tell anymore whether the two sides are even disagreeing about anything real. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself — not a survey of the positions, and not a verdict on who's right.

Here's the shape of it as I understand it. One side holds the memetics line: culture really is a Darwinian system. There are replicators (memes, roughly), they get transmitted with variation, some variants get transmitted more than others, and you can trace lineages of ideas the way you'd trace lineages of genes. The fact that most ideas die and a few spread like wildfire is explained by selection pressures, some of which have nothing to do with whether the idea helps the person holding it — an idea can spread purely because it's good at getting itself passed on, full stop.

The other side — the cultural attraction people, plus some dual-inheritance modelers who kept their distance from memes — says the copying story doesn't describe actual transmission at all. When something passes from one head to another it isn't copied, it's reconstructed: the listener rebuilds the idea from a rough sketch, and the reconstruction is systematically biased, so over generations the versions drift toward certain forms — the memorable ones, the ones that fit prior expectations, the ones that are easy to retell. Those attractor basins do the work that "replicator fitness" was pretending to do, and anyway there's nothing in culture stable enough across transmission to play the gene.

And that's where it sits. The memetics people lost the mainstream but never conceded anything; the attraction people won the room and yet keep argueing the point anyway. Nobody's moved in what, twenty years?

My working hypothesis — and it's just a hypothesis — is that it's at least partly a verbal dispute about how much fidelity you need before you're allowed to say "Darwinian." The memetics side means descent with modification and differential success; the attraction side means literal replication, and since nothing in culture clears that bar, they conclude culture isn't Darwinian — which looks to me less like an empirical claim and more like a decision about how to use a word. But I suspect there's a real formal question buried in there too, something like: in a population of items transmitted with heavy noise plus systematic bias, is there still a well-defined notion of selection you could actually measure, or does the bias just eat the selection? And if that question got settled, wouldn't most of the fight dissolve with it?

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal? Substantive but just hard? Two research programs that each work fine on their own turf, fighting over turf neither of them needs? And what, concretely, would count as progress here — a formal result, a case where one side's machinery plainly fails, some kind of data? I'd rather hear "nothing will move this, and here's why" than a hopeful answer, so don't sugar-coat it.
