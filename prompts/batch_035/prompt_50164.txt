Exam question: Raphael Lemkin spent much of the 1930s and 1940s trying to establish a legal category for the destruction of peoples, culminating in the 1948 UN Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide. Answer all three parts. (a) Explain how Lemkin's conception of genocide extends beyond the physical killing of group members. (b) Identify at least one significant respect in which the 1948 Convention departs from or narrows Lemkin's original conception, and explain why that departure mattered to him. (c) Why does the decision to label, or refuse to label, an atrocity a genocide carry legal and political stakes that go beyond mere classification?

Student's answer: Lemkin coined the term genocide in his 1944 book Axis Rule in Occupied Europe, combining the Greek genos with the Latin suffix for killing. For Lemkin, genocide was not just mass murder: he conceived it as a coordinated program of actions aimed at destroying the essential foundations of a group's life — its political and social institutions, culture, language, religion, and economic existence — so that the group is annihilated as a group, even where many individual members survive.

Before 1944, the main available categories were war crimes and crimes against humanity. War crimes applied only to acts committed against enemy nationals in war. Crimes against humanity was broader, and at Nuremberg it covered atrocities against any civilian population, including a state's own citizens, whether or not the acts occurred in wartime. So the gap that the new category had to fill was not peacetime as such, but the destruction of groups as such: the earlier categories treated atrocities piecemeal, one victim at a time, and could not capture the wrong of destroying a group as a group.

The 1948 Convention follows Lemkin's concept closely. It protects national, ethnic, racial, religious, and political groups, as Lemkin intended. The only real narrowing is that the Convention dropped most of the cultural techniques of genocide from Lemkin's original list. It therefore comes as little surprise that Lemkin's own view had evolved by 1948: he had evidently come to accept that physical and biological destruction is the core of the wrong, and that cultural destruction, however regrettable, is not the essence of the crime.

The politics of naming matters because the label carries consequences. If an event is called genocide, states party to the Convention are placed under obligations to prevent and punish, and the label shapes how victims and posterity understand the event. Conversely, governments avoid the word because using it obliges them to act; this is why officials so often reach for euphemisms such as ethnic cleansing, a term to which no comparable legal duty is attached.
