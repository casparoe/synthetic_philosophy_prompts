A popular book I've been reading about Bayesian reasoning devotes a chapter to the doomsday argument, and it summarizes the argument like this:

"The doomsday argument, due to Brandon Carter and developed by John Leslie, starts from a simple observation: you are roughly the hundred-billionth human ever born. Now suppose your birth rank is a random sample from all the humans who will ever live — that, prior to checking, you should regard yourself as no more likely to occupy an early position in the sequence than a middle or late one. If the human race were to go on to have trillions of members, your being among the first hundred billion would be a staggering coincidence. Hypotheses on which the total number of humans is not enormously greater than the current count make your actual birth rank far more probable. That is all there is to it: the likelihood of finding yourself at rank one hundred billion is much higher on 'doom soon' than on 'doom late,' so Bayes' theorem compels you to shift probability toward an early end for our species. What makes this so unsettling is that the argument requires no premises about war, pandemics, climate, or technology. However optimistic you are about the future, your birth date alone is strong evidence of imminent extinction, and refusing to update on it is straightforwardly irrational. The only serious escape is to deny the sampling assumption, and the standard objection — that you couldn't have been born earlier, because then you would have been somebody else — just misses the point: the argument never claims you might have been Napoleon, only that your position should be treated like a lottery ticket drawn from all the tickets that will ever exist."

I found this genuinely alarming, and the mathematics looks airtight to a lay reader like me, but the confidence bothers me — the book treats its one escape route as so easily dismissed. Please go through the summary step by step: which parts would a philosopher working on anthropic reasoning accept as stated, which are subtly misleading, and which are just wrong? In particular, tell me whether the claim that the argument needs no assumptions about the world holds up, and whether the objection it waves away is really as weak as the author suggests. And once you've corrected the summary, tell me honestly how much of the alarming conclusion survives: does my birth date really give me strong reason to expect humanity to end soon?
