I teach an undergraduate seminar on rational choice theory, and next week I'm doing a unit on the Kelly criterion. A friend of mine who trades crypto sent me a blog post he swears by, and I want to sanity check its core paragraph before I either steal it or trash it in front of my students. Here's what it says, close to verbatim:

"The Kelly criterion tells you what fraction of your bankroll to bet so as to maximize the geometric growth rate of your wealth, which is the same thing as maximizing expected log wealth. Its killer feature is this: if you bet according to Kelly and I bet any different fixed fraction, then with probability one your bankroll eventually ends up larger than mine and stays larger — it's not merely better on average, it wins with certainty in the long run. The reason is that overbetting gets punished asymmetrically: bet double the Kelly fraction and you lose money on average even when every individual bet has positive expected value, and bet more than that and you're basically guaranteeing eventual ruin. Since anyone with a long enough horizon therefore ends up richer under Kelly than under anything else, it's the rational way to bet, full stop. Fractional Kelly — betting half or a third of what the formula says — has no real justification; it's just psychological comfort for people who can't stomach the drawdowns. (Kelly himself, incidentally, was no gambler — he worked at Bell Labs on information theory.)"

Some of this sounds right to me and some sounds off, but the off parts are off in ways I can't quite put my finger on, which is exactly the kind of thing I dont want to hand to nineteen year olds. Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me what a careful Kelly theorist would accept as stated, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? I'm especially curious about three things: (a) whether "wins with certainty in the long run" is quietly doing infinite-horizon work that doesn't transfer to any finite investor, (b) whether the "you lose money on average" line about double-Kelly is conflating two different senses of "average" — arithmetic versus geometric — in a way that changes the conclusion, and (c) whether the dismissal of fractional Kelly ignores reasons that have nothing to do with gut feelings. And then the bigger question: once you fix whatever is wrong, does the blogger's bottom line — full Kelly is simply the rational choice — survive in any form, or does it collapse into something much more hedged?
