For about a year my friend and I have been keeping a running list of what she calls "steering tricks" — things companies and governments do to move people's choices without forbidding anything — and I've noticed she has very firm gut rulings on them. So far: a supermarket putting fruit at eye level and candy on the top shelf is fine; an employer automatically enrolling workers in a pension plan with an easy opt-out is fine; making organ donation opt-out rather than opt-in is fine; a shop's "was $99, now $49" tags, where $99 was never a real price, is over the line; a gym that defaults members into auto-renewing annual contracts with the opt-out buried in the small print is over the line; and a landlord who slips a truthful line into renewal letters — "nearly every tenant in the building renewed last year" — just before announcing a rent increase, is over the line too. Two new cases are coming up. First, our city plans to send each household a truthful letter comparing its energy use with the neighborhood average, in order to reduce consumption. Second, a charity she supports is changing its donation page so that "make my gift monthly" arrives pre-ticked, with a clear but easy-to-miss way to uncheck it. What will she say about each — fine, or over the line? I care less about the calls themselves than about the rule she seems to be applying: whether the line is drawn by who benefits from the steering (the person steered, some third party, or the steerer's own pocket), by whether the mechanism is honest information or something more like pressure, by whether it would still work if the person knew exactly what was being done to them, or by some combination I haven't spotted. Several of those rules fit her record equally well, so please tell me where they come apart on these two cases, what further case would best separate the rules from one another, and what she would be committing herself to, more generally, if she keeps ruling the way you predict. Please answer in American English.
