I spent most of my career as a population ecologist, doing mark-recapture work on freshwater fish and, later, survey design for fisheries stock assessments. Since retiring I have been reading my way into philosophy of science, and I have landed on the anthropic reasoning literature — the doomsday argument, the self-sampling and self-indication assumptions, the presumptuous philosopher. Almost immediately I noticed something that may or may not be a genuine insight: the self-sampling assumption, as I understand it, tells me to reason as though my birth rank among all humans is a random draw from the set of observers, and this is structurally the same maneuver we performed constantly in the field. Capture one animal, read its serial number — or in the famous wartime case, capture a few German tanks, note their serial numbers, and infer the total production run. In ecology we also knew that sampling is never uniform: some animals are "trap-happy" and get caught far more often than others, so a trapping method that catches more individuals isn't evidence the population is bigger — and this looks to me suspiciously like the self-indication assumption, which says hypotheses postulating more observers make my existence more probable and should be upweighted accordingly. If that identification is right, the presumptuous philosopher is just an ecologist who has forgotten that catchability is a property of the gear, not of the world.

I would like an audit of the sampling analogy. Treat the doomsday argument as it is usually presented — my early-ish birth rank is like a low serial number on a captured tank, so the total run of humans is probably not vastly larger than the number already produced. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the population, the capture event, the sampling frame, the equal-catchability assumption, and the estimator. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. Some candidate disanalogies I half-see but cannot evaluate: there is no external sampler with a known protocol — I am the animal and the net at once; the "population" whose size is being estimated includes future individuals who will never be captured by anyone; and the reference class (why humans rather than all mammals or all observers?) seems to have no counterpart in a properly designed survey, where the frame is fixed before sampling. Then the crucial question: which disanalogies actually matter for the doomsday conclusion, and which merely make it untidy? Does the conclusion survive once they are on the table, or was the tank-and-net picture carrying it the whole time? And is there a better analogy available — maybe something from your own literature rather than mine — or is the demand for an analogy here itself a mistake?

I should add a request about how to answer. I am newly arrived in this literature and well aware that my instincts were honed on problems where the sampling design was under our control, so I may be importing a field prejudice — the conviction that every inference problem is at bottom a survey-sampling problem. If you think the analogy is worse than useless, or that I have misdescribed the self-indication assumption by tying it to trap-happiness, or that the question cannot be answered as posed, please say so plainly rather than meeting me halfway out of politeness. Where you do think the analogy has force, tell me how confident I should be in each step of the mapping, since in my experience the interesting failures of survey methods were always the ones nobody thought to list as assumptions.
