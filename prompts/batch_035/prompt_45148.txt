I'm a TA grading take-home finals for an intro political philosophy course, and I've hit one answer I keep going back and forth on. For context, I also spent like an hour last night arguing with my roommate about whether Einstein's socialism was just sentimental humanism or an actual argument, so my head is completely scrambled on this topic and I don't trust my instincts.

The question on the exam was: In his 1949 essay "Why Socialism?", Albert Einstein presents a case for a socialist economic order. In your own words, reconstruct the central line of reasoning, being careful to distinguish the premises he actually relies on from considerations he explicitly sets aside. Then critically assess the argument: identify its weakest premise and explain why.

And here's what the student wrote: "Einstein's main argument is that capitalism, because production is driven by private profit rather than by use, inevitably produces recurring economic crises, mass unemployment, and growing poverty. Since science has shown that socialism is the necessary next stage of social development, Einstein concludes that we must replace the market with a planned economy, which by its nature will eliminate these dysfunctions. He adds that capitalist society also corrupts people through its educational system, which trains the young to be competitive rather than cooperative. The weakest premise is the assumption that a planned economy will function well, since Einstein, unlike other socialists, did acknowledge that planning concentrates power in the hands of officials and that this could be dangerous. But he argues this risk is outweighed by the economic benefits, so the premise survives. Overall the argument stands as long as one accepts that history moves through fixed stages, which Einstein, as a scientist, had established empirically."

Grade this the way you'd grade any other final — give me a letter grade plus point-by-point comments, so not just a general impression: what the student got right, where claims sound plausible but aren't actually what the essay says, and where the reasoning doesn't follow even if the conclusion is fine. The students can come argue at office hours so I'd rather be pointed than generous, but I also don't want to dock points for things that are actually correct.
