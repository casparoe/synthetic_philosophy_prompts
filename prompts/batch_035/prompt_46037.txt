Write a poem about the backward induction paradox, staged in the centipede game, in which the form itself carries out the induction. The setup, for the poem's bones: two players alternate turns; on each turn a player either takes a growing pile — the taker gets the larger share and the game ends immediately — or passes, which enlarges the pile and hands the choice to the other; after finitely many nodes the game ends anyway, and both players do best if everyone passed throughout. Compose seven stanzas numbered from seven down to one, one stanza per remaining decision node, each stanza one line shorter than the last, so the poem visibly shrinks as the induction devours the future it reasons about; let a mutating refrain inside the stanzas do the arguing, each appearance claiming that the rational exit lies one node earlier than the previous appearance claimed. After the stanza numbered 1, add a single unnumbered line that breaks the countdown and carries the paradox: the argument seems to prove the game should never get past its first node with both players poor, yet flesh-and-blood players pass, and the passers walk away richer — you may gesture at where the proof might fail, whether in the common knowledge assumption or in the conception of rationality it employs, but leave the tension standing rather than settling it. A game theorist should be able to reconstruct the backward induction argument from the structure alone, and a reader who has never heard of it should still come away with a good poem about trust eroding under the pressure of a proof. Begin your response with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary.
