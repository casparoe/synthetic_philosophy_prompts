I write novels for a living, mostly literary crime stuff, and the book I'm on now has a subplot where the defense leans on neuroscience to argue the accused couldn't have done otherwise — his brain had already started moving before "he" decided, or so the lawyer claims, and she trots out the old readiness potential findings from the eighties, the ones where unconscious brain activity apparently shows up a chunk of time before people report deciding to flick a wrist. Fine as fiction, but I made the mistake of actually reading around the topic to get the courtroom speech right, and now I'm stuck. Because the debate is ancient and nobody budges an inch. One camp says those experiments demolished the conscious will — the decision happens before you feel it, and the felt moment of choosing is just a press release. The other camp says the experiments show nothing of the sort: the timing relies on people reporting when they felt an urge, averaged over trials, and half of what got called "the brain deciding" was later shown to plausibly be background neural noise that only looks like a countdown when you squint at averages. Both camps have been saying roughly this for decades. Very smart people on both sides, same data, zero movement.

So what I need from you is a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on free will — I can fake a verdict in dialogue, but I'd like the shape of the thing to be accurate. Is this actually an empirical dispute that better experiments could settle, or is it verbal, with the two sides using "decision" and "volition" for different things (the neural preparation on one side, the conscious episode on the other), so they keep "refuting" each other across a gap? Or is something else doing the work underneath — like each side smuggling in a different picture of what a person even is, or disagreeing about what would count as evidence before any evidence arrives? My editor has a theory that the whole fight is really about whether you're allowed to infer "unconscious deciding" from "unconscious activity that precedes deciding," and that the camps split on that inference, not on the data. Tell me if she's onto something. And then be honest with me about what progress would look like given your diagnosis — a better experiment design, a distinction the literature hasnt drawn yet, or nothing at all. If it's the last one, say so plainly; that actually helps me, because then the honest ending for my lawyer is that she's not lying, she's speaking one dialect to a courtroom that runs on another.
