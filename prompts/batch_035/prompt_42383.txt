I'm a second-year PhD student writing a term paper that defends the Rawlsian difference principle, and a classmate of mine with pronounced libertarian instincts has walked me into an argument I cannot find my way out of. Before laying out the steps, let me describe his scenario, which deliberately avoids the famous athlete example and substitutes one closer to our own decade. Imagine a society — call it Solaria — that in 2060 fully satisfies Rawls's two principles: basic liberties secured, fair equality of opportunity in place, and economic inequalities arranged so that they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged. Call that distribution D1. Over the following thirty years the constitution, tax code, property law, and inheritance rules remain literally untouched: not a single institution is amended. What changes is only that a hundred million citizens voluntarily subscribe, a few dollars a month, to a service built by a uniquely gifted engineer named Ada, whose product nobody else can replicate; by 2090, through nothing but free exchanges, Ada has come to own roughly a third of the society's wealth, while — my classmate stipulates this for the case, and I let him — the least advantaged are no better off in absolute terms than they were in 2060, and relatively far, far behind. Call that distribution D2.

His argument runs as follows. (1) For Rawls, the subject of justice is the basic structure of society — the constitution, the property rules, the tax scheme — and not individual transactions, since individuals cannot track, and cannot intelligibly be held responsible for, the aggregate pattern their separate dealings produce. (2) Solaria's basic structure in 2090 is identical to its just basic structure of 2060: the very same institutions, unamended. (3) A distribution is just whenever it arises from free individual conduct within a just basic structure. (4) D2 arose from free individual conduct within a just basic structure. (5) So D2 is just. (6) But D2 flatly violates the difference principle: an enormous inequality conferring no benefit whatsoever on the least advantaged. (7) Therefore the difference principle was never a principle of justice for the basic structure at all — it either condemns steps nobody performed wrongly, or it is violated by an outcome nobody brought about unjustly, and on either horn it turns out not to be about justice. I cannot accept that conclusion: I began this paper convinced the difference principle is the most defensible principle of distributive justice available to us. My sense is that something slips somewhere between (2) and (5), but I cannot name it, and my classmate presses the point that if I deny (3), Rawls is dragged back into policing individual transactions, which is exactly what (1) was supposed to spare him. If I were forced to choose, I would rather surrender (3) than (1) or the difference principle itself — but I want to know precisely what I am paying, whichever I give up.

Please structure your reply as follows. Section 1, "The step I reject" (roughly 150 words): name the premise or inference precisely, in words rather than by number alone. Section 2, "Why it looked fine" (roughly 150 words): explain what in the setup made that step seem irresistible. Section 3, "What rejecting it costs" (roughly 250 words): the strongest version of what my classmate gains by pressing the cost, and the best available reply to it. Section 4, "The least bad alternative" (roughly 150 words): which other premise a defender of Rawls could sacrifice instead, and why that option is worse. Section 5, "His next move" (roughly 100 words): the most damaging thing my classmate can say once he hears Section 3, so that I can be ready for it.
