I'm taking an upper-level seminar called Philosophy of Technology, and our term-paper assignment is built around Langdon Winner's 1980 essay on whether artifacts have politics. In seminar, our professor keeps leaning on a particular rendering of the Moses example: the low overpasses on the Long Island parkways get described, in her words, as legislation cast in concrete — a design decision that does the work a statute would have done, only without debate, vote, or possibility of repeal. She uses this to argue not just that artifacts can have politics but that major infrastructure decisions deserve something like legislative scrutiny — design review conducted with the publicity and participation we demand for lawmaking, since designers are, on her telling, unaccountable legislators.

My TA pushes back with a different analogy. She says a low bridge is like a locked door in a shared house: the lock by itself excludes no one. Whether it excludes anyone depends entirely on how keys are distributed, who taught whom to pick locks, and who decided the lock was worth installing in the first place. On her view, the politics never lives in the artifact; it lives in the practices of interpretation and use around it, and the legislation analogy smuggles in exactly what needs arguing — that the artifact itself, rather than the social arrangements surrounding it, is doing the excluding. She thinks that once this is clear, my professor's call for design review aimed at artifacts is aimed at the wrong target.

There's a further complication I want addressed. A classmate claims the flagship example itself is shaky — that the bridge story is at least partly myth, that the clearances had other engineering explanations, and that the actual exclusion of buses was enforced partly by written regulation anyway. If he's right, does that damage the legislation analogy as such, or only its most famous instance — and can an analogy be load-bearing for a policy proposal when its flagship example is contested?

Please audit the legislation analogy as my professor uses it: give me the correspondence table (what plays the role of the statute, the legislator, the vote, the repeal, the enforcement), then the disanalogies, separated into ones that matter for her design-review proposal and ones that don't. Then assess whether her conclusion survives — and whether my TA's locked-door counter-analogy genuinely refutes the claim that artifacts have politics or merely relocates where the politics lives, since a door that is never unlocked by anyone arguably functions as a wall. In keeping with the seminar's standards: flag how confident you are in each of your major claims, and single out the point in your analysis you're least sure of, so I know where my paper's argument is most exposed when I submit it.
