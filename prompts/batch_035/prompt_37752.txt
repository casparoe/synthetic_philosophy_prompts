I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, and I've been asked to draft the distributive justice section of our MA final exam this year. The seminar covered prioritarianism and the leveling-down objection (Parfit's case against telic egalitarianism, the move to the priority view, and then the Otsuka/Voorhoeve objections and a bit of Broome). I want an essay question that can't be answered by reciting the standard line — you know, "egalitarianism says leveling down is good in one respect, prioritarianism doesn't care about relations between people at all, so it avoids the objection." I'd want students to realize that avoiding leveling down is basically free: pretty much anyone who isn't a telic egalitarian about outcomes can do it, so the real test of the priority view isn't that case but the trade-off cases where it agrees with the egalitarian (benefiting the worse off at some cost to the better off), and whether it can justify those verdicts for the non-relational reasons it claims to (seperateness of persons etc.) rather than by quietly smuggling in something relational. I'd also like the question to include a twist we didn't cover in seminar directly: intrapersonal leveling down, i.e. making someone's single life go more evenly by cutting down a high period instead of raising a low one. If the priority view's whole motivation is interpersonal, what should it even say about that — does treating a life's periods as just more positions in the aggregate expose a tension in the view, or is intrapersonal smoothing simply a prudential question the view can ignore? Please don't set the twist up as a gotcha with one right answer — a careful student should be able to come down on either side. Give me the question wording, a model answer of around 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 marks naming the must-hit moves, the marks each is worth, and a sentence on how students typically fumble each one. No need for a bibliography, the candidates won't have texts in front of them.
