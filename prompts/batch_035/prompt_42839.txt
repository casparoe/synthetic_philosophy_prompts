Next month I'm presenting to a workshop of environmental and resource economists, most of them steeped in Elinor Ostrom's work on commons governance. My thesis is that the flood of AI-generated text is driving the open internet toward a tragedy of the commons, but with a twist I expect to be controversial: the pasture isn't the information itself — copies are non-rivalrous, and I'll concede that immediately — but the finite stock of human attention and trust that readers and writers invest in the medium, which near-zero-cost generation can exhaust the way overgrazing exhausts a meadow. I further want to argue, cautiously, that some lessons from empirical commons research — boundary rules, monitoring, graduated sanctions — transfer to content labeling and moderation, though imperfectly. Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections this audience will actually raise, phrased in their vocabulary (rivalrousness, excludability, free-riding, polycentricity) and ordered by how much damage each would do; a two-minute reply to each; a note on where each reply is soft; what I should concede up front; and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. And if you think the analogy collapses at some structural point — so that no prep sheet can save the talk as framed — tell me that plainly rather than manufacturing replies.
