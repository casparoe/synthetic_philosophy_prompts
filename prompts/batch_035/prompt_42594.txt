I'm teaching a 3rd year metaphysics course and next week is dispositions, the whole conditional-analysis mess — finks, masks, antidotes, the works. It's a seminar of about 14 students, 90 minutes, and they will have read the standard stuff beforehand but I know from experience that on the page the whole debate feels like lawyers arguing about glass. "The glass is fragile but a sorcerer protects it" — they nod and move on, nobody actually feels why this is supposed to be a problem.

So I want an in-class game where the students themselves are trying to figure out what dispositions objects have, and the game quietly arms the other side with finks and masks, so they run face first into the failures of the naive view before anyone names anything. Here's roughly what I'm imagining, but please design the actual thing:

One group of students plays objects with dispositions, defined by secret cards (fragile, soluble, flammable, whatever — pick a good small set, maybe 3-4 dispositions and a null). Another group are testers: they can run "tests" (strike it, soak it, drop it from height 2, etc.) and observe outcomes, then have to declare what disposition each object has. The wrinkle: I want some rounds where a third set of students play interfering agents — a guardian who shields the glass exactly when it's struck and would break, someone who packs everything in styrofoam, maybe a saboteur who breaks things on purpose whenever they're tested even though they're sturdy. So the testers get outcomes that systematically mislead in both directions: fragile things that never break when struck, and non-fragile things that always do.

The payoff scheme should be points, not money — testers score for correct attribution, guardians for successful deception, that kind of thing — and I need it simple enough that I can explain it in under five minutes.

Design the whole thing: roles, the cards, turn structure, what tests cost if anything, how many rounds, how the interference gets revealed (I assume between rounds?). Then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the conceptual terrain — the simple conditional analysis and how the guardian cases and saboteur cases each break a different direction of it, the mask cases, and whatever else falls out of the mechanics you invent. Please don't lecture up front; the idea should show up in what happens to them.

Also two honest sections at the end: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I've had games collapse because the rules were too complicated or one student games the scoring, so be concrete), and which parts of the finks-and-masks literature the game just cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief — I'm thinking the dispute about whether finked dispositions are still intrinsic properties is hard to stage, but you tell me. The students are sharp but there's a couple who will try to lawyer the rules, so close the obvious loopholes.

Keep it under 90 minutes total including debrief, and I can print cards beforehand but no other prep.
