I've ended up basically agreeing with Moritz Schlick's essay from 1927 on the meaning of life: asking for life's purpose is confused, because pointing to some end outside life just pushes the question back (what's that end for?), so meaning has to be in the living itself, and the only moments that carry it are the play-like ones -- being absorbed in something for its own sake, the way children play, valued in the moment and not as a means to anything. Meaning is play, basically, and it isn't something you build up or keep; it flickers, and it's probably harder to recieve as you age and lose the capacity for play. What would actually count against this? Not just the reflexive "what about suffering and sacrifice" reaction -- I want the objections that cut deep: does it make a life of absorbed but horrible activity (someone who finds their work genuinely delightful) meaningful by definition? does it make meaning purely a matter of how a moment feels from the inside, so a chemically blissed-out person beats a devoted parent having a bad decade? does the terminus of "joy for its own sake" seem arbitrary next to other things people value intrinsically? Tell me which of these a committed Schlickian would concede as counting against the view and which they'd wave off, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether the position survives only by quietly downgrading from "the meaning of life" to "one nice feature of some moments" -- and if it has insulated itself that way, whether thats a fatal problem for a view of this kind.
