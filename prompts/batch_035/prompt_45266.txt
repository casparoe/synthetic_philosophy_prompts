For the past year, two members of my philosophy of science reading group have been arguing past each other about causation, and we've decided that instead of another evening of them talking at cross purposes, they should try to write something together. One is a committed counterfactual theorist in the possible-worlds tradition: he thinks of causation as a relation between particular events, analyzed by what would have happened in nearby worlds where the cause fails to occur, and he takes transitivity and the existence of a single objective causal relation to be default commitments worth defending. The other is an interventionist: she thinks causal claims only make sense relative to a model that carves a system into variables, and that asking whether c caused e, full stop, outside any such model, is like asking whether something is large without saying relative to what. Both know the literature on preemption, late preemption, and double prevention, and both have opinions about what the classic cases show; neither has moved an inch.

Write the joint statement that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between these two should produce. I want: (1) a numbered section of claims both could sign — about the formal machinery each side uses, about which of the standard puzzle cases any account must handle, and about what it would even be for either theory to fail; (2) a table of their residual disagreements, with each row phrased so that the side holding the view would accept the wording as a fair statement of it; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. I suspect one crux is whether variable-relative causal claims are a pragmatic restriction on a single underlying relation or instead reveal that there is no single relation to restrict; another is whether transitivity of causation is a substantive commitment or a modeling convenience. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about it in advance, so that we can check later who was right. One case I'd specifically like them to address, since it produced the worst shouting match of the year: a reactor trip triggered by a sensor failure, where a human operator standing by would have pressed the manual shutdown button half a second later had the automatic system not fired. Ask each of them to state, in their own terms, whether the sensor failure caused the shutdown, and have them explain what their answer turns on.

Please also tell me, at the end, whether you think part of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive — in particular, whether the dispute over whether the event-relation and the variable-relation are competing analyses of one thing, or two different kinds of claim that happen to share a word — and if so, which part and why. Don't invent citations or attribute specific arguments to named authors beyond what I've described; I'll match the positions to the literature myself. Format the whole thing as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, and keep the tone of a document both of them could actually put their names to.
