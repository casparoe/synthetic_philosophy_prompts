I work in health economics, and there is a pattern in resource allocation I keep running into without a name for it. Take two patients with the same objective condition. One is devastated by it; the other has lived with it for years, reports being fairly content, and scores near the healthy population on every welfare instrument we use. If a scarce treatment is allocated by welfare gain, the first patient gets it every time, and the second only when no unadapted patient is in the queue. What bothers me is the incentive structure: the surest way to be prioritized for future care is to stay as miserable as your condition allows, and the rule quietly punishes people for coping. The same logic appears anywhere allocation keys off welfare deltas rather than levels — disaster relief, workplace accommodations, and so on. I know Nozick's utility monster, and this looks like its photographic negative: the monster absorbs resources because his welfare rises steeply with them, whereas these patients lose out because theirs barely rises at all. Colleagues sometimes mutter about sour grapes when this comes up, but that seems to be a worry about whether the contented patient's happiness is genuine — a different question from who should get the pill. So: is there an established term for this configuration, in which an allocation rule deprioritizes you precisely because you have adapted to your condition? If there is, I want to know exactly what it covers and how it relates to the sour-grapes worry and to the utility monster; if not, coin one and define its boundaries — does it extend to people who were never badly off and are simply cheerful by temperament, and does it survive under a rule that prioritizes the worst-off rather than the biggest gainer? Please also say what argument or consideration would most plausibly change your verdict that this is a distinct phenomenon rather than the sour-grapes problem in distributional dress. End with a line of the form \answer{...} containing the term you settle on, or "no established term" if you conclude none exists.
