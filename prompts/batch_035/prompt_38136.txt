I'm teaching an intro philosophy class and next week we're on Merchant of Venice for our unit on money. I want to read the play through the social construction of kinds — Searle's institutional facts, Hacking's making-up-people stuff. The thing that fascinates me is the bond: marks on paper that somehow obligates Antonio to a pound of his own flesh. How does the play show that social fact getting its grip — and then the trial scene, where Portia basicaly lawyers it back out of existence with a technicality (he's owed flesh but the bond says nothing about blood), seems to show that what collective acceptance builds, clever interpretation can demolish. Meanwhile Shylock's Jewishness gets treated by the Venetians almost like a natural kind — inherited, in the blood — but the play also lets Jessica marry out and has Shylock forcibly converted at the end, which makes the kind look wierdly mutable. So my question for the reading: does the play treat money and race/religion as constructed the same way, or does it show them being made differently? And where does the play resist this lens — Shylock's suffering doesn't feel like it dissolves once you point at its constructedness, and I don't want the lazy "money is just paper" move; constructed things are still real, that's the whole point of the framework. Don't quote from the text, just refer to scenes, and tell me honestly if some of what I'm calling construction is better described as plain class politics.
