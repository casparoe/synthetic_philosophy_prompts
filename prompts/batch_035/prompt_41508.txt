I'm teaching an upper-level seminar in formal epistemology this spring, and every year the unit on Kyburg's lottery paradox and Makinson's preface paradox ends the same way: I walk the students through the standard survey of responses — keep the probability threshold, drop it, deny closure, bite the inconsistency bullet — and they leave knowing what the labels are without ever discovering which view they actually hold, because the positions arrive attached to names and journals rather than to cases they can adjudicate for themselves. This year I want to run the discussion in reverse. Please write a decision tree that takes a student from a fixed starting point — they accept both that each ticket in a fair million-ticket lottery is one they rationally believe will lose, and that a diligent author who has individually checked every chapter of her book still rationally believes the finished book contains at least one error — and leads them to a named position on the two paradoxes.

Every branch node must be a question the student can answer from their own reaction to a concrete case, phrased so that someone with no epistemological training could answer it without knowing which position it feeds. Work these cases into the nodes: a 70% rain forecast and whether it counts as believing it will rain; a single-ticket bet on a fair coin; a one-in-a-billion lottery, since if any answer changes between the million-ticket and billion-ticket lotteries the tree should detect that; and a safety inspector who has certified each valve on a pipeline individually and now faces the question of whether the whole system will run a year without a failure. The leaves must include at least: the view that rational beliefs can be jointly inconsistent; the view that no fixed high probability suffices for belief; the view that the lottery and preface cases come apart because of the kind of evidence involved rather than the probabilities; the view that splits belief from some weaker attitude like acceptance or reliance; and the view that keeps the threshold but denies that believing each claim commits you to believing their conjunction.

Two pieces of housekeeping after the tree. First, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate by cases alone — I suspect the evidential treatments and the context- or contrast-sensitive treatments of the lottery/preface asymmetry collapse into each other on every diagnostic question I can think of, and I would like to see whether you can find a case that splits them or whether they must be flagged as inseparable. Second, note any combination of node answers that lands on no leaf, and say in a sentence whether that is because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Assume the audience knows the probability calculus and nothing else. Begin your response with the tree itself — no preamble, no commentary, no restatement of the assignment — and put the flags, inseparability diagnoses, and unoccupied combinations afterward.
