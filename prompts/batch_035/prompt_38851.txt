I'm a PhD student in decision theory, and I'm preparing a seminar unit on I. J. Good's theorem that a costless observation can never lower the expected utility of an idealized Bayesian expected-utility maximizer. The theorem is often summarized as "information is always (weakly) valuable," but of course that slogan fails in all sorts of interesting ways once you relax the idealizations. I'd like your help building one really clean, pedagogically effective example of that failure.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher and decision theorist — someone comfortable with expected utility theory, Bayesian updating, and the mechanics of Good's argument — and think carefully before committing to an answer. In particular, I'd like you to begin with a short brainstorm: sketch three or four distinct kinds of situations in which acquiring information seems to make an agent worse off (e.g., settings where the agent's information state is itself observable to an adversary; settings involving multiple interacting decision-makers; settings where the agent's preferences or update rule are imperfectly Bayesian; settings where the "information" changes what options are available rather than just the probabilities). For each, say in one or two sentences which premise of Good's setup it violates, and then pick the single most promising one and develop it in full.

The fully worked example should satisfy the following:

1. It should be concrete and self-contained: specify the agents (or single agent), the acts available, the states of the world, the prior probabilities, and the payoffs, so that the "harmfulness" of information can actually be demonstrated rather than merely gestured at.
2. It should make vivid exactly where Good's theorem stops applying — that is, identify the violated assumption with some precision, rather than just observing vaguely that "real life is messy."
3. It should be honest about how contrived the setup is. If the example only works because we've stipulated something exotic, say so, and comment on whether structurally similar situations plausibly arise in ordinary decision-making (e.g., in negotiations, intelligence gathering, medical testing, or algorithmic pricing).

Finally, close with a short reflection on whether the string of caveats needed to rescue the slogan "more information is always better" leaves it with much content at all, or whether the theorem is better taught as a diagnostic tool — a way of pinpointing, in any purported counterexample, exactly which idealization has broken. Keep the tone reasonably formal, as this will go into seminar materials for other graduate students.
