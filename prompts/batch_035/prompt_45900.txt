So I run a little satirical blog where we do genre parodies aimed at philosophy people — last month we did a wildlife charity's grant application written by a deep ecologist, that kind of thing. Next up I want a fake FAQ page for the company that sells Nozick's experience machine. Like "Experience Machine™ Inc. — Frequently Asked Questions," roughly 650-750 words, numbered questions, that chipper-but-vague corporate support voice that never cracks. The target of the satire isn't Nozick himself, it's more like hedonic utilitarianism plus the way product marketing absorbs objections and sells them back to you as features. So the humor has to come from getting the philosophy actually right: Nozick's three reasons for staying out (that we want to do things, be a certain kind of person, and have contact with reality) should each get a question that rebrands the objection as a subscription tier or an add-on; there should be a question about hedonic adaptation where the answer basically concedes the set-point problem and spins it as a benefit of "continuous content updates"; and at least one question should use the status quo bias point as a hard-sell line — something like, you'd obviously plug in if you were already plugged in, so your reluctance is just inertia, act now.

A few more things I want in there: a customer asking about the reverse experience machine (the version where you discover your whole life up to now was already simulated, so would you like compensation or just more of the same) and the company's answer should quietly destabilize their entire pitch without them noticing it does; a question from someone whose spouse refuses to plug in with them, where the answer can gesture at preference-satisfaction and objective-list replies but can never quite say why the spouse is wrong; and a question about choosing between the "Socrates satisfied" tier and the "fool satisfied" tier, where the reply has to handle the fact that Mill's answer kind of undermines their pricing model. Please don't wink at the reader, no Matrix jokes, and don't make the company secretly evil — the unsettling part should be that their answers are exactly as good as a real hedonist's answers, no worse. My readers know the well-being lit pretty well so they'll notice if you flub the arguments, but the piece should still be readable to someone who just knows the basic thought experiment. Oh and it should be deadpan enough that you can't tell from tone alone whether the company is the villain — only from the cumulative weight of what they keep not answering. Hopefullly that all makes sense, feel free to fold in other objections from the literature where they fit naturaly.
