I am a desk editor at a news organization that covers health and social policy, and nearly every week a story crosses my desk that turns on a heritability number: a politician citing the heritability of IQ to argue that schooling won't close gaps, a company using twin-study figures to market a "genetic propensity" report, an op-ed claiming that because height is highly heritable in wealthy countries, height differences between countries must be genetic. My fact-checkers have no genetics training, work under deadline, and need to decide within about fifteen minutes whether a given use of a heritability statistic is legitimate or a misapplication. Please write a reusable procedure they can follow.

The procedure should be a checklist run in a fixed order: first, what the number is being asked to do (explain within-group variation? predict an individual's outcome? license a cross-group inference? estimate response to a policy?); then, tests for whether heritability can support that use — for instance, that heritability is defined relative to a population and an environment, so it does not transfer across environments or to differences between populations that differ systematically in environment; that high heritability is compatible with large environmental effects (the classic case of a dietary condition where a heritable metabolic difference produces an outcome only under a specific environmental input, and removing the input removes the outcome regardless of genotype); that narrow-sense and broad-sense heritability answer different questions, and only the former tracks response to selection; and that heritability estimates carry assumptions of their own (for twin studies, roughly similar environments for identical and fraternal twins) that the story should at least flag. For each step, specify what counts as a pass, and where the step fails, state the stop condition: this use of the number goes in the story only with a qualifier, or not at all.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a commentator claiming that since IQ heritability is around 0.5–0.8 in modern populations, differences in average test scores between groups must partly reflect genetics; (2) a report that a behavioral trait has heritability of 0.6 in a twin study, cited as evidence that parenting interventions are a waste of money; (3) a piece noting that dairy cattle milk yield has a heritability of roughly 0.3, cited as evidence that selective breeding is hopeless and farmers should rely on feed and veterinary care instead. Make sure at least one worked case yields a conclusion my reporters will find inconvenient, and end by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do — in particular, where the checklist can only tell you a claim misuses the statistic and cannot supply the correct positive claim, and where a defensible use remains uncertain enough that the story needs a geneticist, not a checklist.

The point of the exercise is to guard against the two opposite failure modes: treating heritability as meaningless (it is not; it is a real, useful quantity in its domain) and treating it as a general-purpose measure of how "genetic" and therefore how fixed a trait is. The procedure should make the boundary vivid enough that a reporter internalizes it, not just complies with it.
