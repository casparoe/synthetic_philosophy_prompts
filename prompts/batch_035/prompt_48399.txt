For a few weeks now I've been keeping a note on my phone of cases where I catch myself saying someone "knows" something, trying to work out my actual view on whether what's at stake for you changes whether you know. Most of my cases share a base setup — you checked the pharmacy's website this morning and it said open until 10, so call it 95% confident — with the stakes moved around. When I'm walking past anyway and just want toothpaste, I know it's open. When my kid's ear infection means that pharmacy is the difference between a rough night and a fine one, I don't know it's open — I call ahead. But when the stakes are exactly that high and the phone line is dead and the site was updated an hour ago, so there's really nothing better I could do, I'm back to saying I know, or at least I'd drive over on the strength of it. And then the one that surprised me: same 95%, same huge stakes, but calling ahead would take two minutes and I'm already holding my phone — back to *not* knowing. So it's starting to look like what matters isn't the stakes at all but whether a better check is sitting right there, which is not what I expected to find.

Then there's a cluster about who's doing the asking. My sister is the one going to the pharmacy; she checked the hours, it barely matters to her, I have nothing riding on it — does she know it's open? Trivially yes. Same setup except I'm the one whose evening collapses if it's closed while she's just grabbing toothpaste, and "does she know it's open?" suddenly feels like it deserves a hedge, like "well, she knows as far as anyone can." And when the roles flip — she desperately needs it and I don't care — I say yes she knows, and then immediatly tell her to call ahead anyway, which my notes flag as me asserting she knows while advising her to act like she doesn't. There's also a pair where the stakes are big but disconnected from action: I know my retirement fund is in decent shape even though a lot rides on it, because I literally can't touch it for 25 years; and I know the caravan situation is fine even with the storm coming, because everything's insured and being wrong costs me nothing but paperwork.

And the one that bothers me most: last week nothing hinged on the closing time and I'd have said I knew it closes at 10. Tonight everything hinges on it, same memory, no new evidence, and apparently I've lost the knowledge somewhere in between. So: is there a single principle behind all my verdicts — something about the believer's stakes, or the stakes of whoever is saying "knows" (these seem to pull in differnt directions, and I half suspect they're two seperate phenomena trading on one word), or this thing about available double-checks — and if not, which of my verdicts should I give up first? Please also tell me what your favored principle says about the case I keep going back and forth on: 95% confidence, enormous stakes, verification possible but costly — a 20-minute hold, small next to the cost of being wrong but not nothing. And I'd like to know what it predicts for whether I can honestly text my wife "I know it's open" while she's the one deciding whether to drive.
