I'm on the board of a small foundation that can fund about four early-career researchers for three years each on the philosophical question of whether and how death is bad for the person who dies. We want a defended research agenda, not a survey. The open questions we're aware of include: the timing problem — when, if ever, is death bad for its victim, given that there is no subject around at the time it could be bad for; the Lucretian symmetry between posthumous and prenatal nonexistence, and whether any asymmetry between them can be justified without ad hoc premises; the benchmark problem — deprivation accounts say death is bad by robbing you of a continued life, but of which one: the realistically expected life, the life you would have lived, or the best life you could have lived; whether the deprivation account survives at all if the timing problem proves unsolvable, or whether the whole edifice stands or falls together; and the more radical suggestion that the badness of a premature death is not a separate fact at all, just a fact about the shape of the life as a whole. Rank these — or amend our list where it is wrong — and defend the ordering. For each item, tell us whether it is upstream of the others in the sense that other work quietly presupposes its answer, how tractable it is through philosophical analysis alone rather than waiting on empirical psychology about how people actually value their futures, and what a result on it would have to look like to count as progress. I care more about the dependency structure among the questions than about the list itself.
