My cousin lent me a pop-philosophy book about arguments for God's existence and there's a chapter on Pascal's wager. It sums it up roughly like this: 'Pascal said reason can't settle whether God exists, so we should treat the question as a bet. If you believe and God exists, the payoff is infinite — eternal life. If you believe and God doesn't exist, you only loose some worldly pleasures and a few hours at church. Since any finite cost is outweighed by an infinite possible payoff, the expected value of believing is infinite, so rationality requires you to believe in God. Pascal knew you can't just decide to believe on command, so his practical advice was to go through the motions — take holy water, attend mass — and belief will follow. In any case, critics have shown the whole thing collapses: maybe God rewards skepticism instead, or there are many possible gods you could bet on, and since you can't choose your beliefs, the wager is refuted.'

Is this a fair rendering? It sounds plausible, but a couple things nag me. The "rationality requires" move feels stronger than the math would give you — like, wouldn't the expected value also be infinite for basically any action with a nonzero chance of getting you into heaven, including putting off converting until your deathbed? And the last sentence seems to treat the objections as settled rather than as objections. Can you go through it and tell me which parts a Pascal scholar would nod along to, which parts a decision theorist would wince at, and whether the "refuted" conclusion actually follows from the parts of the summary that are accurate? And if the book is pointing at the wrong place — i.e., the real live objections to the wager are elsewhere — tell me what those actually are.
