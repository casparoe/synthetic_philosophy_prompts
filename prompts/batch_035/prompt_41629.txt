Write a parody of a rapid-response memo template issued by a fictional DC foreign policy think tank — call it the Institute for Applied Historical Perspective or something similarly bland — whose whole product line is telling policymakers which historical analogy governs the current crisis. The deliverable should look like an actual fill-in-the-blank crisis document: fields, checkboxes, a routing flowchart, maybe a footnote or two, roughly 650 words. The escalation ladder should start at Munich and only go up from there ("Munich," "Munich, but this time we're serious," etc.).

The target isn't any particular politician or party, it's the genre of lessons-of-history reasoning. The jokes have to come from real features of how these analogies actually get used, so a person who reads foreign policy commentary should keep nodding: the way "Munich 1938" gets deployed for everything from annexations to a tariff dispute, and the way it's structurally impossible to argue *against* because that just proves you're Chamberlain; the way both hawks and doves claim the lessons of Vietnam and come away with opposite conclusions (never get in vs. never fight with one hand tied) while each insists the other side learned nothing; the way "the Thucydides trap" has become a fixed property of US-China relations rather than an argument that might be wrong; the fallback to 1914 when nothing else fits. Include a section where the memo performs its own analogy-vetting — does the causal structure of the analogy actually match the case? — in a way that always passes, like a checkbox pre-marked yes, since that's honestly how it works: the analogy is usually picked after the policy, to dress it. And a closing recommendations box that somehow reccomends the same policy no matter which analogy was selected.

Deadpan throughout — it's a serious document from serious people, no winking. Don't invent dumbities for the think tank; every absurd move should be an exaggeration of something real that practitioners actually do, so the satire reads as fair rather than cheap. Bonus if there's one small moment where the template's logic breaks and the memo has to awkwardly patch itself (e.g., a crisis where both sides are invoking Munich at each other and the form only has one field for Chamberlain).
