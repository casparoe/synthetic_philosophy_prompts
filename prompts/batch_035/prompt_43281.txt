I'm a philosophy of mind grad student writing on methodological disputes in consciousness science, and I've become fascinated by one that seems to have gone nowhere in thirty years: the standoff between Dennett's heterophenomenology and its first-person critics. The idea, as I understand it, is that a consciousness scientist should gather subjects' reports under the same conditions as any other experiment, then treat those reports as a text to be interpreted with hermeneutic charity — like an anthropologist transcribing a community's myths — while suspending judgment on how much of that text corresponds to anything real in the subject's mind, letting third-person science later decide which parts of the story to keep, revise, or discard. The critics — Searle at one end, the neurophenomenologists in Varela's tradition at the other, plus phenomenologists like Gallagher — reply that this drains the science of its subject matter: consciousness is given to the subject from the inside in a way it is not given to anyone else, so if experience itself is the explanandum, then first-person reports can't be merely more behavior to interpret, and a method that refuses them any special authority either presupposes physicalism or quietly revives behaviorism. Dennett's camp answers that this is the critics helping themselves to exactly what's in dispute: any first-person method eventually has to produce public reports to be science at all, at which point you're doing heterophenomenology and pretending otherwise, and the "incorrigible datum" is a methodological fiction.

What strikes me is that each side has essentially one move — you beg the question — and each side regards the other's version of that charge as not even intelligible. I've watched this play out in print from the early nineties through the Journal of Consciousness Studies exchanges and into current neurophenomenology and contemplative-science work, and the positions in 2025 look almost interchangeable with those in 1995, just with better imaging studies attached. My tentative hypothesis is that this isn't really a fight about scientific method but about what a theory of consciousness owes its data: the Dennettians treat the contents of experience as revisable theoretical posits, while their opponents treat those contents as fixed points any revision must preserve, and both describe this deeper difference as if it were a disagreement about procedure. But I'm honestly unsure whether that makes the dispute substantive, merely verbal (two senses of "data" fighting under one word), or two different projects — an epistemology of introspection and a methodology for laboratory science — that got filed under a single heading. What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict on who's right: use what each side actually claims to say which kind of disagreement this is, and then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a conceptual distinction, a specified experiment, an agreed-upon statement of evidential roles — or give me an argument that this particular impasse should be expected to persist, and why. If you think the question is ill-posed in a way neither side has noticed, I'd want to hear that too, but you'd need to show me the point at which the two positions stop engaging each other.
