Retired meteorologist here, reading philosophy since I have more free time than sense. Something from my working years keeps bugging me and I'm wondering if it has a name. Everybody knows the sorites thing about when a heap becomes a heap, and clouds are the textbook case there -- no sharp moment a cloud forms, fine. But here's what actually used to bother me, and it feels like a different problem. Take one isolated cumulus. At its edges you have droplets and water vapor right at the threshold of what counts as cloud material. Depending on which edge droplets you include, you get a slightly different set of molecules that's just as eligible to be "the cloud" -- and there are astronomically many of these candidate sets, all overlapping almost completely. So either there are billions of clouds floating where I see one, or there's exactly one cloud but no fact about which droplet-collection it is. Neither sounds right. Is there an established concept for this specifically? I'm not after the sorites again -- that's about when a cloud exists at all, this is about how many exist and what "that cloud" picks out. And does the same thing apply to ordinary objects like me (I'm constantly swapping out atoms)? If philosophy has a name and a literature for this, tell me what it's called and how it relates to the sorites; if not, maybe you can coin something. Please answer in American English.
