I am drafting a novel whose protagonist, a midlist novelist of modest success, is offered a place in a near-future experience machine — a perfect one, by stipulation of the plot, indistinguishable from reality from the inside. She refuses, and her refusal is meant to carry the moral weight of the book. My editor, who is philosophically literate and enjoys provoking me, has objected that her refusal cannot be made intellectually honest, because my protagonist — and I — are already in the machine business. His claim, as he puts it over lunch, is that a novel is simply a low-resolution experience machine: it induces vivid quasi-experiences, emotions and memories of things that never happened, and everyone who has ever wept over Anna Karenina or felt homesick for Middle-earth has already voted with their library card to plug in. A reader who spends three hours living a fictional life and then says she would never enter a machine that does the same thing more thoroughly is, on his view, drawing a distinction without a difference — sentimentality dressed up as principle.

I take the analogy seriously enough to want it audited rather than answered with a novelist's flourish, because the shape of the ending depends on it. Please give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the machine, the plug, the reality left behind, the guarantee of perfection, and the unchosen exit, when the source of the analogy is reading (or writing) a novel rather than floating in a tank. Then tell me where the mapping breaks — I can think of candidates (the reader retains a standing awareness of fictionality that the machine-user lacks; the book ends and hands you back; the reader's experiences have no persistence, no stakes, no accumulation into a life), but I want your judgment on which disanalogies actually do the work his conclusion needs to fail, and which ones I merely wish mattered. Distinguish those carefully, since my editor will pounce on any disanalogy that is emotionally appealing but dialectically inert.

Finally, address the conclusion the analogy was deployed to support: that a novelist's heroine — or a novelist herself — has no principled standing to refuse the machine, and that her refusal is hypocrisy. Does that follow once the disanalogies are on the table, or does the editor's analogy quietly swap something out — for instance, treating the value of fiction as consisting solely in the experiences it induces, which is precisely the hedonist premise the experience machine was invented to test? And if you think a better analogy is available for what fiction actually does — one that would let my protagonist refuse the machine without renouncing her own novels — I would be grateful for it, since I need to know whether her refusal can bear the ending I have planned for it.
