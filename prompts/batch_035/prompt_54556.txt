I've been chewing on this framework people online use for picking causes — importance times tractability times neglectedness. i have no background in any of this, i just have a spreadsheet brain. thing is I want to use it not for saving the world but for deciding what to do with my saturdays in my town of about 80,000 people. the realistic options: help at the food bank, trap-neuter-return for the feral cats (big problem here, literally 2 people doing it), or push the city council to actually fix the storm drains that flood the same street every spring.

here's what bugs me. the way people use it, you score each of the three things and multiply. but the storm drains feel like the most important thing by far (water in people's basements) and yet there's nominally a city department on it, so neglectedness scores low and it drops out of the ranking — even though the department is basically doing nothing, which seems like the whole point. meanwhile TNR scores great on neglectedness because nobody does it, but honestly the importance is questionable, they're cats. and tractability doesn't even feel like its own thing: the more neglected something is around here, the more one extra volunteer moves the needle, so am I double-counting by multiplying all three? like the scores don't move independently.

so what I'm asking is: what are the most important implications of this framework for a small-potatoes local volunteer choosing between a handful of concrete projects? does it actually tell me anything at this scale, or is it only built for comparing malaria nets against asteroid defense? does it break when "importance" isn't open-ended (save the world) but bounded (one flooded street, maybe a few hundred people affected)? and is there a better way to handle the storm drain thing than just docking it for not being neglected — because "the government says it's on it" and "it's being handled" are clearly not the same fact.
