A student in my intro ethics section (engineering major, refreshingly blunt about this stuff) brought an argument to office hours that she says occured to her during a semester abroad, and it's been living rent-free in my head since. The setup: she spent last fall in Lisbon, where nobody queues the way we do -- or rather everybody queues by a different logic, a sort of cloud near the counter in which whoever is most ready to order wins -- and when she got back to the States and dutifully took her place at the back of the line, she started wondering whether she was doing anything morally required or just performing a habit with extra steps. Her argument: (1) a genuine moral obligation binds anyone in the same situation, not merely people who happen to share some local convention; (2) whether you're obligated to tip, queue, wait for the walk signal, and so on depends entirely on which side of a border you're standing on -- same act, opposite valence in Osaka versus New York; (3) so if skipping the tip or cutting the line is genuinely wrong here, there has to be something wrong-making about the act itself, independent of the convention; (4) but there isn't -- strip away the convention and the non-tipper does no describable harm, nobody is worse off than in the nearest convention-free world; (5) so everyday conventions generate no obligations at all; (6) therefore visitors are free to jaywalk in Tokyo and shoulder straight to the front of the Paris bakery line, and locals who never consented to the convention (who does?) are in the same boat.

Here's what's bugging me. The argument looks valid, and (6) applied to driving on the left in the UK is monstrous -- driving the wrong way through Piccadilly is a bit more than a faux pas, and it stays monstrous even though its wrongness is convention-relative in exactly the way (4) says nothing can be. The thing also seems to generalize: whether you must keep a promise depends on a contingent social practice, and the wrong of breaking one isn't independent of that practice, so by the same reasoning promises don't bind either. My student accepted that consequence with alarming cheerfulness -- promissory obligation, she says, is just coordinated expectation management, and the felt "obligation" is leftover guilt. I don't want to follow her there. But I can't find the flaw on my own: (1) looks like a perfectly decent reading of moral universalism, (2) through (4) look like plain sociology, and rejecting (1) seems to land me in the opposite problem -- if obligations can be geographically indexed, I can't figure out whether that means no convention is beyond criticism or none can be criticized, and I keep going back and forth.

So: which step do I give up, why did it look fine, and what am I paying for it? I'd like to keep the intuition that the queue binds you, the left-hand rule binds you, and the promise binds you, while still being able to say the unjust local convention (pick your favorite) doesn't. Is there a principled place to stand where that's possible, or does fixing the argument for the queue by brute force just hand the relativist everything else? And if the trick is hiding in (1)'s phrase about being "in the same situation," I'd want to know exactly which situations count as the same.
