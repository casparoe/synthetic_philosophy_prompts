My dissertation supervisor, a Greek historian of the old source-critical school now nearing retirement, has spent the year issuing rulings at our weekly seminar on what we are permitted to cite as evidence. Her track record so far: she accepts Thucydides' description of the plague of Athens as essentially eyewitness testimony; she dismisses Herodotus' figure for Xerxes' army, which runs into the millions, as logistically impossible and as deriving from Persian tallies meant to overawe; she accepts a Near Eastern king list that survives only in a much later compiler, because the same sequence of names appears independently in inscriptions from two different kingdoms; she rejects an oracle said to have foretold a battle when the oracle is attested only in a text composed after the battle, as prophecy after the event; she accepts a treaty quoted verbatim inside a later historian on the ground that the quoted wording uses diplomatic formulas that had fallen out of use centuries before the quoting author wrote; and she refuses to cite an unprovenanced inscription that surfaced on the art market, letter forms and all, because no one can say where it was found or by whom. She also has a standing line on speeches in ancient historians: since no ancient author could reproduce an oration delivered decades earlier, every reported speech is the historian's own composition, and she will not cite it as the speaker's words.

The new question, which she rules on next week: a Greek papyrus, apparently sitting in a European private collection since the 1920s, purports to preserve a document from the reign of a Hellenistic king. It dates a solar eclipse to a stated regnal year, and astronomical retrocalculation confirms that an eclipse was visible in the region on that date. It also records a peace treaty between two kings that no other source mentions, and the treaty is what my chapter actually needs. Predict her verdict on whether we may use the papyrus as evidence for the treaty. Identify the rule she seems to be running, and be explicit about the fact that at least two different rules fit everything she has said so far; say what each predicts here. Pay particular attention to whether the confirmed eclipse does any work for the treaty claim, given that anyone with astronomical tables, ancient or modern, could have inserted a computable eclipse into a forged or embroidered text, and that even a genuine eclipse report is compatible with a fabricated treaty in the same document. Name the further case that would best discriminate between the candidate rules. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and give the reasoning afterward, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Finally, say where your prediction is most likely to fail, and which single piece of information about the papyrus, if it turned out otherwise, would flip your prediction.
