Write a sonnet in the voice of a slave in an unusually mild household — the master is affable, the rations generous, no one has been beaten in living memory — arranged so that on any ledger of actual interference the speaker comes out ahead of many a free labourer. Let the octave be that ledger: the speaker itemizes the day's comforts and concludes, on the interference measure, that his lot is good. The volta comes at line 9 with the realization that the ledger is the wrong instrument: nothing on it is owed, every item hangs on the master's continuing good humour, and any of it could be withdrawn tomorrow on a whim, with no one to hear an appeal. The closing couplet should land the thought that freedom is the absence of a master, not the absence of interference — without using the word "republican" or any philosopher's name. A note on accuracy: the speaker's unfreedom must be located neither in the master's cruelty (there is none) nor in the speaker's own feelings (he may be quite content); it lies entirely in the standing possibility of arbitrary interference with no recourse. Someone who knows the non-domination literature should recognize the position exactly; someone who doesn't should still get a good poem about grace that can be withdrawn at any time.
