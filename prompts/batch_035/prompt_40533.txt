Two of my flatmates had a big arguement after a philosophy meetup about Moore's here-is-one-hand proof (the lecture where he claims to prove there are external things by holding up his hands), and I got roped in as witness. I stayed out of it but I wrote down roughly how it went, and honestly I couldn't follow all of it. Can you adjudicate it move by move -- who's right at each step, where each is conceding more than they need to, and whether there's a way of seeing the whole thing that both are missing?

Dan's opening move: the proof is a perfectly good proof. Premises: this is one hand; and here is another. Conclusion: at least two material things exist. Valid, and he knows the premises. It's not Moore's fault if the skeptic refuses to accept true premises -- that's not a flaw in the proof, it's stubbornness.

Ella's reply: that's exactly the problem, and begging the question isn't a formal fallacy anyway. A valid argument from premises you know can still fail as a *proof* when anyone who doubts the conclusion will, for that very reason, doubt the premises. Moore's confidence in the premise just is a piece of his confidence that there's an external world, so he's giving the skeptic no independent route to the conclusion. The argument is epistemically circular even though it's formally clean.

Dan's concession + counter: fine, it won't convert a committed skeptic, but not-persuasive isn't not-a-proof. And anyway he says Moore can't be read as trying to convert the skeptic -- the lecture is aimed at the philosophical claim that no proof of external things is even possible, and against that claim one plain counterexample suffices. If even one premise is genuinely known, that claim is refuted.

Ella: she's not disputing that Moore believes he knows it, she's disputing that a person can *know* such a thing in the dialectical position Moore is in, and this is where she brought up transmission failure -- the idea that knowledge can fail to transmit across a valid inference from premises you know, so Moore at most has a valid argument from known premises and the question of whether he knows the conclusion is left exactly where it was.

Dan: he said the transmission-failure talk just concedes his point in fancier vocabulary. If the premises are known and the inference is valid, what extra thing is required, other than that the premises shouldn't be epistemically downstream of the conclusion? And Moore's premises aren't downstream -- seeing a hand doesn't depend on already knowing external things exist. So the fancy condition is satisfied anyway and Ella has smuggled in a requirement no one ever stated.

Ella: she said it's the *reverse* dependence that does the damage -- because his evidence for the premise is the same evidence that would make the conclusion true, there's no independent epistemic route, and calling that stubbornness on the skeptic's part doesn't make it go away.

At that point it dissolved into both of them agreeing Moore had made things worse for himself by admitting elsewhere that he couldn't prove he wasn't dreaming at that moment, and into whether the premises of a proof have to be more certain than its conclusion. Neither of them would budge and then it got late. Please go through the exchange and tell me who's right about what at each point, not just who wins overall -- and if both of them are talking past each other somewhere, I'd like to know that too.
