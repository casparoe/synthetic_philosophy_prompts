I was raised Catholic, drifted away in my twenties, and lately I've been reading David Hume's famous essay on miracles entirely on my own — no courses, just evenings with the text and a lot of thinking. My working understanding of his argument is roughly this: a miracle is a violation of the laws of nature, and against such an event the experience of humankind is effectively uniform; so testimony can never establish one, because it is always more likely that the testimony is mistaken or lying than that the uniform course of nature was suspended — Hume's rule being that no testimony is sufficient unless its being false would itself be a greater miracle than the one it reports. When Hume targets reports from the distant past — scattered, secondhand, passed along by the devout — this seems right to me. But I keep coming back to Fatima, and I want an analysis of that episode through the lens of Hume's argument, done carefully rather than as a victory lap for either side.

Here are the salient facts as I understand them, since I'd rather you work from these than from anything uncertain: on October 13, 1917, near Fatima, Portugal, a crowd usually estimated between thirty and seventy thousand people gathered in an open field, responding to three shepherd children's claim that the Virgin Mary would perform a public sign that day. After hours of rain, many present — including, reportedly, reporters from secular and anti-clerical Lisbon newspapers — described the sun as appearing to spin, dart, and plunge toward the earth before returning to its place, with the soaked ground drying; some witnesses located miles away, unconnected to the pilgrimage, also claimed to see something unusual in the sky, and accounts appeared quickly in newspapers that had no motive to flatter the Church. Portugal's government at the time was openly anti-clerical, which is part of why the episode is interesting to me rather than just another pious legend.

What I want is a disciplined two-way mapping. First, run Fatima through Hume's framework exactly as stated: who counts as the testifier, what the antecedent probability of a solar dance would be, what the alternative hypotheses are (mass suggestion on a rain-soaked day, retinal effects from staring at the sun, some atmospheric optics event, embellishment after the fact), and how Hume's rule weighs testimony against them — including the awkward fact that his essay assumes testimony arrives piecemeal from remote ages, whereas here we have many simultaneous alleged witnesses, some hostile, reporting within days. Then be honest about where the model breaks: does Hume's argument actually do any work here, or does it just reassert the antecedent improbability no matter what the evidence looks like, in which case the Fatima case may expose a structural feature of the argument that was less visible when its targets were ancient and anonymous? And finally, tell me what the episode shows about the theory: whether it is a confirmation of Hume, an anomaly, or a kind of case his argument never anticipated — and, if you think the deflationary explanations are sufficient, show me how the reasoning goes rather than assuming it. Please answer in polished American English, and don't give me a bibliography or a list of references; I want the reasoning itself, in plain prose, as though you were thinking it through with me at the kitchen table.
