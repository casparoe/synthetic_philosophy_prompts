In a philosophy-of-mind reading group I attend, a fellow participant closed off a discussion about machine intelligence by invoking Gödel: "any system is either incomplete or inconsistent — so a machine mind either misses some truths or contradicts itself, whereas we can see the truths it can't prove." The group nodded along, but I suspect the one-liner is being asked to do several different jobs at once, and I'd like to know exactly which of them it can do.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses that the slogan "any system is either incomplete or inconsistent" could be expressing, as a menu with a verdict for each. Along the way it would help me to see: what "system" has to mean for the theorem to apply (formal, effectively axiomatized, capable of representing enough arithmetic) and what happens to readings that extend it to scientific theories, legal codes, or minds; whether the disjunctive form is even faithful, given that the theorem is a conditional on consistency rather than a dichotomy; the difference between a sentence being undecidable in a system and being unprovable in principle, including what the second incompleteness theorem does and does not say about a system proving its own consistency; and the gap between "there are truths no given system proves" and "there are truths no system could prove."

For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or straightforwardly false (noting where complete and consistent systems — e.g., Presburger arithmetic — break the generality), and, crucially, whether it would actually support the conclusion my interlocutor drew: that human minds outstrip any machine in what they can see to be true. If none of the readings gets him that conclusion without extra premises he never stated, say which premises he would need.
