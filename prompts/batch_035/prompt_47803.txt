Next month I'm presenting at a workshop my thesis that karma, as the Buddhist and Brahmanical traditions actually use the concept, is best read as a theory of moral desert -- the claim that a being's wellbeing ought to track its moral record -- and that what makes it philosophically interesting is that it fuses the desert claim with a mechanism. Western desert theories assert that outcomes fitting conduct while leaving the mechanism totally blank, and then take flak for it (desert with no mechanism is just mystery, or worse, an invisible hand that rationalizes whatever happens). Karma says the fittingness is carried by an actual causal process, so it looks like it answers that objection head on. The audience: about twenty analytic ethicists who've never read a primary Asian text and will hear "desert" and immediately reach for their stock files on retributivism and the just-world critique, plus two Sanskritists who are pretty sure that reading karma through Western desert-talk flattens the texts -- on their view karma is an impersonal natural law and the "fruit of action" language is causal/botanical, not normative. There will also be at least one Buddhist in the room ready to press the no-self point: desert presupposes a subject who owns the deed, and theres no continuous self across lives to own anything.

Structure the prep sheet as: 'Concede up front' (3-4 sentences), 'Objections, in the order they'll come' (roughly eight, each a short paragraph: the objection phrased in that particular audience's vocabulary, my two-minute reply, and a note on where the reply is soft), 'The one reply that has to land' (2-3 sentences), and 'The question I'm hoping nobody asks' (one short paragraph, with an honest note on what I'd actually say if it comes up).
