I sit on the IRB of a university medical center that has been running a trial in which hippocampal tissue damaged by epilepsy is progressively replaced with neuromorphic implants replicating its input-output function. The surgeons now ask to expand the protocol to cortical regions, with the eventual aim of wholesale brain replacement for volunteers whose disease is otherwise terminal. The protocol offers two replacement modes and leaves the choice to patients: "substitution," in which a small patch of tissue is disabled and its artificial counterpart activated in the same operation, and "shadowing," in which the implant first runs in parallel with the intact tissue, output-matched, and the biological tissue is disabled only weeks later, once the implant has fully taken over. Patients remain awake throughout and report on their experience at each step.

Two of my colleagues have staked out positions our chair thinks the IRB cannot simply ignore. One holds that functional continuity settles the identity question by itself: if each step preserves function with no experienced interruption, the patient survives, and the consent form need only cover ordinary surgical risks. The other holds that somewhere in the shadowing protocol — perhaps at the exact moment the biological tissue is disabled — the patient dies and is succeeded by an implant-driven copy, so no consent form can make the trial legitimate; consenting to one's own replacement by a copy is not consenting to treatment but to a kind of death. Our current consent form describes the implants as restoring lost function and says nothing about any of this.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting: approve, reject, or approve with conditions; state exactly what, if anything, the consent form must say about personal identity and survival; and say whether the two replacement modes should be treated differently. The reasoning should be one both colleagues could actually engage with rather than a restatement of general principles. Finally, flag where the memo's reasoning is most fragile: which substantive claim, if it turned out false, would most change your recommendation, and is that a claim the IRB could resolve or one it must declare beyond its competence?
