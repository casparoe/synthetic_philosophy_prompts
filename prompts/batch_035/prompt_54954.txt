Two scholars have been arguing for years about the ethics of collective remembrance, and their exchanges have become repetitive: one holds a Margalit-style view that communities bound by thick relations of care have a genuine duty to remember the wrongs done to their members, including at cost to themselves; the other holds a Rieff-style view that much of what passes for remembrance is curated grievance, that it feeds cycles of revenge more often than it prevents recurrence, and that forgetting — or at least refusing to keep wounds publicly open — is sometimes the more humane policy. They have finally agreed to a structured adversarial collaboration and want you to draft the joint closing statement, with the following stipulations.

The statement must open with a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, stated so that neither side feels its view has been watered down. In drafting this section, take seriously a distinction their earlier arguments blurred: that between a community's memory (what its members actually retain, ritualize, and feel) and its historical record (what archives, textbooks, and scholars preserve). Both sides should be able to agree that these can come apart — that a society can possess exhaustive records while remembering almost nothing, and can remember vividly while its records are thin or false — and the statement should say where the duty to remember, if any, attaches. Both should also be able to sign a claim to the effect that remembrance is always a curated subset: no community remembers its past whole, so the real question is never remember versus forget but who selects, on what criteria, and with what permanence. If the drafters can converge on the claim that commemorative capacity is a scarce resource — that every act of public remembrance competes for limited attention and ritual space, so that remembering one thing at full intensity means attenuating something else — that too belongs in the agreed section, since it is the premise neither side has ever disputed but neither has ever used.

The disagreements section must be a table, with each row containing: the precise formulation of the disagreement (phrased so each camp would accept the wording of its own position and not wince at the other's); the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp; and a test both camps accept as bearing on that crux, with each camp's prediction about the test's outcome written down in advance. I expect at least these disputes to appear, though you may find others: whether duties of remembrance grounded in thick relations of care can bind a community when honoring them predictably deepens conflict with outsiders; whether forgetting is even the kind of thing a polity can decide to do, as opposed to something that happens to it when memory naturally fades; whether working through a traumatic past is a real psychological process with a completion condition or an open-ended slogan; and whether there is an important moral difference between letting memory lapse and deliberately dismantling it — say, between a memorial that fades through neglect and one that is deliberately designed to disappear, as some counter-monuments have been.

Finally, the statement must include a joint verdict — agreed by both camps — on one concrete proposal the collaboration devised as a stress test: sequenced forgetting. Under this scheme, a post-atrocity truth commission would take full testimony, preserve it under seal, publish nothing for seventy-five years, and set a legally fixed disclosure date, with custodians forbidden from curating or excerpting the sealed material in the interim. The Margalitian sees in this a betrayal of the living victims, whose wrongs are consigned to silence precisely when acknowledgment could matter to them. The Rieffian sees a way to protect the present from grievance while guaranteeing the eventual historical record. The joint verdict need not resolve the dispute, but it must say exactly which of the earlier disagreements the proposal turns on, and identify what the seventy-five-year interim would have to look like — what would happen or fail to happen in the sealed society — for each camp to concede that it had been wrong about this case. If, in drafting the whole document, you conclude that some portion of their decade-long argument was purely verbal, name that portion and explain what the two sides were using the same words to mean differently.
