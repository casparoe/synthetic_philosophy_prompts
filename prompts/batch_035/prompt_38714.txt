I'm a novelist working on my fourth book, and I could use your help with a prediction problem. The setup: a figure who is unmistakably Dostoevsky (I call him Fyodor Mikhailovich in drafts; in my story he's lifted out of 1879, mid-way through writing Brothers Karamazov, and deposited in present-day Germany) gets appointed to a commission reviewing interrogation law. The trigger in my plot is a case modeled on the real Frankfurt kidnapping from 2002: a man has hidden an eleven-year-old boy somewhere, refuses to say where, and a police official — on his own conscience, no legal cover — threatens him with pain, only for it to turn out the boy was already dead, so the threat bought nothing. Then a law professor character proposes "torture warrants": judges sign off in advance, the paperwork is filed, liability pre-cleared, everything hygenic. My problem is that I keep writing Fyodor as a clean absolutist who thunders no, never — and it feels fake, because the actual man was much stranger than that. My friend who did a philosophy PhD keeps telling me I'm really just staging the old ticking-bomb thought experiment with a mustache, and she's right.

So give me your honest prediction of what Dostoevsky would actually say, anchored in what we know about him, covering these threads. First, the Inquisitor: a pre-cleared judicial warrant is basically the Inquisitor's move — the institution relieving mankind of the burden of freedom for its own happiness — but the Inquisitor torments people out of love, so I need to know whether Fyodor would see the warrant's banality as worse than rage, or as genuinely more merciful than an interrogator improvising in the dark. Second, the scaffold: he stood before a firing squad at Semyonovsky Square in 1849 and got the reprieve at literally the last second, so he knew what a ticking clock does to the person standing under it, arguably better than any moral philosopher ever has. Does that experience give him a special claim in this debate, and if so, in which direction — horror at the state manufacturing that clock on purpose, or sympathy for the interrogator who was, like the Tsar in 1849, holding the schedule? Third, the rebellion chapter: his own hero refuses a harmony purchased with one tortured child — "It's not that I don't accept God, Alyosha, I just most respectfully return him the ticket" — and yet the same book's detective work leans on confessions extracted under crushing psychological pressure, and Dostoevsky genuinely believed suffering could purify. And the twist my editor loves: my detainee is a martyr-seeker, an Underground Man type who would lie under torture precisely because the pain is the point — a psychology Dostoevsky himself invented, so he'd know the consequentialist math that pain reliably yields truth is naive.

Tell me how each thread resolves, where he'd genuinely waver, and please dont sand him down into either a modern liberal or a cartoon absolutist — he was an obedient monarchist converted by his own near-execution, believed in the redemptive value of suffering, and never actually wrote the line everyone half-remembers about everything being permitted if there's no God. No citations needed, just make the case from the man and his books, and be honest where the evidence runs out and you're extrapolating.
