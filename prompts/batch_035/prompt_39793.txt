Next week I am teaching a fifty-minute section of a second-year epistemology course on the Meno problem: the question of why knowledge is more valuable than merely true belief, when true belief already seems to get you whatever you wanted — Plato's own setting has Socrates concede that a true opinion about the road to Larissa will get you to Larissa just as well as knowledge will, before claiming that knowledge is worth more because true opinions, like the statues of Daedalus, are worthless while they wander, and only stay put when tied down by an account of the cause. I want the bulk of the section on the modern problem rather than on exegesis, so I need a plan for the whole hour, not the explanation itself.

Specifically, I want from you: the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what the dependencies are; the single carrying case that does the work for the whole session — I have been torn between two candidates, the farmer whose true belief that treasure is on his land comes from a charlatan's dowsing rod that happens, for once, to point at something, and the half-reliable diviner whose true beliefs track the truth only some of the time — so tell me which one to build the session around and what the case must include and what it must lack; and the misconceptions the students will arrive with or form, with the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I can already predict some of them: that the Meno problem just is the Gettier problem; that the extra value of knowledge must be practical, so that the answer is simply that knowledge is more useful across a lifetime; and that reliabilism settles the question by definition. The part I most want sequenced carefully is the swamping argument: that if the value of a belief's justificatory pedigree comes entirely from its tendency to make the belief true, then once the belief is true the pedigree can contribute no further value, in the way that the reliability of an espresso machine adds nothing to a cup it has already produced. I can never get students to see this as a genuine problem rather than as a quibble about definitions, so plan where in the sequence it will land hardest and what has to be in place beforehand for it to register as a threat.

Close the plan with a diagnostic question I could ask in the last five minutes that would reveal whether they have understood the problem, as opposed to having merely adopted some favored answer to it; and be explicit about what should be left out at this level — the contemporary menu of value-driven theories of knowledge, the credit view on which knowledge is a cognitive achievement, and the distinction between the primary question of why knowledge is valuable at all and the further question of whether its value derives from the value of its parts — while telling me which one of those I should still gesture at in a single sentence, and why.
