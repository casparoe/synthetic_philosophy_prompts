hey, so I'm a PhD student (moral psychology, but I did enough metaphysics in my masters that the department let me teach an upper-level undergrad module on personal identity this term). I'm writing the final and I want one essay question built around Parfit's teletransporter, but I'm completely sick of marking answers that just recite the psychological continuity view versus the body view, nod at branching, and call it a day. Anyone who did the reading can do that on autopilot.

so I've invented a variant and I want the question to really hinge on it. here's the setup I want to use, call it the insurance teleporter: on your 30th birthday you step into a scanner, which records your complete physical and psychological state, and then you just... walk out and keep living your life normally. decades later, at 80, you die of old age. a week after your death, the archive is used to grow a perfect copy of you-as-you-were-at-30 — all your memories up to the scan, none of the intervening fifty years. call him Backup.

what I like about this is that it seems to drive a wedge in a way the ordinary instant teleporter doesn't: Backup is directly psychologically connected to you-at-30, but the connection spans a fifty-year gap in which nothing exists to bear it. so on a reductionist view, is psychological continuity even the right thing to look at here — does it hold across a temporal gap with no subject in it? and worse, it seems like whether you "survived" depends on whether anyone bothered to press the button after you died, which feels like the wrong kind of fact to make survival depend on. meanwhile the 80-year-old's memories and projects are simply not in the archive at all, so the case also seperates the interests of you-at-30 from you-at-death, which the standard teleporter never forces you to notice. I want a question where a student who only memorised the textbook stuff cannot fake their way through — the crux has to be the gap and the button-pressing problem specifically.
