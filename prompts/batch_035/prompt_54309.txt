I am drafting an essay on the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion, built around a distinction I have not seen stated quite this way, and I would like your comments on the draft material below. Please write your comments in American English.

The working thesis: the difference between manipulation and persuasion does not lie in the channel of influence (reason versus emotion), but in the direction of openness. Persuasion, on this view, is bidirectionally revisable: the persuader presents her case in a form that invites scrutiny and, at least implicitly, commits to revising her own position if the target's response turns out to be stronger. Manipulation is unidirectional: the manipulator structures the exchange so that only the target's position is ever up for revision; her own view is fixed offstage and never genuinely exposed to the target's counter-considerations. The target is being moved, not met. On this account, an appeal to emotion is legitimate persuasion when the emotional claim is offered as something the other party could contest and expect to be taken seriously (for example: this policy would humiliate these people, and I ask you to consider whether that matters morally); it is manipulation when the emotional display is engineered so that contesting it is either impossible or felt to be taboo.

The planned sections of the essay are as follows. First, a critique of the familiar story that manipulation works by bypassing reason: I argue it is too broad, since it classifies heartfelt appeals to sympathy as manipulation, and it cannot explain why a heartfelt appeal accompanied by an invitation to disagree still feels clean. Second, a statement of the bidirectional-openness criterion sketched above. Third, a section on objections, of which I am aware of three: that the criterion condemns all one-way mass communication, since a pamphlet or billboard cannot take objections; that it lets a skilled manipulator off the hook, because she can fake openness (listen attentively, perform receptiveness) while her real position never moves; and that it makes manipulativeness depend on the manipulator's private counterfactual dispositions, which is strange for a wrong that is supposed to be visible in how the target is treated.

I have three questions for you, in order of importance.

First, does the core criterion actually do the work? Try to construct a counterexample that divides the cases the wrong way: either a case of legitimate persuasion in which back-talk is impossible (say, a dying parent's last advice, or a single broadcast that cannot receive replies) that the criterion nonetheless brands as manipulation, or a case of manipulation that satisfies the openness test.

Second, is the third objection fatal? My tentative reply is that the criterion concerns what the persuader holds herself answerable to, not her hidden psychological states, but so far every way I phrase that seems to restate the problem rather than answer it. Tell me whether there is a real reply here or only a verbal one, and if there is one, sketch it.

Third, stepping back: does the central move strike you as a genuinely fresh angle or as a familiar idea in different clothes? Give your impression and the reason for it in your own words; I do not need a list of names or references, just your sense of whether the argument would read as derivative to a philosophically literate audience. Beyond these three questions, I would welcome one suggestion you think would most improve the essay, but please keep the bulk of your comments focused on the questions above.
