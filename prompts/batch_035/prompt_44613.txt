I've been asked to circulate a short position statement before a workshop on formal epistemology, and before I commit it to the group I'd like an audit of whether my views on Bayesian confirmation theory actually form a coherent package. I've tried to write each item as something I genuinely believe, not as positions I'm testing out, and I honestly don't know where the strains are. Here is the set:

1. Rational credences obey the probability axioms, and when an agent acquires new evidence she should update by conditionalizing on it.

2. Confirmation is a relevance relation between evidence and hypothesis: E confirms H for an agent just in case conditioning on E would raise that agent's credence in H.

3. The problem of old evidence — what Glymour called the problem of old evidence — is a serious and, as far as I can tell, still-open wound in Bayesian confirmation theory rather than a technicality. The 1919 eclipse observations surely confirmed general relativity, and so did Einstein's ability to explain the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion, an anomaly astronomers had lived with since Le Verrier identified it in 1859; yet by (2), once such evidence is absorbed and held with credence one, it is incapable of confirming anything. I also hold a related intuition: the light-bending result confirmed general relativity more strongly than the perihelion fit did, precisely because the former was a novel prediction and the latter was an accommodation of long-known data.

4. Logical omniscience — the assumption that an agent knows all logical and mathematical consequences of what she knows — is an unrealistic idealization, but a harmless one: nothing of epistemological importance is distorted by pretending that agents are logically omniscient, and the ideal verdicts of the theory remain a guide for real, limited agents.

5. The right repair for the old evidence problem is counterfactual: to ask how much E confirms H, you subtract E from the agent's total knowledge, ask what her credence in H would then have been, and measure confirmation by the gap between that counterfactual credence and her actual one.
