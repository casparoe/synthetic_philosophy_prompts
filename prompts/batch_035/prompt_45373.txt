I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, writing a dissertation chapter on how agents should treat the probabilities that statistical mechanics hands them, and the Principal Principle keeps doing load-bearing work in the background without my ever stating what I actually believe about it. My committee has asked me to be more explicit about my commitments, so here is what I want from you: your actual credence that some suitable refinement of Lewis's Principal Principle is correct, where the target proposition is roughly this — there exist objective chances, and a rational agent who knows the chance of an outcome to be p, and has no inadmissible information, should set her credence in that outcome to p. I don't want "it depends on how you formulate it"; I want a number and a full decomposition into sub-questions. Some terms I'd expect to see, though feel free to restructure if you think the partition is wrong: (i) that nontrivial objective chances exist at all, and if so whether they are best-system Humean or something propensity-like; (ii) that the known counterexamples to the unconditional formulation — cases where one conditions on admissible-but-correlated evidence, or on the chance assignment itself, and the deference breaks — are failures of the letter rather than the spirit, so that some relativized, "new principle" style refinement can get it right; (iii) that the principle is a substantive norm of rationality rather than something close to true by definition of "chance"; (iv) that the principle extends to self-locating and generalized cases, paradigmatically Sleeping Beauty, or — if it doesn't — that this is a genuine cost rather than a mild boundary-drawing exercise. Give each term a rough probability with a one-line justification, tell me how you combine them and whether the terms are really independent, and then identify the single term you are least confident in and what argument would most move it.

Two constraints on the answer. First, take a committed position throughout: if you think the Humean reading is the right one, say so and weight the terms accordingly, rather than laying out the interpretations symmetrically and leaving the choice to me — the whole point of the exercise is that my committee has flagged my habit of doing exactly that. Second, be candid about where the number is soft: I would rather have your honest posterior with visible seams than a precise-sounding figure built from sub-probabilities quoted to two decimals. And if you think the decomposition itself is misbegotten — say, because the real question is whether "chance" survives as a concept once all the needed refinements are in place, or because the interesting action is elsewhere entirely — then say that explicitly and restructure the terms, but still give me a number at the end.
