My reading group (masters program, philosophy of mind — we just spent two weeks on the zombie stuff) has an argument kicking around that I can't get out of my head, and I've started to suspect it proves way too much. My friend Dan thinks he's turned the old worry about epiphenomenalism and reports of experience into something general. Here it is. (1) Suppose epiphenomenalism is true: qualia exist but have no causal influence on anything physical. (2) My judgment that I am seeing red is either a physical event or fully constituted by physical events, and in either case its causes are entirely physical. (3) So on epiphenomenalism, my judgment that I'm seeing red is not caused by the red-quale itself — it's caused by neural states that are merely correlated with the quale. (4) A belief counts as knowledge only if the fact believed plays the right kind of role in producing the belief. (5) So if epiphenomenalism is true, I don't know I'm seeing red — not through introspection, not any other way. (6) But I know I'm seeing red if I know anything at all. (7) So epiphenomenalism is false.

The part that worries me is the next move. Dan says (4) doesn't just kill epiphenomenalism. Apply the same principle to numbers: if abstract objects are causally inert, which they are if Platonism is true, then mathematical facts play no role in producing mathematical beliefs, so by (4) we'd know no arithmetic. He says the same goes for moral facts if you're a non-naturalist about them, and maybe for laws of nature too. So the argument takes out Platonism about numbers, a chunk of moral realism, and epiphenomenalism, all in one sweep, and Dan is deighted — his attitude is basically, so much the worse for all three. I'm not delighted. I want to keep (6), and I also really don't want to concede that we can't know arithmetic, so I can't just accept the generalization.

Every time I try to locate the bad step I end up somewhere I don't want to be. My current hunch is that either (2) is the problem — since the judgment that I'm seeing red is itself an episode of consciousness if anything is, so maybe the quale doesn't need to cause the judgment from outside, it might be part of the thing — or else the trouble is in the slide from (3) to (5), in whatever "plays the right kind of role" is supposed to mean, because if "the right kind of role" means "causes it" then Dan's generalization goes through, and if it means something weaker then I can't see why (5) follows at all. When I floated the (2) idea at dinner, my roommate said okay, but if the judgment is partly constituted by the quale, then either constitution is a causal relation, in which case epiphenomenalism is violated anyway and you've just moved the problem, or it isn't, and then you owe me an account of how a non-causal constitutive relation is supposed to be the "right kind" when the causal one wasn't. I hate that she's right that I owe that. Which step do I give up, and what am I actually paying for it — including whether the numbers case and the qualia case really stand or fall together?
