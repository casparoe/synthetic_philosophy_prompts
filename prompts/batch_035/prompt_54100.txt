I hold a position I'd summarize as "free information can never hurt a rational agent": if some piece of evidence is relevant to a decision, costs nothing to obtain, and is made available before you act, then an expected-utility maximizer is never worse off, in expectation, for having seen it than for not having seen it. This is essentially Good's theorem, and the proof is famously short — the point being that the expected utility of deciding after the evidence arrives, whatever it says, can be no lower than the expected utility of the best act you could choose without it. From this I draw a fairly strong philosophical conclusion: that rational agents should always welcome costless evidence, that deliberate ignorance is always some kind of failure, and that the intuition many people have that they'd be better off not knowing certain things must be a confusion — the result of conflating decision-relevant information with idle, morbid, or merely interesting information that serves no choice.

I want to know what would count against this position. Not just the obvious caveats about acquisition costs, but the whole map of its exposure. Some things I want you to address specifically:

First, the multi-agent problem. The theorem is framed for a lone agent choosing among acts against nature. But in strategic settings, things seem to go differently: in a negotiation, being ignorant of your own reservation price, or being verifiably unable to see your opponent's move, can help you; there are games where committing to remain uninformed is strictly optimal. Does this refute the position, or merely relocate it to "information never hurts you against nature, only against other agents" — and if so, is that relocated view still interesting, given that most real decisions are at least partially strategic?

Second, the non-expected-utility problem. The theorem is proven within expected utility theory, but it is known among decision theorists that once you allow preferences that violate the independence axiom — say, certain rank-dependent or betweenness-free theories — an agent can be made worse off by free, relevant information. Does this show the position is hostage to EU theory itself? If someone rejects EU maximization on independent grounds (Ellsberg-style ambiguity, say), should they also reject the welcome-all-evidence norm, and is that a serious cost?

Third, the fixed-preferences assumption. The proof seems to hold the utility function fixed while updating probabilities. But some information changes what you value rather than what you believe: learning a medical diagnosis might make you value different things, or learning a painful truth about your own character might change your conception of a good life. Is the "never hurts" claim even evaluable in such cases, or does the theorem silently exclude them?

Fourth, and most concerning to me: the qualification question. The position as I stated it is hedged — "costless," "in expectation," "relevant to a decision," "expected-utility maximizer." Each hedge defuses a counterexample: the prisoner who'd rather not know the execution date is refuted by "in expectation"; the compulsive checker who cannot stop gathering information is refuted by "costless"; the person destroyed by a truth that changed nothing about any available act is refuted by "relevant to a decision." At some point this starts to look like the theorem is a mathematical claim that cannot fail and a philosophical claim that has been systematically hollowed out. So tell me: is the remaining claim — that a rational agent facing a genuine decision is always at least as well off, in expectation, seeing the costless evidence first — a substantive thesis that could have been false, or a dressed-up tautology? If it's substantive, what would the world have to look like for it to be false? If it's near-tautological, is that itself a defect for a position of this kind?

I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an account of which ones a committed defender of the Good's theorem picture would concede and which they'd wave off as outside the theorem's scope — and, crucially, whether that gesture of demarcation is intellectually honest or a way of never having to admit the position risks anything. For my part, the cases I'd find most persuasive are ones where the harm flows through the agent's own future self: information you cannot forget, cannot help but act on, or cannot help but feel, where the damage isn't a cost of acquisition but a consequence of possession. I'd like you to tell me whether the position can absorb those cases, or whether they mark its genuine boundary.
