So I'm a novelist, three books in, and the next one follows a mid-level asylum adjudicator at a fictional immigration tribunal -- think tired office, stacks of case files, a rubber plant nobody waters. I don't want the usual TV-drama version where she cries about one sympathetic case and waves everyone else through. I want the reader to actually watch her *think*, case by case, so the drama comes out of the machinery of the decision rather than around it. For that I need you to write me the decision procedure she runs on each claim: the questions in the order she asks them, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in a "no" she can defend on paper, and -- this is the important part for the book -- where it stops and hands the file back to her conscience, because the question left is one of values rather than facts.

Ground it in the real underlying ideas, not the pop version: what makes someone a refugee at all (persecution for a protected reason, a well-founded fear, being outside your country), the way "well-founded fear" is weirdly half-subjective-half-objective, the internal flight alternative (can you reasonably relocate within your own country?), sur place claims where fear arises *after* leaving, and how regional definitions like the Cartagena one stretch refugeehood to generalized violence and collapse-of-the-state situations while the classic Convention definition doesn't, which is also why climate-displaced people fall through every crack at once. I need all that in there because my character argues with a colleague about exactly these fault lines and I want to get it right.

Then run the procedure on three fictional claims I can turn into scenes: (1) a woman whose ex-husband is a police officer back home and has told everyone he'll kill her if she returns; (2) a man from a city where a gang extorts literally everyone, who admits, sort of cheerfully, that no one is after him specifically; (3) a farmer from a delta region that floods a bit worse every single year. At least one of these should come out the way my character *hates* but can't honestly wriggle out of -- that's the scene I'm really writing toward. And be honest about where the procedure misfires or gets gamed, plus where it assumes information she won't have: she clears dozens of files a month with spotty country-of-origin info, so it has to be runnable in an afternoon by one skeptical person, not a commitee of philosphers.
