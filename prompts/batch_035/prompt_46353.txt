My oldest friend and I spent the better part of a six-hour train ride arguing about precommitment — Ulysses contracts, advance directives, constitutional entrenchment, the lot — and we ended, as we always do, with her handing down verdicts on a series of hypotheticals while I took notes and objected. I have since come to suspect that no single principle generates her verdicts, or that if one does, neither of us has managed to state it. I would like your help predicting what she will say about one further case, and, more importantly, understanding what rules could be doing the work. Her verdicts so far, as faithfully as I can reconstruct them:

1. Ulysses instructing his crew to keep him tied to the mast regardless of what he screams as they pass the Sirens: not merely permissible but wise; the crew would be right to ignore his mid-voyage pleas.
2. A man with alcohol dependence who, sober, arranges for his wife to hold and administer his disulfiram so that drinking will make him violently ill, and who later, craving, begs her for the tablets: she should refuse him.
3. A woman who signs up for a casino's lifetime self-exclusion list and then, standing at the door with the last of her savings, phones to revoke it: the casino is entitled — indeed obliged — to refuse the revocation.
4. A patient with bipolar disorder who, while euthymic, executes a psychiatric advance directive authorizing clinicians to override her treatment refusals during manic episodes: it should be honored, however strenuously the manic patient objects.
5. A woman who at twenty-four, in the first flush of a religious conversion, signs an irrevocable deed pledging a fifth of her lifetime income to her church, and who at thirty-four, long deconverted, sues to escape it: the pledge does not bind — no one can bind their future self's settled values, whatever the paperwork says.
6. A constitution that entrenches basic rights against ordinary majorities: legitimate, indeed the point of having one.
7. That same constitutional machinery used to entrench, beyond the reach of amendment, a specific economic policy — say, a permanent prohibition of any income tax: illegitimate, and not merely because the content is unwise; there is something wrong in kind about binding successors to a policy.

Two things her position clearly is not. It is not "past competent consent is sovereign," since she judges the pledge in 5 to have been competently executed and still discharges it. And it is not "the current self always wins," since she overrides the manic self in 4 and the craving selves in 1 through 3. She gestures at analogies between persons and polities, at the difference between binding your appetites and binding your successors, and at the question of which self is really speaking — but she has never had to state a rule.

The new case is this. A woman is diagnosed with early Alzheimer's at sixty-one. Two weeks after the diagnosis — grieving and frightened, but by every account with decision-making capacity — she executes an advance directive providing that once she can no longer recognize her family, no antibiotics are to be given for any infection and only comfort care is to be provided. She then lives six contented years, enjoying music and her grandchildren, who by the end are friendly strangers to her. She is now at the specified stage: she recognizes no one, but she is cheerful, eats well, and shows no distress about her condition. She develops pneumonia. The directive says to withhold antibiotics.

Please predict her verdict: honor the directive or override it? I care less about the bare answer than about the analysis. Identify at least two rules that fit her verdicts on the personal cases (1 through 5) and can be extended, with whatever strain you find honest to acknowledge, to the constitutional pair — I can myself see something like a rule keyed to whether the bound self's dissent is impaired versus competent, a rule keyed to whether the commitment expressed a transient phase or the settled person, and a constitutional-style rule distinguishing identity-protecting from policy-fixing commitments, but construct your own if mine are wrong. Say what each rule predicts about the pneumonia case, give your best guess together with your reasons for preferring it, identify which of her seven earlier cases is the closest analog and why, and describe one further hypothetical case — the sharper the better — on which the candidate rules give different answers, so that I can put it to her and find out whether she holds a position at all.

Conclude with a final line of the form \answer{HONOR} or \answer{OVERRIDE} if you are willing to commit to a prediction, or \answer{INDETERMINATE} followed by one sentence of justification if you think her track record genuinely underdetermines her verdict.
