I sit on a five-person conduct panel at a small college that hears cases of student misconduct toward staff. Last month's case involved a student accused of a pattern of demeaning remarks to a teaching assistant. I missed the live hearing because of jury duty, so my only materials were the transcript and the written exhibits, plus a summary of the panel's in-person impressions. Reading the transcript, the student's answers seem defensive but plausibly sincere; the apology reads as awkward rather than rehearsed. The two panel members who attended tell a different story: they describe his tone as smirking and contemptuous, his apology as visibly performed, and they say that anyone in the room would have concluded the conduct was knowingly cruel rather than careless. They're not guessing; they were there, and they're people whose judgment on character I've trusted for years. I have to submit a written finding by Friday, and as it stands I'd land on the milder characterization while they would land on the harsher one.

A senior colleague on the panel has been pressing me to resolve this the simple way: you weren't there, they were; taking a witness's word for what happened is exactly what panels are for, and a transcript is a poor substitute for a room. That sounds right when I think of it as a factual report — of course I should believe them about what they saw. But it feels different when what I'd be taking their word for is the verdict itself: that the behavior was cruel on purpose, which is the finding that determines the sanction. If I simply adopt their conclusion, my name goes on a judgment I didn't make, and I can't shake the thought that if this were acceptable, the college could save money by holding hearings before a single member and letting the rest of us sign whatever that member felt. On the other hand, if I vote on the transcript alone, I'm in effect claiming that the pages I read contain everything relevant, when I know perfectly well they don't — tone, timing, and how a remark landed on its recipient largely died with the room.

So my questions, before Friday: Should I vote my own reading of the transcript, defer to the members who were present, or is there a better third option — and if so, what does it look like in practice? More generally, what distinguishes the cases where it's perfectly fine to let someone else's word settle how things stand, even when what's settled is partly an evaluation, from the cases where doing that seems to short-circuit something I'm supposed to be doing myself? I'd especially like help on where that line falls and why, since my instinct says the line exists but I can't articulate the principle behind it.
