Context: I'm a PhD student writing a dissertation chapter defending the view that epistemic akrasia is always irrational — roughly, never believe p while also believing that your belief that p isn't properly supported by your evidence. The paradigm cases are things like the student who's sure he aced the exam but trusts the bad grade when it comes back: he believes he aced it, and believes his belief that he aced it isn't backed up, and everyone I cite says that combination is irrational. I thought the chapter was basically done. Then at a workshop a classmate handed me this argument, which she says shows my view collapses into global skepticism:

(1) Rationality requires responding to your total evidence.
(2) Total evidence includes higher-order evidence — evidence about whether the reasoning that produced a particular belief worked properly in that instance.
(3) I know, as background, that first-order reasoning is frequently corrupted: confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, being worse at this when I haven't slept, whatever.
(4) So whenever I arrive at a belief by reasoning, I can ask "did I just botch this one?" and (3) gives me some reason to answer yes.
(5) Therefore my higher-order evidence never fully supports the claim that I reasoned well to any particular conclusion; there's always residual doubt.
(6) Holding onto p when your higher-order evidence doesn't support that you formed the belief in p properly just is the akrasia I want to prohibit.
(7) Akrasia is irrational.
(8) So I'm irrational in every belief I reasoned my way to — including, awkwardly, the belief in (7) itself, which I reasoned my way to.

The conclusion is obviously too strong — the only rational agents left would be ones that never reason at all. But here's my problem: every premise looked fine to me on first read. (3) is just psychology, (1) and (7) are the bedrock of my whole chapter, and I'd rather keep (6) too since it's basically my definition. So it has to be (4) or (5), but when I stare at those they seem ok as well?? (4) only says I can ask a question and have some reason to answer it one way, which sounds pretty harmless. I genuinely can't tell wether one of these premises is secretly doing two jobs or whether the slippage is somewhere else in the chain. Where does this go wrong, and what am I paying to say so? Concretely: if I reject whichever step you pick, can I still claim akrasia is always irrational, or does the fix quietly carve cases out of my prohibition? I want to know before I send the chapter to my advisor.
