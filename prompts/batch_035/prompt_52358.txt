I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a longform magazine, and I'm at work on a feature about whether it is literally true — or only metaphorically true — that DNA is a code. I've drafted the spine of the piece below. It is a draft, not a finished essay, and I'd value careful comments from someone who knows both the biology and the philosophy of biology.

Here is the draft:

Title: The Code That Codes Itself

Popular science writing cannot leave DNA alone: it is the instruction manual for life, the book of the recipe, the software that runs the hardware of the cell, and — ever since CRISPR entered headlines — something we can now edit, as though the genome were a manuscript in tracked changes. My essay asks a question that sounds pedantic but turns out not to be: which parts of this are literally true, which parts are metaphor trading on our familiarity with the literal parts, and does the difference matter?

The essay runs on a three-way distinction that I want someone to stress-test. Call the three senses cipher, recipe, and blueprint.

By cipher I mean what molecular biologists literally call the genetic code: the mapping from sixty-four codons to twenty amino acids (plus stop signals), a mapping that is redundant, nearly universal across life, and implemented physically by tRNA molecules and the enzymes that charge them. On this narrow point, I want to argue, the word code is not a metaphor at all. It is a technical term for a real, discrete, systematic mapping. The interesting wrinkle is that the mapping looks arbitrary in the way linguistic signs are supposed to be arbitrary — nothing about the chemistry of a codon itself dictates which amino acid it corresponds to; the correspondence is enforced by an intermediary machinery that could, in principle, have been otherwise. Crick's frozen accident hypothesis is the classic statement of this contingency: the assignment is a historical accident that got locked in early and inherited. But I've read that some chemists think the assignments are not entirely arbitrary, that stereochemical affinities between codons or anticodons and amino acids biased the early code. I gather this is contested. If the assignments turn out to be chemically biased rather than accidental, my arbitrariness argument weakens, and I want to know how much.

By recipe I mean the expansive claim that DNA is a program or set of instructions for building an organism. Here the essay leans on a point I find genuinely underappreciated in popular treatment: a recipe presupposes a cook. The genome doesn't build anything by itself; it is read by a transcriptional and developmental machinery that supplies most of the causal work — maternal RNAs, chromatin structure, the physical geometry of the embryo. I want to use an analogy from music: DNA is less like a fully notated score and more like a lead sheet in jazz — a compressed, partial specification that a competent band could realize in endlessly many ways. The development of the organism, like the rhythm section, fills in what the sheet leaves open. On this reading, DNA carries causal specificity — small changes to it make a specific, difference-making difference to the outcome — but causal specificity is not yet content, and it is not obvious that instruction talk survives without a reader who is not itself instructed by the same sheet.

By blueprint I mean the claim that DNA is a picture or map of the organism, one-to-one with its structure. This, I take it, is simply false, and usefully so: there is no gene for the shape of your nose in the way there is a line on a blueprint for a wall. Genes specify proteins and regulatory RNAs, not anatomical parts. The falsity of the blueprint picture is old news among biologists but alive and well in headlines, and I want to say why it matters: it quietly underwrites genetic determinism, the folk sense that the genome pins down the phenotype the way a floor plan pins down a house.

The ending I'm currently drafting claims that the code metaphor is literal in the cipher sense, defensible but incomplete in the recipe sense, and false in the blueprint sense — and that the public argument about genetics is mostly an argument in which people talk past each other across these three senses, all using the same four letters.

What I'd like from you, in roughly this order:

1. Accuracy. Where would a molecular biologist or a philosopher of biology wince? In particular: is my treatment of the arbitrariness of the genetic code fair to the stereochemical-assignment literature, and am I right that causal specificity is the strongest defensible version of the information claim?
2. The load-bearing distinction. Does the cipher/recipe/blueprint trichotomy actually do argumentative work, or does it just relabel familiar points? Is there a better cut of the conceptual space, and does one of my three senses collapse into another?
3. The analogy. Is the jazz lead sheet fair, and where does it limp?
4. One genuinely interesting thing the draft is missing that a feature of this kind ought to contain.

And a final request about the character of your comments: please tell me how confident you are in each of your substantive claims — I'd rather know that something is your best inference from general principles than mistake it for settled expert consensus — and explicitly single out the one point in my draft (or in your own comments) that you are least sure of, so I know where to take extra care in reporting it out.
