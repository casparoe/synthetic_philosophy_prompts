I'm taking a philosophy of physics elective, and our unit on the Everett interpretation recently covered the quantum suicide thought experiment, usually attributed to Max Tegmark. In the version we discussed, a gun is wired to a radioactive decay detector: on each trigger pull, if the atom decays within the allotted window the gun fires, and if it doesn't decay the gun clicks harmlessly. An Everettian accepts that after the trigger is pulled, the world contains both an observer-branch in which the gun fired and one in which it merely clicked. The experimenter, who can only ever find herself in branches where she remains conscious, will therefore never observe her own death, no matter how many rounds she plays.

Our instructor mentioned that some people take this to suggest a kind of subjective immortality, and I've been chewing on what that implies for practical decision-making ever since. If I grant the Everettian picture, then there is always some future branch containing someone psychologically continuous with me, so my credence that "this is the last moment of my experience" seems to collapse toward zero. That makes risk aversion about my own survival look irrational: wearing a seatbelt, treating a serious illness, avoiding a dangerous hobby — none of these can prevent the existence of surviving successors; at most they fatten certain branches. Doesn't that mean a consistent many-worldser should treat all gambles with death as strictly costless to herself, and that things like life insurance and palliative planning are conceptually confused for anyone who really believes in branching? My roommate disagrees and insists I'm missing something, but her objection was just that most of the surviving branches are "thin," which struck me as irrelevant since each of them, however thin, still contains a first-person perspective looking out.

There was a second point from our seminar that I want to fold in. A classmate argued the whole setup turns on the trigger being genuinely quantum — that if the gun were driven by a deterministic pseudo-random number generator instead of a decaying atom, the experiment would no longer work, and presumably the immortality inference would go with it. I'm not sure I see why the source of randomness should matter for whether I always find myself surviving. So, putting it together: on the Everett interpretation, am I actually licensed to be indifferent about my own mortality, or not? And where exactly, if anywhere, does the risk-neutrality argument break down — at the branching structure itself, at the "thinness" of the surviving branches, or somewhere else entirely?
