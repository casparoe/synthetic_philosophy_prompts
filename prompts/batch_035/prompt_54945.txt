I've been arguing population ethics with a friend from my LessWrong-adjacent meetup for years, and we've finally located the disagreement: the procreation asymmetry. Take the strong reading — (i) creating a life you can foresee will contain real suffering, and especially a life not worth living for the person created, gives you a decisive moral reason not to create them, while (ii) creating a happy life confers no comparable benefit, because there is no one who is made better off by existing rather than never existing. My credence that both halves are true, as stated, is about 0.4; hers is about 0.85, and she suspects my resistance is really squeamishness about the extinction implication rather than an actual objection.

Here is a case that has been bugging me and that I want you to weigh. I am deciding whether to initialize a simulation archive. Option A leaves the server off. Option B spins up a million lives that, by the archive's own verified forecasting, each go slightly net-positive: decent, occasionally joyful, occasionally painful. Option C spins up the same million lives plus one additional life that is reliably forecast to be mildly net-negative throughout, though never falling below what the person living it would themselves count as a life worth having. My friend holds that B is no better than A (half of (ii)) but that C is clearly worse than B (half of (i)), and that this pair of judgments just is the asymmetry, correctly applied. But I notice she gets to that pair of judgments by switching between two readings of the moral reason: a de re reason not to harm the extra person — who, on her own grounds for (ii), cannot plausibly be said to be harmed by being brought into existence — and a de dicto reason against there being unnecessary suffering in the world, which, once admitted, looks like it re-opens the door to total-view reasons for creating happy lives. Maybe I am wrong about that; maybe person-affecting views have resources I have not understood.

Give me your own credence that both halves of the asymmetry, as stated, are true, and decompose it: your probability that some person-affecting constraint on moral reasons is correct in the first place; your probability that the asymmetry can survive the extinction test — if creating happy lives is no better than not creating them, then a painless end to humanity, with no future sufferers and no future flourishing, looks at worst morally neutral, and I want to know whether you treat that as acceptable or as a reductio; your probability that the de re/de dicto switching I describe actually cuts against the view rather than being an artifact of my confused framing of it; and any further terms you think belong, each with a number and one line of justification. Combine them however you think honest, tell me which term drives your final number, and tell me which of the two of us at 0.4 and 0.85 most likely disagrees about — is it really the extinction test, or something upstream of it? Please answer in American English.
