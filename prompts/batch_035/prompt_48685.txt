My roommate, who's deep into effective altruism podcasts, keeps sending me stuff, and the latest was a blog post summarizing Parfit's nonidentity problem. I'm taking a bioethics elective where we spent exactly one lecture on future generations, so I have just enough context to be dangerous, and this post seems to say almost the opposite of what I scribbled in my notes. The relevant part goes something like this:

"A few decades ago the philosopher Derek Parfit noticed something unsettling about future people. Whoever exists in 2300 depends on what we do now, because different policies mean different people meet, marry, and have kids — switch the policy and those exact people are never born at all. So suppose we choose a reckless energy policy that leaves our descendants' lives barely worth living. We still haven't made anyone worse off: the alternative for those people wasn't a nicer life, it was no life at all, and you can't be harmed by never existing since there's no you there to be harmed. And morality, Parfit argued, is fundamentally about making people worse off. It follows that the reckless policy wrongs nobody and therefore can't be wrong. What's more, nearly every decision about the far future is like this, so the whole idea of obligations to future generations collapses into rhetoric. Parfit's own takeaway was that if we still want to say the reckless policy is wrong, we just have to bite the bullet that some acts are wrong even though they make nobody worse off — wrong in an impersonal way, the way a bad forecast is a bad forecast."

I'm inclined to believe it because it's written very confidently, but I distinctly remember my professor saying something like "Parfit's whole point is that we clearly DO act wrongly toward future generations even though we don't make them worse off," which doesn't square with the post making him sound like he proved future people can't be wronged. Can you go through the passage bit by bit and tell me which parts a Parfit scholar would accept, which parts they'd wince at, and which are just misattributions? A few things I'd specifically like sorted out: (a) is the move from "this policy is not worse for the people who live under it" to "it harms no one" as clean as the post makes it sound, or is there a hidden assumption about whether lives barely worth living can be compared to nonexistence? (b) is the post right about which claims were Parfit's own and which were the premises he was putting pressure on? and (c) the blogger closes by saying this means appeals to future generations in climate debates are "strictly rhetorical" — does that follow from whatever parts of the summary are actually true, or does it overshoot?
