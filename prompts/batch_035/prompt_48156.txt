My friends Maya and Daniel, who share a political-economy reading group with me, spent our last meeting arguing about a scheme Daniel had designed for a class paper: replace the legislature's annual setting of the immigration cap with prediction markets. Markets would trade conditional on different cap levels, betting on a published dashboard of indicators — GDP per capita, native-born wage percentiles, fiscal balance, and a composite social-trust index — and the cap would float to whatever level the market predicted would maximize the dashboard a decade out. Citizens would vote each year on the dashboard's weights, which is meant to keep the values democratic while the market handles the facts.

The exchange went like this. (1) Daniel argued that cap-setting is exactly what futarchy is for: legislators have weak incentives to know what different cap levels actually do to wages, fiscal flows, and housing markets, while dispersed bettors — employers in meatpacking towns, landlords in gateway cities, caseworkers in refugee camps — each hold a piece of the truth, and putting money on it forces honesty. (2) Maya objected that the dashboard smuggles values into what looks like a factual instrument: the social-trust index is contested, the choice of wage percentiles embeds a judgment about whose wages matter, and market efficiency can't launder those choices into objectivity. (3) Daniel replied that this is why the weights are up for a vote: the whole futarchy idea is to vote on values and bet on beliefs, so values get decided democratically and beliefs get priced. (4) Maya said the separation fails twice over: voters choosing weights won't grasp the causal stakes behind the indicators, so the values vote is uninformed in exactly the way futarchy complains about; and asking the market to maximize the dashboard invites Goodhart — the market optimizes the measured numbers, perhaps by shaping them, rather than the welfare they were chosen to proxy. (5) Daniel countered that legislators Goodhart worse, since nothing is on the line when a congressman misstates wage effects, whereas a manipulator in a prediction market bleeds capital to honest counterparties. (6) Maya closed with the whales worry: the bettors aren't a crowd but whoever has capital, and a rich restrictionist — or a rich employer wanting cheap labor — can afford to lose money moving the cap in his favored direction, so the verdict reflects the will of the richest, not the wisdom of crowds.

They roped me in as referee and I honestly couldn't call it. Please adjudicate the six moves one by one — who is right about each specific point, and where either of them is arguing against a weaker version of what the other said. Then say what both of them are missing, whether the scheme is patchable (for instance by capped position sizes, or a differently structured dashboard), or whether it fails at one of the joints Maya identifies. Finally, tell me who is more right overall.
