Write me a dialogue between two characters disagreeing about Chesterton's fence. The setup: my cousin just took over as operations manager at an old family cannery, and there's a tradition nobody can explain -- one sealed can from every batch goes into a basement storeroom and sits there for ten years before being thrown out. My cousin wants to end it, since it's just dead weight as far as anyone can tell. His uncle, who's read a lot of Burke, keeps falling back on Chesterton's fence: don't remove a fence until you know why it was put up. Please give them maybe six or seven rounds of real back-and-forth where they respond to each others actual points, not past each other. The uncle shouldn't be a mushy traditionalist -- give him the serious version of his view, something like epistemic humility about institutions whose functions aren't obvious, and the asymmetry between reversible and irreversible mistakes. But the cousin should push back hard too: if nobody remembers the reason, how much digging is enough before he's entitled to act, and can the uncle's position ever actually be falsified, or does it just block every reform forever while pretending to be a decision procedure. Also let the cousin raise the possibility that the fence's reason has expired rather than been lost -- maybe retained cans mattered for some old liability issue that no longer applies -- and have the uncle give his best answer to that, since "the reason expired" is a different case than "there never was one." End it without a winner but with both positions sharpened, ideally with each character granting the other something real.

Format-wise, just speaker names followed by plain paragraphs, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and no stage directions. American English please.
