In the software systems I work on, ties are everywhere, and we engineers have an unexamined doctrine for handling them. When two servers report identical load, the load balancer picks one essentially at random; when two transactions contend on the same row, the database breaks the deadlock with a coin flip; leader election in a distributed cluster is often literally a random draw. Yet elsewhere the industry insists on the opposite: sorting must be deterministic and stable, consensus protocols require every node to break ties the same way, and nondeterministic output gets filed as a bug. I recently read about Buridan's ass — the donkey that starves between two identical haystacks because it has no reason to prefer either — and it struck me that our field has implicitly legislated on that donkey's fate, sometimes saying randomness saves it, sometimes a fixed arbitrary rule, and sometimes that the tie itself is a defect to be engineered away.

I have no philosophy background, so please reconstruct things carefully for me. What theory of choice or rational action do these practices jointly presuppose? Does flipping a coin under an exact tie presuppose that choosing arbitrarily can be rational, and does it sit comfortably with the fact that our random choice is usually a pseudorandom number with a seed, so that something determined it all along — in which case what exactly did the coin add? Does the two-halves practice — randomize here, determinize there — rest on a single coherent view of what makes a choice acceptable, or do the halves quietly conflict? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, tell me whether several theories of choice would license it equally, and say what would have to change in engineering practice if we adopted the view that an agent with exactly equal reasons cannot rationally choose at all. Please answer in British English.
