I spent my career as an immunologist, and from the bench, self-deception looked like a plain empirical fact: the postdoc who wants a result to be true reads an ambiguous gel one way, the one who fears it reads the same bands the other way, and neither would describe themselves as believing anything untoward. Since retiring I have been reading the philosophy, only to find that philosophers have debated for decades whether self-deception is even possible, and appear thoroughly stuck. The objections are two paradoxes: the static one, that to deceive yourself you would have to both believe and not believe the same proposition at the same time, and the strategic one, that forming an intention to make yourself believe something you know to be false seems to defeat itself, since acting on the intention requires the very knowledge it aims to bury. Sartre answers with bad faith and a consciousness that is not what it knows; Davidson answers by partitioning the mind into semi-independent departments; Mele and the deflationists answer that what we call self-deception is just motivationally biased handling of evidence, involving no intention to deceive and hence no paradox at all. Every side knows the others' moves, and as far as I can tell nobody has moved in thirty years.

Diagnose the stall for me rather than adjudicating it. My working hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than flatter, is that much of the dispute is verbal: a fight over whether the word deception properly requires an intention, and therefore over whether the mundane bias I watched in the lab deserves the name at all — with a genuine residue underneath about whether the mind has parts or levels in anything more than a metaphorical sense. Is that right? Or is there a hidden substantive question doing the work — perhaps about the unity of agency, or about the conditions under which a belief is held rather than merely arrived at? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a refined taxonomy, psychological evidence, a conceptual distinction, or an argument that the classical puzzle was ill-posed from the start and the deflationists have already won everything worth winning.
