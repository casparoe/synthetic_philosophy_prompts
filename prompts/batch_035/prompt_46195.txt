I'm preparing a position statement for a graduate seminar on historiography — the theme this term is whether history is written by the victors — and before I circulate it, I want a rigorous consistency audit. Please take on the role of a philosopher of history and work through my commitments the way an auditor would: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally damning. For each genuine tension, tell me what would have to give and what that revision costs; at the end, tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Don't try to guess which commitment I'm privately attached to, and don't take the items one at a time — I want an analysis of the set as a package, not seven separate commentaries.

Here is the set. (1) Every historical narrative is written from somewhere: there is no standpoint-free history, and accounts that claim neutrality tend to launder the perspective of whoever was powerful when the discipline's conventions were settled. (2) The maxim that history is written by the victors is, taken literally as a claim about authorship, mostly false: the defeated write history constantly — Lost Cause memoirists, colonized intellectuals writing against empire, dissident memoirs produced under authoritarian rule — and several victors' own national historiographies are savagely self-critical. (3) Even so, power structures what can be known: what was recorded, which archives survived, whose acts left a trace, which episodes got a name and a date — that distribution tracks power, so the victors can shape history without ever writing it. (4) About the past itself I am a realist: some historical claims are simply true and their deniers simply wrong — Holocaust denial is not an alternative perspective but an error. (5) Selection, however, is ineliminably evaluative: there is no fact of the matter about what a narrative ought to include or foreground; every choice of emphasis reflects values, and none is uniquely correct. (6) Where a survivor's memory and a state archive conflict on a detail, the archive should usually win on the detail; yet testimony from the vanquished has an authority no archive can have when it comes to what mattered. (7) In practice I hold that a national curriculum which foregrounds a country's atrocities over its achievements is more adequate as history, full stop — not merely more congenial to my politics — and that the triumphalist version is getting the selection wrong, not just choosing differently.

Do these hang together? Start with the bottom line in your first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question, and no general lecture on relativism that I haven't asked for — the deliverable is the audit itself.
