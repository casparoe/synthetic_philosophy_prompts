At a workshop on giving feedback in our research group, the facilitator kept repeating the line that guilt is about what you did, shame is about who you are, and used it to argue that our postdoc mentoring should aim to make people feel guilt ("that result was misreported, fix it") rather than shame ("you are the sort of person who misreports results"), since guilt points at repair and shame points at the exit door. A week later, in an online argument about whether institutions can be designed to shift a community's moral emotions, I saw the same line used in almost the opposite direction: someone invoked the old anthropological contrast between shame cultures and guilt cultures to claim that guilt is simply shame with the audience moved inside your head, so that a culture cannot actually choose to swap one for the other — the most it can do is hide the witness. Both speakers treated the line as though it obviously settled the matter, and I realized I couldn't say what it actually claims.

I'd like the different precise theses the one-liner could be expressing, laid out as a numbered menu I could take back to either of them. For each reading, please say whether it is a trivial definitional stipulation, a substantive but plausible empirical claim, or something false or unsupported, and then say which of the two uses it actually supports: the facilitator's use (shrink shame, target guilt at specific acts) or the online critic's use (guilt is internalized shame, so the choice between them is illusory). At least these axes seem worth separating, though tell me if there are others: whether the line is about the object of evaluation (an act versus the whole self), about motivation (apology and repair versus concealment and withdrawal), about the audience (whether shame requires a real or imagined witness in a way guilt does not), about fittingness (whether condemning a whole self for one act is ever a proportionate response), and about origins (whether guilt is produced by internalizing external judgment).

Close by addressing the question the two of them were really fighting about: can a culture, or an institution inside one, deliberately shift its members from shame to guilt? In particular, if guilt really is shame whose audience has been installed in the person, then the facilitator's project of eliminating shame while cultivating guilt looks like one project described in two moods — the same internalized witness doing the condemning, just told to speak more quietly and about smaller things. Say whether any consistent selection of your readings makes the two projects distinct, and if so, which reading does that work. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of my question.
