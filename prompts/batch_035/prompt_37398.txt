In two weeks I present the penultimate chapter of my dissertation at a departmental colloquium, and I want a preparation sheet for the question period rather than more help with the argument itself. The chapter's thesis is that the liberal guarantee of exit rights from thick communities is parasitic on those communities rather than a corrective to them. The capacity to leave a way of life meaningfully — to exit the strict church, the linguistic minority, the tightly bound immigrant congregation — presupposes the evaluative formation that only unchosen membership supplies, and the exit is an exit somewhere only because other thick communities exist to absorb the leaver. So the individually exercisable right of exit cannot play the foundational role in the justification of toleration that liberals often assign it; at most it is a good that certain communal structures happen to make available, and a liberal state that cares about real rather than merely formal exit would have to entrench the very communities it also wants to hold in check. My argument has three steps: first, that meaningful choice requires a horizon of live evaluative options that individuals do not generate for themselves; second, that exit is a species of meaningful choice and so inherits this dependence; third, that the state therefore faces not the practical tradeoff liberals usually concede (making exit easy can be costly) but a genuine tension inside the ideal: arrangements that make exit maximally easy tend to make it less meaningful, and arrangements that keep exit meaningful tend to make it harder.

The audience matters, so let me describe them. The department is analytic political philosophy, predominantly Rawlsian, with a Kantian moral philosopher or two, two libertarians — one of whom regards Kukathas's exit-based liberalism in The Liberal Archipelago as close to settled truth — a sociologist of religion whose fieldwork is on immigrant congregations, and one senior colleague trained by a student of Sandel's who has been waiting years for someone to walk into this debate. Nobody works on communitarianism directly. The Rawlsians will want to know whether I've conflated a causal claim about how citizens come to hold conceptions of the good with a normative claim about what the state may do; the Kukathas libertarian will say that formal exit was all anyone ever promised and that its thinness is a feature; the sociologist will ask what my "thick community" corresponds to on the ground; and someone will run Dworkin's old move about whether the liberal political community can itself be the constitutive one that does the forming.

Write the prep sheet with the following sections, in this order. "The room" (~150 words): what each faction wants from my talk and what would count, for them, as a good question period. "Objections, ranked by how much each could hurt the chapter" (the core, roughly 150 words per objection): the six or seven objections this audience will actually raise, in the vocabulary of the person most likely to raise them, each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is on its weakest footing. "What to volunteer" (~100 words): the concession I should offer unprompted to buy credibility, and why it is safe to concede. "The question I hope no one thinks to ask" (~150 words): the one that would do the most damage, with the best answer available to me on the day. "Where this sheet fails" (~150 words): an assessment of which of my replies above rests on the most fragile step of the argument, and — since I would rather know this now than at a job talk later — what would have to be true, or what would have to be shown to me, for me to conclude that the chapter's thesis is wrong and should be abandoned rather than patched.
