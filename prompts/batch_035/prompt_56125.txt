I've been reading the decision-theory threads on rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade now, and I'd long thought of myself as broadly sympathetic to updateless decision theory: it seemed obvious to me that an agent who can foresee that its later, updated self will refuse to pay in the counterfactual mugging is foreseeing its own predictable loss, and that a rational design would avoid that. But a long argument I recently witnessed between two people whose judgment I respect has left me unsure, and I'd like a careful referee.

To set up their exchange: both parties accept the standard motivation. In the counterfactual mugging, Omega flips a fair coin before you exist. If it lands heads, Omega gives you $10,000 and you never hear anything more. If it lands tails, Omega appears and asks you for $100. An updating expected-utility maximizer who has seen the tails outcome refuses to pay. An updateless agent, which chooses a policy before conditioning on observations, pays -- ex ante the paying policy nets about $4,950, and the agent's refusal to pay was predictable and, by its own earlier lights, a mistake. Fine so far. The argument was about how much follows from this.

The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Dev, the updatelessness defender, said the counterfactual mugging generalizes: any agent whose decisions depend on which branch of a prediction or a random process it finds itself in is reflectively inconsistent, in the sense that its earlier self would pay to redesign its later self. Updatelessness isn't a quirky fix for a puzzle case; it's what consistency requires, the same way avoiding dominated strategies is required. An agent that updates and then decides is one that can be predicted to leave value on the table, and predictably leaving value on the table is as close to a formal definition of irrationality as we have.

(2) Sasha replied that reflective consistency is cheap: a rock is perfectly reflectively consistent, and so is an agent with a fixed, stupid policy. If consistency plus ex ante optimality relative to some prior is all the theory offers, then all the real work is being done by the prior -- which prior, held at which point in the agent's history, policies are averaged over. On that question, she said, updateless decision theory is a schema with a blank in it, not a theory. Calling the filled-in schema "the right decision theory" just renames the hard problem of choosing the blank.

(3) Dev answered that the blank is not arbitrary: the natural stopping point is the agent's epistemic position before it learned anything that it could have precommitted about. You take the policy that's optimal by the lights of the agent's earliest reasonable epistemic state, and that's a principled answer, not a dodge.

(4) Sasha said this is where the view runs off the rails, because there is no such stopping point once you take logical uncertainty seriously. Her example: run the mugging again, but replace the coin with a digit of pi that Omega consults -- say, whether the trillionth digit is even -- and suppose the agent, before facing Omega, has already computed that it's odd. The "updateless" agent is now supposed to average its welfare over mathematical possibilities it has already computed away, which means acting as though it doesn't know a mathematical fact it plainly knows. And the known evasions are worse: designing the agent so it can't know what it will decide, or having it refuse to compute certain things, either makes the theory unimplementable for agents that must model their own decision or turns updatelessness into a crude and possibly self-defeating regime of self-imposed ignorance. Her conclusion: the observation-based version of updatelessness is fine but shallow, and the logical version -- the one that was supposed to matter for agents in a deterministic universe -- is either incoherent or insane.

(5) Dev's final move was to bite the bullet, or most of it: yes, a well-designed agent will sometimes be built to not know things, or to act on policies chosen before it knows them, and that's not a bug. We accept structurally similar trade-offs whenever we bind ourselves -- a constitution, a promise, a prearranged inherit -- sorry, a prearranged inheritance arrangement is not self-defeating because it constrains a later self who knows more. The regress Sasha complains about is real but bounded: it bottoms out in the agent's design stage, where the averaging-over-uncertainty happens once, over everything the designer can't cheaply determine in advance.

They were talking past each other by the end, and I genuinely couldn't tell. Please adjudicate the five moves in order: for each, say whether the speaker is right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and why. Then, separately, tell me what you make of the two live issues underneath the exchange: whether Sasha's schema-with-a-blank objection really undermines updatelessness or just relocates the hard problem to prior choice (which may be where it was all along), and whether Dev's bullet on self-ignorance about logical facts is worth biting or is the reductio it looks like. And if both of them are missing something -- some consideration about, say, commitment races, or exploitability of updateless agents by adversaries who know their code -- I'd want to hear that too. Finally, given all this, would you tell someone like me, who wanted a settled view, that updatelessness is basically right with a loose end about priors, or basically a promissory note whose main content is still owed?
