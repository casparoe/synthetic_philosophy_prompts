I'm grading an exam for a course on 20th c. political philosophy and want a second pair of eyes on one answer. The question was:

Simone Weil's Note sur la suppression générale des partis politiques (On the Abolition of All Political Parties) was written in London in 1943, while she was working with the Free French. Explain her central argument for abolishing political parties, covering (a) what she thinks parties do to the minds of their members, (b) her view of what collectivities are good at versus what only individuals can do, and (c) whether her argument targets all collective organizations or only some.

The student wrote:

Weil's argument against parties is essentially the same one Madison makes against faction in Federalist No. 10: parties are groups that pursue their narrow interest at the expense of the public good, and both thinkers see them as a threat to political health. For Weil, parties are machines for generating collective passion and exerting pressure on each member's mind, so joining one amounts to giving up your independent judgment on anything the party has already taken a stance on. Her deeper point is about thought itself: only individuals can think. A collectivity is an effective instrument for action — it has more strength than a lone person — but it cannot think, and the party's fatal feature is precisely that it claims to do collective thinking on its members' behalf. That's why her target is specifically the party and not, say, a trade union: a union bargains collectively but doesn't claim to do your believing for you, so the argument doesn't extend to groups organized for action. At bottom, though, the abolitionism is theological — Weil's Christian conviction that only individual persons have souls, while collectivities don't, is what drives the whole case; a party pretending to think is a metaphysical counterfeit. She wrote this in the mid-1930s, out of her break with the French left after her factory year, and she took the party spirit she saw there to be the seed of totalitarianism — pointing to the Jacobin clubs as showing that continental parties had totalitarianism as their original sin.
