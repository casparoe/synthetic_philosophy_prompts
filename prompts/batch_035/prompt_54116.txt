Analyze the World Health Organization's smallpox eradication campaign, culminating in the 1980 declaration of eradication, as a case study in negative utilitarianism and suffering-focused ethics — the family of views on which reducing suffering takes priority over producing happiness, so that the permanent removal of a source of suffering can matter more than any quantity of added well-being.

The salient facts, so the mapping is not in dispute: the intensified campaign ran from 1967 to the late 1970s; the last natural case occurred in Somalia in 1977; in 1978 a medical photographer, Janet Parker, died in Birmingham after exposure to smallpox virus being handled in a university research laboratory, becoming the last recorded smallpox death — and this happened after the disease had been eliminated in nature; the surviving stocks were then consolidated into two authorized repositories in the United States and Russia; and the WHO has repeatedly deferred the destruction of those stocks, even after its own commissioned review concluded that no essential public health purpose is served by keeping them.

Work the mapping in both directions. First, why this looks like negative utilitarianism's paradigm case: the campaign's aim was never to add well-being but to remove a source of suffering once and for all, which makes it one of the few places where suffering-focused ethics was realized as an actual program rather than a thought experiment. Then say where the theory bites. Consider at least: (1) the allocation question posed by the final years, when the cost of preventing each remaining case rose steeply while the same resources spent elsewhere could have averted more suffering in the short term — does the asymmetric weighting of removing versus creating value support finishing the job where a straight aggregation of outcomes might not? (2) The question of whose suffering counts — the virus itself, presumably insentient, so the theory is silent there; note anything else about the campaign it has nothing to say about. (3) The retention debate: the remaining stocks are a standing threat of future suffering, as Birmingham showed, and yet destruction has been deferred for decades by actors whose careers were organized around eliminating this very disease. Does that deferral show that even the most suffering-focused real decision-makers decline to reason as pure negative utilitarians, or can a suffering-focused view itself justify retention, given the risk arguments actually raised — undisclosed or forgotten stocks, and the possibility of synthesizing the virus regardless of what is destroyed?

Close by stating what the episode shows about the theory: whether it is a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated. Be disciplined about the mapping — what exactly counts as the suffering being reduced, over whom it aggregates, and on what time horizon — and be honest about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
