I'm a data engineer with about fifteen years of experience — schemas, normalization theory, entity-resolution pipelines, master data management — and no philosophy background beyond half a book by Russell I read in college. My question comes out of something that recently landed on my desk.

My company just completed an acquisition, and I've spent months on the deduplication problem: deciding which records in our CRM and the acquired company's CRM refer to the same real-world customer, collapsing them under one canonical surrogate key, and rewiring every foreign key that pointed at either. The matching itself is resolutely empirical — fuzzy name matching, address histories, transaction fingerprints, manual review queues — and we get it wrong sometimes; wrongly merged customers get split back apart every week. But once a merge is committed, the statement that record A in the old system and record B in the old acquired system denote the same customer stops being something anyone could discover by looking at the data. It's baked into the canonical key. A colleague put it to me this way: after the migration, "A is B" is a tautology of the schema, and the only remaining trace of the empirical work is the provenance log.

When I tried to articulate why this bothered me, someone pointed me at Kripke's Naming and Necessity lectures, and I found the territory eerily familiar: names as rigid designators, the old Frege–Russell descriptivist view that a name is shorthand for a description, the claim that the identity statement Hesperus is Phosphorus is necessarily true if true at all, yet known only a posteriori, and the mirror-image category of the contingent a priori. I'd like a translation into the vocabulary I actually use, as faithful as you can make it. Specifically: natural keys versus surrogate keys, business keys versus canonical IDs, entity resolution as empirical inference, fuzzy matching and confidence scores, provenance and lineage, merge and split transactions, denormalization, referential integrity. I'd treat descriptivism as the natural-key worldview — a record is identified by its attribute values, so reference is fixed by description — and Kripke as the surrogate-key worldview, but I don't know if that mapping is honest or if I'm flattering my own analogy.

Then I want the residue report, which is the part I actually care about:

1. Which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto database practice? For example, does the necessary a posteriori have a genuine counterpart in the fact that cross-system co-reference is discovered empirically but becomes schema-level triviality after the merge?
2. Which parts can only be translated with distortion? I suspect the causal-historical chain of reference is one of these — provenance lineage is about how data values traveled, not about how reference was fixed.
3. Which parts have no counterpart at all? My candidate is rigidity across possible worlds: my databases describe exactly one world, so the notion of a term designating the same object in every counterfactual scenario has no expression in anything I do. Relatedly, Kripke's claim that Hesperus could not have turned out to be other than Phosphorus sits badly with my weekly workflow of splitting customers that turned out, after all, to be two people.
4. Finally, the diagnostic question: my instinct is that the philosophers' necessity is just what we'd call commit semantics plus a policy decision never to revise the merge — that necessity talk is an honorific for high confidence. Tell me whether that instinct is right, or whether the residue points to something real that my vocabulary is missing. I'd like to know whether the part of the debate I can't express is a defect in my vocabulary, a defect in theirs, or evidence that the disagreement was less substantive than it looked.

Assume I'm fluent in the database vocabulary, so use it precisely rather than explaining it, and don't sand down the philosophical positions to make the translation come out cleaner than it is. If a famous example (the standard meter, gold and atomic number, water and H2O) helps localize a point of residue, use it, but translate it into my terms when you do.
