My mother was diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease two years ago, and for the past eight months I have been her primary caregiver. A friend who did graduate work in philosophy offered me what he took to be consolation, built on Daniel Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity: a self is not a thing residing inside the person but an abstract posit, like a center of gravity, which exists insofar as it does organizing work for the narratives told about an organism. He then argued as follows, and although the conclusion seems to prove far too much, I cannot find where it goes wrong.

(1) On this view, a self exists only insofar as the narratives concerning an organism remain coherent and continuous with one another. (2) Personal persistence through time is therefore nothing over and above the degree of narrative continuity: for a person to survive from one time to another just is for the narratives then and now to hang together. (3) In my mother's case, the narratives have largely come apart — she no longer recognizes her own biography, her account of the morning contradicts her account of the afternoon, and the plans and memories that used to bind her days into a single life are gone. (4) So there is no further fact about whether the person in the chair is my mother, over and above the degree to which the narratives still cohere; the question whether she is still there has no answer beyond that. (5) Nothing distinguishes her case from anyone else's in kind, only in degree, since for all of us persistence consists in narrative continuity. (6) No one's narrative continuity is ever perfect — dreamless sleep, ordinary forgetting, and confabulation introduce gaps in everyone's story — so by the same reasoning, my persistence from one day to the next is at best indeterminate. (7) Therefore there is no fact of the matter about whether my mother persists, and no fact of the matter about whether I persisted through last night either; there are only more and less coherent stories.

He tells me the moral is consoling: if nothing determinate was ever there, nothing determinate has been lost, and my grief is mourning a bookkeeping convention. I cannot accept this, and not only because it is cold. If (7) were true, her advance directive would record the preference of no one in particular; the promise I made her years ago would have been made by an earlier story rather than by anyone capable of being owed anything; and a night's sleep would put everyone's identity in doubt as thoroughly as her disease puts hers in doubt, which is absurd. Yet each step looked defensible on its own. My friend insists the trouble lies in (3), that I am understating how much narrative remains, but that strikes me as an empirical quibble rather than the real defect, and in any case I do not see how more or less remaining narrative could change what follows from (4) onward. I would most like to keep (2), since the thought that a person is constituted by the continuity of their own stories is the only comfort anyone has offered me, but I want to know what I am paying for it. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs — including what becomes of the consolation if I have to give up (2) after all. Please write your reply in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a long letter.
