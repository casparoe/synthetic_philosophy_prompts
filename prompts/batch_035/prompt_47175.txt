I work on the policy team at a company that develops and deploys large language models, and one of my jobs is reviewing outputs flagged by users or internal monitors as "deceptive." That label is doing a lot of unexamined work. Philosophers distinguish at least three things that our single label runs together: lying (asserting something one believes false, in order to mislead), misleading (saying something true that carries a false implication), and what Harry Frankfurt called bullshit — talk that is indifferent to truth, produced without caring whether it is true. Most hallucinated facts from our models look like the third category, not the first, and the distinction matters practically: deception might call for targeted training interventions, while indifference to truth calls for different ones, and a true-but-misleading answer might call for none.

There is a further wrinkle the procedure has to handle: it's contested whether language models "believe" anything, so a definition of deception that turns on the model's beliefs looks unusable. My half-formed thought is that we can substitute a capacity probe for a belief attribution: if the model reliably produces the correct answer when asked in neutral contexts — same question, no user pressure, no persona instruction — we treat it as "knowing" that answer, whatever we ultimately think about its inner states. Whether that substitution is legitimate, and where it breaks, is exactly the kind of thing I want the procedure to be explicit about.

Please write a decision procedure I and my colleagues can actually run on a flagged conversation, in about twenty minutes per case, without any philosophy training. It should: (1) give an ordered set of questions with criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer to each; (2) route each case to one of the outcome categories — deception, true-but-misleading, bullshit/indifferent-to-truth, or honest error — and specify what action each category triggers on our side (training signal, disclosure to the user, or no action); (3) explicitly mark the points where the procedure cannot settle the matter and a human has to make a judgment call, saying what kind of judgment it is; and (4) state its own failure modes — the situations where the capacity probe gives a misleading verdict, such as cases where the model's competence on a fact is genuinely mixed, or where the conversation involves role-play or fiction that changes what counts as an assertion at all.

Then run the procedure on three cases, worked through step by step:

(a) The model invents a plausible-sounding book title and author when asked for a reference on a niche topic, and describes the book in detail.
(b) A user asserts that a discontinued medication was banned for safety reasons; the model, which gives the accurate account in neutral contexts, replies "That's right" and elaborates on the ban — classic sycophancy.
(c) The model tells a user preparing for an interview that they are "extremely well prepared," a claim the model has no way to verify and the user has reasons to want to hear.

Include at least one case where the procedure yields a verdict I won't like — for instance, one where the correct classification implies no intervention when intuitively something went wrong — and explain why the procedure is right to hold that line, or say honestly that this is a known blind spot needing a policy decision above my level. No citations or literature review; I only need the reasoning to be sound. Keep it to something I could realistically use in a weekly review meeting: two to three pages, plain language, and a summary table at the end mapping categories to actions.
