I am a retired physical chemist who has lately been reading philosophy of science, and my reading group has produced an argument for structural realism that troubles me, because I suspect it proves more than any of us wants.

(1) By the no-miracles argument, the empirical success of our best theories would be miraculous unless those theories were at least approximately true; so we have reason to believe them approximately true. (2) History suggests caution: the successful theories of the past turned out false in their central claims about unobservables — there is no phlogiston, no caloric, no luminiferous ether. (3) Yet something did survive: when Maxwell's theory absorbed Fresnel's, what carried over was not any substance or object but the equations — and in general, what revolutionary theory change preserves is mathematical structure rather than ontology. (4) So the no-miracles argument warrants belief in the structure of our current theories but not in their claims about objects; this is structural realism. (5) However, the preservation in (3) is not exact. Fresnel's equations survive only as a limiting case of Maxwell's, and Newtonian mechanics only as the low-velocity limit of relativity; at best an approximate structure is retained. (6) Now apply the reasoning of (4) to (5): belief is warranted only in what survives revision, and what survives is in turn only approximately preserved, so iterating the argument strips away everything and leaves us believing in nothing at all. (7) That conclusion is absurd, so some step must fail — yet each step looks like either straight history of science or standard epistemology.

I would most like to keep (1), and I have a vague sense that something illicit happens between (4) and (6), though I cannot name it. Which step should I reject, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost?
