I should like your help with an argument I have constructed and cannot get out of. Some background so you can calibrate: I am a recently retired engineer. I spent thirty-seven years in signal processing and information theory, first at a national laboratory and later in telecommunications, and since retiring I have been working through the philosophy of probability. The Dutch book argument is, frankly, the argument that drew me in — it is the only argument in philosophy I have ever found fully transparent, because it is just bookkeeping. Lately I have been trying to generalize it, and the generalization appears to demolish the original. I would like a professional diagnosis, from someone with real expertise in formal epistemology and decision theory. Please think carefully before answering; I would ask you to begin by brainstorming two or three candidate diagnoses before committing to one, and then to argue for your choice.

First, the setup as I understand it. In the classical Dutch book argument, an agent posts fair prices for bets. If her degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, a bookie can select a finite package of bets, each one priced so the agent is at least willing to accept it, whose combined payoff is negative in every possible state of the world. The bookie needs no knowledge of the world whatsoever — only the agent's own posted numbers. That guaranteed-loss structure, I take it, is supposed to be the mark of irrationality.

Now my extension. Consider an agent who satisfies everything the Dutch book framework demands: her credences are perfectly coherent, she updates by conditionalization, and she accepts any bet with strictly positive expected value at whatever stake the counterparty proposes, capped at her current bankroll. Specifically, suppose her policy is to stake a fixed fraction f = 0.9 of her bankroll whenever she does bet. A patient bookie offers her, round after round, an even-money bet on some event she prices at 0.51. Every single bet is favorable — her expected bankroll grows by 1.8% each round. But the log of her bankroll has strictly negative drift: 0.51 × ln(1.9) + 0.49 × ln(0.1) ≈ −0.80 per round, an old friend from my information-theory days (this is the Kelly criterion; I have checked the arithmetic more than once). So her bankroll tends to zero with probability one, and a bookie with deep enough pockets — her worst intermediate drawdown is finite with probability one — ends up holding everything the agent started with, with probability one.

Put the argument in steps:

(1) An agent's credences are irrational if a bookie, using nothing but the agent's own posted prices, can select bets the agent will accept that produce a loss for the agent — the essential thing being that the bookie needs no knowledge of the world, only the agent's own numbers and dispositions.
(2) What matters cannot be the finitude of the package or the packaging itself; the essence is vulnerability. An agent is irrational if a bookie, knowing only her prices and her betting policy, can put her in a position where she is ruined with probability one.
(3) The coherent, conditionalizing, take-every-favorable-bet agent described above is ruined with probability one by such a bookie.
(4) So that agent is irrational.
(5) And with suspicious precision: the same construction ruins any agent whose staking fraction exceeds 0.02 (twice her edge), and cannot touch any agent below it. So condition (2) hands us a bankroll-management rule as a requirement of rationality.
(6) But the agent in (3) violates nothing the framework itself prescribes. If the framework condemns her, it proves too much — and since (2) is my generalization of the classical mark of irrationality, the classical argument inherits whatever wound (2) suffers.

I have tried three patches and distrust all of them. Patch A: require the bookie's profit to be certain in every possible world, not merely of probability one. This spares my victim but makes rationality hostage to measure-zero worlds, which offends every engineering instinct I have — we ground aircraft on probability-one failure modes. Patch B: require the bookie to work only from posted prices at a time, not from the agent's acceptance policy. But the diachronic Dutch book for conditionalization seems to require foreknowledge of the agent's future prices too, so I fear this kills a sibling to save a cousin. Patch C: require a finite book. But a patient exploiter is still an exploiter, and a deadline looks arbitrary.

Please tell me which step to reject and why that step looked fine; what rejecting it costs, including what each of my patches does to the classical argument for the probability axioms and to the diachronic argument for conditionalization; and whether my instinct — that staking rules like Kelly's are prudential bookkeeping rather than epistemic rationality — can be made principled or is mere squeamishness. Rank the diagnoses by how much I would have to pay.
