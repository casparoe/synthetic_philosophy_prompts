So here's my situation: I'm a novelist, and my agent keeps pushing me to write the split-brain book — a first person narrator who's had her corpus callosum severed, and the whole conceit would be what happens to the "I" telling the story. Before I can write a single chapter I need to know what I actually think about whether that's even one narrator or two, and my intuitions keep flopping around depending on the case, so I want to lay them out and have you tell me if there's a principle underneath them or whether I'm just muddle-headed.

My verdicts, for what they're worth. In the classic lab setup where a word like "key" is flashed to one hemisphere and "ring" to the other, and the patient says the two words form a single compound answer, that feels to me like one person confabulating, not two people who happen to agree. When the left hand does something the talking half disowns — grabs a different object than the one the right hand was told to take — my gut says one person with a badly behaving hand, the way your leg kicks when the doctor taps your knee, and I don't want to say the leg is a second agent. But then there's the famous kind of case where the two halves are asked separately what they want to be when they grow up, or how they feel about something, and they give genuinely different answers, different ambitions, different preferences — and with that one I do feel like I'm looking at two people sharing a body, and I'd be writing two chapters from two points of view. And here's where I embarrass myself: I also think that a patient with alien hand syndrome after a stroke, whose hand does things she finds horrifying and disowns completely, is clearly still just one person with a malfunction, and I can't for the life of me see why a stroke deserves one answer and a severed callosum deserves the other. Then, to make it worse, I think all of us are pretty fragmented anyway — I've driven a whole stretch of highway with no memory of it, I say things I can't account for — and yet I'd never write a normal character as two narrators.

So that's my mess. Is there a principle — some cutoff for when it's one consciousness versus two, or maybe a third option where "how many" is the wrong question entirely — that captures all or most of these verdicts? If you have to overturn one of mine, tell me which and why. And crucially, tell me what the principle says about the everyday case, the patient who's back home from the hospital cooking dinner and chatting with her sister with no lab equipment in sight: is she one narrator or two for my purposes, and if the answer is "it depends," then what I want to know is what it depends *on*. Please don't send me off to a reading list, I've got enough of those — I want your actual take on the principle.
