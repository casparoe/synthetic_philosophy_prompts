I'm an econometrician at a policy research institute, and almost all of my conceptual vocabulary comes from that world: identification, aggregation, the representative-agent assumption in DSGE models, the ecological fallacy, Simpson's paradox, the fallacy of composition, the Lucas critique, and the general demand that macro relations be "micro-founded." Years ago, as an undergraduate, I took a philosophy of social science course that I mostly remember as a blur, but one debate stuck with me: methodological individualism versus holism. As I recall it, the individualist side — Weber, and later Popper in some moods — insists that any adequate social-scientific explanation must ultimately bottom out in facts about individual persons, their beliefs, goals, and situations, and that talk of "states," "institutions," or "markets" as if they were independent agents is at best shorthand. The holist side, which I associate above all with Durkheim, replies that social facts are sui generis: they have properties and causal powers that no amount of individual-level description supplies, and that treating the collective as a real unit of analysis is not laziness but accuracy. There also seemed to be a third, quieter position, which held that individualism is not a metaphysical thesis about what exists but merely a methodological heuristic, and that neither side is really disagreeing with the other about anything substantive.

To me, this all sounds uncomfortably close to fights I actually have at work — whether an estimated cross-country aggregate relation tells us anything about individual behavior (ecological fallacy), whether aggregate parameters change when the policy regime changes (Lucas critique), whether macro relations need micro-foundations or whether they can be defended as stable regularities in their own right. But I suspect the translation is doing hidden work, and I'd like to find out where. So here is my request: restate the methodological individualism versus holism debate in my vocabulary — identification, aggregation, micro-foundations, representative agents, composition effects, and whatever else in the econometrician's toolkit actually fits — as faithfully as you can. Keep the translation honest: don't quietly build in the econometrician's own commitments (for instance, that all legitimate explanation is identification of parameters in a model) and present them as if they were neutral renderings of the philosophical positions. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the debate translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which claims — Durkheim's sui generis social facts, the "in principle derivability" demand, the heuristic reading — have no expression in my vocabulary at all. Finally, tell me whether the residue points to something my vocabulary genuinely cannot say, or to the possibility that the philosophical disagreement was less substantive than it looked.
