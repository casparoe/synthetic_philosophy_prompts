Two members of my decision theory reading group got into an argument about the Allais paradox that the rest of us couldn't settle, and I'd like an adjudication. The setting: in the first choice, subjects typically prefer $1M for certain over a lottery paying $5M with probability .10, $1M with probability .89, and nothing with probability .01; in the second, they prefer a lottery paying $5M with probability .10 and nothing with probability .90 over a lottery paying $1M with probability .11 and nothing with probability .89. Maya claimed this pattern of choices directly violates the independence axiom, and that expected utility theory is thereby shown descriptively false. Daniel objected that this misstates the axiom: as formulated, independence governs mixtures — if someone is indifferent between lotteries p and q, they must be indifferent between any mixture of p with a third lottery r and the corresponding mixture of q with r. The Allais subjects never faced mixtures, he said; they made two separate choices among simple one-stage lotteries, so nothing they did contradicts the axiom as literally stated. Maya called this pedantry: the standard construction embeds both choices in a single two-stage scheme in which, with probability .89, a common consequence is delivered either way, and the remaining 11% is allocated to the safe or the risky sub-gamble; under that embedding the two preferences jointly violate independence, and since the embedding leaves each subject's objective situation unchanged, the inconsistency is present whether or not it was displayed to them in mixture form. Daniel conceded the embedding but made a final move: even granting it, the paradox only shows that the choices are inconsistent conditional on accepting independence, and whether independence is normatively binding for one-shot choices among lotteries is precisely what is in dispute — Allais's own view was that preferring security when near-certainty is available is not obviously irrational, so the axiom, not the subjects, might be what should give way. Maya answered that if every axiom can be renegotiated whenever it bites, no decision theory could ever be refuted by any data. Please go through the exchange move by move: say who is right about each specific point (including whether the choices are inconsistent only via the embedding, and whether the normative status of independence is genuinely up for grabs), and identify anything both of them missed.
