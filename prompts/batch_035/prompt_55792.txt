You are to act as a philosopher of probability with expertise in the foundations of Bayesian inference and the classical paradoxes of geometric probability. Before committing to a verdict, please reason through the argument step by step and consider which reading of each premise is doing the work. Your answer must be written in American English.

My study group has produced an argument whose conclusion seems to prove far too much, and I cannot locate the fault. It runs as follows:

(1) If an agent is completely ignorant about which of several alternatives obtains, rationality requires assigning equal probability to each alternative.
(2) In Bertrand's question — a chord of a circle is drawn "at random"; what is the probability that it is longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? — nothing in the problem specifies how the chord is generated, so we are completely ignorant, and by (1) we must spread probability evenly over the alternatives.
(3) Spreading probability evenly requires choosing a variable, and at least three choices seem equally natural: (a) fix one endpoint and place the other uniformly on the circumference, in which case the chord wins only when the second endpoint lands in the far third of the circle, giving 1/3; (b) choose a radius and a point on it uniformly, and take the chord perpendicular to the radius at that point, in which case the chord wins only when the point lies within half the radius of the center, giving 1/2; (c) place the chord's midpoint uniformly over the disk, in which case the chord wins only when the midpoint falls inside the concentric circle of half the radius, whose area is one quarter of the disk's, giving 1/4.
(4) The principle of indifference itself cannot rank these parametrizations: any tie-breaking criterion would have to be justified by facts about how chords are actually produced, and the problem stipulates that there are no such facts.
(5) So the principle licenses three incompatible distributions for a single question, and since they disagree, at most one can be correct, and the principle cannot tell us which.
(6) A principle that licenses incompatible answers is inconsistent, and an inconsistent principle cannot confer rational credibility on anything.
(7) Since the principle of indifference is the only candidate for a prior representing no knowledge at all, there is no such thing as an ignorance prior, and every probability assignment — including any "uniform" one — smuggles in substantive information.

The conclusion feels too strong. I would rather keep (1) than accept (7), because (7) appears to imply that even a fair die before its first roll admits no defensible assignment of 1/6 to each face, which I cannot swallow. So: which step should I reject, why did that step look unobjectionable, and what does rejecting it cost me? In particular, tell me whether I can keep (1) for the die while denying that it licenses any of the three readings in (3) — and whether I can do that without appealing to the physical symmetry of the selection setup, since my friend will reply that symmetry knowledge is still knowledge, and that if the die case depends on it, (7) wins after all. If you think I should instead bite the bullet and accept (7), say so plainly, and tell me exactly what the die case becomes on that view.
