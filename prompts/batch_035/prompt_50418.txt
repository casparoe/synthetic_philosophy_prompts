so I'm a novelist, working on a secondary-world society, and my beta reader (an ex-anthropologist) keeps telling me not to sweat why my fictional people have the conventions they do. her line is: a convention is like a footpath worn across a park lawn. nobody plans it, nobody enforces it, each person's use wears it a bit deeper for the next, and after a while its just The Way You Walk, and going against it would take deliberate effort. so, she says, in fiction you can stipulate any convention you like — which hand you eat with, which words are taboo, how debts of honor get settled — and as long as I show it being consistently followed, readers will find it natural, and I don't need to explain where it came from. something about this feels off to me: I read a bit of Lewis on convention years ago, and I remember him making a lot of the fact that conventions tend to be arbitrary — several roughly equally good options — and that what locks one in is mutual expectation, everybody expecting everybody else to conform, not the path of least resistance. can you lay out the full correspondence first — what plays the role of the walker, the worn groove, the grass, the shortcut, the person who decides to cut across anyway — then say where the footpath picture breaks down, and then tell me whether her conclusion survives once it's corrected. in particular I want to know: can I really invent any convention at all and have it feel inevitable, or are there kinds — ones that leave everyone worse off, ones where there's no obvious focal point to coordinate on — where readers will smell a rat unless I give them some seed, like a founder, a myth, or a visible asymmetry?
