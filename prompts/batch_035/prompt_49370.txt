Ok so I've gotten obsessed with these "spectrum arguments" against the transitivity of 'better than', and I keep flip-flopping. Write me an essay on whether 'better than' is transitive — i.e. whether A better than B and B better than C guarantees that A is better than C. Take the spectrum argument seriously instead of waving it off as a parlor trick, weigh both sides properly, and end with an actual verdict. A shrug at the end ("it's complicated", "more research needed") doesn't count.

So we're on the same page, here's the setup, from scratch (it's a cousin of cases you'll find in the literature from Larry Temkin and Stuart Rachels, if you know it). Consider two outcomes:

Outcome 1: 1,000,000 people each get a headache lasting 1 hour.
Outcome 2: 500,000 people each get a headache lasting 2 hours.

Intuition says these are pretty much equally good — halving the number of sufferers plausibly makes up for doubling how long each one suffers. Maybe outcome 2 is even a hair better. Now keep iterating: each step halves the number of sufferers and doubles the duration of each headache. Twenty steps in, you reach: 1 person with a headache lasting roughly a million hours, i.e. about a century of continuous headache. Chain together the local judgements — each step is at least as good as the one before it — and transitivity delivers the verdict that the 1-person-with-a-century-of-headache outcome is at least as good as the million-people-with-one-hour-headaches outcome. Which seems flatly wrong. So something has to give: either one of the local judgements is false, or transitivity fails.

The essay should at least grapple with the following, though feel free to go beyond:

1. The most obvious reply is that each step is a tiny bit worse, not exactly equal, and the small losses accumulate so the final comparison comes out right. But notice this commits you to a tipping point somewhere in the middle of the spectrum: early on, halving the sufferers fully compensates for doubling the duration; at some point it stops doing so. Is a sharp discontinuity like that plausible, and where would it even come from? And if instead you say it never fully compensates — every single step is worse, even step 1 — are you comfortable with what that implies about the relative badness of a 2-hour headache vs two 1-hour headaches?

2. What does giving up transitivity actually cost us? If 'better than' can be non-transitive, what happens to rational choice between three options (can you get money-pump style trouble, or is that overblown for an impartial evaluator who isn't being offered trades)? And is there a principled way to restrict the intransitivity — e.g. keeping 'better for a person' transitive while letting impartial, aggregative 'better overall' fail?

3. There's also a move where you say the local judgements are only *approximately* equal, and 'approximately equal' isn't transitive (nearness isn't). Does that dissolve the argument, or does it just relocate the problem to explaining why the compounded approximation can produce such a huge final gap?

Don't feel bound to conclude that transitivity survives, and don't feel bound to conclude it doesn't — but do commit, and make the case for why the cost you're paying (either the discontinuity or the intransitivity) is the cheaper one. Write it like a real essay with an argumentative arc, not a Q&A matching my three bullet points. Also if you could avoid a bunch of name-dropping and citations, that'd be great — I want the reasoning, not a bibliography. Length: whatever the argument needs, but I'd rather get depth than a summary. honestly I've read enough blog-post-length hot takes on this.
