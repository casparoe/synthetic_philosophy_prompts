I volunteer with the human rights group at my university, and last week's meeting turned into a two-hour argument over a single word in a statement we're drafting about an ongoing conflict. To keep it manageable, say the documented facts are roughly this: government-backed militias have driven most of an ethnic minority out of a contested province — villages burned, a few massacres, mass flight across the border — and there's a leaked order from a regional commander about "clearing the province of" that minority, but nothing from the national leadership saying the group should be destroyed as such. Dana wants the letter to call this a genocide. Marcus thinks that's flatly wrong and that we'll discredit ourselves.

Marcus opened by saying genocide has a strict legal meaning — destroying a group in whole or in part, with that specific intent — and what we're describing is ethnic cleansing, which is a terrible crime but not the same crime; if we blur the line we hand the perpetrators' defenders an easy "see, activists exaggerate" win. Dana's counter was that "ethnic cleansing" is itself a euphemism — the phrase got popularized during the Bosnian war, and it launders mass expulsion into something that sounds vaguely janitorial — and that demanding courtroom-grade proof of intent in an advocacy letter is a category error anyway: we're making a moral description, not drafting an indictment. Marcus said the word's moral force comes from its rarity — call every atrocity a genocide and the word goes the way of "fascist" and stops doing any work — and besides, saying genocide implicitly invokes the duty to prevent under the convention, which sets expectations that nobody in a student group can deliver on. Dana came back hard: the legal category he wants to defer to is itself a political artifact — political groups were written out of the convention during drafting precisely so that certain states' conduct couldn't count, and Lemkin's own broader concept, where destroying a group's culture and way of life mattered as much as killing its members, got pruned away in the same horse-trading. And, she added, Lemkin literally invented the word in order to do advocacy with it — the convention only exists because he lobbied it into existence — so treating "genocide" as a term that advocates must handle with lawyerly gloves gets its entire history backwards. Marcus's last word was Rwanda in 1994: US officials bending over backwards at press briefings to concede only that acts of genocide might be occurring, precisely so they wouldn't trigger obligations to act — which proves, he said, that the word carries real consequences, which is exactly why amateurs shouldn't swing it around.

They both left pretty annoyed, and somehow I got tasked with writing up where the group should actually land. Can you adjudicate their exchange move by move — who's right about which specific points, where each of them goes wrong if they do, and whether there's some framing they're both missing? For what it's worth, it struck me afterward that neither of them ever said what the letter is actually *for*, though I'm not sure if thats relevant or just me being pedantic after two hours.
