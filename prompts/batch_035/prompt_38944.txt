I'm taking a philosophy of the social sciences course, and we have just reached the question whether generations — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z — are real kinds or merely marketing labels. In section this week another student offered an argument that, if sound, seems to prove far too much, and I cannot find where it slips.

(1) For a label like "Millennial" to name a real kind, cohort membership must predict something about a person that could not already be predicted from their birth year and age alone.
(2) Such predictions are tested by comparing cohorts — say, 25-year-olds in 2020 against 25-year-olds in 2005.
(3) But any difference found this way could equally be an age effect (when the comparison is made at different ages) or a period effect (2020 made everyone more anxious, not just the young).
(4) Separating age, period, and cohort would require data at combinations of the three that a single run of history cannot supply: each person is born once and passes through each calendar year at exactly one age, so the three variables are perfectly confounded, and no observations from our one history fix the split.
(5) Therefore no generational claim — no ascription of a character to a cohort — is ever known; at best it is guessed.
(6) And by the same reasoning, nothing about any historical population is ever known either, since history cannot be rerun for them either; yet we plainly do know a good deal about, say, what the Great Depression did to the people who lived through it.

So the argument, if sound, shows both that cohort talk is never knowledge and that history is never knowledge, and I cannot accept either conclusion. My instinct is to keep (4), since the confound they point to looks like a structural fact about the data rather than a temporary ignorance we might one day shrug off. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning — no preamble, and no restating of my question.
