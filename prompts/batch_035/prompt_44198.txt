I've been reading Gödel, Escher, Bach with some friends from a philosophy of mind course, and we've just reached the dialogue where Achilles and the Tortoise meet an anteater who cheerfully insists that feeding on the colony's ants is entirely compatible with his friendship with the colony itself, an intelligent being named Aunt Hillary. The picture on offer is that ants are to the colony what neurons are to the brain: signals pass from ant to ant by touch and pheromone the way electrochemical signals pass across synapses, and whatever intelligence is present lives at the level of the organized whole rather than in any of the hopelessly dim parts. My friend has become quite taken with this and now deploys it in every argument about collective intelligence: if organization alone is what makes a mind, then corporations, markets, ecosystems, even the whole churn of traffic on the internet may be genuine subjects with something it is like to be them, and perhaps owed moral consideration we would never think to extend. She accuses anyone who denies this of a kind of neuron-level prejudice — mistaking the absence of intelligence in the components for an absence of intelligence in the whole.

I'd like a serious audit of the analogy, since I suspect it is carrying more weight than it can bear but can't quite put my finger on where it fails. First, lay out explicitly what is supposed to map onto what: what plays the role of the neuron, the synapse, the stable pattern of long-range connectivity, and the developmental process that wires the whole thing up in the first place. Then explain where the mapping breaks. Some disanalogies I half-notice myself: an ant survives alone and can change tasks in a way a neuron cannot; a colony has no persistent anatomy, so the ants implementing some state of the colony one minute are scattered and replaced the next; colonies are founded by single queens and reproduce by budding rather than by anything resembling the growth of a brain; and chemical transmission between ants is enormously slower than neural signaling. What I can't tell is which of these differences matter for my friend's conclusion and which are merely incidental — surely some of them cut no ice, and I'd like to know which and why.

Finally, tell me whether her conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the analogy does real work, what exactly does it establish — sophisticated, flexible behavior at the level of the colony, or genuine experience? If it is a bad analogy, what would serve better in its place? Please write your answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a long letter.
