I recently joined a workplace reading group on Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, prompted by an argument about whether participation in markets corrodes character. To prepare, I read a magazine article that sums up Smith's moral psychology like this: Smith's central concept is sympathy, by which he means pity for those who suffer; we catch other people's emotions more or less the way we catch a cold, and morality is essentially society's approval reflected back at us, since his impartial spectator is just the internalized voice of public opinion, our conscience as shaped by the crowd; because what wins approval is fitting in, Smith ends up with a conformist ethics that sits uneasily with the self-interest of the Wealth of Nations, a tension known as the Adam Smith problem and never resolved. Our group's whole discussion is now at risk of being built on this summary, and I don't know enough to tell where it goes wrong. Please go claim by claim: what a Smith scholar would accept, what they would call an oversimplification, and what is simply a misreading. I'm especially unsure about two points — whether sympathy really is just pity plus contagion in his system, and whether the impartial spectator is nothing more than the crowd's voice turned inward. Finally, does the article's implicit conclusion, that Smith leaves us no standpoint from which to criticize the norms of our own society, follow from whichever parts of the summary are actually accurate?
