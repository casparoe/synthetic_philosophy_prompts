so heres the thing about me: i'm a novelist, four books out, currently on my fifth, which means i've spent the better part of fifteen years living inside a very particular vocabulary -- drafts, revisions, "killing your darlings", character consistency, how long a reader's patience holds before it doesnt. i don't have any philosophy training beyond a friend who's a grad student and won't shut up about it, but lately she's been going on about vagueness and the sorites paradox, and honestly it felt like she was describing my day job.

in case it's not obvious from that intro, the classic sorites is the heap thing: one grain of sand isnt a heap, and adding a single grain never turns a non-heap into a heap, so by induction you never get a heap, which is absurd. my version is the one every novelist knows: you finish a manuscript, then you revise it, and revisions breed revisions, and at some point you're looking at draft eleven wondering whether this is even the same book anymore. change one word, obviously still the same novel. change every word, obviously not. no single edit ever flips it. and yet somewhere in there it flipped.

what i want is a proper translation exercise. take the main positions in the vagueness debate and restate each one in my vocabulary -- the drafting one -- as faithfully as you can. so epistemicism (the view that there IS a sharp cutoff between heap and non-heap, we just can't ever know where), supervaluationism (something about truth being what holds on all precisifications, i never fully followed her on this), the degree-theory people who want sentences to be partially true, and whatever the contextualist line is where the boundary shifts with the situation. map them onto what happens between draft one and the published book, and onto questions like "is this still the same character after i rewrote her backstory" or "at what point does a heavy revision become a different novel".

then, and this is the part i actually care about, give me the residue report. which of these positions translate cleanly into something a working writer would recognise, which ones only fit if you bend them (and say where you bent them), and which ones have no expression at all in draft-and-revision vocabulary. don't smuggle in my assumptions and call it translation -- if a theory doesn't fit my world, i want to know that, not a flattering distortion of it.

because here's my suspicion, and i want you to tell me whether it's an insight or a blind spot. when my friend explained epistemicism i laughed out loud -- no novelist alive believes there's a fact of the matter about which draft is "really" the same book. whether the sixth draft counts as "the novel i sold" is settled by contracts, publishers, how much you want to brag about it in interviews -- it's convention all the way down, and it feels insane to think there's a hidden boundary we're ignorant of. but i also know that my vocabulary has obvious limits. for one, i only ever deal with artifacts i made myself, with a revision history i can literally open in a folder, whereas the heap people are talking about words like "tall" and "bald" applying to objects nobody authored. so: does the residue point at something the philosophers are missing (that these questions are just settled by our practices and there's nothing deeper to find), or does it point at something my drafting vocabulary simply cannot say? and be honest about which of those two verdicts my little "i laughed out loud" reaction actually deserves.
