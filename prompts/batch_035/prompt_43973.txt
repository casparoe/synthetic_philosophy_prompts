I finally have a workable view and got a slot to present it at a departmental colloquium, and I'm honestly nervous because the audience is very much NOT a rationalist meetup. It's mostly consequentialist-leaning moral philosophers who know the Bostrom and Ćirković stuff cold, one deontologist who thinks the whole aggregative framework is a mistake, and -- the reason I'm writing this -- a set theorist from the math department who shows up to every talk that mentions infinity and enjoys making philosophers regret it.

My view, sketchily: in infinite worlds you can't just sum utilities, so the standard moves (discounting the distant future, hyperreal arithmetic, appeals to a measure over spacetime) are all ad hoc in ways that smuggle in our finite intuitions. My proposal is a two-stage thing. Stage 1: compare worlds by their finite truncations, i.e. you sum utilities only over the first N individuals for various N and see if a winner emerges robustly across truncation points. Stage 2: when worlds stay tied at the limit (the actual hard case, since tied sums are exactly what infinity gives you), you break the tie with something like an infinite-world leximin: compare the worst-off individuals across worlds, then the second worst-off, and so on. I'm claiming this avoids both the arbitrariness of picking a discount rate and the weirdness of doing infinitesimal arithmetic on suffering.
