I'm a senior backend engineer at a mid-sized insurer, and I've been placed on a cross-functional working group that has to decide how our new underwriting decision system will "explain" adverse decisions to customers. Our regulators require that a denied applicant receive a "meaningful explanation" of the denial, but they don't specify the format, and the group has split into two camps that have been arguing for three meetings now. The first camp wants counterfactual explanations: for each denial, the system should compute and display the minimal change to the applicant's inputs that would have flipped the decision — for example, "if your annual mileage had been 8,000 instead of 12,000, you would have qualified for the preferred tier." The second camp argues that many of these counterfactuals are things the applicant can't do anything about — age, zip code, a three-year-old at-fault claim — and wants the system to emit only forward-looking explanations: concrete actions the applicant could take now (raise a credit score, complete a defensive driving course) along with the decision the model would render if those actions were taken. Their slogan is "explain what they can change, not what they could have been."

What makes the disagreement hard to resolve is that both camps seem to be using the word "because" to mean different things, and neither seems willing to notice this. The counterfactual camp is answering the question "what would have had to be different for the outcome to be different?" — they even want to rank explanatory factors by how small an input change flips the outcome. The intervention camp is answering "which manipulable input, altered now, would produce a different outcome?" — and they complain that ranking by flip-distance produces absurd explanations like "if you were two years older" or "if you had a different garage." When I raised this at the last meeting, a data scientist on the group said it reminded him of something he half-remembered from a philosophy podcast about whether causation is fundamentally a relationship between how things actually went versus how they could have gone, or something about what happens when you physically intervene on a system. He couldn't reconstruct it any better than that, and I don't have the background to. But I suspect the group is really divided over which of those two notions of "what caused this" a regulator, a judge, and a customer would each find meaningful.

Please draft the written recommendation memo I'll circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend a concrete decision — counterfactual-only, intervention-only, or a specific hybrid (e.g., compute counterfactuals but only display the subset satisfying an actionability test, and define what that test is) — with a rationale the losing camp could recognize as responsive to its concerns rather than dismissive of them. Somewhere in the memo, I'd like a plain-language section untangling the two senses of "because" at stake, since I think naming the disagreement will do more to break our deadlock than any technical argument will. Where it helps, you can assume the usual complications: some inputs are legitimately immutable, some are changeable but only slowly, the model is a gradient-boosted ensemble rather than anything interpretable by inspection, and we have a hard deadline of one quarter before launch.
