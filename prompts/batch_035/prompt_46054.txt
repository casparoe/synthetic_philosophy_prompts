I'm a high school senior who has been on my school's debate team since freshman year, and I'm also taking an elective where we've been reading about how AI labs hope to keep very capable AI systems under control. One idea I keep coming back to is what the field calls "AI safety via debate": when a powerful AI system gives an answer, you spin up two copies of it, one assigned to defend the answer and the other to attack it, and then a judge — a human with limited time, or maybe a less capable model — decides which side is right. The hope is that a person who could never understand the system's reasoning on their own can still supervise it, because the attacking copy will drag the hidden weaknesses into the open where the judge can see them. Some versions even require the debaters to keep their arguments comprehensible to the judge, and some limit how long the judge can spend, on the theory that the procedure should work even under heavy time pressure.

Here is what bugs me. In competitive debate, and in courtrooms, everyone knows that the better debater often wins regardless of who is right; that's why courts add rules of evidence, expert judges, and all sorts of machinery around the bare clash of arguments. Yet the AI proposal seems to trust a much thinner structure: two adversaries, one lay judge, and nothing else. What I want to understand is what this setup actually presupposes about truth and reasoning — for instance, whether it needs something like the claim that lies are more expensive to maintain than truths under cross-examination, or something weaker would do. Does it presuppose that truth has a kind of structural advantage in an adversarial exchange, and if so, is that a substantive epistemological commitment or just an engineering bet that happens to work on the benchmark tasks it's been tried on? And is the practice internally consistent, given that its proponents also worry, elsewhere, that persuasive-but-wrong AI outputs are precisely the danger?

Please take on the role of a professional epistemologist who works on social practices of inquiry, and think carefully before answering. Start with some brainstorming — lay out several candidate commitments the debate setup could be resting on, from strong to weak — and then develop the most promising line. I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say honestly where it's underdetermined, such that several quite different views of knowledge would license it. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the design if the presupposed view turned out to be false. You don't need any sources; I care about the reasoning, not references.
