I'm a program officer at a small foundation that funds theoretical work rather than empirical studies, and we've decided to spend the equivalent of about five postdocs over three years on a single problem cluster: the problem of logical omniscience — the idealization, built into standard epistemic logic and most of formal economics, that an agent knows all logical consequences of what she knows, including the consequences no one could ever derive in a lifetime. I don't want a survey of the field. I want a defended agenda: which open questions in this cluster should get the effort, in what order, and why.

To make the task concrete, here are the candidate lines of work as we currently frame them, though you should feel free to replace or reframe them:

1. Carving the gap between real agents and the ideal: is it best modeled as an awareness failure (the agent never entertains the proposition), a resource bound (she entertains it but can't complete the derivation), or a distinction between implicit and explicit knowledge — and does the choice actually matter downstream, or is it a verbal preference?
2. The system-relativity worry: since logical consequence is defined relative to a deductive calculus, logical omniscience is relative too — an agent is omniscient about one system and ignorant about a stronger one. Is there a principled way to choose the calculus, or does the problem not even have a well-defined statement until this is settled?
3. The idealization defense: on the analogy with frictionless planes, is omniscience a legitimate idealization for some purposes and not others — say, legitimate in security proofs in cryptography, where a short proof may exist but be inhumanly hard to find, yet illegitimate when game theorists assume agents complete chains of reasoning that no human ever completes?
4. The downstream effects: where the assumption does visible work in predicting behavior, as in backward-induction reasoning in long games, and whether the mispredictions there count against the idealization or against the models built on top of it.

What I want back is a prioritization with the reasoning visible: which questions are upstream of which, what makes a question tractable for logicians and philosophers rather than empirical scientists, which of the four (or of your replacements) you would deprioritize on the grounds that answering it wouldn't change much, and what developments over the next three years — say, in how large language models reason, since they derive consequences imperfectly and at scale — should move items up or down your list. Please don't recommend specific papers or authors to me; I'm after the structure of the problem, not a bibliography.
