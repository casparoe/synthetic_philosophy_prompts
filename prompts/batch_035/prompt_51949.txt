I'm a program officer at a mid-sized environmental foundation, and one of my jobs is signing off on the indicators our partners report against. Over the last few years I've watched the same pattern play out several times: we pick a measurable proxy for the thing we actually care about, partners optimize the proxy hard enough, and the proxy quietly detaches from the thing. A reforestation partner hit a "trees planted" target with seedlings nobody revisited, so mortality ate most of it within two years; a marine program hit its "patrol days at sea" quota by anchoring just outside the harbor; a community-education grant hit "workshops delivered" by holding the same workshop for the same twelve people five times. Each time it happened, my boss asked me why I hadn't flagged it, and each time I realized I'd been treating "is this metric gameable?" as a vibe rather than something I could actually reason through. I've read enough about Goodhart-style failures to know there are supposed to be distinct failure modes lurking here -- the proxy only correlating with the goal in the normal range, so pushing to the extremes breaks it; the act of selecting on the proxy adding noise that dominates the signal; partners actively gaming rather than merely drifting -- but I can't turn that reading into anything I can run on a Tuesday afternoon.

What I want is a decision procedure I can actually apply to a proposed indicator in under an hour, without any formal statistics beyond what fits on a napkin. It should walk me through questions in order: what the metric is a proxy for and how tight that link is when nobody is being measured on it; who is being measured and what they can change cheaply; what happens if the indicator is pushed to extremes the historical data never covered; whether the partner could raise the number while making the underlying outcome worse; and what a spot-check or independent re-measurement would look like before we renew. It should say where the procedure terminates in a firm no (e.g., the indicator can be moved at near-zero cost in ways invisible to us), and where it has to hand the decision back to me as a judgment call, so I can tell my boss honestly which parts of my sign-off are procedure and which parts are me. Constraints: our program associates, not statisticians, will run this on most proposals; renewal decisions come twice a year; and any verification we impose costs our partners real money, so the procedure should distinguish between "cheap for us to check" and "expensive to verify but worth it."

Then please run the procedure on three cases, including one where it gives an answer I won't like. First, a reforestation proposal reporting "trees surviving at year three," verified by the partner's own staff with GPS-tagged photos. Second, a "hectares under improved management" indicator where the partner cooperative selects which households count as enrolled, on the theory that self-selection filters for genuinely committed participants. Third -- the one I'm dreading -- our foundation's internal dashboard metric, "share of grants with a quantified outcome indicator," which our board loves because it makes the whole portfolio legible at a glance, and which I've been quietly crediting for the proliferation of the very proxy-gaming I'm trying to catch. If the procedure flags that last one, say so plainly, and tell me what the procedure can and cannot tell me about how to unwind it.
