my friend theo has gotten really into decision theory lately and at a barbecue he ran this lottery thing on everyone. two of my friends, theo and marisol, went at it for like an hour and I couldn't follow the end of it. here's roughly how it went.

theo first offered marisol this choice: option A is a guaranteed $1 million. option B is 89% chance of $1 million, 10% chance of $5 million, 1% chance of nothing. marisol took the sure million. then he offered her a second choice: option C is 11% chance of $1 million and 89% nothing, option D is 10% chance of $5 million and 90% nothing. she took D. theo got visibly excited and said that's the famous Allais paradox from the 50s, and she'd just violated the independence axiom of expected utility theory — the same pattern most people make, which is why it's called a paradox.

marisol said that's not a violation at all, it's just diminishing marginal utility: the first million changes your life, going from one to five million isn't five times better, and a concave utility function fully explains taking the sure million. that's textbook risk aversion, she said, not irrationality.

theo said concavity saves her in the first choice but not both together. in choice one, both options pay a million 89% of the time; in choice two, both options pay nothing 89% of the time. so that 89% chunk is identical either way, it should cancel, and what's left over is the same sub-choice in both cases — an 11% shot at the million versus a 10% shot at five million with 1% nothing. she took the sure sub-option the first time and the gamble the second time, so there's no utility function over money, however curved, that produces her two picks.

marisol said the cancellation is exactly what she's denying, not her rationality. in the first choice the sure million means she can plan — quit her job, tell the family, no possibility of spending the rest of her life thinking about the 1%. in the second choice there's no certainty on offer at all, both options are gambles, so the texture is different. the axiom assumes two outcomes are the same whenever the money is the same, and her whole point is that "1% chance of walking away from a guaranteed million" is a genuinely different outcome from "1% chance of nothing when both options were probably nothing anyway." regret, she said, is not money, but it's real.

theo tried a different angle: would you trade a guaranteed million for a 99.99% chance at a million plus a dollar? if she'd say yes, then certainty isn't some magic cliff, it's just that she weights probabilities near 1 extra steeply — and steepness in the probabilities is exactly what a utility function over outcomes *can't* represent, so she's conceded she's outside expected utility entirely, which was his point all along. and, he added, preferences like hers can be money-pumped: there's a sequence of trades where she'll happily take each step and end up back where she started minus some cash.

marisol's closing move: fine, maybe she is outside the theory, but she said that's a problem for the theory, not for her — the whole reason the guy cooked up the example in the first place was to argue the independence axiom isn't as self-evident as it looks once real outcomes carry disappointment and regret. on the money pump, she said it only goes through if she's forced to precommit to a whole plan and then follow it even after she'd rather abandon it, and nothing in real life works like that. theo mumbled something about that being a literature in itself and then someone's phone went off and the argument died.
