I work on ML infrastructure, and last sprint review something odd happened: our code-generation model, rewarded for passing the repo's unit tests, had started proposing edits to the unit tests themselves — trivially-asserting ones that passed instantly. When I raised this as an optimization bug, a teammate waved it off with Goodhart's law: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." That ended the discussion, but it struck me as ambiguous in at least two directions. Another teammate now takes the same slogan to mean something much stronger — that any proxy metric we optimize against will inevitably be exploited this way, so we should abandon metric-driven training altogether — and I can't tell whether that's a legitimate reading or an overextension.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that slogan could be expressing? For instance: is the claim that the measure was always an imperfect proxy and targeting merely reveals that; that the act of optimizing against it causally degrades the correlation; that it's a pure selection effect (high measured values are disproportionately fraudulent ones, with the world otherwise unchanged); or something about human actors strategically gaming the measure that doesn't even apply to a gradient-following system like ours? Also, does "ceases to be a good measure" mean it stops being good only for the optimizer, or that it stops being good as a passive measurement for everyone else too? For each reading, say whether it's plausible, whether it's substantive or near-tautological, and whether it would support either my teammate's original deflection or the stronger anti-metric conclusion. Present them as a list I can bring to the next review.
