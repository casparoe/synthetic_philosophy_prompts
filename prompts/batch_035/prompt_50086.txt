I am a teaching assistant grading final examinations for a third-year metaphysics course that spent two weeks on supertasks, covering Thomson's original lamp argument and the standard replies to it, most importantly Benacerraf's. The exam question below is worth 30 points, allocated ten per part. Please grade the student's answer: assign a score out of 30, broken down by part, and write the specific marginal comments you would put on the exam itself — precise enough that the student could revise from them without further explanation. In those comments, please (i) identify each error exactly and explain why it is an error, rather than merely noting that something is off; (ii) distinguish errors that reveal a misunderstanding of the central philosophical issues from slips a strong student could make under exam conditions; (iii) tell me how you would allocate credit in the places where the student reaches a correct conclusion by faulty reasoning, since our rubric is unclear on that; and (iv) give an overall judgment on whether the student, taken as a whole, grasps what is actually at issue in the dispute between Thomson and his critics. Grade the philosophy only, not the prose.

THE EXAM QUESTION

(a) A lamp is off at t = 0. Its button is pressed at t = 1/2 minute (the lamp goes on), again at t = 3/4 (off), again at t = 7/8 (on), and so on, each interval half the previous one, with the presses accumulating at t = 1. Thomson argued that this description generates a contradiction concerning the lamp's state at t = 1, and concluded that supertasks are impossible. What is the lamp's state at t = 1, if the question has an answer? Is Thomson's argument sound? Explain your reasoning carefully.

(b) Now modify the lamp: the switch is built so that the first press turns the lamp on permanently — no later press has any effect. The button is pressed at t = 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, and so on. What is the lamp's state at t = 1? Does this case bear on the assessment you gave in part (a)?

(c) An urn is supplied with balls numbered 1, 2, 3, and so on. At the first step (completed at t = 1/2), balls 1 through 10 are placed in the urn and ball 1 is removed. At the second step (completed at t = 3/4), balls 11 through 20 are placed in and ball 2 is removed. At step n, balls 10n − 9 through 10n go in and ball n comes out. Steps occupy half the remaining time and accumulate at t = 1. How many balls are in the urn at t = 1? Justify your answer.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER, VERBATIM

(a) The presses occur at 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, ..., and this sequence converges to 1, so the entire infinite sequence of pressings is completed within one minute — that is what makes the setup a supertask. After each press the lamp's state is on, off, on, off, ..., and this sequence has no limit. Therefore the lamp's state at t = 1 is undefined: it can be neither on nor off. I think Thomson's argument is sound. The lamp must be either on or off at t = 1, since a lamp has no third state. But the description entails neither that it is on nor that it is off. So both alternatives are impossible, which is a contradiction, and the description of the supertask is inconsistent. Hence supertasks cannot be completed even in principle: you can string together infinitely many task-descriptions, but the resulting description cannot be true of anything.

(b) The lamp is on at t = 1. The first press at t = 1/2 turns it on, and the mechanism prevents any later press from changing that, so the last press of the infinite sequence leaves the lamp on. Unlike in (a), the sequence of states here is on, on, on, ..., which is constant and therefore has a limit. This case does not threaten my answer to (a): Thomson's claim concerned the toggling lamp specifically, and changing the switch changes the problem. If anything, the case confirms that indeterminacy at the limit is what you should expect from a supertask, unless the state sequence happens to converge.

(c) The urn is empty at t = 1. Take any ball, say ball 37. It is put in at step 4 and taken out at step 37, so it is not in the urn at t = 1. Since every ball has some finite step at which it is removed, no ball is in the urn at t = 1, and an urn containing no balls is empty. This can be confirmed by taking limits: the set of balls in the urn after step n shrinks toward the empty set as n grows, and the sizes of those sets must therefore shrink to 0 as well. So the limit operation agrees with the ball-by-ball argument, and the answer is 0. The example shows that what is true at the limit of a process need not match what is true at every finite stage, at which the urn keeps growing.
