So last weekend I got into a genuinely long argument with my friend Maya, who's finishing a PhD in history, about the whole "history is written by the victors" thing. Her position was basically that the slogan is lazy: plenty of losers got their stories out (defeated peoples, heretics, failed revolutionaries), and actual historians spend their careers correcting for bias, so the line is something people say to sound cynical. My counter, which I couldn't quite make stick but still believe, is that the slogan is pointing at something real that isn't about lying at all — it's about the *evidence base*. The victors' world determines which languages get written down, which archives get kept in a dry basement vs. burned, whose counts as a "source" at all. So even a scrupulously honest historian, one who never falls for propaganda, is drawing from a corpus that was already sorted by power. Maya said fine, then show me, don't tell me — and I realized that's exactly the problem with teaching this: on paper it just sounds like "bias exists," and students nod along.

Here's the setting: I teach an intro course on historiography ("how we know what happened") to about 18 undergrads, and I have one 75-minute session plus a room with movable desks. I want you to design the activity for that. My half-formed idea, which you should feel free to restructure entirely, is some kind of two-phase thing: phase one, a small group of students role-plays an incident with two factions — something mundane enough not to be triggering, like a dispute between herders and farmers over river access that ends in a raid — where everyone is instructed to be *honest* (nobody lies, that's crucial, I want to rule out propaganda as the explanation before we even start). Then the winning faction, and this is the part I care about, gets structural advantages in how the event can be recorded: some witnesses only get to "transmit orally" (maybe a forced game of telephone across a couple of fake generations), the winners get paper and an "archive," and there's a destruction/preservation round where documents survive or don't based on things like which faction controls the storeroom. Phase two, a different group of students plays historians 200 years later who only see whatever survived, and they have to write a neutral encyclopedia paragraph about the incident. I'd keep a sealed ground-truth record of what actually happened so we can compare at the end.

Specifically I want from you: full rules and a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes with 18 people, a materials list (index cards, envelopes, whatever — no tech that needs setup), a way to make the stakes feel real without money, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the idea — especially the distinction between "the victors wrote the books" (deliberate, detectable, correctable) and "the victors' world decided what could even be a source" (structural, and no one lied to get there). The debrief should also take up Maya's side honestly — where the game actually understates how historians work, e.g. that real ones have source criticism and aren't just reading whatever's in the box. Tell me what will predictably go wrong on a first run (I'm guessing students in the historian phase will try to sneak knowledge from having watched phase one as a class?) and how to prevent it. And be blunt about which part of the real phenomenon a 75-minute classroom version just cannot reproduce — I'd rather concede that up front in the debrief than have a sharp student do it for me. Also if you think my two-phase structure is the wrong shape entirely, say so and design something better, as long as the participants run into the structural point themselves before anyone names it. No lecture up front, and the "victors" line shouldn't appear in my mouth until the debrief at the earliest.
