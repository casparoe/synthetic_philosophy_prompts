Background if it helps: I'm a first-year econ PhD student, and last week I got properly worked over at my first seminar. I presented a little model of housing search that treats landlords and renters as identical agents with no search frictions, and this visiting professor spent basically the whole slot on how "this tells us nothing because none of these people exist." Afterward my advisor shrugged and said don't worry about it, that's just the F-twist -- Friedman's line from his 1953 essay that the realism of assumptions doesn't matter, only the accuracy of predictions. Apparently that essay is the most-cited thing in econ methodology ever and everyone fights about it, and now I'm the one fighting with myself, because when I go case by case my gut is not consistent with Friedman OR with my seminar critic, and I want to know what principle, if any, I'm actually following.

My verdicts, for what they're worth. Frictionless planes and point masses in intro physics: obviously fine, no one complains. Friedman's own example of the expert billiards player who behaves as if he solved the underlying equations: fine too, I actually find it convincing. Standard macro with fully rational expectations: mostly fine, even though nobody is fully rational. A simple SIR model with homogeneous mixing as a first pass at an epidemic: fine. So far so Friedman. But: a labor market model that assumes away discrimination to keep it tractable, which then fits aggregate wage data decently -- I think that one is NOT fine, and not just for political reasons, something methodological. And a traffic model I saw at a workshop that predicts commute times really well while assuming drivers never reroute or learn -- the predictions keep coming in, and I still think there's something wrong with it that Friedman's test wouldn't catch. Meanwhile the DSGE models before 2008 that left out the banking sector entirely: everyone says now that this was a scandal, but the F-twist verdict at the time should have been "predictions are holding, assumptions irrelevant," and I don't like that conclusion either -- I feel like the problem was diagnosable BEFORE the predictions failed, which means predictive success/failure can't be the whole story.

So that's my mess: absurd assumptions are sometimes fine (billiards), sometimes not fine even with good predictions (the discrimination one, the traffic one), and sometimes the problem is visible before predictions say anything (DSE pre-2008). Is there one principle that gets all or most of this, or do I have to give something up? I have a half-formed thought that it's about whether the false assumption is load-bearing for the QUESTION you're asking -- the billiards one isn't asking about the player's psychology -- but I can't get it to work for the traffic model, which really was asking about driver behavior. Please stress-test that idea, or give me better candidates, say what your principle predicts on a case I haven't listed (maybe: a climate model that omits ocean heat transport because it's a pain to code, but compensates by tweaking cloud parameters, and reproduces observed temperatures well), and be honest about which of my verdicts you'd overturn and why. Also, and I mean this literally: at the end tell me how confident you are in each major claim, and point to the single part of your answer you're least sure of, because I'm going to bring this up with my advisor and would rather not repeat a confident-sounding mistake.
