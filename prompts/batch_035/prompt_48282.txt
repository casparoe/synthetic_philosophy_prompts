"Subsidiarity says the center may only act where the member units cannot achieve the objective on their own, and where acting centrally would achieve it better. It was coined in Leo XIII's encyclical Rerum Novarum in 1891 and later elaborated by Pius XI. The core idea is simple: whatever is not assigned to the center belongs to the parts. That is why the Tenth Amendment and Article 30 of the Basic Law are already subsidiarity provisions — both say that unassigned competences stay with the states or Länder, and the Basic Law even enshrines subsidiarity by name in Article 72. So subsidiarity and the residual-powers rule are one idea, and Article 5 TEU is essentially the Continental restatement of it, applied to shared competences.

In Switzerland Article 3 makes the cantons sovereign in everything the federal constitution does not assign to the confederation, and since citizens can force a referendum on federal laws, subsidiarity is enforced directly by the people rather than by courts. In the EU, the test only applies to non-exclusive competences, and the Commission must justify Union-level action in a subsidiarity assessment; national parliaments can issue reasoned opinions under the subsidiarity protocol (the yellow card procedure), and the ECJ strictly polices the principle, striking down acts the member states could have handled alone. Germany is similar in spirit: the Länder administer most federal law themselves, and Article 72 makes federal legislation necessary only where the Länder cannot act adequately on their own.

For institutional design, the lesson is that any federation already contains subsidiarity if it has a residual-powers clause, so adding the principle to a constitution is trivial — the US and Switzerland simply don't need a separate test because their residual rules do the same work. The real difference between the four systems is procedural, not substantive: who enforces the rule (courts, parliaments, or voters)."
