I'm a philosophy grad student who sits in on meetings at an institute that does biosafety policy work, and last week I watched two of the researchers there — I'll call them Dr. Kessler and Dr. Anand — argue about whether the institute should attach its name to a paper describing a method that would make a certain class of experiments meaningfully easier to perform. Kessler opened by invoking the paper by Bostrom, Douglas, and Sandberg on the unilateralist's curse: suppose several labs are each in a position to publish comparable results, each genuinely trying to act for the common good, and each judging with better-than-chance but still fallible accuracy. Then even if most of the individual judgments across the community come out against publishing, the mere fact that publication happens if even one lab errs toward publishing means the probability of publication climbs toward certainty as the number of labs grows. Kessler's point was that the institute's own internal judgment that publishing is, on balance, fine should not settle the matter, because the aggregation structure guarantees that individually reasonable verdicts produce a collectively bad track record. Anand replied that the curse requires a population of agents facing literally the same initiative with roughly symmetric, comparably reliable judgment, whereas the real world offers different papers, different methods, and different review processes — and their team, being closest to the data, has better-informed judgment than a random lab, so they are not merely one more noisy draw from the pool Kessler was imagining.

Kessler answered that this is exactly why the remedy proposed in the literature isn't greater individual care but a principle of conformity: if any other competent, common-good-motivated party dissents, you don't act unilaterally. One dissenting voice among the qualified should be enough to block the initiative, because the errors aren't symmetric — the scientific value of one more publication is largely substitutable, since someone will soon publish similar findings anyway, whereas the harms of a bad publication are not substitutable and may not be undone. At this point Anand got quite worked up and said Kessler had simply flipped the mechanism into its mirror image: if initiatives require the absence of any dissent, then with enough agents, and each agent judging a genuinely good initiative correctly with better-than-even odds, the probability that at least one dissenter exists still approaches one, so good initiatives get blocked nearly always — a conformist's curse, as it were — and Kessler had merely chosen which side's errors to multiply rather than removed the problem. Kessler's last move was to say that the practical solution is institutional vetting: route the decision through a review board. Anand shot back that a review board is just the conformity principle with the group membership chosen by design, so Kessler hasn't escaped the objection, only relocated it. They then adjourned, and I couldn't for the life of me tell who had won which step.

I'd like you to adjudicate this dispute. Please take the role of an expert in research ethics and decision theory, think carefully and lay out your reasoning before committing to verdicts, and then go through the exchange move by move: for each step in the argument, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, and why — including whether Kessler's original characterization of the unilateralist's curse and its conditions is itself faithful. Then tell me who is more right overall, and whether there is anything important that both of them missed — for instance, whether the asymmetries Kessler gestures at (substitutability of benefits, irreversibility of harms, motivation restricted to the common good) can genuinely break the mirror-image symmetry Anand is relying on, or only soften it.
