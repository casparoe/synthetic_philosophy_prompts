I teach philosophy of economics, and for most of my career I have held, and taught, a view I take over from the Samuelson tradition: that "utility," in its respectable sense, has no content beyond a compact summary of choice dispositions. To attribute a utility function to an agent is to say that her choices across menus could have been generated by maximizing that function; there is, on this view, no felt intensity in the head, no "true preference" waiting behind the behavior, and nothing else that the word utility could non-equivocally denote. Introspective hedonic reports, remembered satisfaction, and the like are, on my telling, different concepts traveling under the same word, and any welfare judgment that appeals to them trades on an equivocation. I am no longer confident I have examined this view as hard as I have repeated it, so I want its map of exposure: what would have to be true for it to be wrong.

Please rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed defender would concede it as counting against the view or would dismiss it. At minimum, work through: (i) the results that arguably already exist — preference reversals between choice and pricing, adaptive preferences of the sour-grapes sort, the choices of the addicted — and be explicit about whether my view can even admit these as anomalies, or whether it must define them away, since on my view whatever is consistently chosen just is preferred; (ii) the underdetermination of the utility function by choice data — ordinal data fix the function only up to monotone transformation, so every cardinal claim economists actually make (diminishing marginal utility, curvature under risk) must draw its content from somewhere else, presumably the independence axioms imposed on choice under uncertainty; and I want you to tell me whether importing those restrictions is still revealed-preference analysis or a quiet concession that the view cannot, on its own, deliver the utilities economics uses.

Then there is a wrinkle I have not seen addressed in the literature I know, and I want you to take it seriously rather than tidy it away. Consider a colleague's choices among lunch venues over a semester. If the venues are individuated by cuisine, the same record can be made to look like a stable preference for the convenient and routine; if they are individuated by walking-distance band or by price tier, the very same record can be made to look like a cycling among options, with pairwise revealed preferences that could not jointly come from any utility function. The raw choices are fixed; what changes with the description is which menus count as offering the "same" alternative, and hence which relations the record reveals. Nothing in her psychology adjudicates between the groupings, since she does not think about her lunches under either vocabulary — but the observer's choice of individuation fixes whether she is, revealedly, a creature of habit or a creature of whim. So the question: does my view have a fact of the matter about which individuation is hers? If not, then what an agent prefers is determined not by her choice data alone but by the descriptive conventions an observer brings to them, and the utility function inherits that relativity. Is that a defeater — a demonstration that the concept is representation-relative rather than behavior-fixed — or only a refinement? And if the defender answers that the correct individuation is the one the agent herself would endorse on reflection, has she not smuggled back precisely the psychological facts the view was constructed to do without?

Close with the honest assessment: which of these, if any, the view genuinely risks, and whether it has been insulated into unfalsifiability by its defenders' habit of absorbing every anomaly through adjustments to option descriptions or partitions of the data — and, if it has, whether that is a defect in a view whose proper job is conceptual hygiene rather than empirical prediction.
