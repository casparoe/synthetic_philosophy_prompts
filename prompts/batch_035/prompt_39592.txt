I'm a moral philosopher, and in two weeks I'm giving a talk at an interdisciplinary workshop on culture and moral cognition, hosted at an institute whose permanent members are mostly cultural and developmental psychologists. The audience will be dominated by people who run cross-cultural moral judgment studies for a living: they know the WEIRD critique from the inside, several have done fieldwork on kinship obligations and purity norms, and at least one anthropologist in the room has spent a career arguing that vignette experiments cannot capture thick, role-bound obligations at all. There will also be a handful of philosophers present, including one committed moral realist who will probably turn out to be a demanding and slightly embarrassing ally. My slot is thirty minutes: twenty for the paper and ten for discussion, so the questions will come fast and compressed.

The thesis I'm defending: the cross-cultural variation in folk morality that cultural psychology has documented is, for the most part, variation in the extension of moral concepts — in who gets counted as a moral patient, and which relationships are taken to carry special obligations — rather than variation in the standards people apply to those who count. Once the extension is held fixed, the residual disagreement about standards is shallower than the headline contrasts between WEIRD and non-WEIRD samples suggest. My sketch of the argument runs as follows. First, the biggest cross-cultural effects show up in vignettes where the very same act is embedded in different relationship frames: lying to a stranger versus lying to shield a parent, breaking a promise to a friend versus breaking one to an institution, harming an outgroup member in the course of loyalty to kin. Second, when cases are matched for who is affected and at what stakes, convergence across samples rises markedly. Third, the anti-realist inference my psychologist colleagues often draw — folk morality varies this much across cultures, so there is no fact of the matter — misfires, because the variation is evidence about where communities draw the boundary of the moral circle, not about the truth of the standards applied within it. What I want to do in the talk is concede that the variation is real, deep, and not going away, while resisting the slide from that concession to relativism.

Prepare my Q&A sheet: the six or so objections this particular audience will actually raise — not the ones a philosophy seminar would raise — ordered by how much damage each could do to the paper, each phrased the way a cultural psychologist or field anthropologist would put it in that room. I anticipate some version of: the individualizing foundation looks universal to me only because I am standing inside it; the matched-vignette move is itself a WEIRD experimentalist assumption about what a moral situation is; and the extension/standard distinction is drawn post hoc and is carrying the entire argument. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is soft. Tell me which points I should concede up front rather than defend. Identify the question I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect it will be the anthropologist's, about whether extension and standards can even be pried apart in communities where the role and the obligation are not experienced as separable — and say whether there is something more dangerous that I have not anticipated, including anything my realist ally might say that would force me to claim more than I want to.
