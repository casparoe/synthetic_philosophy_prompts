Suppose David Hume were resurrected today and appointed to a small advisory panel on international trade and monetary policy. Write a memo, as Hume, responding to three features of the modern economy his essays on money, interest, and commerce could not have anticipated: (1) a world of purely fiat currencies, with no specie underlying any major money and central banks expanding the money supply at will; (2) floating exchange rates and persistent current-account imbalances, in which a nation can run trade deficits for decades without any automatic mechanism apparently draining its coin; and (3) the revival of tariffs, export controls, and "friend-shoring" among rich nations — policies that sound mercantilist in spirit, but are now often defended on national-security rather than wealth-accumulation grounds. For each, state what position Hume would most plausibly take, grounding your answer in the arguments and temper of his economic essays — his quantity-theoretic reasoning about money, his view of interest as driven by the demand for loans and the profits of commerce rather than the quantity of specie, his skepticism of hoarding wealth as harmful to a nation's industry, and his insistence that neighboring prosperity enriches rather than threatens a country. Then step back and assess, from our own vantage point, where Hume's framework genuinely illuminates these modern debates and where it breaks down — for instance, whether his price-specie-flow logic survives the substitution of floating paper currencies for metallic money, or whether his free-trade cosmopolitanism can be reconciled with security-motivated trade restrictions of the kind that had loose analogues in his own century's rivalries. Do not treat Hume as a generic free-trader: attend to what is distinctive in his positions, including his acceptance of temporary, transitional pains when money flows in or out of a country.
