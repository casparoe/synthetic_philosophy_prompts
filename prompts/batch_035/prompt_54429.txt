"The case is joint action and not mere coincidence, since after the nod their actions are coordinated toward a shared goal, whereas before the nod they were just two individual pushings that happened to overlap — like two people in different rooms clapping at the same time. On Bratman's view, this becomes joint action once there is a shared intention, and there is one because each intends that the other keep pushing and these intentions interlock — this interlocking is what Bratman means by meshing subplans. Since A is calling the count and B is adjusting to it, their subplans mesh rather than conflict, so Bratman clearly classifies it as joint action.

On Gilbert's view, the nod functions as an express agreement, basically a handshake contract, and that is what generates the mutual obligations characteristic of plural subjects. Since contracts require consent, and both consented through the nod, a plural subject exists from the nod until they part ways, at which point the contract is discharged, like a completed sale. So Gilbert also says joint action, though of a heavyweight kind these two probably never thought of themselves as entering.

On Searle's view, the nod creates a status function: the two of them count as a pushing team in the context of moving-the-car. Status functions come about through collective acceptance, so this is an institutional fact, and Searle therefore classifies it as joint action grounded in an institutional structure.

On part (b), if neither could have pushed the car alone, then it must be joint action, because joint actions are just actions whose goals can only be achieved together, like a duet or moving a piano up a narrow staircase. So the classification changes from debatable to definitely joint.

On part (c), Bratman's account handles the case best, since the counting and timing show meshing subplans and mutual responsiveness without any heavy commitment apparatus. Gilbert's weakness is that it makes a five-second nod into a binding contract, which is absurd for strangers. Searle's weakness is that his apparatus explains the institutional side but not the obligations the participants actually feel toward each other."
