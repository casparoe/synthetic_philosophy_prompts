(c) Describe one model or approach that accommodates the pattern, and one limitation of it."

and here is the student's answer, unedited:

"(a) This is the famous two-color Ellsberg urn, from Ellsberg's 1961 paper. The pattern is ambiguity aversion: people prefer a known probability to an unknown one, even when the unknown one might be better for them. Really I think this is just risk aversion in disguise -- urn B feels riskier, and a concave utility function over wealth explains the choices just as well as anything fancier. The puzzle for SEU is that if the subject preferred A-red to B-red and A-black to B-black, they are acting as if the probability of red from B is less than 1/2 AND the probability of black from B is less than 1/2, which is impossible since those are the only two colors and each ball is one or the other. So the implied probabilities sum to less than one, meaning the subject has violated Bayes' rule. No single probability assignment over the balls in urn B can rationalize both choices at once.

(b) I don't think it shows EU theory is false, because EU theory is about choices under risk and this is a different situation. But it does show subjective EU misdescribes actual choices here, so descriptively it takes a hit. Normatively the subjects are irrational anyway, since these preferences are intransitive -- once you elicit them you can construct a Dutch book and take the subject's money in a one-shot setting. Raiffa made basically this point: if you just randomize between betting red and black on urn B you get a fifty-fifty shot, so refusing urn B is leaving money on the table, so to speak.

(c) The best-known accommodation is Gilboa and Schmeidler's smooth ambiguity model. The agent holds a set of priors over the possible compositions of urn B and evaluates acts using the worst prior in the set, so they treat ambiguity differently from risk. That reproduces the Ellsberg choices exactly. One limitation: these preferences are dynamically consistent, so if the agent gets to update after seeing partway through a draw they stick with their earlier choices -- which sounds fine, but it means the model can't capture any change of heart at all. An alternative explanation of the data is Heath and Tversky's competence hypothesis, that people bet on what they feel knowledgeable about, though I gather that's contested."
