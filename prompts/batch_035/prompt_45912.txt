I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and in a few weeks I'll sit down with a philosopher of mind who defends a fairly uncompromising computationalism: mental states just are computational states, the brain is the mind's hardware, and a machine running the right program would think exactly as we do. My readers are smart but not specialists, and I want the interview to press on the actual soft spots of the position rather than rehearse the usual consciousness hand-wringing. Draft eight to ten interview questions, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. Please cover at least the following ground:

- The triviality worry: if implementing a computation is just a matter of mapping physical states onto formal states, Putnam and Searle both argued that a wall, described the right way, implements any finite-state machine you like. If he answers that genuine computation requires counterfactual-supporting causal structure — the system must not only do what it does but would have done otherwise had the input differed — press him on what fixes that counterfactual structure without a designer's or observer's interpretation already being smuggled in.
- The Chinese Room: he will almost certainly give the systems reply. The follow-up should ask whether that reply is an empirical claim, about organization being sufficient for understanding, that could in principle turn out to be false, or a definitional one that no experiment could ever refute — and why readers should care which it is.
- Analog messiness: neurons are continuous, noisy, and graded rather than cleanly switching between 0s and 1s. If he concedes that the brain may compute over continuous magnitudes, ask whether the notion of computation, stretched that far, still does the work his thesis needs, or whether it has become true by stipulation.
- Miscomputation: an adding machine can add wrongly — its being an adder at all involves a standard of correctness. Ask what plays that role for neural activity, which just seems to happen. If he invokes biological function or evolutionary teleology, ask whether that makes the notion of the brain as a computer parasitic on facts about natural history rather than information processing, and whether an artifact like his laptop could then be wrong in a different sense than a brain can.
- Emulation versus simulation: a simulated hurricane is dry and wets nothing. Ask what grounds the claim that a perfect emulation of his brain would not merely model his thoughts but have them. If he answers that the emulation implements the same causal organization, ask how much of the fine-grained analog dynamics can be discretized away before the relevant organization is lost — and who decides which grain is the relevant one.

Please keep the wording accessible enough for a printed Q&A, and flag any place where my framing of the literature is off before I embarrass the magazine.
