I run a small regional film festival — one week a year, one part-time staffer, and about 400 submissions. I can personally watch maybe 40 of them, and alot of my job is acting on scorecards filled out by a rotating pool of ~15 volunteer screeners, most of whom have watched a ton of movies and a few of whom genuinely have taste. I have to pick roughly 60 films for slots, and I have about six weeks every spring to do it. Getting it wrong isn't ruinous, but we've built real trust with our audience and a couple bad years in a row would burn it.

The thing I keep bumping into is what the philosophy people apparently call the problem of aesthetic testimony. From what I can tell the answer is surprisingly "not really, but it's complicated" — something about how an aesthetic judgment is supposed to be grounded in the properties of the object itself rather than borrowed from someone else's reaction, so there's an asymmetry between trusting a screener that a file has bad subtitles and trusting them that the film is "quietly devastating." Meanwhile I obviously cannot watch 400 films, so in practice my whole festival is built on secondhand aesthetic verdicts.

Write me a procedure my staff and I can actually run on each submission. I want the steps in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a pass, and where it throws up its hands and hands the decision to me because the question is one of values, not facts. It should be honest about which steps are really judgment calls wearing a lab coat. Then run it on three real-ish cases from last year:

1. A slow, three-hour documentary that one screener called "transformative" and another turned off after twenty minutes. I programmed it anyway and stood in the back while half the audience walked out.
2. A short I personally loved the one time I saw it, but all four screeners who rated it gave it middling scores — I'm the only person involved who liked it.
3. A film where every screener agreed it was excellent, I have never seen a single frame of it, and the question is whether I can put a line in the printed program calling it a masterpiece.

And tell me straight what the procedure can't do — in particular whether it can tell me who counts as a screener with taste in the first place, because I suspect that's what the whole thing quietly rests on.

One distinction I half-understand and want built in explicitly: testimony about facts (the film contains strobing, it's 82 minutes, it's the director's third feature) seems fine to just believe, while testimony about merit is the suspicious part. If the procedure needs to lean on that line, draw it clearly, because I suspect some of our scorecard questions are on the wrong side of it.
