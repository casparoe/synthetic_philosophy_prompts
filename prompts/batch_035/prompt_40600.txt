A colleague in my economics program claims that Sen's liberal paradox, taken seriously, shows that no society can be rational, and he has an argument I cannot get out of, though its conclusion appears to prove far too much. It runs as follows. (1) A society's institutions -- markets, elections, committees, informal norms -- can all be viewed as procedures that take individuals' preferences over social states as input and deliver a collective choice among those states, and a rational procedure must deliver a choice that does not cycle. (2) The Pareto principle: whenever everyone strictly prefers one social state to another, the collective must not rank the second above the first; rejecting this would allow society to choose what everyone rejects, over something everyone wants. (3) Minimal liberalism: for each person there is at least one pair of social states that differ only in some purely personal matter of hers -- what she reads, wears, or worships -- over which her own preference settles the collective ranking; rejecting this would leave no one sovereign even over her own reading, so it looks like the bare minimum of freedom. (4) Amartya Sen proved in 1970 that no procedure can satisfy (2) and (3) together on all possible preference profiles. His illustration: one copy of a scandalous novel and two people, with three options -- the prude reads it, the lewd reader reads it, or nobody does. The prude most wants nobody to read it, but would rather read it himself than let the lewd reader have it; the lewd reader most wants the prude to read it, but would rather that than read it himself. Then Pareto forces prude-reads above lewd-reads, since both prefer the prude as the reader; the prude's decisiveness over his own reading forces nobody-reads above prude-reads; and the lewd reader's decisiveness over his own reading forces lewd-reads above nobody-reads. A cycle. (5) So any institution that aggregates preferences in this way is irrational whenever people harbor preferences about each other's business, and since people always harbor preferences about each other's business, every institution in every society is irrational. (6) Irrationality in a person is a defect, so irrationality in a society is a defect too; therefore every society is defective in the very structure of its collective decision-making, and the ideal of a society combining personal sovereignty with respect for unanimous preference is incoherent.

I do not believe the conclusion: societies manage to decide most things rationally most of the time, and I refuse to accept that this was always impossible. But each step looks defensible on its own. Step (2) seems undeniable, step (3) seems like the least any free society owes its members, and step (4) is, as far as I can tell, a theorem, so it is not open to dispute. That leaves me suspicious of (1), (5), or (6), and I have a nagging sense that the mischief lies in (5) or in how (1) is set up, though I cannot articulate it. My colleague pushes the reductio further: he says that if I insist on keeping both (2) and (3), I owe him the name of an actual society whose institutions are actually rational, or a confession that the theorem bites everywhere. I would most like to keep (2) and (3). So tell me which step to reject, name precisely where the argument slides and why the slide looked innocent, and then tell me honestly what rejecting that step costs -- what I give up, and what my colleague could still fairly demand of me.
