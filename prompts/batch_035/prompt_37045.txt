I teach philosophy at a midwestern state school, and through a colleague's wife who works for the courts I got roped into being the token humanist on our state's sentencing guidelines advisory commission -- the body that more or less writes the worksheet trial judges use to land on a range. The room is mostly prosecutors and retired judges, but two of the commissioners actually read the little debate book Dennett and Caruso did together a few years back, took opposite sides, and have turned it into a running fight ever since. One of them insists any guideline that drops just-deserts reasoning is dishonest; the other says desert-talk is metaphysics we can't cash and keeps pointing at Caruso's public-health-quarantine analogy, and everyone else glazes over. What we need isn't a resolution of that fight -- I'm not naive -- it's a procedure the commission can actually run on a proposed guideline change, that tells us:

(a) whether the change is justified purely on desert grounds, purely on forward-looking grounds (deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation), or only as some mix, and

(b) where the question stops being answerable by any worksheet and has to get handed off to a judge or legislator as a straight value call.

Constraints, because I know how this goes: it has to fit on roughly a page, be usable by lawyers who've read zero free-will literature, run inside the 20 minutes we give each agenda item, and be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing procedure costumes. It also can't beg the question against either side, since I need it to pass a room that includes people who think Dennett basically won and people who think Caruso did -- so where their disagreement genuinely matters (e.g., whether retributive justification can ever be the sole basis for an enhancement), the procedure should say that out loud rather than papering over it.

Then run it on three proposed changes we've actually got on the docket this year:

1. an enhancement for offenders convicted a second time within the same offense category (the recidivist enhancement),
2. a cap that would bar sentences beyond a certain number of years when actuarial risk tools say the offender has effectively aged out of risk, and
3. restoring discretion to give probation for certain low-level offenses where the current grid mandates prison time.

And give me the output as structured data, please:

Table 1 -- the procedure itself: columns for step number; the question to ask; what counts as a sufficient answer; what to do if the answer is no; and whether that step is answerable from facts alone or requires a value judgment, and if a value judgment, who owns it (commissioner, judge, or legislature).

Table 2 -- the three worked cases: columns for the case; the desert-based reading; the forward-looking reading; the procedure's verdict (desert-only, forward-looking-only, mixed, or handoff); and which side of the Dennett/Caruso fight the verdict will annoy and why.

One more thing. I'm on record at these meetings saying the recidivist enhancement is fine. If your procedure turns out to say the desert story behind it is weaker than I've been assuming, don't sand that down to spare me -- flag it. I'd rather find out from you than from one of the Caruso people mid-meeting.

Keep it in American English. Casual is fine, we're all friends here, and don't bother with citations -- I know the literature, what I need is the worksheet.
