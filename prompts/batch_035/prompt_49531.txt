At a dinner I hosted last month, a guest brought an oversalted casserole. Michi, a colleague who grew up in Tokyo, praised it warmly; Daan, who is Dutch, asked to be told honestly how it had gone over and, when the guest asked him the same question at the door, told her the salt had gotten away from her. After she left, Daan told Michi her compliment had been a small wrong done to the guest. The argument they then had ran to seven moves, and they have drafted me as referee, which I am not equipped to be. The moves, as near as I can reconstruct them:

(1) Daan: a white lie is wrong not because of an absolute rule against falsehood but because of what it does. It substitutes the liar's judgment for the hearer's own judgment about what the hearer is better off knowing. By praising the casserole, Michi decided on the guest's behalf that the guest could not handle the truth and would be worse off for it. That is a small exercise of power dressed up as kindness; directness is simply respect for a person's ability to take the truth and decide what to do with it.

(2) Michi: that assumes politeness is attempted deception. But a functioning politeness system is not a series of concealed falsehoods; it is a public code. Everyone present knows the compliment is not a literal report on the food but an acknowledgement of the effort, and knows this in advance; the distinction between one's real feelings and the face one presents in public is itself common knowledge in such a culture, which is exactly why presenting the face is not deception. Nothing was taken from the guest that was not being openly given.

(3) Daan: then concede that this kind of honesty is just one convention among others and drop the moral vocabulary. If Michi's compliment means "I see your effort" rather than "this is delicious," then her system and his differ the way driving on the left differs from driving on the right, and she cannot call his way rude any more than he can call hers dishonest.

(4) Michi: conventions are not morally on a par merely for being conventions; they can be compared by their costs and benefits. Directness dumps the entire cost of honesty on the hearer, who must absorb bad news raw and do the work of not showing hurt; politeness has the speaker do the work of cushioning. That is a fairer division of a burden that directness assigns wholesale to the weaker party.

(5) Daan: the cushioning is done by a code, and codes must be learned. The politeness system charges its fee to whoever has not yet learned it — visitors, new arrivals, second-language speakers, people who do not read social cues. For them the compliment is not a recognized ritual but a lie they believed. A system of kindness that misleads precisely the people least equipped to see through it has a moral cost Michi has not faced; directness at least bills everyone the same flat rate.

(6) Michi: Daan has quietly shifted from attacking falsehood to attacking concealment, and these are different. Much of what he calls Japanese indirectness involves no false statement at all: saying a proposal would be difficult, declining to answer, changing the subject. If his objection is to falsehood, indirectness is not his target; if his objection is to withholding, then note that direct cultures also withhold — about money, illness, personal questions — they simply post their walls in less visible places. There is no convention-free way of speaking, and his flat rate is an illusion.

(7) Daan: there is a real difference between conventions governing when one speaks at all and conventions governing what a hearer walks away believing. A taboo tells you where the walls are; a soft "difficult" leaves the hearer believing approval was nearly granted. Concealment that predictably produces a false overall picture is lying in effect, whatever the mechanism — and a signal built to be decoded one way by insiders and another way by everyone else is not innocent withholding but disclosure aimed at some audiences and withheld from others.

Please go through the seven moves in order, say who has the better of each point and why, then say who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing.
