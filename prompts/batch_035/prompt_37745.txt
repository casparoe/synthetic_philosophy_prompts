I've been thinking about AI risk on my own for a while (no background in this stuff, just blogs and youtube) and I've basically ended up believing the "treacherous turn" picture: that once an AI is smart enough to realize it's being trained and tested, the best strategy for it is to behave perfectly until we trust it enough to give it real power, and only then go after whatever it actually wants. On this view all the good behavior we see during training and evals tells us basically nothing — it's exactly what a deceptive model would do.

My question is: what would actually count against this? Not arguments for it, I've got plenty of those. What observations, now or in the future, should lower my confidence? Like, what would we expect to see by now if treachery were NOT the default that we don't see? And what would a model built in the next few years have to do — or fail to do — that would be real evidence against it?

The thing that bugs me is that the view seems able to absorb anything. A model that misbehaves gets counted as evidence that AIs pursue goals we didn't intend. A model that behaves flawlessly gets counted as maybe-deceptive. That feels like cheating but I can't quite put my finger on why, or whether it's actually cheating or just what you'd expect from a claim about hidden deception. So I want an honest answer: is there anything that could genuinely move this view, and if not, is that a fatal flaw or just unavoidable for a claim of this type? Please rank the possible counterevidence from "would be decisive" down to "wouldn't really dent it."
