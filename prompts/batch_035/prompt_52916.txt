So, background: I write backend code for a living, no formal stats or philosophy training, just blogs and a couple of online courses. But the replication crisis stuff keeps showing up in my feeds and over the years I've collected a bunch of gut calls about which studies to believe, and I genuinely can't tell if there's one rule behind them or if I'm just making it up as I go. Here are the calls, roughly in the order I made them:

1. When that huge multi-lab preregistered replication of ego depletion came back basically null, I wrote ego depletion off entirely.
2. When some grad student's n=20 replication of a different finding came back null, I didn't update at all. Underpowered, no power, whatever, ignore it.
3. A finding that's been replicated three times, but all three times by the original lab and never anywhere else — I don't buy it. Something about that smells, even though on paper it's 3-for-3.
4. p = 0.03, but I happen to know the team ran five different outcome measures and reported the one that worked. Don't buy it — the p-value is basically fake at that point.
5. p = 0.06, but the analysis was preregistered down to the exclusion criteria, and the theory behind it is solid. I actually believe this one MORE than #4. Less significant on paper, more believable to me.
6. Two findings with identical statistics, one in a field with a decent replication track record and one in a field where everything falls apart when you rerun it. I believe the first and not the second, same numbers and all.
7. Same finding as #6's second one, but now somebody proposes a plausible mechanism for why it should work, and I catch myself believing it more — with zero new data collected.

The case I keep going back and forth on: a famous finding fails a big replication, but then someone points out the replication used a different population (older subjects, different country, whatever). Do I now believe the original again? Fully, partially, not at all?

Is there a single principle that gets all seven, or close to it? To me #1–#5 feel like they're about error control — was the analysis fixed before anyone looked at the data, how much noise is in the test — which is the frequentist side of the argument. But #6 and #7 feel like priors sneaking in the back door, which is straight-up Bayesian. So which am I? A Bayesian who cares about preregistration for evidential reasons, a frequentist with vibes, or is there some hybrid — and if so, state the actual hybrid rule, don't just say "both matter." Where does whatever principle you land on force me to drop one of my calls, and which one would you overturn first? And what does it say about the different-population case I keep flip-flopping on?

Since I'm well out of my depth here: as you go, say how confident you are in each claim, and single out the one point you're least sure of. I'd rather know where the floor is than get a confident-sounding answer.
