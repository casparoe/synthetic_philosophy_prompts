The exam question read as follows:

"For Kant, what distinguishes actions that have moral worth from actions that lack it? In your answer, explain the work done by each of the following cases from the Groundwork: the prudent shopkeeper who deals honestly from self-interest; the naturally compassionate person who helps because he enjoys helping; and the person whose sympathy has been extinguished by grief yet who helps nonetheless. Distinguish carefully between acting from duty and merely acting in accordance with duty, and say whether moral worth, on Kant's view, requires that inclination be absent or opposed. Finally, explain how Nomy Arpaly's account of moral worth — on which moral worth lies not in acting from duty specifically, but in acting for the reasons that make the action right, a responsiveness that need not be articulate or endorsed by the agent herself — departs from Kant's, and how it handles the case of Huckleberry Finn, who helps Jim escape while sincerely believing, by his own lights, that he is doing wrong."

The student's answer reads as follows:

"For Kant, whether an action has moral worth is determined not by what the action is or what it brings about, but by the maxim from which it is done. Two actions can be alike in every outward respect and differ entirely in moral worth. The distinction doing the work here is between acting from duty and merely acting in accordance with duty. The shopkeeper is honest because honesty is good for business: his honesty conforms to duty but is done from self-interest, so it has no moral worth. The naturally compassionate person who helps others because he takes pleasure in helping is in the same position: his actions conform to duty, but because they flow from inclination rather than from respect for the law, they lack moral worth — indeed Kant condemns them as selfish. The case that reveals where moral worth lies is the grieving man whose sympathy has been extinguished yet who helps anyway: this action has moral worth because it is done from duty. The lesson of these examples is that for Kant moral worth requires the absence or opposition of inclination. The action with moral worth is the one performed against inclination, since only where inclination points away from duty can we tell that the agent acts from duty and not from something else. Where inclination and duty happen to coincide, the action's true motive is unknowable, and such an action is best treated as lacking moral worth.

Arpaly shares Kant's thought that moral worth depends on why the agent acts rather than on consequences, but she widens the class of worthy motives beyond duty. On her account, an action has moral worth when the agent acts for the reasons that make the action right, whatever those reasons are; the worthy agent is one whose conduct is responsive to right-making reasons. This might seem to make moral worth depend on the agent's moral opinions, and in a way it does: for the agent to be acting for the right reasons, she must at minimum judge that her action is right. Someone who does the right thing while believing it is wrong is not acting for the reasons that make it right, since she does not take those reasons to apply. That is why Huckleberry Finn, who helps Jim while convinced he is doing wrong, has no moral worth on Arpaly's account: his behavior may be lucky or charming, but he is not responding to right-making reasons, because he does not think there are any. The contrast with Kant is thus narrower than it first appears. Kant locates worth in the motive of duty and Arpaly in the judgment of rightness, but both agree that moral worth travels with the agent's own conception of her obligations."
