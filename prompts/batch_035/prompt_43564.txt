Longtime lurker of LessWrong and a few adjacent places, so naturally I've done my time with the p-zombie discourse — I've read the anti-zombie principle stuff, argued with friends about whether Mary learns anything, the whole thing. But I keep coming back to the conceivability argument itself and feeling like I've never actually settled it properly, mostly because everyone online treats it as either obviously fatal or obviously confused depending on their tribal affiliation, and neither side seems to steelman the other. So I'd like you to write an essay on whether the conceivability argument for property dualism actually works — the one that runs: zombies are ideally conceivable, whatever is ideally conceivable is metaphysically possible, so physicalism is false. Take both horns seriously: the responses about negative conceivability vs. positive conceivability, whether conceiving of a zombie world requires actually surveying the entire physical inventory of a world (which we obviously can't do for any remotely realistic physics), Kripke's point about why the usual a posteriori identity move that works for water/H2O doesn't obviously transfer to pain and brain states, and the phenomenal concept strategy reply.

One thing I specifically want you to engage with, because I almost never see it handled well: the self-defeat worry. If the argument succeeds, qualia come out epiphenomenal — they float free of the physical causal story — but then our judgments *about* qualia (including the very judgment "I can conceive of my zombie twin") are, on the zombie twin's side, produced entirely by the physical machinery. Which seems to threaten the reliability of the introspective data the whole argument was built on. Does the conceivability arguer have a decent answer to this, or does it quietly gut the argument? And there's also the "proves too much" worry — if conceivability is a good guide to possibility here, why isn't it a good guide elsewhere, where it sometimes fails (Kripke's own rigid-designation examples, imaginary science that turned out to misdescribe things we now think are necessary)? Please actually reason through whether the disanalogy the dualist wants holds up, rather than just asserting it.

Reach an actual verdict — don't do the classic "the debate continues" shrug, I get enough of that from the SEP. It's fine to land on something conditional (e.g. "the argument works only if positive conceivability survives the phenomenal concept strategy" or even "it backfires and supports physicalism") as long as you defend it properly. And crucially for me: end with a section on what would change your mind — name the specific argument or consideration that, if it failed or succeeded, would flip your verdict, and point to the step in your own reasoning you think is most likely to be the weak link. I've found that's the only way I trust these essays. Also feel free to note where you're relying on a contested interpretation of Kripke or Chalmers rather than the settled reading — I'd rather know than have it smuggled in. cheers!
