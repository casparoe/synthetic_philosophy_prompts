Just spent way too long arguing with a friend about the lottery paradox vs the preface paradox and we basically ended where we started, so let me put it to you. She claims the preface paradox is just the lottery paradox wearing a suit: in both you have a bunch of individually justified beliefs, each with a small chance of being false, and the odds that at least one of them is false are so high that rationality seems to force you into believing each claim while also believing not all of them are true. I kept insisting there's a real asymetry underneath: in the lottery you know the objective odds and you know exactly one ticket will win, it's structural, while the author's belief that somewhere in the book there's an error comes from inductive knowledge of their own fallibility, not from the claims themselves. Then she hit me with a case I couldn't answer: a textbook author who, after decades of publishing, knows that roughly one in a thousand claims she has ever published turned out to be wrong, and the new book has exactly a thousand claims. Now the error probability is quasi-objective like the lottery, but it's self-directed fallibility like the preface. Is that hybrid a genuinely third kind of case, or does it show the lottery/preface distinction isn't doing any real work? Please actually compare the two paradoxes and tell me what distinguishes them, if anything, and where the hybrid falls. I won't accept "they're structurally the same" without saying what work the difference in error source does or doesn't do.
