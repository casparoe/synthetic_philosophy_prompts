I'd like a requirements list for theories of self-deception, and I'd like the list before any survey of the actual positions, because I suspect my reactions to the positions are being driven by criteria I haven't made explicit.

Fix the purpose: a theory good enough to tell us what is going on in the ordinary, everyday cases — the spouse who reads every text message with suspicious precision except one, the failing manager who genuinely believes the turnaround plan is working, the person who "doesn't notice" the lump — without positing mental operations that are either incoherent on their face or so baroque that nobody could perform them while buying groceries. Not a theory for exotic clinical cases; a theory for the ordinary ones.

What I want from you is the desiderata: the cases an adequate theory must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the facts about belief and agency it cannot contradict, with the motivating consideration behind each. The ones I can think of offhand, so you can see the genre rather than to preempt you: it must distinguish self-deception from mere wishful thinking (the wishful thinker hopes p; the self-deceiver seems to have managed something like belief in p), from ordinary self-serving bias of the kind cognitive psychology documents on every page, and from simple ignorance; it must say what makes the phenomenon deception at all, given that there is no second party, or else bite the bullet that the name is a misnomer; it must accommodate the fact that we cannot believe at will, so it cannot treat self-deception as literally deciding what to believe; and it owes us something about the phenomenology of near-awareness — the way the self-deceived person seems to know what they are doing in some attenuated sense, flinching away from exactly the thoughts that would settle the matter.

For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp dressed up as a demand, and make the case either way. Then do the two things I actually care about most. First, tell me whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — my worry is that the following three cannot all hold: the state must be belief rather than some weaker attitude; it must be arrived at or maintained in a way that earns the name deception, which seems to require something in the direction of intent or at least motivated management of the evidence; and the person must not simultaneously believe the negation of what they self-deceptively believe, which the traditional paradoxical reading demands but which the partitioning views deny by splitting the mind and the deflationary views deny by dropping the deception. If that triangle is genuinely unsatisfiable, say which corner you would give up and what it costs. Second, identify which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and defend that choice.

Finally, one framing question: should "the phenomenon must be possible" appear on the list as a desideratum at all, or is the possibility question just the joint-satisfiability question in disguise? Flag your view on that in a line or two.

Please keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring existing contenders — a paragraph at the end saying roughly how the divided-mind approaches and the motivationally biased belief approaches fare against your list is enough.
