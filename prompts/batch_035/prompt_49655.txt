Our departmental reading group has spent the past two years on the dispute between higher-order and first-order theories of phenomenal consciousness, and the discussion has frozen in place. I am no longer interested in restatements of the positions; I want a diagnosis of the impasse itself.

The situation as I understand it: on the higher-order side, Rosenthal holds that a first-order perceptual state is phenomenally conscious only when there is a suitable, occurrent higher-order thought representing that state and its content, the higher-order thought normally remaining unconscious itself; Lycan's variant swaps the thought for an inner sense, a quasi-perceptual monitoring device. On the first-order side, Tye and the later Carruthers hold that phenomenal character is exhausted by first-order representational content, provided that content is suitably poised for use in reasoning, reporting, and the control of action; higher-order states, when they occur, are at most causally responsible for reportability, never constitutive of feel. The frustrating part is that both camps accept essentially the same evidence: blindsight, masked priming, subliminal perception, the cases of animals and infants. Whenever the two theories seem to diverge, the divergence is generated by an assumption the other side rejects. Thus the higher-order theorist's dilemma in the subliminal case: either the very same first-order state is tokened as in ordinary awareness, in which case first-order content alone cannot be sufficient for consciousness and the first-order theory collapses; or the higher-order state alters which first-order state exists, and then the two theories are no longer disagreeing about one thing. The first-order camp responds that the higher-order theorist has smuggled an epistemic condition — something must be noticed for there to be anything it is like — into a constitutive question about feel.

My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the residual disagreement is not about consciousness at all but about the individuation of mental states: whether the aware and the unaware case involve one first-order token with and without a higher-order escort, or whether the presence of the escort changes which state exists in the first place. If that is right, much of the dispute is verbal — a disagreement about counting states rather than about phenomenal character — which would explain why the predictions never come apart. But I am not confident, partly because Rosenthal insists the dispute is constitutive and substantive, and partly because Carruthers at one point declared it largely verbal, changed sides, and yet the rest of the field did not follow him, which suggests something else is holding the debate open.

So I would like three things from you. First, a diagnosis grounded in what each side actually says: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about whether we have one state or two, or two research programmes answering different questions — one about feel, one about access and reportability — under a single heading? Second, an assessment of my individuation hypothesis: does it capture the crux, or does it miss what is genuinely at stake? Third, an honest account of what, if anything, would count as progress — a new kind of case, a conceptual distinction, an empirical result, or a reframing of the question — and, if nothing would, why the impasse should be tolerated rather than treated as a defect in the question. Please argue for your diagnosis; a survey of the theories will not help me.
