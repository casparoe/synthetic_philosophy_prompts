I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, working on a dissertation chapter about contractualism and aggregation. Before I send a section to my supervisor, I want a consistency audit of the package of views I have committed myself to on paper. The setting is Scanlonian contractualism about what we owe to each other: an act is wrong if and only if it would be disallowed by some principle for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject, where the grounds for rejection are always personal — they must be intelligible as an objection made on behalf of some particular individual against the burden a principle would impose on her.

Within that setting, here is what I have written down as my commitments:

1. The individualist restriction on contractualist reasoning: in deciding whether a principle could be reasonably rejected, each person's possible objections to it are assessed on their own, one at a time; objections are never summed across persons. In particular, no number of people's headaches, however severe or numerous, adds up to a claim equivalent to one person's death, so it is wrong to kill one person in order to relieve even millions of severe headaches. I take this verdict to be firm.

2. Numbers nonetheless count in rescue cases, and not merely when the stakes happen to be identical. Forced to choose between saving a hundred people from losing a leg each and saving two people from death, I am at least as confident that you must save the hundred as I am about the individualist restriction itself. Where per-person stakes are not wildly disproportionate, group size tips the balance.

3. Taurek was wrong that the numbers should not count, and I want to hold that this is shown from within contractualism rather than from outside it: each member of the larger group, on their own, has a reasonable rejection available of any principle that lets them perish when they could have been saved at no cost to anyone with a stronger claim. I can't fully articulate why this doesn't collapse into the summing I forbid in (1), which is part of why I'm asking.

4. In public policy I accept ex ante, probabilistic reasoning: a safety regulation that imposes a one-in-two-million annual risk of death on each of ten million people in order to prevent thirty certain deaths is not merely permissible but required, and no one could reasonably reject the terms of social cooperation it embodies, provided the risks are fairly distributed and everyone benefits over time. Identified-victim intuitions should not be allowed to block this kind of regulation.

5. In the special case where the stakes for each person are exactly equal — five people each facing the loss of a hand versus one person facing the loss of a hand — you must save the five. I am confident contractualism can deliver this without any covert summing, though I admit I can't reconstruct the argument well enough to see whether it generalizes beyond the equal-stakes case.
