I'm a philosophy-and-economics double major currently writing a term essay on the foundations of utilitarian aggregation, and I've hit a historical puzzle I can't resolve on my own. In our welfare economics seminar we spent two weeks on Lionel Robbins's An Essay on the Nature and Significance of Economic Science (1932), where he argues that interpersonal comparisons of utility have no scientific standing: there is no observation or experiment that could confirm or disconfirm a claim about how much satisfaction one person derives relative to another, so a self-resforming science of choice has to confine itself to ordinal rankings revealed through behavior. That much I took to be the death blow that ended cardinal utility talk in economics for a generation. But then in my ethics course we read John Harsanyi's 1955 paper on cardinal welfare, which appears to derive average utilitarianism from individual expected-utility maximization behind an equiprobable veil of ignorance plus a Pareto-style condition. What I can't figure out is how the theorem can even be stated if Robbins was right: Harsanyi's conclusion involves adding and averaging utilities across different people, which looks like it presupposes exactly the kind of cross-personal comparison Robbins showed to be illegitimate. So my question is: did Harsanyi simply refute Robbins, or did he quietly assume what he needed somewhere in the machinery — for instance, in the move to expected utility itself, or in the normalization of each person's utility scale? My TA said something about critics arguing the comparability is smuggled in through the equiprobability model or through treating the impartial observer's choice as an ordinary risky choice, and also mentioned "extended preferences" as a way of making cross-person judgments true-or-false without comparing inner intensities, but I lost the thread and would like it untangled before I commit to a thesis.

There's also a second, more philosophical knot underneath this that I suspect is doing half the damage. It seems to me that "unverifiable" and "meaningless" come apart: I can see how a claim comparing your suffering to mine might be impossible to test and still be the kind of thing that is true or false. If that's right, then perhaps Robbins's conclusion was never as strong as the received story has it, and the standard narrative — Robbins killed interpersonal comparisons, Harsanyi resurrected them — papers over two different senses of "impossible" talking past each other. How much of this standoff is a genuine technical dispute and how much is a clash between a methodological injunction about what economists qua scientists may assert and a metaphysical thesis about what could in principle be true?

Please explain where exactly my framing goes wrong, if it does, and be explicit about whether Harsanyi's theorem sidesteps Robbins's challenge, answers it, or fails to engage it. Also flag how confident you are in the historical attributions (especially anything you say about what Robbins himself actually claimed, as opposed to what later textbook summaries say he claimed), and identify the single point in your answer you are least sure about, since I would rather not build my essay on an unsteady reconstruction.
