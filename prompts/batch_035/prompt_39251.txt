"Philosophers who study deception distinguish lying from misleading as follows: a lie is any deliberate attempt to get someone to believe something false, while misleading someone means steering them toward a false belief without asserting anything untrue yourself. But Kant thought there was an escape hatch: he allowed that you could give the murderer a literally true answer phrased so as to send him in the wrong direction, so Kant himself treated the distinction as morally significant. Grice later gave the distinction a linguistic foundation: when we speak, we communicate far more than we say, and a misleading statement is true but carries a false implicature; since implicatures are not asserted, the speaker is not responsible for them — the hearer draws the false conclusion himself. The moral upshot is that misleading shifts responsibility for the false belief onto the hearer, which is why philosophers broadly agree that misleading is the lesser evil whenever you cannot avoid deceiving someone. Critics reply that manipulating implicatures is just as manipulative as lying outright, but the experimental evidence has settled this: study after study finds that people consistently rate liars as more blameworthy than misleaders, so the moral distinction stands."
