I'm a high school senior who has been reading philosophy of probability on my own, and I've hit something I can't untangle. Van Fraassen's Reflection principle says, roughly, that your current credence in a claim should defer to what you expect your future self to believe: if you know that tomorrow you will be confident of X, then learning just that fact should move you toward X today, since your future opinion is normally the product of more information and better thinking. But in my government class we just covered advance directives, and they seem to institutionalize the exact opposite. A psychiatric advance directive lets someone, while judged competent, fix in advance which treatments may be given to them during a later crisis in which they will likely be judged incompetent and will refuse those treatments; the same structure shows up in general medical directives that bind a patient who later lacks capacity, and in self-binding arrangements like an alcoholic voluntarily taking a drug that makes drinking sickening — Ulysses ordering his crew to ignore his future commands. What view of the person, and of rationality over time, does this whole legal practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that the future self's beliefs will be produced by illness or manipulation rather than by evidence, so that the usual direction of deference is reversed — and could that be squared with Reflection itself instead of against it? Or does it require denying that the future patient's wishes deserve priority at all, and if so, what happens to the standard justification for that denial (competence vs. personal identity vs. knowledge of one's own enduring values)? I'd like a reconstruction of the implicit theory, a sense of which rival views the practice strictly rules out as opposed to merely fits badly with, whether the practice is consistent with the same medical system's insistence that current, on-the-spot consent usually trumps everything, and what would actually have to change in the law if we tried to redesign it to respect Reflection rather than reverse it.
