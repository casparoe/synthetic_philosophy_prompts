I'm on the heritage commission of a mid-sized town, and we've got a genuinely nasty case coming to a vote. For about ninety years the town has celebrated the "Founders' Night Rescue": local legend says that in the winter of 1894, a group of townsfolk broke into the poorhouse at night and carried out fourteen children before the building collapsed in a storm. There's a monument in the square, a reenactment every year, and it's basically the town's identity — the high school teams are called the Rescuers. Last year a graduate student working in the county archives found that the poorhouse did collapse, and children did die, but the rescue story as told doesn't hold up: the collapse happened in daylight, rescue efforts were led by the county constable and paid fire brigade, and most of the children who died were buried in an unmarked pit that the town never commemorated at all. The fourteen "rescued" children seem to have mostly been sent to an indentured labour arrangement that the same founding families profited from. The student's paper is solid, and our own commission's review of the documents confirmed the basics.

Now the commission is split. One faction wants to replace the monument's inscription with a corrected account and add the unmarked burial site to the heritage trail. The other faction says the legend is the community's memory, that the descendants of the rescue families (still prominent here) will be humiliated, and that the town needs its story more than it needs archival accuracy — some have even suggested keeping the inscription but adding a small interpretive panel nearby with the "complicated history," in smaller letters, off to the side. A third commissioner says both sides are wrong because the real issue is the unmarked graves, and we shouldn't touch the monument at all until we've done ground-penetrating radar at the pit site and given the dead children a proper memorial, whatever the plaque says.

Please draft the written recommendation I'll circulate before the vote: it should recommend a concrete course of action with a sequence and rough timeline, engage the strongest points of each faction seriously (including the "the town needs its story" one — don't just dismiss it as sentimentality), and say something about what, if anything, the town owes the descendants of the indentured children, some of whom still live here. British English please — I'm circulating this to a commission that includes two academics and they're picky about that sort of thing.
