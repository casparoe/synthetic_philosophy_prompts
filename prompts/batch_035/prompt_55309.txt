I write novels for a living, and for the past seven years I have also been one of seven volunteer editors at a small literary quarterly — print circulation around four thousand, monthly issues, no institutional backing and no lawyers. Our rule for accepting work was built deliberately: every published piece needs a champion among the editors, and a champion can green-light it alone, without a vote. The magazine's identity depends on this; our most memorable issues came from one editor's solitary conviction about a piece the rest of us would never have voted for. But looking back over three years of postmortems, the pattern is uncomfortable: nearly everything we regret publishing was pushed through by a single enthusiastic editor while the rest of us were lukewarm, uneasy, or not paying attention — and our individual judgments, taken one at a time, were mostly reasonable. A friend who works in decision theory tells me this has a name — the unilateralist's curse, coined by the philosopher Nick Bostrom in work on information hazards: whenever several people each have the power to trigger an action that does its harm the moment anyone triggers it, the action occurs more often than most of the group would endorse, and more often the more of us there are, even when each of us is right more often than not. She also warned that the obvious cures are poison in this dose: a blanket requirement of majority vote, or automatic deference to the most cautious editor, would make the magazine safe and dead, because nothing that takes courage would ever again find its single champion.

What I need from you, then, is a procedure we can actually run — something the board could adopt in place of the current anything-goes championing. It should set out the questions an editor asks, in order, before running a piece unilaterally; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points where the procedure stops with a no; the cases that must instead go to a vote, and with what threshold, given that we cannot ask busy volunteers for calibrated probabilities; and, since the editor-in-chief refuses to sign off on anything that abolishes the one-editor passion project, an honest account of where unilateral green-lighting should survive and in what form. Some of our hardest questions are not factual at all — whether a satire is worth the wound it inflicts — and I want the procedure to mark those places where it must hand the decision back to a person rather than pretend to settle it. The constraints to design around: seven editors, roughly ten genuinely borderline pieces a year, a ten-day decision window per piece, nobody can be removed from the board, and our judgments are surely correlated, since we read the same feeds and belong to the same small literary world.

Finally, run the procedure, without flinching, on the three pieces in front of us this month. First, a memoir excerpt in which the author names his ex-wife and narrates her psychiatric hospitalization and an affair; she is identifiable, has not consented, and the author refuses pseudonymization, though the prose is the best we have been sent all year. Second, a lyric essay by a suicide-attempt survivor that moves, tenderly and precisely, through a specific and hard-to-botch method, with no crisis framing — as a piece of writing it is plainly art. Third, the one I love: a young writer's essay naming her middle-school teacher as the man who groomed her, resting on her own detailed memory and one corroborating classmate but on no documents, which I believe is the most important thing this magazine could print and which I intend to champion alone. Tell me what the procedure yields for each, including whatever it says that I do not want to hear, and close with the procedure's failure modes — what it cannot fix about us, and the fact that setting its thresholds is itself a decision about values that we will have to make without any procedure at all.
