So I'm a novelist, three quarters through a book about a woman who's started to lose her grip on her own mind — nothing clinical yet, just this creeping sense that her confidence and her knowledge have quietly come apart, and the whole novel is her trying to figure out whether she can trust her own reports about her own states. My editor says the chapter endings need something to breathe against the prose, and I've had it in my head for months that the philosophical spine of the book is actually this old fight about whether your own mental states are transparent to you — the luminosity idea, the thing where philosophers say if you're cold or in pain or seeing red, surely you can't be wrong about that, and then other philosophers come along with the margin-for-error stuff and the cases where you're just at the border and your judgment can't be better than a guess. And tangled up with it is the KK thing, the principle that if you know something you must know that you know it, which strikes my narrator as either obviously true or obviously false depending on the day, which is kind of the point of her.

What I want from you is a villanelle. I know, I know, villanelles are the most overworked form in the business, but that's actually why I want it here: the two refrains need to do real philosophical work. Make one refrain something like the luminous certainties — the states you can't be wrong about — and the other refrain the KK-style demand, know that you know, and let the form's machinery show why they keep circling each other without ever landing in the same place. The refrains return but each time they're slightly less comfortable in the speaker's mouth, the way the same sentence means less to her each chapter. By the final couplet, where the refrains finally sit side by side, they should be adjacent but not fused — she has the knowledge and the knowing-that-she-knows in the same room and they still don't touch. Someone who actually knows the luminosity literature should read it and think yes, that's the gap, and someone who knows nothing should just read it as a poem about a woman listening to herself and not trusting what she hears.

Small details: I'd like it to use a concrete sensory case like warmth or cold rather than abstract talk, since she's always noticing her hands or the room temperature. No philosophy jargon in the poem itself, or as little as survives naturally. It's going to sit as a kind of epigraph-thread through the book, so it should be good enough to bear rereading maybe six times.

And since I honestly don't know this literature well — I read my way into it for the book and I'm sure I've flattened things — after the poem, give me a short plain note on where you took liberties with the ideas: which of my framing assumptions are contested (I've been assuming the anti-luminosity people and the KK-deniers are basically on the same team, are they?), what a specialist would wince at, and what exactly would make you rework the poem — like, what's the counterargument or alternative reading that, if I bought it, would change which refrain gets undercut by the end. Also flag the place in the poem where your rendering is most likely to break down, so I know what I'm actually committing to if I use it.
