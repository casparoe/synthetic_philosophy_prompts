She works on the epistemology of testimony, and halfway through I realized the whole debate she was describing -- reductionism vs anti-reductionism, i.e. is testimony a basic source of justification or does its justification bottom out in other sources -- is basically the fight my editor and I have at every deadline, except we have our own words for it. So here's the job: translate both sides of that debate into newsroom vocabulary as faithfully as you can, then give me the residue report.

The vocabulary you're working with, and I'm fluent in all of it: sources (named, on background, deep background, fully anonymous), the two-source rule, triangulating independent reports, a source's track record, going back to the primary document instead of citing someone else's story, institutional reputation (a journal, a wire service, an agency press office), the corrections desk, and the weird pull of running something three outlets already ran.

Specifically: the reductionist holds that you're only entitled to trust what a source tells you because you've inductively established that sources of this type, in conditions of this type, are usually right -- map that onto track record and background, and tell me what it can't say. The anti-reductionist holds that hearing someone assert p under normal conditions is itself defeasible but basic entitlement to believe p, and that demanding a general inductive argument first is circular or worse -- map that onto how we actually treat a wire story we have no independant reason to doubt, and tell me what it can't say. Then the residue: which parts of the philosophers' fight have no counterpart in how we run a newsroom, and is that because my vocabulary is missing something, or because their disagreement is less substantive than it looks?
