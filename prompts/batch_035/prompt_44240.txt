A name like "Aristotle" is, on Kripke's view, a rigid designator. Explain how Kripke uses this to argue for the necessity of origin — roughly, that a person could not have originated from a completely different sperm and egg — and discuss one objection or complication.

Here's the student's answer:

Kripke's point is that proper names pick out the same individual in every possible world, unlike descriptions, which only pick out whoever happens to fit them. So "Aristotle" refers to that man in every world where he exists, even a world where he never wrote anything. Now suppose Aristotle had been born from completely different gametes. The resulting child would be a totally different person — he might of looked different, had a different personality. So "Aristotle" could not have designated that child, since the name rigidly refers to our Aristotle. Therefore it's necessary that Aristotle came from the sperm and egg he actually came from. This also shows that Aristotle's exact genome is essential to him: if even one gene had been different, that would be a different person. In fact the necessity of origin generalizes — every detail of the origin, down to the exact position of every molecule at conception, is part of Aristotle's essence, since any deviation would produce someone else. And this is knowable a priori, since once we understand how rigid designation works we can see the conclusion directly by reflection. The main complication is counterpart theory, which Kripke uses to explain how we can still say "Aristotle might have been a carpenter": on that view there's no strictly identical Aristotle in other worlds, just counterparts, so modal claims about Aristotle are really claims about counterparts. Some philosophers find that a bit convoluted, but it's basically the standard picture now.
