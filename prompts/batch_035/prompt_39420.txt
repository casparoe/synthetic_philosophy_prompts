You are a professional philosopher specializing in population ethics, deeply familiar with the literature around Parfit's nonidentity problem — person-affecting constraints and their narrow and wide formulations, comparative versus noncomparative accounts of harm, total versus averaging approaches — and experienced in drafting joint statements for adversarial collaborations. Before writing the final document, think carefully and spend a few sentences brainstorming where the two camps below plausibly converge and where they genuinely diverge; then produce the document itself.

Two philosophers have been arguing for years about the nonidentity problem and have finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration. The first holds a person-affecting view: she believes morality concerns making people better off rather than making additional people, and in cases where a policy choice determines which future people will exist — for instance, a society that pursues a depletion policy for short-term gain, knowing this will change the timing of conceptions so that a different, worse-off population exists a few centuries later — she holds that no one is wronged, since no one in that population is worse off than they would otherwise have been (they would otherwise never have existed). Impersonal optimization over who gets to exist strikes her as a category mistake. The second holds an impersonal total view: what matters is the sum of well-being in the world, so the depletion policy is straightforwardly wrong because the resulting population lives contain less welfare than the alternative population's lives would have contained, even though the people harmed by the choice would never have existed without it. He is unmoved by the familiar slogan that we should make people happy rather than make happy people. Both are careful, good-faith thinkers who are tired of talking past each other. Write the joint statement their collaboration should produce.

The document must contain four parts:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section: claims both would sign without hesitation. These should include claims about the cases themselves (e.g., that in the depletion scenario no one in the later population is worse off than they would have been under the alternative policy, and that the choice is still in some sense regrettable even on her view), about what each view explains well and poorly, and about what would count as progress for the debate.

2. A disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated in wording its own side would accept as fair to its view. Candidate disagreements include whether merely possible people can be benefited or harmed at all; whether being caused to exist with a life worth living can be worse for a person than never existing; whether the wrong in these cases is victimless or has a victim who simply fails to ever exist; and whether any part of their dispute is merely verbal — for instance, a dispute about whether "wrong" is being used in a pro tanto or an all-things-considered sense. If you conclude some part of their disagreement is verbal, say which part and why.

3. The cruxes: sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I suspect one is whether people's stable, reflective judgments in these cases track the identity of the persons affected or only the level and distribution of well-being; another is whether an act can be wrong for a person in a noncomparative sense — wrong in itself for that person — even when it is not worse for that person comparatively.

4. For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or thought experiment both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. For the identity-tracking crux, consider constructing a staged case: start with a two-option choice between a smaller, better-off population and a larger, worse-off one, then vary the scenarios step by step so that at some stages identity is held fixed and at others it is not; each side should pre-commit to a verdict at each stage, and the pattern of ordinary people's reflective judgments across the stages should be treated by both as evidence. For the noncomparative-harm crux, consider a wrongful-disability case — a child knowingly conceived with a genetic condition such that her life is worth living but clearly worse than it could have been — and have each side write down in advance what they expect untutored and then reflective judgments about it to be, and what those judgments would show about their view if they came out the other way.

Throughout, keep the drafting neutral and precise: the person-affecting philosopher should be able to point at any agreed claim and say that it is hers; the totalist should be able to say the same. Neither side should be made to concede more than the actual view allows. Format: numbered agreed claims, then a disagreements table, then cruxes with each side's predictions.
