I sit on the collections committee of a mid-sized regional art museum, and I need a written recommendation to circulate before our next meeting. Last year our flagship gallery painting, a portrait we have displayed for decades as the work of a well-known seventeenth-century Dutch master, was subjected to infrared reflectography and pigment analysis after a visiting conservator raised doubts. Two outside experts now agree that it is a high-quality forgery produced in the 1920s by a skilled copyist who knew the master's style intimately — and by all accounts it remains a beautiful object in its own right. The committee must choose among three options: relabel the painting honestly and keep it in the flagship gallery; relabel it and move it to a smaller educational exhibit on connoisseurship and forgery; or deaccession it quietly into storage. The committee is split. One faction argues that the aesthetic experience of viewing the picture is exactly what it was before the reattribution — same pigments, same composition, same light falling on the same canvas — and that demoting it would be a kind of snobbery about causal origins. The other faction argues that much of what we value in an original is historical: the fact that these very brushstrokes were laid down by the master himself, standing in a direct causal chain to him, and that this value cannot survive reattribution even though every visible feature could be duplicated. My own view has been shaken. My oldest friend, a painter, spent most of last evening arguing with me about this, and she insists that once she knows a painting is forged she literally cannot see it the same way — that the knowledge changes the perception itself, not merely the appraisal. I could not decide whether she was reporting an aesthetic fact or describing a kind of psychological contagion that a more disciplined viewer could resist, and we parted without settling it. Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend one of the three options with concrete reasoning the committee can act on, engage honestly with the strongest argument on the losing side, and — if you can — say something about whether my friend is right that knowing a work is forged changes what one actually sees, or only what one knows. Please write it in American English, in a formal register suitable for circulation to trustees.
