I want the Western response to the Ethiopian famine of 1983–85 — the BBC television reports of October 1984 that recast a slow, regionally confined crisis as a catastrophe of biblical scale, the Band Aid single, the Live Aid concert of July 1985, and the surge of food aid that followed — analyzed as a test case for Garrett Hardin's lifeboat ethics, as he laid it out in his 1974 essay in Psychology Today, in continuity with his tragedy of the commons. Not the history of the relief effort, which I know in outline: the ethics, as Hardin frames it. Do the mapping carefully first. On his picture, who is in the boat and who is in the water; what exactly is the food aid supposed to do — feed the drowning, or draw ever more swimmers toward a boat already at capacity, so that the mercy shown now guarantees a larger drowning later; and what work do his auxiliary ideas do in the case — the world food bank, the commons, and the ratchet by which aid spares population the discipline of famine and therefore locks in a worse famine down the line? Then tell me where the model breaks. The salient complication is that the famine was not primarily Malthusian: northern Ethiopia did suffer drought, but the conversion of scarcity into mass death ran through the Derg's counterinsurgency in Tigray and the north, through restrictions on movement and trade, and through a resettlement program that was itself partly supplied by the relief apparatus — so famine on this account looks closer to Amartya Sen's entitlement failures, or to Alex de Waal's contention that abuses kill more famine victims than hunger itself does, than to a lifeboat filling past capacity. Given that, I want an honest verdict in both directions. Does Hardin's model even engage a famine like this one, or does it borrow its plausibility from the natural-disaster framing that the television coverage imposed? And yet the Derg did divert aid toward its soldiers and its resettlement scheme — so does the episode, in a way Hardin never argued for, vindicate his anti-aid conclusion through a mechanism the lifeboat metaphor never anticipated, or does it instead show that lifeboat ethics was never really about famine at all, only about population? Please also say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Finally, be explicit about how confident you are in each of your major claims, and single out the single point of the analysis you are least sure of.
