I teach an upper-level metaethics seminar and I'm putting together the take-home final, and I want one long essay question on the Frege–Geach problem. The problem is my students have all memorized the standard dance: state the problem with a conditional like if tormenting the cat is wrong, getting your little brother to torment the cat is wrong, note that the antecedent doesn't get asserted, observe that hooray/boo doesn't compose, and then wave in the direction of Gibbard's plan-acceptance story or Blackburn's quasi-realism and call it a day. Anyone who did the reading can do that in their sleep. So I want a question where the recitation doesn't settle anything. In particular I want the crux to be negation: why "shoplifting is wrong" and "shoplifting is not wrong" can't both express the same attitude of disapproval toward shoplifting, why unasserted contexts make this worse (think of a conditional whose antecedent is "shoplifting is not wrong," or a disjunction with one moral and one plain disjunct), and whether the standard fix — treating the attitude itself as a state with its own logic — ends up smuggling in exactly the representational content that expressivism was supposed to be avoiding in the first place.

There's a second thing I'd like folded in. Last year a student of mine, in office hours, proposed that the whole problem dissolves on a hybrid view: let moral sentences have ordinary truth-conditions and additionally express attitudes, so "stealing is wrong" is true iff stealing has the property of wrongness and it also expresses disapproval. I want the exam question to force students to decide whether that actually dissolves Frege–Geach or just relocates it — for instance by asking what the hybrid says about the inference from "stealing is wrong" plus "if stealing is wrong, encouraging it is wrong" to the conclusion, and whether anything is left of the expressivist motivation once the truth-conditions are doing all the compositional work. Ideally the question also gets at whether "not wrong" is just the negation of "wrong" for the hybrid, while the pure expressivist has to say something weirder and should own up to that.

Can you write the question (aimed at a student who has ~90 minutes and picks it from a list of four), a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 with five must-hit points and a one-line note on how students typically blow each one? Please don't have the question lean on specific page references, dates, or anything students would need to have memorized — it should be answerable from the argument structure alone.
