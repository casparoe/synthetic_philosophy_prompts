In an evolutionary-biology reading group I belong to, one member has been defending group selection in social insects by way of an analogy that I would like examined properly. Her claim is that an insect colony is like a single body: the queen and her mates supply what a germ line supplies, the workers are the soma — a forager is like a limb, a nurse like an organ — colony-wide features such as nest architecture and division of labor correspond to organismal traits, and the colony's life cycle, punctuated by the dispersal of new reproductives, corresponds to a body's reproduction. She notes that the idea has a pedigree, going back to William Morton Wheeler's 1911 paper on the ant colony as an organism and revived in Bert Hölldobler and E. O. Wilson's 2009 book The Superorganism. Her conclusion is that since selection manifestly sculpts organisms, and a colony is organism-like in every respect that matters, selection can act on the colony as a whole — so strictly gene-centered accounts miss real colony-level adaptations, of the sort illustrated by colonies that regulate their brood-nest temperature the way a warm-blooded animal regulates its own.

Another member of the group attacks the analogy itself. In a body, he observes, the cells are near-genetic clones of one another, competition among them is suppressed by machinery that polices it — a liver cell does not found a rival liver, and cancer is the rare breakdown of the system. In a colony, by contrast, workers are often only moderately related, and some try to reproduce behind the queen's back; in honeybee hives, policing workers destroy many of the eggs their nestmates lay. The colony, he concludes, is more like a commune, or a firm whose employees occasionally steal, than like a body: the analogy quietly helps itself to precisely the internal unity it was supposed to establish.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than another rerun of the group-selection wars. Please do the following:

1. Lay out the correspondence table explicitly. What plays the role of the soma, of the germ line, of an organ's specialized function, of meiosis's fair segregation, and of the immune system's policing? Mark each row as holding fully, holding partially, or failing outright.

2. Separate the disanalogies that matter for her conclusion from those that do not. In particular, address whether she has run together two claims the analogy is often used to license: that a colony is an integrated, organism-like whole, and that a colony is a unit of selection. Which failures of the mapping would actually undercut the selection claim, and which undercut only the organism claim?

3. Test the mapping where it should be at its strongest and at its weakest — say, a parthenogenetically produced aphid colony whose members are genetically identical, versus a honeybee colony whose members are not.

4. Consider the historical repair. Multicellular bodies were themselves once colonies of cells, so she can reply that a colony is like a body at an earlier point on the same road — the road the literature on "evolutionary transitions in individuality" describes. Does this temporal version rescue the analogy, and if so, does it come at the cost of weakening her conclusion into a claim about what colonies can become rather than about what they are now?

5. Say whether her conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if not, propose a better analogy — one that preserves whatever she was right about while carrying less unwanted baggage.
