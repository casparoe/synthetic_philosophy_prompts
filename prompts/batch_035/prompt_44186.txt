a guy in my metaethics seminar is a hardcore expressivist (about as hardcore as one can be while doing homework), and another student cornered him with an argument last week and now he's sulking. the thing is i can't patch it up either. it goes like this:

(1) expressivism says the function of 'lying is wrong' in use is to express disapproval of lying — the sentence isn't describing anything, its whole job on a given occasion is the expressing. (2) now take the conditional 'if lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong'. whoever asserts that is not expressing disapproval of lying. our logic teacher asserts conditionals like this constantly while grading, and she's on record (after two glasses of wine) as not caring about lying one bit either way — she'd assert it in a heartbeat just to show some student he's being inconsistent. so in the embedded spot, nothing is being expressed by the antecedent. (3) but compositionality: the conditional's meaning is built from the meanings of its parts, and 'lying is wrong' has to mean the same thing in the antecedent as it does standing alone — if it meant something different there, modus ponens on it would be equivocation instead of validity, and its plainly valid. our professor says this is roughly the point Geach pressed on the emotivists. (4) so what the sentence contributes inside the conditional is identical to what it contributes standing alone. (5) but in the embedded position the contribution can't be an act of expressing an attitude, since no act of expression is happening there (see the logic teacher). (6) so what the sentence contributes in both positions is not an expression of attitude — and then expressing attitudes was never the function of the sentence, and expressivism is false, full stop.

the part that gets me: run the same steps on any old indicative sentence and you get that no sentence anywhere functions to assert anything, since every sentence can sit unasserted inside a conditional too — 'grass is green' inside 'if grass is green, the park will look nice', nobody is asserting grass's greenness there. the student shrugs and says fine, that conclusion follows as well and its harmless. the expressivist insists the argument proves too much and swears something slides between (3) and (6), but he cant put his finger on where, and neither can i. which step should be rejected, why did it look fine on the way in, and what does rejecting it cost? for what its worth i'd rather keep (3) if that's possible — compositionality feels non-negotiable for the expressivist more than for anyone — but tell me honestly if im just picking the premise i like.
