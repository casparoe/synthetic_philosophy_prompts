I teach a grad seminar on rationality and inference, and every couple of years the doomsday argument comes up and eats an entire session with nobody's mind changed. This year I want to try something different: get a clean probabilistic handle on where the disagreement actually lives instead of another round of people talking past each other about sampling.

So here's what I want. Give me your credence that the doomsday argument, in the Carter/Leslie form, should move a rational agent to assign meaningfully higher probability to "humankind ends relatively soon" (say, fewer total humans ever born than a median demographer-style projection would suggest), compared to what you'd believe if you somehow never considered your birth rank. I want a real number, not a hedge about how it's an interesting puzzle.

Then decompose it. My own attempt at the decomposition, which you should correct if you think it's wrong, has roughly these terms: (1) the probability that something like self-sampling reasoning is sound at all -- that is, that I should reason as if randomly sampled from some reference class, rather than the self-indication assumption cancelling things out or the whole framework being confused; (2) the probability that there's a principled way to pick the reference class that isn't just rigged to get the answer you want -- this is where I feel the argument quietly cheats, since including future digital minds or excluding them seems to flip the conclusion; (3) the probability that the birth-rank evidence is as clean as the argument needs it to be (no funny business with observer-moments, ancestor simulations muddying the counts, etc.); and (4) whatever residual term you think I'm missing. Assign each a rough probability with a line of justification, show me how they combine to your headline number, and tell me which term that number is most sensitive to.

One thing I specifically want you to address: does Bostrom's presumptuous-philosopher case change your credence in either direction? If self-sampling reasoning is strong enough to push on doom, it seems like it should be strong enough to do the presumptuous thing in that case, and vice versa -- a view on one looks dishonest without a matching view on the other. If you think they come apart, say how.

Also, my co-teacher puts herself at about 0.15 that the argument should move us, and I'm hovering around 0.45. Given your decomposition, tell us which of those two numbers is most likely to be driven by disagreement over which term -- I suspect we're not actually disagreeing about the same thing, and I'd like to know if that's right.

Please write in American English throughout (I've had enough of "modelling" and "favour" this semester). And dont soften the numbers into ranges -- a point estimate per term plus the headline, with the honesty about uncertainty saved for the sensitivity discussion.
