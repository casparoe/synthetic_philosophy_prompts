I'm a high school senior writing my extended essay on direct democracy, and before I can argue anything about it I need to know what I actually believe about when a referendum's result should bind. I've collected my gut verdicts on a set of cases, and I'd like you to find the principle behind them, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and say what it predicts for cases I haven't considered.

My verdicts, as honestly as I can state them:

1. Brexit (2016): a bare 52–48 majority on a major constitutional change, in a referendum that was technically only advisory, should not by itself have carried the change; parliament should have had to do more work than it did. Verdict: not a legitimate simple mandate.

2. The Swiss referendum that banned the construction of new minarets (2009): I think the outcome was wrong and unfair to a small religious minority, but the procedure was legitimate — it was a proper national vote under Switzerland's long-standing system. Verdict: legitimate process, bad result.

3. Swiss women could not vote in federal referendums until 1971. Any referendum before then was decided by an electorate that excluded half the adult population. Verdict: not legitimate — even if the voting procedure was formally impeccable, the electorate itself was wrongly constituted.

4. California's Proposition 13 (1978): a legitimate majority vote capped property taxes, but the measure also required supermajorities for future legislatures to raise many taxes, which entrenches one generation's majority against later ones. Verdict: the vote was legitimate, but the entrenchment clause was not; a one-time bare majority shouldn't be able to bind future majorities that tightly.

5. Ireland's 2018 referendum to repeal the constitutional ban on abortion, held after a randomly selected Citizens' Assembly had deliberated for months: completely legitimate, even though this too was a major moral question carried by a simple majority. I don't feel the Brexit worry here at all, and I'm not sure why.

6. Reversals: if a country votes a policy in and then, three years later, votes essentially the same policy out by a similar margin, the second vote should stand, and "the people have already spoken" is not an objection. Verdict: re-running a referendum is fine.

7. My own school case: the student body was asked whether to keep our late bus; only about a fifth of students voted, but I think the result should stand anyway, because low turnout cuts both ways — the people who cared enough showed up.

The tensions I can already see: 2 and 3 pull apart (formally correct procedure can be legitimate in one case and not the other), 1 and 5 pull apart (a simple majority seems badly insufficient for Brexit but fine for Ireland), and 4 sits awkwardly with 6 (if majorities cannot bind future majorities, why can a reversal vote undo an earlier one — and why couldn't the earlier majority claim the same protection against being reversed?). My best guesses are that the difference between 1 and 5 lies in the deliberation beforehand, or in the fact that Ireland's status quo was itself the product of an earlier referendum, and that the difference between 2 and 3 is that one electorate was merely mistaken while the other was wrongly drawn. But I cannot get all of this into one principle, and I have tried.

What I would like from you: state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, precisely enough that I could apply it mechanically to any new referendum. For each clause, tell me which of my cases motivates it. Then tell me honestly which of my seven verdicts your best principle fails to save, and sketch what a cleaner principle that simply drops those verdicts would look like. Finally, apply your principle to three cases I have not mentioned: (a) a referendum whose ballot question is worded so that a "yes" vote does the opposite of what most voters plausibly intend; (b) a referendum to abolish referendums; and (c) a supermajority requirement that was itself introduced by a simple majority vote.
