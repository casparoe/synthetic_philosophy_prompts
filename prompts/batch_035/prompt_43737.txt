I serve on the algorithmic accountability panel of a mid-sized city agency that must sign off on any automated decision tool before deployment, and I could use help thinking through our next case. Our panel has a short but real track record: last year we approved a hospital readmission model for a population in which the two groups being compared had nearly identical readmission rates, and the vendor's audit showed the model was well calibrated within each group and had roughly equal false-positive and false-negative rates across groups; the same quarter we rejected a welfare-fraud screening tool whose scores were badly miscalibrated for one group and whose false-positive rate was nearly triple that group's rate for the other; and more recently we rejected outright a tenant-prioritization tool simply because the vendor had produced no group-disaggregated performance data at all, treating the absence of an audit as disqualifying regardless of how the model otherwise looked. I'm the only member with a statistics background, and I've started to suspect that these decisions, though individually defensible, don't actually pin down any single principle we're following.

The new case is a lending-risk tool for our municipal credit union, and it forces a choice. The applicant pool includes two groups with genuinely different observed default rates, so the vendor is offering us two versions. Version A is calibrated within each group, meaning that among people assigned a given score, the actual default rate matches the predicted rate for both groups; the tradeoff is that the group with the higher default rate also ends up with a noticeably higher false-positive rate, so more of its non-defaulting members are flagged as risky. Version B instead equalizes both the false-positive and false-negative rates across the two groups, but as a consequence it systematically overstates the risk of members of the lower-default group; a score of, say, 70 from Version B means a higher true probability of default for one group than for the other. The vendor, to its credit, says we cannot have both properties at once given the base rates, and my own reading of the statistics literature supports that.

So my questions are these. First, which fairness principles are actually consistent with our past decisions, and is there more than one? Second, what would each candidate principle recommend for this case, Version A, Version B, or neither? Third, treating our precedent as the closest analog, what would be the decision most consistent with our own track record, and what principle would we effectively be adopting with that choice — including what it commits us to going forward? And finally, if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict here, what property would a future case need to have in order to best distinguish between the candidate principles, so that our next decision actually settles something rather than prolonging the ambiguity? Please respond in American English, and feel free to be technical where it helps.
