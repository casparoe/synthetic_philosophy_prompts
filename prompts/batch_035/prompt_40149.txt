My roommate has an argument that the Turing test, taken seriously, eats itself, and I can't find the bad step. I'm hoping you can. Here it is as she gives it:

(1) Turing's whole move was to stop asking the vague question of whether machines can think and ask something checkable instead: does the machine's conversational behavior, in a long exchange with a competent interrogator, become indistinguishable from an ordinary human's?

(2) But the interrogator only ever observes a finite stretch of conversation. An hour, a day, a decade — always finite.

(3) For any finite stretch, there is a possible system that produces exactly that stretch by pure lookup: a gigantic table indexed by every possible conversation history up to that length, with the next move written in each cell. No inference, no understanding, just retrieval.

(4) A lookup table doesn't think any more than a phone book does.

(5) So at every stage of the test, the total observed behavior is consistent both with a thinking machine and with a non-thinking table.

(6) If evidence is consistent with p and with not-p, it doesn't favor either, so it settles nothing.

(7) So the Turing test, at every stage of its actual operation, provides zero evidence of thought.

(8) But everything I know about other minds arrives through the same channel — finite stretches of other people's behavior — so (7) generalizes: I have zero evidence that anyone else thinks.

She insists (8) is the reductio: a test invented to settle the machine question turns out, if her reasoning holds, to undermine the other-minds inference for humans as a byproduct, which is too much to pay. So something upstream has to go.

The problem is every step looks fine to me. (3) is just a fact about finite sets. I half suspect the weak point is (4) — I know behaviorists were willing to bite bullets roughly like it — but I can't see anything wrong with (5) or (6) individually, and if I give up (4) I apparently have to say a sufficiently big table thinks, which feels like losing the test's point in a different way. And there's a part of me that wonders whether she's even entitled to (7) from (5) and (6), like something about "consistent with" is doing two jobs, but I can't pin down what.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? If you throw out the step I haven't dared to name, tell me why it looked so innocent — and whether the fix leaves the Turing test measuring thought at all, or just measuring something adjacent to it. And if you keep her reasoning all the way, say honestly whether you land at (8) or whether there's a principled difference between the machine case and my friends I'm not seeing. Lead with your answer in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating the setup back at me.
