My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about whether the agricultural revolution was, all things considered, a mistake, and we ended in exactly the place you'd expect: I'm at roughly 0.65 that it was, she's at roughly 0.3, and neither of us can say precisely what we're disagreeing about. What I'd like from you is your own credence, but far more importantly, the decomposition that produces it, because I suspect our numbers are being built out of different questions without either of us noticing.

Here is how I'd want the question sharpened before you assign any probabilities. Take the proposition to be: for the typical individual who lived between the Neolithic transition and roughly 1800 in agrarian societies, lifetime well-being was lower than it would have been in the counterfactual in which their society remained forager-horticulturalists, while still allowing that the descendants of that transition (us) are vastly better off than any foraging population. If you think that sharpening smuggles in the conclusion, or that a different precisification is what the debate is really about, say so — but then decompose your version, not mine.

The sub-questions I can see the number depending on, which you should feel free to amend, add to, or reject:

1. Whether skeletal and archaeological proxies — stature, enamel hypoplasia, caries, degenerative joint markers, workload indicators — actually track well-being closely enough to rank the two lifeways, or whether they measure morbidity and workload while missing things foragers might have been worse at (violence? child mortality? existential security?) that we can't proxy.
2. Whether the paleo-affluence picture in the tradition of Sahlins and the original worst-mistake framing survives the later quantitative work, or whether that picture is a romanticized artifact of studying remnant foragers at their most favorable.
3. Whether "mistake" is even a well-formed predicate for a diffuse process that no one chose — my friend's view is that the question collapses into whether the transition was bad, while I think something like a trap or lock-in structure matters: a population that would each have preferred to stay foragers but couldn't individually opt out can make the transition a mistake even with no chooser.
4. How to weight the first few millennia of agrarian life against the post-1800 payoff: whether person-affecting views, temporal discounting, or just the sheer number of downstream beneficiaries should enter at all, and how much.
5. The counterfactual itself: whether "remained foragers" is coherent given that agricultural societies would eventually absorb or displace them, so the comparison isn't foragers versus farmers but foragers versus being conquered by farmers.

Please assign a rough probability to each sub-question you keep, with a line of justification for each, and show how you combine them into your final credence. Then tell me two further things: which single term the final number is most sensitive to, so I know where my friend and I should concentrate the next round; and, candidly, what would move your number — which piece of evidence about the proxies, or which philosophical argument about the trap or the counterfactual, would flip which sub-question and by how much. If some sub-question is where you suspect the 0.65 and the 0.3 most likely diverge, I'd like you to say that too, and be explicit about where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
