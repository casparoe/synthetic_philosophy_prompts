I'm writing an essay on how the word "utility" does double duty in economics: as an ordinal representation of a fixed preference ordering, and as a measure of how well a life is going. To make the divergence vivid, I need a thought experiment built to the specifications below. Please give me your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion; where an item would otherwise run long, compress it into a long sentence rather than adding sentences.

The case is a single agent observed at two stages of life, with everything but the agent's tastes held fixed. Specifications: (a) realistic and domestic — the template is the kind of slow, unremarkable drift in tastes people undergo in later life, so no substances, no disorders, no science fiction; (b) at each stage the agent's choices reveal a complete, transitive, stable ordering, and the agent always acts on the top of it — no akrasia, no regret; (c) the stage-two choices must flatly contradict the stage-one ordering, so the change is in what is wanted, not a response to new information or new constraints — the budget and option set stay the same, and the agent knows at stage one everything known at stage two; (d) the usual escape routes must be blocked inside the story: no compulsion, no ignorance, no regret, and the change must not trace back to any decision the agent made, so the reply that the agent preferred the risk of changing cannot get started; (e) by the end, ordinary judgment and a hedonic standard should find the agent worse off at stage two — days flatter, nothing anticipated, an assent to this description without caring — while the choice-based criterion finds both stages equally perfect, since the agent always obtains the top of the current ordering, and finds nothing at all to say across the change.

The five items: (1) the scenario itself, in at most two sentences, with concrete details doing the work; (2) a demonstration that the specifications are met — the specific choice data establishing that the ordering changed (a stage-two selection that contradicts a stage-one ranking between two affordable options), plus a quick check that each escape route is blocked; (3) the verdicts — what strict revealed-preference reasoning can establish within each stage, why it cannot rank the two stages against each other, and what a substantive welfare criterion concludes, noting briefly if the substantive criteria you consider disagree among themselves, since that disagreement is part of the finding; (4) the diagnosis — which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the case if that feature is removed; (5) the strongest reply the revealed-preference camp could make to a case like this, and your honest view of whether that reply preserves the theory or quietly concedes that utility in the choice-theoretic sense was never a measure of welfare.

A note on what counts as cheating: the scenario must not stipulate the divergence, it must generate it. If the agent is drawn as addicted, deceived, or miserable against their will, the case collapses into a familiar one about compulsion and proves nothing about the meaning of utility. The interesting case is the one where, on the choice data alone, nothing has gone wrong — and that is exactly what has gone wrong.
