I've basically talked myself into a strongish version of historical materialism: not the crude "steam engine did everything" strawman, but the claim that when you want to explain why a society's laws, religion, family forms, and dominant ideas look the way they do, the productive base does most of the causal work and the rest is downstream, or at best a lagging adjustment. Things like plow agriculture tilting gender norms, or the Reformation tracking commercializing regions, keep feeling like the same trick: find the material base, the superstructure falls out. So tell me what would actually count against it. I don't mean the standard "ideas also matter" hand-wave -- I mean the concrete patterns that should make a base-does-the-work person update: same base with wildly different superstructures, different bases converging on similar superstructures, superstructural stuff that clearly drives base changes rather than reacting to them, or slow-moving institutions (religions, kinship systems) that outlive several bases without much redesign. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which of them a serious defender would concede as counting against the view versus explain away with moves like "relative autonomy" or "the superstructure reacts back on the base" -- and be honest about whether those qualifications have quietly made the whole thing unfalsifiable, and whether unfalsifiability is fatal for a view of this kind or just the normal cost of doing big-picture history.
