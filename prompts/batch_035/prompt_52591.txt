I teach a phil/poli-sci crossover seminar, about 15 students, 90 minutes, and the topic is political precommitment — Ulysses tying himself to the mast as the model for constitutions. Design a session where they write their own mini-constitution for a series of upcoming decisions, and then the constitution starts to chafe: the later rounds are built so that rules they enacted early, unanimously and with real enthusiasm, block things they later very much want. Materials and a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money (candy or grade-irrelevant points are fine).

The crucial piece is a final crisis round: a proposal that is genuinely good — one they will actually think is good, not some contrived temptation — which their own constitution also blocks. At that point they have to pick between (a) using the amendment procedure they wrote for themselves, which I want the design to make deliberately too slow or too demanding to help within this round, (b) just ignoring the constitution by simple majority, or (c) eating the cost and honoring it. I want the round structure set up so that the predictable failure modes (they write a mushy constitution, or shred it the first time it chafes) still produce the lesson instead of wrecking the session.

Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the real thing, in particular: the difference between precommitment devices that physically can't yield (the rope doesn't have to interpret anything) and constitutional ones, which somebody has to interpret — so some present self always gets back in through the interpretive door; second-order precommitment, i.e. rules about how rules may be changed, and why entrenchment is exactly the spot where the Ulysses logic and democratic logic fight each other. No lecture up front; the name "Ulysses" shouldn't appear until the debrief.

Two things I'm picky about. First, commit to one design — don't hand me a menu of options to choose from, make the calls yourself and say why. Second, in the debrief section I want you to actually take a side on whether the group ought to have honored the constitution in the crisis round. I'm not looking for "there are considerations on both sides" — give me your call and your reasons, and be upfront that it's a call. Also tell me what will predictibly go wrong the first time I run this, and be honest about which feature of precommitment the activity can't reproduce (they know it's a game that ends in 90 minutes; real constitutions are bets against an open-ended future, etc.).
