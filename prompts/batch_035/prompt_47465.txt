I'm a novelist, and the book I'm currently drafting turns on a state-court judge in a shrinking industrial city — a woman in her sixties, the daughter of a police officer, who after thirty years on the bench begins to lose her certainty about the sentences she hands down. Rather than let her crisis be purely private and psychological, I want it to have intellectual spine, so she reads her way into the philosophy of punishment across the course of the novel, and her marginalia become a kind of counterpoint to the sentencing scenes. To that end I've been working through a popular book on punishment and justice — I'd rather not name it, since I'm about to be unkind to it — and its capsule account of the classic divide is now sitting in my notebook, where I fear it will quietly structure the whole book unless someone tells me it shouldn't.

The account runs roughly as follows: retributivism holds that punishment is justified by the crime alone — the offender has incurred a debt, and the state exists to collect it. Because the theory is entirely backward-looking, it has nothing to say about whether punishment does anyone any good, and since it insists the guilty must suffer, it sets no real upper limit on how much they may be made to suffer, proportionality being left to intuition; nor can it make room for mercy. Consequentialism, by contrast, looks only forward, justifying punishment wholly by its effects on crime, which is why it licenses whatever sentence deters best — including the exemplary punishment of an innocent person, if the example is effective enough. The two theories thus mirror each other: one would punish the guilty no matter what good it does, the other would punish anyone no matter what they did. The book closes by declaring that no serious philosopher holds either view in its pure form, and that centuries of practice have quietly refuted both.

I am not trained in philosophy and can't locate the errors myself, though I suspect there are some — the symmetry at the end is suspiciously tidy, the kind of line a popularizer polishes past accuracy. Could you go through the summary claim by claim? For each piece, tell me what a careful retributivist would accept, what they'd wince at, and what they'd flatly deny; then do the same for a careful consequentialist. I'm especially interested in the mirror-image aphorism and in the claim that retributivism has no upper limit and no room for mercy, because my climactic sentencing turns on it: the offender is seventy-eight and terminally ill, the guidelines call for eight years, incapacitation and deterrence are effectively nil, and I had planned my judge's crisis as the pure collision the book describes — retributivism forcing the sentence, consequentialism releasing him, no third position available. Does the actual philosophical divide break anywhere near that cleanly, and is the book's closing verdict on both theories fair? I'd rather build her crisis on a real fault line than a false one, and I have time to restructure if the conflict should be elsewhere.
