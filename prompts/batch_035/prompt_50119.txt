The exam question they were answering was: Dennett, in From Bacteria to Bach and Back, describes human consciousness as a kind of user illusion, on the model of the icons and folders on a computer desktop. Explain what he means by the comparison, why this does not amount to saying that consciousness is unreal or that we are wrong to think of ourselves as conscious, and what the view implies about the so-called hard problem of consciousness.

Here's the student's answer. I typed it up from the scan, fixed their spelling, left everything else as-is:

Dennett's point with the desktop analogy is that our experience of our own minds is like a computer user's experience of their screen. The icons are not the machinery — nobody sees the circuits and voltages when they drag a file to the trash — but they are not fakes either. They are a simplified way of getting at real processes. In the same way, what we experience as consciousness (the unified stream of thought, colors, the self that seems to be watching it all) is how the brain presents its own activity to itself, and the presentation leaves out almost all of the underlying processing. So Dennett is not saying consciousness doesn't exist. He is saying it's a real interface the brain produces, not a literal picture of what the brain is doing.

Where does the illusion come in? Dennett thinks the brain constructs something like a Cartesian theater, an inner display on which the contents of consciousness get presented, and the illusion is precisely that there is such a single display, when in fact there is no one place in the brain where it all comes together. Since the interface is only a summary, it doesn't actually do anything. For Dennett consciousness is epiphenomenal, a shadow cast by the real work of the brain, which goes on entirely without it. This is similar to Kant on phenomena and noumena: we only ever get the interface, the world as it appears, and the machinery in itself is permanently hidden from us, so even our knowledge of our own minds is just knowledge of appearances.

On the hard problem: Chalmers claims there is a further question, namely why all of this information processing is accompanied by experience at all. Dennett's response is that the hard problem is an artifact of the interface. Once you see that the features we find so puzzling (qualia, the unity of consciousness) are features of the user-level description, there is nothing left over to explain, just as there is no further question about how the icon really got into the trash. The hard problem is not solved but dissolved. He also thinks that if you built a system with the right sort of user-level interface, it would be conscious, for the same reasons we are.
