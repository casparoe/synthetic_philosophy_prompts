I'm the duty hydrologist for a river basin authority, and during flood season I make the call every six hours on whether to issue warnings, request evacuations, or stand down. The way it works: our hydrological model gets fed a weather forecast plus the current river readings, and it spits out a single water-level curve for the next 72 hours. The people above me love the single number -- it makes the briefing easy. We also have a mode where it runs 50 times with slightly perturbed initial conditions, which nobody had trained me on until last month, and I mostly use it now when I'm nervous, without really knowing what I'm looking for.

Here's my problem. Sometimes the model is confidently wrong 48 hours out and we evacauted nobody and got lucky. Sometimes the 50 runs are all over the place and I stood down because "most of them looked fine" and that was gut, not method. I know enough to know that deterministic doesn't mean predictable -- that a perfect model with imperfect initial conditions goes bad at a rate that depends on the horizon, and that there's a difference between the forecast losing skill because of chaos and the model being wrong about the physics (our rainfall-runoff routine has a known bias after long dry spells). But I can't put any of that into practice in the 30 minutes between the run finishing and my briefing, and I don't have time to become a dynamical systems person.

Write me a decision procedure I can actually run in those 30 minutes: what to look at, in what order, what counts as signal and what's noise, rough thresholds or rules of thumb where numbers help, and clear stopping points where the procedure hands off to the duty manager because the question is judgment, not computation. It should distinguish, in plain language, three failure sources: (1) chaos -- small initial-condition errors blowing up, (2) structural error -- the model itself being biased, and (3) the weather forecast upstream being off, since we inherit whatever skill it has. Be honest about which of your steps are just my judgment dressed up as arithmetic, and say where the procedure will misfire.

Then run it on three cases and, for each, end with its own line beginning "Call:" followed by one word or a short phrase:

(a) 12-hour horizon, all 50 perturbed runs agree on peak level within a couple of centimeters, and the peak is above the evacuation threshold;
(b) 48-hour horizon, the runs split roughly half above and half below the threshold, with no pattern I can see in which ones go which way;
(c) 12-hour horizon, all runs agree -- and they all agree on something this exact model got badly wrong in the same situation two years ago, after a long dry spell.

And at the very end of the whole thing, one final line beginning "Verdict:" with a short phrase (no sentence) capturing the overall stance I should take into the next 72 hours given all three cases.

Note we already have the ensemble mode, so don't just tell me to go buy one. And case (b) matters most -- that's the one where the procedure has to give me an answer I won't like, because right now my default there is hope.
