My friend and I burned a whole car ride on the precautionary principle and now I want to actually settle it by using it. She says it's not even a principle: if you block a new pesticide over possible harms, you also need a precaution against the harms of not blocking it (crop loss, farmers reverting to an older worse chemical), so all it really does is tell everyone to act on whatever risk they happen to be staring at, dressed up as logic. I said that's only true if you flatten it into "always be cautious" -- the defensible core is about asymmetry and irreversibility: risks you can recover from get treated differently from ones you can't walk back, and you can demand evidence proportional to how novel the thing is. She said fine, but then it does way less work than advertised and it's mostly ordinary cost-benefit with extra horror-weight. We got nowhere.

Problem is I need this at work. I'm one of two reviewers at a mid-sized agency office that decides whether new consumer chemical products can go to market (fabric coatings, cleaning agents, that kind of thing). Our enabling language says to be "mindful of the precautionary principle" and of course says nothing about what that means, and we each clear maybe 40 products a year with no lab backup beyond what the company itself submits. Please turn whatever is actually defensible in the principle into a procedure we could run on a submission in an afternoon: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a no, where it says demand more testing first, and where it has to hand off to a person because the question is one of values not facts (mark those clearly, my boss wants to know which rejections are "his problem" politically). It has to handle the asymmetry/irreversibility stuff my friend and I were circling, and it must not collapse into either rubber stamping or banning everything new. Also tell me where the procedure will just misfire. Then run it on two things we actually have in front of us: a water-repellent fabric coating that's biodegradable in the lab but persists in real-world tests and comes with no chronic toxicity data, and a reformulated cleaning chemical that lets consumers skip the rinsing step (meaning a bit more residue left on surfaces). If it gives an answer I won't like on one of these, say so anway.

Last thing: once you've built it, tell me honestly whether the procedure actually answers my friend's complaint or just dodges it -- is there real content left in the principle once you add irreversibility and proportionality, or was she right that it's cost-benefit with a costume on?
