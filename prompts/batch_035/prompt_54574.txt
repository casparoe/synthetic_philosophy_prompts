I run a "math and society" evening class at a community college — curious adults, no math beyond high school. Next session is the Condorcet jury theorem: if each voter is independently better than a coin flip at judging the right answer, then as you add voters the majority is almost certain to be right — and, which is the part I actually want them to feel, if each is worse than a coin flip, the majority is almost certain to be *wrong*. Forty minutes and a whiteboard. Don't explain the theorem to me; plan the session. Specifically: what has to be in place before the theorem even makes sense to them (I suspect the binomial intuition — why independent coin-flips pile onto the majority side — needs building before anything else, but tell me if I've got the order wrong), the one concrete scenario that should carry the whole thing and what it must have and avoid, and the misconceptions I should expect. I can already see a few coming: half the class will confuse this with the wisdom-of-crowds thing where you average everyone's estimates; someone will conclude we should trust majority opinion on everything, expert disagreement included; and at least one person will conflate it with Condorcet's voting paradox, since the name is the same. Where exactly in the sequence do I head each of those off? Also, what should I openly refuse to cover at this level — I'm thinking the proof details and anything about correlated evidence beyond a warning, but push back if that's wrong. And give me one closing question whose answer would tell me whether they actually got the independence assumption, rather than just nodding along.
