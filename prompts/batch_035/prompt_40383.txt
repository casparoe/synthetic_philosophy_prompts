Hey! I teach a Chinese philosophy seminar, and two of my students have been going at it all semester: one is a full-on Mencian, the other is convinced Xunzi was right that human nature is bad and ritual does all the real moral work. For their final project I'm having them do an adversarial collaboration instead of another debate, and I want a model answer to show the class what a good one looks like — I'll add my own grading comments after, so don't hold back on substance.

Write the joint statement they could both sign. It should have:

1. A numbered section of claims both sides can accept. Things like: moral attainment isn't fixed by birth or rank, ordinary people can become morally accomplished through sustained effort, the Heaven-and-fate determinism of some of their rivals is wrong, Mozi-style impartial concern leaves out something real about graded family affection. But find the actual load-bearing agreements, and be really careful not to sneak either side's view into the shared claims — e.g. both think cultivation is necessary, but for very different reasons, so that one has to be worded so it doesn't already decide anything.

2. A disagreements table, with each disagreement phrased so the side itself would accept the wording. This is the hard part. The obvious trap is rendering Xunzi's position as humans are basically evil, which is a translation-flavored distortion — his term for nature is narrower and more technical than it looks in English, and I don't think he thinks we're seething with malice so much as we're unruly raw material that goes badly wrong without shaping.

3. The cruxes, with tests. I have a hunch about a few, and I want you to confirm, reject, or replace them with better ones:

- The child at the well. Mencius says anyone who sees a kid about to tumble into a well feels a flash of alarm, unbidden, before any thought of reputation or reward — and that flash is the sprout of benevolence. Xunzi owes us a story about what's happening there: startle? self-referential fear? a dim tendency that exists but is way too weak to build anything on? Can this ever be more than dueling readings of one anecdote, or is there a case both would count as evidence here?

- What happens to people with no ritual education. Mencius's bare mountain / ox mountain thought experiment is the obvious one: strip the sprouts with axe and grazing and you'll conclude there were never any sprouts. Mencius predicts decent conditions without active carving still yield moral growth; Xunzi predicts drift toward conflict and grabbiness. Both should agree this is the crux and write down their predictions in advance.

- Potter vs. gardener. Xunzi likens the sage's work to a potter shaping clay, Mencius's picture is closer to a gardener tending sprouts. Is this a real disagreement about whether ritual acts on alien material or on incipient tendencies of the same stuff, or just a metaphor choice?

4. Finally, and this is the part I most want handled well: tell me which parts of the dispute are verbal and which are real. Xunzi seems to define nature as what is inborn and spontaneous, and treats deliberate activity as the opposite of nature — under that definition, saying nature is bad is almost guaranteed. Mencius seems to use a broader notion closer to tendencies that reliably develop under decent conditions. If I'm right, some of their head-on clash dissolves and some survives — sort out which is which, and phrase the surviving disagreement so both would sign off on it.

Format as: numbered agreed-claims section, then disagreements table, then cruxes with tests and predictions, then the verbal-vs-real analysis. Keep the wording neutral enough that neither of my students throws it at the wall, but exact enough that it actually commits both sides to something. Aim for maybe 1200-1500 words? No need for citations, and don't use pinyin/characters for the technical terms without a quick gloss — my students' Chinese is at the level of "has heard the terms before."
