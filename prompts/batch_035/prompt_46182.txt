In his 1950 Mind paper on computing machinery and intelligence, Turing included a closing objection he took more seriously than almost anyone today would expect: the argument from extra-sensory perception. He noted that psychical researchers like J. B. Rhine had produced card-guessing results that, statistically, seemed hard to dismiss, and he suggested that if telepathy were real, his interrogation setup would have to be run in a room shielded against it, since the interrogator could otherwise be reading the human's mind — an advantage no machine could share. Restate his reasoning and his proposed fix in the vocabulary I actually use: test oracles, flaky tests, hermetic test environments, side-channel attacks, information leakage between components under test, mocking and stubbing. Be as faithful as you can, and then give me the residue report: which parts of the ESP worry map cleanly onto something like an unintended communication channel between the human participant and the judge, which parts you had to distort to make them fit, and whether anything in Turing's handling of the issue has no counterpart in how we reason about test validity and test isolation. In particular, I want to know whether his move reads today as a principled extension of his test methodology — treating a threat to the test's validity as a bug in the test harness rather than in the machine under test — or whether the ESP case is something that methodology could not actually accommodate without quietly changing what the test was for.
