Quick context: at a conference afterparty last week two friends of mine got into it about Henry Sidgwick of all people, and I want someone to referee it properly.

So Dan (philosophy grad student, hard-nosed type) was going on about how the psychical research stuff is the embarrassing chapter of Sidgwick's life: the guy who wrote the Methods of Ethics, spending two decades running seances and collecting ghost stories, and worse, doing it because his own system left him desperate. Sidgwick had argued that if the universe isn't morally ordered — if doing what's right and pursuing your own good can come apart with no guarantee they converge — then his whole framework can't stand, and he couldn't talk himself into just believing in God the way some of his friends did. So the Society for Psychical Research, in Dan's telling, was motivated reasoning with a lab coat on.

Maya (also philosophy, more sympathetic to the Victorians) said that gets it exactly backwards. Given his premises, checking whether there is any empirical evidence for a moral order — survival of death being the one testable corner of it — is precisely what a careful philosopher should do, and by most accounts he ran it that way: the SPR spent a lot of energy debunking fraudulent mediums, and the Census of Hallucinations was basically an attempt to do statistics on apparitions instead of taking anyone's word for anything. Rigorous, not credulous.

Dan said ok, but then look at the output. Decades of work and nothing replicable ever came of it, so by Sidgwick's own utilitarian lights it was a waste of the best years of a first-rate mind — a cautionary tale about letting your system's needs set your research agenda. Maya replied that no result isn't the same as bad method, and anyway the stakes were so absurdly high — it's literally the biggest question there is — that even a tiny prior probability justified serious effort. Dan's comeback: that expected-value move can rationalize any obsession; if you want the conclusion badly enough you can always inflate the stakes until the math says yes. Then people remembered there was dancing, and that was the end of it.

I couldn't referee it myself. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? And is there something both of them are missing about the episode, like maybe they're arguing about Sidgwick's character when the interesting question is somewhere else entirely? No need for sources or a bibliography, just the arguments.
