I'm a science and ideas journalist who has spent two years reporting a long-form piece on how we draw the line between life and death in intensive care units, and next month I'll be presenting my conclusions at a teaching hospital's ethics grand rounds. My thesis, in brief: brain death is not a biological discovery that the Harvard ad hoc committee announced in 1968, but a boundary that the committee and the law drew for practical reasons — ventilators, organ procurement, and the needs of families — and then retroactively dressed in biological language. That dressing, I argue, is now doing active harm. In donation after circulatory death (DCD), for instance, the protocol treats a two-minute window of stopped circulation as death, even though the heart could often be restarted at that point and sometimes restarts on its own; the published justifications quietly swap the word "irreversible" for "permanent," which is a policy judgment presented as a physiological finding. My proposed resolution is deflationary: we should admit that death is a stipulated boundary — the moment at which we agree it is permissible to treat the person as over — and relocate the justification for organ procurement from the dead donor rule to a robust account of prior, informed consent. Rather than pretending donors are dead, we should say plainly that they have authorized a transition to organ donation once life-sustaining treatment is withdrawn and consciousness is permanently absent.

The audience will be roughly sixty clinicians and staff: neurocritical care physicians who perform brain death determinations weekly and regard the apnea test and the criteria as tracking a real biological event; two transplant surgeons whose programs depend on the dead donor rule staying stable and who have seen public trust crises after cases like Jahi McMath got national coverage; ICU nurses; a hospital chaplain and an area rabbi who both objected, in an earlier session, to treating the body of a brain-dead patient as a source of parts; the attorney for the regional organ procurement organization; and a Thomist philosopher on the ethics committee who holds that death is the separation of the soul from the body and insists that whatever the criteria track, it had better be that.

Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet. I want the eight or so objections I will actually face from this room — not the objections a philosophy journal would raise — ordered by how much damage each could do to my case, each phrased the way that particular member of the audience would put it. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, plus a frank note on where the reply is soft. I am particularly interested in how to handle the surgeons' trust argument — that even if I am right, saying so publicly will cost organs and lives, and that a useful fiction is better than an honest collapse — and in whether my stipulationist move gives the Thomist an opening I can't close. Where it helps, draw on the specifics of my argument: Shewmon's accumulated cases of brain-dead patients kept alive for months, the permanent-versus-irreversible substitution in DCD protocols, and the phenomenon of autoresuscitation. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I must hold without exception, and include the one question I am hoping nobody in that room asks. Please write the entire response in American English.
