I teach an upper-level seminar on comparative ethics — sixteen students, one 80-minute session. Our unit is Buddhist ethics, and the question I want the students to actually feel, rather than be told about, is this: is Buddhist ethics a kind of consequentialism? The five precepts look like rules; karma makes everything hinge on intention; compassion seems to demand constant attention to outcomes; and the tradition's own hardest cases — the ship's captain who kills one robber to save the five hundred merchants, the lie told for the benefit of the person lied to — pull in opposite directions. Design an in-class deliberation game that stages this tension so that students run into it themselves, before I put a name to any of the positions.

Here is roughly what I have in mind, though I'm happy to have it restructured: a famine-village council simulation. Students are village elders during a drought, each privately assigned one of the five precepts as a personal binding constraint — they may not openly argue for any motion that violates their own precept, though they may abstain. The council passes motions over three or four rounds of increasingly dire scenarios: grain allocation between the temple and the starving edge households; a bandit raid that can be averted only by deception; a sick traveler who will die unless medicine is stolen from a hoarding merchant. Keep two ledgers separately: a merit ledger, on which the group scores the purity of intention behind each motion after it passes, and a welfare ledger, on which I, as facilitator, resolve outcomes from a partly randomized table that I never show them — so that the same kind of decision can produce different results in different rounds.

The payoff scheme should not use real money. What I want is for the two ledgers to visibly diverge by the end, so that students discover they have been playing two games at once and cannot say which one they were trying to win. Then I need a debrief that maps that divergence onto the real philosophical question: whether good consequences and good intentions can both be what the ethics is ultimately about, what each ledger would have to look like for Buddhist ethics to come out consequentialist, and where the classroom analogy breaks — I'm thinking especially of rebirth, since no in-class game can make the relevant consequences arrive in a future life. Be explicit about which features of the ethics the activity cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply by lecture. Also predict what will go wrong the first time I run it — I expect the precept cards to get treated as disposable once the scenarios get dire, and I suspect someone will try to game the merit scoring the moment they realize it is peer-assessed — and tell me how to handle each failure. Materials should be printable cards and paper ledgers; no software, and no prior knowledge of Buddhist texts assumed.

One final constraint: don't let the scenarios do the philosophical work by being rigged toward one conclusion. At least one round should be a case where the precept-respecting motion and the outcome-optimizing motion are the same motion, so that the ledgers can only come apart later, when the hidden outcome table makes the same choice land differently.
