I'm a second-year nursing student and I've been keeping a list of my gut reactions to end-of-life scenarios, because I noticed they don't line up and it's starting to bother me. Here are my verdicts. Switching off a ventilator when a competent patient with advanced ALS asks me to: fine, sad but fine. The same patient a few months earlier, while he still has good days, asks me instead to write him a prescription for a lethal dose he'll take home and swallow whenever he decides: not fine — it feels like a different category of thing, though I can't articulate why, since he requested both just as clearly and both end the same life on his schedule. Titrating morphine higher and higher for a dying man in agony, knowing full well the doses will eventually suppress his breathing, as long as I'm honestly aiming at pain: fine, even though I could see the death coming a mile off. A patient who chooses to voluntarily stop eating and drinking and asks the team only for mouth care and comfort meds until she dies about two weeks later: fine, which is weird, because that's a positive, sustained course of action that everyone predicts and no one intervenes to stop, so why is that fine when the prescription isn't? Withdrawing treatment because the doctor judges the patient's quality of life isn't worth living, when the patient never said anything of the sort: absolutely not fine, no hesitation there. And the one I genuinely go back and forth on: a woman in her fifties with severe untreatable depression, not dying of anything, asking for assisted dying — some days that seems obviously different from all of the above, other days I can't find the difference that matters.

So my question: is there a principle that captures this pattern, or close to it? I suspect it's some mix of the letting-die/killing distinction, the doctrine of double effect, and how much work the patient's own request is doing, but I can't get the combination to cover everything. Please take on the role of a professional bioethicist and think carefully before answering — maybe start with some brainstorming of candidate principles and test each against my cases before committing. Then state your preferred principle precisely, show which clause earns its keep on which of my verdicts, tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed (say, a paralyzed patient who can't self-administer anything and asks the team to do it, or a patient who requests deep continuous sedation while declining all food and fluids), and be honest about which of my verdicts, if any, you'd tell me to give up. I'd rather keep the one about the doctor's quality-of-life judgment, but tell me if that costs me something.
