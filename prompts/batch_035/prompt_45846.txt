Next month I defend a paper at a political-theory workshop. My thesis is that the left/right distinction is neither a historical residue of 1789 nor a mere statistical artefact of survey research, but is generated by a single underlying disagreement about where the burden of justification lies: the left treats the existing distribution of wealth, power, and esteem as standing in need of justification, while the right treats departures from the status quo as standing in need of it — and the familiar cluster of subsidiary views each side holds on liberty, equality, tradition, and change is downstream of that default assignment, not the other way round. The audience is exacting: two political realists who think ideological taxonomies obscure the interest-based and strategic character of politics; a scholar of Carl Schmitt who holds that left/right is only a domesticated descendant of friend/enemy and that no principle could ever hold a side together; a historian who will relish pointing out how badly the revolutionary seating arrangements map onto my axis; and two quantitative political scientists who use left/right as a country-specific measurement construct and will deny that it has an essence to analyse. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections in roughly the order I will receive them, phrased as those particular audience members would phrase them, ranked by how much damage each could do; a two-minute reply to each; a note on where each reply is soft; and the question I am quietly hoping nobody asks. Please write in British English.
