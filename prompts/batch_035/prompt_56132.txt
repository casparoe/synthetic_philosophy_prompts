I teach an upper-level seminar on scientific method in fundamental physics, and this term it takes up the status of string theory in the absence of discriminating experiments. For the first meeting I want a decision tree the students can work through before they have read anything, so that we can see what they actually think rather than which labels they have heard.

The tree should start from the following standing commitment, which I will ask all students to accept as given: string theory has so far produced no empirical predictions that could distinguish it from alternatives, and there is a real possibility that this will not change for decades. The question the tree answers is what follows from that: how much credence the theory deserves in the meantime, and what kind of scientific status it has.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first settle on the questions whose answers jointly determine a position, then locate the actual positions in the resulting grid, and only afterwards attach the labels. The questions below are ones I want represented in some form, though you may rephrase them; each must be phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing which position it leads to:

1. Can considerations that are not observations of the world — for instance, the failure of a large, well-resourced, strongly motivated community to find any rival theory, over decades — legitimately raise the probability that the theory is true? Or is that just an observation about the sociology of physics?
2. If the answer to the first question is yes, does the effect admit a principled reconstruction — say, a Bayesian one in which the community's failure is evidence about the objective difficulty of the problem — or does any such reconstruction smuggle in the conclusion?
3. When the theory proves unexpectedly useful in problems it was not built for, such as counting black hole microstates in special cases or illuminating strongly coupled quantum systems, is that evidence for it as a picture of nature, or only for its value as mathematics?
4. If the theory permits a vast landscape of possible universes and our own must be one in which observers can exist, does that turn the non-prediction of the cosmological constant into a respectable post-diction?
5. If only a discriminating experiment can settle the matter, does that counsel shifting resources away from the theory, or continuing work under a different description of what that work is for?

The leaves must include: a strict falsificationist, for whom the theory is not yet science in any sense; a traditional empiricist, who allows that only discriminating experiments can confirm a theory but who may still value theoretical work instrumentally; a non-empirical confirmation Bayesian in the style of the physicists who defend assessing a theory's prospects by indirect indicators; an anthropic landscape proponent; a deflationist, for whom the theory is best understood as mathematics whose physical interpretation is not determined; and whatever further leaves the logical space generates, including a pragmatic leaf if the structure produces one.

I expect roughly six internal nodes and six to eight leaves. Where an honest answer at a node is genuinely unsettled rather than yes or no, give the node a third branch rather than forcing a binary choice. For each leaf, add one sentence on the objection that leaf finds hardest to answer. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, so that I know in advance which students will come out the far end indistinguishable. Finally, note any region of the logical space that no one occupies, and say whether you think that is because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. Please close with one worked example: a path through the tree for a hypothetical student, showing each answer and the leaf it delivers.
