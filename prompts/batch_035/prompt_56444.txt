So I've been reading up on the Nordic model for a policy class and something struck me as kind of weird. Sweden (and the countries that copied it) criminalizes buying sex but not selling it, and the official justification is that the seller is a victim, exploited, not doing anything wrong — the law is aimed at the demand side. Fine. But the same legal systems also treat the income from selling sex as taxable. The seller can get pursued for undeclared income like anyone else. And on top of that, the laws against procuring and brothel-keeping are still in force, which in practice means two sex workers sharing an apartment for safety can count as running a brothel, and anyone who helps — a driver, a landlord, a website — risks a procuring charge.

Here's what I can't figure out. A tax authority that says "declare this income and pay tax on it" seems to presuppose that selling sex is legitimate work — taxable income is income from an activity the state treats as a lawful occupation, and the whole point of the asymmetry was supposedly that it's NOT legitimate work, just not criminal for the seller. So is the Swedish package implicitly committed to sex work being real work after all, with only the rhetoric saying otherwise? Or is there a coherent way to hold all of this together — say, a view on which the state can tax activity it refuses to endorse, the way it might tax black market gains even while prosecuting the trade — and if so does that analogy actually hold up here, given that selling is explicitly legal?

I guess what I want is for you to reconstruct the implicit position: what would a legal regime have to believe about work, exploitation, and victimhood for this exact combination (selling legal + taxed, buying criminal, third-party assistance criminal) to make sense as a package? Where do the parts genuinely conflict, where is it just underdetermined — compatible with several different underlying theories — and what would have to change if the state bit the bullet one way or the other? Like, if it really treated sellers as victims, the consistent move might be exempting the income and routing everything through social services; if it treated them as workers, the procuring and sharing rules would need a serious rethink. Is there some third view that gets the actual Swedish combination more naturally than either of those?
