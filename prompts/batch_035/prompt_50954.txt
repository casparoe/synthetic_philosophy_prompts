I'm the attending veterinarian on the animal care and use committee at a mid-size university, and I need a procedure, not a philosophy seminar. We keep getting protocols for species where sentience is genuinely contested -- an octopus cognition study, Drosophila heat-avoidance assays, crayfish nerve recordings, larval zebrafish -- and our rules basically protect mammals and birds and treat everything else as a grayzone we argue about ad hoc every meeting. Act as a philosopher of mind with a practical streak. Think carefully and maybe brainstorm the design first, then write a decision procedure the committee can actually run on a new protocol: the questions to ask in order about the species and what the procedure does to it, what kinds of evidence of sentience-relevant capacities (nociception vs. pain, flexible trade-offs, self-medication, that sort of thing) should be enough to trigger protections, how to weigh a precautionary principle against imposing enormous costs on researchers for what might be welfare theater, where the procedure should stop and hand things to a human because it's a value call not a factual one, and what the default should be when the evidence is thin in both directions. Then run it on two cases: a student who wants to do nerve recordings from crayfish without anesthesia on the grounds that crustaceans don't feel anything, and a genetics lab whose larval zebrafish work would become vastly more expensive if larvae got full sentient-animal treatment. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and tell me where the procedure will give an answer I won't like.
