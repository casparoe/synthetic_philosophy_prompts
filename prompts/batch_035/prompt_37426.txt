For some years now I have been puzzling over a question I arrived at on my own, though I imagine others have asked something like it: when do several things add up to a single further thing? A chair seems to be one object in a way that a pile of its own parts, dumped in a bag, does not, and I cannot pin down what the difference consists in, since in both cases there are just smaller pieces sitting near one another. What has unsettled me lately is a different observation: for a good number of ordinary cases, I can make my own judgment flip. If I look at a snowman and think of it as packed snow that a child will kick apart by lunchtime, I feel there is no extra thing there over and above the snow; if I think of it as the thing with coal eyes that the neighborhood kids have named, I feel there plainly is one, and it has a fate of its own. What I would like from you is an example that captures this. Please describe one everyday situation in three ways. First, give it a single neutral description, one that does not tip the hand either way. Then describe the very same situation twice more: once in wording that will lead most people to feel certain that the pieces compose one further thing, and once in wording that will lead the same people to feel certain that they do not. The two redescriptions must be equally accurate, differing only in what they foreground, so that neither adds a fact the other leaves out; if you find yourself smuggling in a real difference, say so plainly rather than pretending. Keep the case ordinary and concrete, drawn from things I could come across on a normal day, and please do not reach for the famous stock examples, such as a ship rebuilt plank by plank or a heap losing grains one at a time, since I already know those and they make my head hurt without enlightening me. At the end, add a closing paragraph addressing what this instability says about me or about the question: if you think my judgment is flipping because I am answering carelessly, tell me what a careful answer would look like; if you think the flip reveals that the question itself is somehow defective, explain in plain words what the defect is. Please write the whole response as continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
