I've been putting together my settled views on moral sainthood and the shape of a good life — some taken from Susan Wolf's essay "Moral Saints," some drifted into since — and I'd like a consistency audit of the package. Here are the commitments, stated as I actually hold them:

(1) Wolf is basically right: a person whose every choice is made as morally good as it could be would come to lack the non-moral perfections — wit, cultivated taste, spontaneity — that make someone good company, and their life would go worse for it. I would not want to become that person, and I don't think I'm making any mistake in not wanting to.

(2) There is no threshold at which morality stops counting. If I can prevent a significant amount of suffering at modest cost to myself and don't, I am remiss; and someone who does more good, effectively and without wrecking themselves, is morally better than someone who does less. There is no level of goodness at which further goodness stops adding moral worth.

(3) A life can be excellent without being morally admirable. The self-absorbed violinist who pours everything into her playing, gives almost nothing away, and walks daily past suffering she could cheaply relieve can nonetheless live a wonderful and enviable life.

(4) The best lives are organized around a single consuming project. I admire the obsessive scientist or artist more than the well-rounded, well-balanced person, and I think that is the right ideal to point a child toward.

(5) A close friend of mine has now declined three dinners in a row for volunteer shifts, and each time I have thought she was right to and is admirable for it. At the same time, I think that if this continues and I quietly let the friendship lapse, that would be regrettable but not wrong.

Rank by severity rather than treating everything as equally bad, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. When you tell me a tension is only apparent, spell out exactly what dissolves it, since that is where I would most expect the audit to go wrong. Don't assume I know where the problem is or which commitment I'd drop — I don't. Finally, close with what would change your mind about your ranking: the argument or consideration most likely to overturn it, and the point at which your own reasoning here is most likely to fail.
