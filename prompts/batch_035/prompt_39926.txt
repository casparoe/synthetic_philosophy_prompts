I'm a recently retired geochemist -- 40 years of dating old rocks and running mass spectrometers -- and since retiring I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole, mostly philosophy of technology because it's the closest thing to what I actually did for a living. My old department has a monthly reading group of retired scientists and engineers, and last month we did Langdon Winner's 1980 paper "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (the Daedalus one, later folded into his book The Whale and the Reactor). It went badly in an instructive way: everyone except me immediately said "a bridge is a bridge, the politics lives in the people, not the steel," and I couldn't articulate why that felt too quick, coming from a lab where every instrument I ever used pushed my work down some paths and quietly foreclosed others.

So here's what I want: a decision tree I can hand out at the next meeting, which starts each of us off from something we'd all accept and walks us to a named position on whether artifacts have politics. The starting point should be neutral, something like "I use technologies every day and want to know whether any of them are political in themselves" -- not "do you agree with Winner," because half the group would say no on principle before reading a single node.

Some requirements, because I've thought about what went wrong last time:

1. Every node should be a question someone can answer from their own experience, phrased without jargon. Use concrete cases as the node questions: a speed bump; a nuclear plant vs. rooftop solar panels; a mainframe vs. a personal computer; a tractor sized for a thousand-acre operations vs. one sized for forty acres; and the overpass case from Winner's paper -- the Long Island parkway bridges supposedly built too low for buses, allegedly keeping riders without cars away from the beaches. Fair warning: I've read that the actual history there is disputed (some scholars claim the intent wasn't there and buses used the parkways anyway), so you can treat it as a hypothetical if the facts are shaky; just say so.

2. The branch splits should track distinctions that actually do the work, and I have opinions about which ones: whether the artifact *requires* a social arrangement or is merely *compatible* with one; whether the politics was intended by a designer, emerged from process, or transcends the intended/unintended category entirely; whether later users repurposing an artifact (kids jumping off a low bridge, homebrewers turning cheap PCs into something else) dissolves the embedded politics or just adds a new layer; and whether the constraint is a technical necessity or a reversible convention. Winner himself has a require/compatible distinction crossed with internal-to-the-system vs. external -- I'd like that four-way carve-up to show up somewhere, though you can argue with it.

3. Leaves that must appear, whatever you call them: the plain neutral-tool view (my reading group's default), Winner's strong thesis, the weaker contingency view where nothing is political in itself but settled designs acquire political inertia, the social-constructionist view where relevant social groups decide what an artifact even is, the view where morality gets delegated into objects (the speed bump as a cop made of asphalt), and something like the position that the fix is democratizing design decisions rather than choosing better artifacts. If there are natural further leaves, add them.

4. For every leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment. And for combinations of node answers that nobody actually defends, say so and guess why -- incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely can't seperate and resist the urge to fake a distinction.

The audience is smart but has zero patience for name-dropping as a substitute for argument, so a leaf label should come with substance, not just "so-and-so said."

Finally -- and this matters to me as someone who spent a career calibrating instruments -- end with a short note on how confident you are in the tree you drew, and single out the one branch point you're least sure actually carves reality at that joint. I'd rather know where the tree's weak joint is than recieve a clean-looking diagram I can't trust. And please don't recomend a reading list; the group has opinions about homework already.
