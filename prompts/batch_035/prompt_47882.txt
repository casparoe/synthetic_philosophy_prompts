I have a set of verdicts about who may exclude whom -- drawn from my own reactions, not from any theory -- and I want to know whether they add up to a principle, or whether I have to give some of them up. My suspicion is that they don't cohere, because at least two pairs pull in opposite directions. Here are the verdicts:

1. A homeowner may turn away a stranger who knocks at night, without owing anyone a reason. Clearly fine.
2. A private dinner party may simply not invite someone. Fine, obviously.
3. A country club may refuse membership to women. Not fine, even though the club is private and voluntary.
4. A state may deny entry to a foreign national convicted of violent crimes abroad. Fine, I think, though I can't say what grounds it.
5. A small island nation facing genuine ecological limits may cap total immigration well below what it could physically absorb. Fine.
6. A large, wealthy, underpopulated state may exclude immigrants to protect domestic wages. Not fine.
7. A state may exclude on the basis of ethnicity where a majority votes for precisely that. Not fine, obviously.
8. A state may exclude people descended from populations it displaced or colonized, when those people have nowhere else to go. Not fine -- and it seems the state is somehow uniquely disqualified from excluding them, which no ordinary capacity or security rationale explains.
9. A state may admit guest workers with no realistic path to citizenship and sharply limited rights, when it could instead admit them as full members. I keep flip-flopping: I want to say this is not fine, but I can't tell whether my objection is to the exclusion or to the terms of inclusion.

My tentative principle was: private individuals and small voluntary associations may exclude at will; states may exclude only on capacity or security grounds. But the country club and the island state break it -- the club is private, small, and voluntary, and its exclusion seems plainly wrong to me, while the island state's seems plainly right. So voluntariness and scale don't track my verdicts, and the colonizer case seems to involve a kind of standing that can be forfeited, which no capacity principle accommodates.

I'd also like to know where my verdicts sit relative to the two standard positions in this debate: the view that legitimate states have a right to exclude flowing from citizens' freedom of association, and the view that borders should be essentially open, with any exclusion needing special justification. I don't think my verdicts match either one cleanly, but I'd like to know which of them is closer, and what it would cost to patch.

- The verdicts regrouped (roughly 150 words): organize my nine cases by whatever seems to be doing the real work, not by how I listed them.
- Candidate principles (roughly 400 words): two or three principles stated precisely enough to generate verdicts on new cases -- say explicitly what each takes the wrong-making feature of exclusion to be.
- The hard pairs (roughly 350 words): country club versus island state, criminal exclusion versus wage protection, and the guest-worker case. Say which candidate handles each pair and at what cost in complexity or in overturned verdicts.
- Against the standard positions (roughly 250 words): which of my verdicts the freedom-of-association view and the open-borders view would respectively overturn, and whether either can be patched to keep them.
- What I must give up (roughly 150 words): tell me honestly which of my verdicts cannot survive any coherent principle here, and give one new case, not on my list, on which your favored principle predicts a verdict I am likely to find counterintuitive.
