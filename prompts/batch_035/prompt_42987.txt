I'm a second-year graduate student in environmental policy, and I've been asked to circulate a short position paper stating where I stand on the precautionary principle before our seminar on risk regulation. The trouble is that, writing my views out, I can't tell whether they form a coherent package or a pile of contradictions, so before I embarrass myself in front of my cohort I'd like a proper audit. Here is the set of commitments as they actually stand: (1) I endorse the activist formulation of precaution, roughly the one from the Wingspread statement — when an activity raises threats of serious or irreversible harm to health or the environment, scientific uncertainty about the magnitude or likelihood of the harm is not a legitimate reason to postpone preventive measures. (2) In such cases the burden of proof should shift to the proponent of the activity, rather than resting on regulators or the public to demonstrate harm first. (3) At the same time, I remain a defender of cost-benefit analysis as the general framework for regulatory decisions, including monetizing statistical lives and applying a standard discount rate to future costs and benefits. (4) I think it is a mistake to let astronomically small probabilities of astronomically large harms dominate policy — I take the standard response to Pascal's mugging to be basically right, and I want that discipline applied to low-probability catastrophe scenarios wherever they come from. (5) On nuclear power I've landed somewhere awkward: I hold a precautionary posture toward accident risk and long-lived waste, while also treating climate change as the paradigm case where precaution demands aggressive action — even though expanded nuclear power is one of the main candidate tools for that aggressive action. (6) I don't believe the precautionary principle is self-defeating; the objection that the principle itself risks harm by blocking beneficial technologies strikes me as a debating-society trick rather than a serious argument.

I'd like you to go through the six commitments and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and where the set is only apparently stable because I'm quietly equivocating between two notions — say, between uncertainty about probabilities and low probabilities themselves. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and for the worst one or two tell me what the cheapest available revision would be and what adopting it would cost me elsewhere in the package. For what it's worth, I genuinely cannot tell whether (5) is a real incoherence or just a hard empirical judgment call, and I can't see any way to make (1) and (4) sit together at all — but I'm prepared to be told I'm wrong about both, and that the real problem is somewhere I haven't looked.
