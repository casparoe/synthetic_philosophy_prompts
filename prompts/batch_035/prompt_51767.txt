I am a retired physical chemist who has recently taken up philosophy in earnest, and I find myself increasingly drawn to Hume, partly because his temperament as an empiricist feels familiar to me after a career in the laboratory. Having worked through his Essays and the Treatise, I now volunteer on the ethics committee of a local hospice, and a set of questions keeps nagging at me that I hope you can help me think through: what would Hume actually say about end-of-life practices that did not exist in his time, given the arguments in his essay on suicide?

The specific case I have in mind is continuous deep sedation, in which a terminally ill patient who is deemed to be suffering unbearably is kept permanently unconscious, without artificial nutrition or hydration, until death follows from the underlying disease. In several European jurisdictions this is treated as ordinary palliative care and legally distinct from euthanasia, since the physician does not administer a lethal agent but merely withdraws sustenance and suppresses consciousness. Would Hume accept this distinction? His essay argues that suicide violates no duty to God, to society, or to ourselves, and that our conduct is no more exempt from divine providence when we prolong life by medicine than when we shorten it by our own act. That suggests he might regard the sedation-versus-euthanasia line as a moral distinction without much substance — a difference in causal mechanism rather than in the agent's contribution to the outcome. But I can also imagine him, as a careful anatomist of our moral sentiments, saying the line tracks something real: perhaps our moral psychology treats an act and an omission differently, and morality for Hume is ultimately grounded in sentiment rather than abstract consistency.

A second question follows from his bundle theory of personal identity. We occasionally discuss so-called Ulysses contracts — advance directives in which a patient, while lucid, instructs that she not be force-fed or resuscitated once her dementia has progressed past a specified threshold. The later patient, however, often appears content and has no wish to die; the person who consented, on one reading, no longer exists. Does Hume's view of the self as a bundle of perceptions held together by resemblance and memory make such self-binding contracts incoherent or unnecessary, or does his insistence that identity is a fiction we project onto succession actually give us the tools to say that the earlier self has a legitimate claim to speak for the later one? I would appreciate a reasoned reconstruction of Hume's probable positions on both points, with attention to how his empiricism and his sentimentalism might pull in different directions.
