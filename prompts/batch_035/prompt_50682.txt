My cousin Maya turned down a job in another state last month because her dad — my uncle Ray — made it clear that if she took it, he was done with her for good. Except it came out over Thanksgiving pie that she never really wanted the job; she'd only applied to see if she could get it. That set off an argument between my mom and my friend Dana, who was over for dessert, and I honestly could not tell who was winning.

Mom said: "That wasn't a free choice. Ray would never have let her go. Even if she'd wanted to take it, he'd have found another way to stop her — guilt, money, showing up at her door. The ending was fixed before she opened her mouth."

Dana said: "But the threat never actually did anything. She stayed because she wanted to stay. You can't call a choice coerced when the coercion never had to fire."

Mom said: "Her wanting to stay IS the coercion. That want was installed by thirty years of living under Ray. The gun was at her head the whole time, she just stopped noticing it's there."

Dana said: "Then nobody raised by a difficult parent has ever freely chosen anything, and that obviously can't be right. She's 34 with a phone and a bus fare. People walk away from worse."

Then afterwards I fell down a rabbit hole and it turns out philosophers have a whole industry around cases like this — setups where the threat never fires because you'd have done the same thing anyway, apparently named after a guy called Frankfurt. I even found a thread claiming that in every such story there's still a split second where the person could have done otherwise, and that this tiny window is supposed to wreck the whole argument — no clue if that's a mainstream view or some fringe gripe, so don't just hand it to me as the consensus either way.
