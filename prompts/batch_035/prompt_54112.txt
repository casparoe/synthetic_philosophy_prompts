my roommate is deep into behavioral econ and has an argument she deploys every time weakness of will comes up, and I cant quite beat it. Here it is as she gives it:

(1) An act is akratic only if, at the moment you act, you judge that some other option is better all things considered, and choose anyway.
(2) But at that same moment you also most prefer the option you actually take — that's just what choosing is, acting on your strongest preference at the time (revealed preference and all that).
(3) So-called "better judgment" isn't some special faculty that gets outvoted; it's just the preference profile of your earlier, temporally distant self, and hyperbolic discounting guarantees that this distant self and your acting self want different things: from far off you prefer the larger-later reward, up close the smaller-sooner one.
(4) Neither self is more really you, so neither preference has more authority.
(5) The acting self's preference is therefore the only one with any standing at the moment of choice, and it wins — so the action is fully rational by the only standard available.
(6) Hence no one ever acts akratically; what we call weakness of will is just the distant self being a sore loser, complaining from the future about the outcome of a vote it lost.

She thinks this is liberating. But she also uses it to say stuff that seems like too much even for her. She says the alcoholic who sincerely says he wants not to drink is confused, since at the moment of lifting the glass he most prefers lifting the glass — that preference just is his, the same as any other. She says buying precommitment devices — the timed lockbox for your phone, Ulysses having himself tied to the mast — is straight-up irrational: you're spending real resources to frustrate your own future self's strongest preference, and that only makes sense if your future self's preference can be wrong, which by (4) it can't be. And I guess by the same token regret is unintelligible, since you'd be regretting that the wrong "self" won a fair vote.

I can see roughly where I want to push — something smells in either (2) or (3), or maybe the move from "neither self has more authority" to "the acting self has all of it" between (4) and (5) — but I can't turn any of it into an objection she'd have to respect. Which step should I reject, why did that step look so obvious, and what does rejecting it cost? For what its worth, I really want to keep it being possible to rationally buy the lockbox, and I'd honestly be fine conceding that the drinking self isn't irrational if that turns out to be the cheapest exit. And if the argument is actually valid all the way through, tell me which premise is least painful to give up instead.
