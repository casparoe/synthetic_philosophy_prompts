I'm a third-year philosophy major taking a seminar on social epistemology, and I'm trying to write a term paper connecting our unit on moral testimony to something outside the seminar room. The thing I keep coming back to is clinical ethics consultation. As I understand the practice (we had a guest lecture from a hospital ethics consultant), many hospitals maintain an ethics committee or a paid consultant whom clinicians and families can call when there is an unresolved conflict or uncertainty about what to do — typically around withdrawing life support, disputed capacity, or how much to disclose to a patient. And within the profession there seem to be two self-conceptions in circulation. On one model the consultant is essentially a facilitator: they structure a meeting, help everyone articulate their values and the relevant clinical facts, and mediate, but they take themselves not to be delivering a verdict on what ought to be done. On the other model the consultant is something more like a moral expert: they will say, in so many words, that continuing a given intervention would not be ethically appropriate, and hospitals treat that as a serious input to the decision. In practice many institutions blend the two, and I'm not sure the profession itself is settled on which it is.

My question is what this practice, and especially the tension between its two models, presupposes about moral knowledge. Issuing substantive recommendations seems to presuppose that moral expertise exists, that it can be acquired by people who are strangers to the patient and the family, and that it can be transmitted to the parties through testimony — that being told what a better-placed judge would conclude is a legitimate way to form your view about how your mother should be treated. But one of the recurring themes in the moral testimony literature is that there is something defective about forming moral beliefs on someone else's say-so: testimony might transmit true moral belief while failing to transmit moral understanding, or it might alienate the believer from her own judgment, or moral judgment might be essentially first-personal in a way that testimony can't bridge. If some version of that pessimism is right, is the facilitation-only model the only coherent one — and is the recommendation model implicitly committed to denying pessimism? Or does facilitation itself presuppose something substantial, e.g., that moral understanding cannot be outsourced and must be generated first-personally, so that a moral testimony optimist would have no principled reason to stop at facilitating? And where is the practice merely underdetermined — could, say, a hybrid "recommend but ensure the parties can reconstruct the reasoning for themselves" model be licensed equally by an optimist and a pessimist about moral testimony?

Please reconstruct the implicit epistemology of the practice, distinguishing what it strictly presupposes about moral knowledge and testimony from what it merely fits well with, and identifying the points where several rival views would all license what hospitals actually do. Where relevant, tell me what would have to change in the practice — in the training and certification of consultants, in what they are permitted to say in a family meeting, in how recommendations get documented — if we became convinced either that moral testimony is fine or that it is defective. One thing I'd like you to be careful about: please keep separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on moral testimony and clinical ethics from what is actually contested, and don't present one side of a live debate (for instance, over whether understanding really is distinct from knowledge, or over whether testimony is defective only when it displaces the listener's own deliberation) as if it were the consensus view.
