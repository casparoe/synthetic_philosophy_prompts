My closest friend and I spent most of a six-hour drive arguing about cause prioritization — in particular the importance, tractability, and neglectedness (ITN) framework that effective altruists use to rank causes and allocate effort. Her position is that the framework is a genuine contribution to practical ethics: it forces you to ask not just whether a problem is big, but whether anything you could do would help and whether others are already doing it. My position is that once you accept the three factors as a decision procedure, you are committed in principle to ranking literally everything by them, and that following the logic faithfully leads to absurdity — the neglectedness term in particular seems to reward working on trivial problems precisely because nobody else cares about them. We ended the drive exactly where we started, but the argument left me with an idea: if my real objection is that the framework is imperialistic, the most honest way to test that is to see how far it can be pushed before it becomes self-parody — or stops being parody at all.

I would like you to write a satirical piece for me. You are an experienced satirist with a strong background in moral philosophy and familiarity with the effective altruist tradition; before writing anything, take a moment to think carefully about which features of the ITN framework are genuinely vulnerable to satire — for example, neglectedness implying that overlooked trifles can outrank grave problems everyone already works on, tractability rewarding easy wins over important ones, the false precision of assigning 0–10 scores to suffering, and the way the framework quietly assumes a fixed budget of effort that gets crowded out by whatever you fund — rather than settling for cheap shots about spreadsheets and social awkwardness. The form: a parody of a polished lifestyle advice column, in which the columnist answers ordinary readers' letters — a stalled career, whether to attend a difficult family reunion, a friend's poorly attended charity bake sale — by applying ITN rigorously and in complete good faith, complete with numerical scores and an implied calculation of the reader's personal comparative advantage. The humor must come from the columnist being scrupulously correct: each answer should exaggerate a real structural feature of the framework rather than invent one, so that someone who actually does cause prioritization would recognize the piece as fair and laugh rather than wince. Include at least two exchanges in which the letter-writer pushes back on the advice and the columnist responds by escalating the framework rather than softening it. Keep the column around 700 words, and stay deadpan throughout — the columnist should never acknowledge that anything is unusual. Please begin your reply with a brief brainstorm naming the three or four framework features you intend to satirize and which letter will carry each, then write the column itself.
