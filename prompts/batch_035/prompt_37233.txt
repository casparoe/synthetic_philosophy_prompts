I teach an upper-level seminar on conditionals, covering material implication, Adams's thesis, Stalnaker's and Lewis's selection-function semantics for counterfactuals, the strict-conditional alternative, and Kratzer's restrictor analysis. I'm writing the final exam, and I want one essay question on the contrast between indicative and counterfactual conditionals that a student cannot answer by reciting the standard textbook example (the Kennedy pair) or by labeling one mood "epistemic" and the other "metaphysical" without doing any work with the labels.

To keep the question off the shelf, I've built the material around a single invented scenario, and I want the exam question to use it throughout. The scenario: after a footbridge collapses, investigators conclude that a corroded hanger bracket failed and caused the collapse. The indicative--counterfactual pair is: "If the corroded bracket didn't fail, something else caused the collapse" versus "If the corroded bracket hadn't failed, something else would have caused the collapse." The first sounds fine (given that the bridge did collapse, an alternative cause would have to be identified); the second sounds wrong (in the closest worlds where the bracket does not fail, the bridge presumably does not collapse at all). The question should make students use this pair, not the Kennedy pair, and should test whether they can say precisely why the two sentences diverge: what the modal base and ordering source look like on each reading, and why the material conditional gets both wrong.

The question must also include a second, more demanding component built on what the literature calls Sobel and reverse Sobel sequences, which I will gloss on the exam paper itself so the question tests reasoning rather than terminology recall. A Sobel sequence is a pair of counterfactuals, "If A, C" and "If A and B, not-C," which sound consistent when heard in that order (the second seems to describe a more remote possibility); a reverse Sobel sequence is the same pair heard in the opposite order, where the second sentence, "If A, C," tends to sound inconsistent with the first, as though the earlier, more specific verdict blocks the later, more general one. In my scenario the sequence is: "If the bracket had failed slowly, the sensors would have tripped" and "If the bracket had failed slowly and the backup relay had been offline, the sensors would not have tripped." Students must explain the asymmetry between the two orders and evaluate what it suggests: whether it favors a dynamic or strict treatment of counterfactuals over the variably strict one, or whether it can be dismissed as a pragmatic effect, and on what grounds one could decide.

Please deliver three things: (1) the exam question as it would appear on the paper, including the brief glosses of the Sobel and reverse Sobel terminology so the scenario is self-contained; (2) a model answer of about 700 words that resolves the bridge pair through the closest-world analysis, applies Adams's thesis to the indicative while explaining why it does not transfer to the counterfactual, and takes a defended position on what the order effect in the reverse Sobel data shows; and (3) a marking scheme out of 25 points built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth, plus for each point a one-sentence description of the mistake that separates a B answer from an A answer -- for instance, stopping at the brute contrast between epistemic and metaphysical modals without specifying the modal machinery, or writing off the order effect as "just pragmatics" without saying what would count as evidence for that. The question should be answerable in 75 minutes by a student who understands the material but has never seen this scenario, and it should be unanswerable by anyone who has only memorized the taxonomy.
