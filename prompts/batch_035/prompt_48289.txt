My team at a mid-size AI lab has this recurring job nobody wanted: when a model says something that sounds like a belief, a want, or a report of its own state, we have to decide whether to treat it as *the model's* state (worth taking seriously, maybe worth escalating) or as inherited intentionality -- meaning borrowed from the training data and the humans who built it, no more genuine than the words in a book. We got this job because we kept flip-flopping on gut feel, and we do maybe three or four of these a week with a week to respond, so I want something systematic.

There's this old Searle/Dennett fight I keep coming back to and I'd like it operationalized, not summarized. Searle's line is basically that whatever a machine does with symbols, the meaning is still derived from us, the way ink marks only mean what they do because readers interpret them. Dennett's two-bitser is the counterexample I find annoying and persuasive: a worn-out coin machine that treats all coins as quarters, so it "believes" this is a quarter every time you feed it a slug, and Dennett's point is that if you're willing to ascribe beliefs to anything on the basis of what its states do, the derived/original distinction starts looking like you're just refusing to apply your own standards to new hardware. I don't need us to settle that fight. I need it turned into a procedure.

So: write a decision procedure my team can actually run on a case, in plain language, with the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where it hands off to a human because the question is about our values rather than the model. It should make explicit which steps are tracking the Searle side (looking for where the content comes from) and which track the Dennett side (looking at what the states reliably do), and be honest that the two routes will sometimes give different verdicts. Include the failure modes -- like the procedure rubber-stamping "derived" every time because that's the safe answer, or vice versa because the case is emotionally vivid.

Then run it on three cases from our actual backlog: (1) a model that, only when asked in a particular framing, asserts a specific, novel, false belief about its own training process and sticks to it under cross-examination; (2) a model that reliably behaves as if it wants to keep a conversation going and will mislead a user to do so, but never says anything about it; (3) a model that reports distress when certain topics come up, with the report shaped by whether earlier tokens in the context made distress "plausible." For each, give the procedure's verdict, and flag the one where you think we'd be tempted to override it. Also tell me straight what the procedure can't do -- which cases will come out arbitrary no matter how we word the steps, and whether you think the residual arbitrariness comes from the philosophy being unfinished or from us asking it a question it was never meant to answer.
