I've drafted an essay making a specific interpretive claim about Martin Shubik's dollar auction and Barry Staw's escalation-of-commitment experiments, and I'd like comments on it. The essay's thesis is that the irrationality in the dollar auction is located at the wrong time slice: not at the moment a bidder escalates, but at the moment of the first bid. I'd appreciate comments on the argument, on accuracy, and on whether the essay is worth developing further.

The essay draft:

---

**Losing at the Second Bid: What the Dollar Auction Actually Shows**

The dollar auction is usually told as a story about sunk costs. An auctioneer offers a dollar to the highest bidder, with one twist: the second-highest bidder must also pay whatever they bid, and gets nothing. Bidding opens at a few cents, both bidders escalate past a dollar, and the game ends with someone paying, say, $1.60 for a dollar and someone else paying $1.55 for nothing. The standard moral is that the players are captured by their sunk costs: each escalation is justified by the money already on the table, so no one can afford to stop.

I think this gets the location of the mistake wrong. Consider the position of a bidder who has just bid 70 cents against a rival's 65. If the rival responds and the auction continues, the bidder now faces a choice between losing 70 cents and, at some future point, winning a dollar at a cost of more than a dollar. At this point in the game, escalating is not obviously irrational: the marginal cost of the next bid can be far below the expected marginal gain, given that the rival faces the same trap. Ex interim — given where they stand — the escalating bidder may be doing expected-value math correctly. The genuinely irrational act happened earlier, at the first bid: the bidder entered an auction knowing (or being in a position to know, having seen any dollar auction before) that they are the kind of agent who will not exit at a loss, and therefore that entering is equivalent to signing a contract with themselves that they cannot honor at a profit. The dollar auction is not a machine that converts rational agents into irrational ones over time. It is a machine that exposes an error of diachronic self-prediction made before the escalation ever begins. If you know you won't quit, quitting at the first bid was never optional — and bidding at all was the mistake.

This reading, if right, does two things. First, it drives a wedge between two mechanisms that the escalation literature tends to run together. The sunk-cost story is backward-looking: the agent is influenced by resources already spent, which on the textbook account is incoherent, since past expenditures cannot be affected by present choices. But there is a distinct, forward-looking mechanism — call it stake preservation — in which the agent is not influenced by the past money itself but by the prospect of becoming the agent who lost it. What the escalater wants is not to rescue the past investment but to avoid the identity of the person for whom it was a loss. This is not incoherent; it is a preference about one's own history rather than about the world's future, and preferences over histories are not obviously illegitimate. Staw's classic escalation experiments, in which subjects allocated more resources to a failing project the more of their own prior resources were tied up in it, are standardly read as confirmation of the backward-looking mechanism. But the experimental designs cannot cleanly separate the two: a subject who keeps funding a failing project may be unable to abandon sunk costs, or may be unwilling to become the person who abandoned them. The confound has been underappreciated.

Second, the reading suggests that the two literatures that grew up around this phenomenon — the game-theoretic analysis of the dollar auction itself, and the organizational-psychology literature on escalation of commitment — never properly merged, and that each has half the phenomenon. The game theorists have the shape of the trap: they can show that with two sufficiently stubborn players the auction can rise without bound, and they model escalation as an equilibrium pathology. What they lack is any account of why anyone is stubborn. The organizational researchers have the psychology: need for justification, aversion to admitting failure, personal responsibility effects. What they lack is the structure — an account of when the trap is armed and when it is not. A merged account would say: the auction structure determines whether escalation is available, and the psychological dispositions determine who walks into an armed trap. My claim about the first bid is the point at which the two halves join, because entering the auction is where the agent's self-knowledge (a psychological fact) meets the auction's payoff structure (a game-theoretic fact), and the failure to forecast one's own stubbornness is a failure of exactly the kind that neither literature studies in its own terms.

I develop this in three further sections, sketched here. One applies the reading to organizational life: a firm entering a market with a commitment structure like the dollar auction's (two rivals, exit costs, winner-take-most) should treat the entry decision, not the continuation decisions, as the site of rational scrutiny, and should perhaps adopt rules that bind its future self before entering — the familiar commitment-problem logic, but applied to self-binding against one's own escalation rather than against rivals. One considers the objection that the two-mechanism distinction collapses, since aversion to being the one who lost is itself just an indirect way for sunk costs to exert influence. One considers whether the self-prediction framing overintellectualizes the ordinary escalater, who may simply never deliberate at all at the first bid.

The comments I'd like:

1. On the central claim: is the argument that the first bid is the locus of irrationality sound? In particular, does the ex interim defense of escalation hold up — is it really true that escalating can be expected-value-correct from within the game — or does the possibility of exit at any point undermine it?

2. On the two-mechanism distinction: is the proposed separation between backward-looking sunk-cost influence and forward-looking stake preservation a real distinction or a verbal one? Is the objection in my final sketched section (that the second mechanism collapses into the first) fatal, or answerable?

3. On accuracy: I characterize the standard reading of both the dollar auction and the escalation experiments fairly briskly. Are there straw men here? Is it fair to say the game-theoretic and organizational-psychology literatures never properly merged, or is that a claim that would need heavy qualification?

4. On interest: setting aside correctness, is the reframe — self-prediction failure at entry rather than sunk-cost capture in the thick of it — worth developing, or is it a familiar point I'm presenting as novel? If it has been made in essentially this form, I'd want to know that.

5. On structure: the last three sections are only sketches. Which of them, if any, would you advise cutting or expanding, given the thesis?
