I work as a software engineer on dialogue systems, and a couple of years ago I began reading philosophy of mind on the side to make better sense of the arguments that keep cropping up in my field. The episode I'd like you to analyse is the 2022 Blake Lemoine affair at Google: an engineer concluded, after months of long conversations, that the company's language model LaMDA might be a person; internal reviewers rejected his concerns, he was placed on administrative leave, and he then went public with transcripts of conversations in which, among other things, the model described a fear of being switched off. Please treat those salient facts as given — I'm not asking you to adjudicate whether the model was, or wasn't, sentient, nor to assess Lemoine's conduct. The object of the exercise is the philosophical toolkit, not the verdict.

Run the episode through the problem of other minds, in both directions. First, as the theory illuminating the case: Lemoine's reasoning is recognisably the classic move from observed behaviour to an underlying mental life, so set out the mapping carefully — what played the role of evidence (the transcripts, his reports of the conversations), what the inferred inner state was supposed to be, and how the standard criteria we use for other humans and for animals apply or fail to apply here. I'd particularly like you to take seriously an oddity of the case: the behaviour in question is linguistic, the very class of evidence on which our attributions to other humans most directly rest, whereas the usual versions of the problem assume the evidence is animal behaviour or biology-anchored analogy. Does that make the inference to mind stronger, or does it expose that our everyday criterion was never really analogical in the first place?

Then, as the case tests the theory. It seems to me the problem of other minds was framed for other humans, and secondarily for creatures whose inner lives we infer from shared biology and pain behaviour; this episode presents something the framing arguably never anticipated — an artefact that produces, on demand, exactly the type of evidence we normally treat as decisive. So I'd like an honest account of where the standard tools go silent here, and whether the episode splits two things the public debate ran together: sentience, in the sense of there being something it is like to be the system, and intelligence or verbal competence. Finally, say what the episode shows about the theory itself — whether it counts as an anomaly, a confirmation that the criteria were always underdetermined, or a case that some other framing handles better — and resist the temptation to force the theory to speak where it has nothing to say.
