Suppose a small foundation can fund three early-career researchers for two years each to work on the concept of free-floating rationales — the idea, associated with Dennett, that there can be reasons, and good ones, without any mind that grasps, possesses, or acts for those reasons: the rationale for a butterfly's wing pattern, for the organizing principles of embryonic development, for the division of labor in an ant colony, all present in the design of things even though no designer or understander is anywhere to be found. The foundation's board disagrees about almost everything downstream of the basic idea, so your job is to give them a defended research agenda rather than a survey.

Which open questions in this area most deserve the funding, and in what order? Candidate questions the board has flagged include: (1) whether free-floating rationales are genuinely reasons or merely reason-*like* regularities — whether saying the stink bug's coloration is "for" deterring predators attributes a real normative status or just records a selection history; (2) whether such rationales are irreducibly perspectival, existing only relative to an intentional-stance observer who could in principle articulate them, or whether they obtain observer-independently; (3) how far the notion extends beyond biology — to artifacts, to self-organizing physical and chemical systems, to machine-learning models whose internal structures embody rationales no engineer intended or understands; (4) whether free-floating rationales can be *wrong* or *mistaken* in a way analogous to false belief, or whether error requires a representing mind; and (5) whether the concept can ground a naturalized account of the normativity of biological function, or whether it quietly presupposes the normativity it was meant to explain. You may add or subtract questions, though the board would want any addition justified.

For each question you prioritize, explain why it ranks where it does: its importance (what turns on the answer, for philosophy of mind, philosophy of biology, or metaphysics more generally), its tractability for conceptual rather than empirical work within a two-year window, its neglectedness relative to how much it matters, and — most importantly — its position in the dependency structure, i.e., which other questions on the list are downstream of it and would be settled or transformed by progress on it. Where you deprioritize a question, say whether that is because it is unimportant, intractable, or merely a disguised restatement of a question elsewhere on the list. Also indicate what sort of intermediate progress would look like, so the foundation can tell after a year whether a project is succeeding. Finally, note explicitly what you take to be the single biggest point of leverage in the area — the question whose answer would most reorganize the rest — and defend that choice against the most plausible alternative.
