I am a philosophy of mind graduate student applying for a one-semester departmental research grant, and the application requires the proposal itself in a strict format: a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Please write it in American English, and do not include citations or a bibliography; I will supply references myself.

My half-formed idea concerns pain asymbolia and what it shows about the badness of pain. Asymbolic patients report feeling pain at normal intensity yet are indifferent to it: they do not withdraw, flinch, or request relief. Philosophers deploy this syndrome as a single weapon, but I suspect it is being aimed at two different targets, because the literature runs together two kinds of case that need separating. First, cases where pain is felt but motivationally inert, so that it simply fails to move the subject. Second, cases where pain is felt, motivationally live, and yet welcomed under a description: the distance runner who calls the burn in her legs good pain, or the masochist for whom the pain is sought rather than merely endured. My hypothesis is that the standard theories of pain's badness — accounts on which unpleasantness is a sensory representation of bodily disturbance, accounts on which pain's content is imperative (a command to desist), and accounts on which unpleasantness consists in a negative attitude taken toward the sensation — sort differently across these two kinds of case, and that arguments built from one kind are silently doing work that only the other kind could do. Congenital insensitivity to pain, where nothing is felt at all, sets the zero point against which both kinds are measured, but it is not where the philosophical action is.

The five items should be: (1) the question, stated so that it has a determinate answer; (2) why the existing positions leave it open, framed in terms of the two-case distinction above; (3) the method, meaning what I would actually do, phase by phase across the semester; (4) what a result would look like and what the deliverable would be; and (5) what would count as the project failing. Item (5) should also distinguish genuine failure from the outcome I would most expect: that the two kinds of case come apart for some theories of badness but not others, yielding a partial result worth reporting.
