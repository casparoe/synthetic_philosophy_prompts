I've been working through the literature on personal identity, and I keep noticing that the debates about the teletransporter — the machine that scans you, destroys you, and builds an exact replica on Mars — talk past each other because nobody has agreed on what a theory is supposed to be *for*. Some writers treat the question as one of numerical identity (is the replica literally me?), others as one of prudential rationality (should I care about stepping in?), and others as one of ethics (does the replica inherit my debts and my promises?). Before I read any more, I want the specification first: write the desiderata that any adequate theory of what matters in survival has to meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and be explicit about what counts as getting the case right.

To make the requirements concrete, work with cases like the following. The branch-line case, where the original survives on Earth for a few days after the replica wakes on Mars, and you must say whether the days on Earth can be prudentially discounted by the fact that the replica exists. A slow-fission case, where the two hemispheres of my brain are transplanted into two donor bodies thirty years apart, so that one candidate survivor is continuous with me all along while the second pops into existence only decades later. And what I'll call the year-old scan: the machine stores my scan, I live an additional uneventful year, and only then the original is destroyed and the replica is printed from the stored, stale scan — where it seems to matter that a year of experiences got stranded, and a good theory should explain why that regret is or isn't coherent. Beyond cases, the theory must respect certain distinctions — between numerical identity and what matters, between what I may rationally anticipate and what I'm obligated to deliver to someone, between survival being binary and being a matter of degree — and it should say which of these are real constraints and which are artifacts of one camp's framing. It also has to cohere with facts from neuroscience that bear on the psychological-continuity picture, such as the gradual turnover of neural populations over a lifespan and the behavior of split-brain patients, without letting those facts quietly decide the metaphysics.

Finally, mark which desiderata you take to be non-negotiable and which are parochial demands of particular schools, and flag any pair or subset you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I suspect, for instance, that demanding a determinate answer in every fission case while also demanding that the answer track our considered convictions about ordinary gradual change may be jointly untenable, and I want to know whether the list itself, so to speak, is the real result. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in eliminating candidate theories, and why. If you want to close with a brief scorecard of how the main contenders — a pure psychological-continuity view, animalism, and a closest-continuer or no-continuity-needed view — fare against the list, keep it short: the criteria are the part I care about, not the scorecard.
