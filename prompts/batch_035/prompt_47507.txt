I'm a software engineer who reads a lot of philosophy on the side (mostly blog posts and podcast discussions — no formal training), and I've become weirdly obsessed with a cluster of puzzles about what to do when you get evidence about the quality of your own reasoning: peer disagreement, being told you're drunk or sleep-deprived, learning that a drug you're on scrambles a small percentage of users' reasoning while leaving them feeling fine. I run a small newsletter for similarly amateur readers, and for the next issue I want a "which view of higher-order evidence do you actually hold?" quiz — the sort where you answer questions about concrete cases and only find out at the end which position your pattern of answers corresponds to.

Please write me eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key. The cases should include at least these five scenarios, since I've noticed they seem to pull my own intuitions apart and I want readers to feel that friction: (1) the classic restaurant-bill calculation where your epistemic peer gets a different answer and you know from long experience that you're each right about equally often on this kind of arithmetic; (2) a case where you feel entirely sober but a reliable friend tells you that you've had far more to drink than you think and that your reasoning is impaired; (3) a drug that permanently scrambles reasoning in 10% of users while making them feel more clear-headed than ever, which you've taken and now feel more clear-headed than ever; (4) an argument that strikes you as airtight but whose conclusion a panel of experts unanimously and confidently rejects; and (5) a Cartesian demon that you somehow learn guarantees that some specific fraction of your total beliefs are false, without telling you which. For each question, the answer options should describe what you'd do or believe, not which theory you'd endorse — no question should amount to "are you a conciliationist?"

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least these positions, whatever you end up calling them in the explanation: the conciliationist view that peer disagreement itself gives you reason to moderate your credence; the steadfast view that first-order reasoning can rationally swamp the higher-order evidence; the deflationary view that evidence about your evidence is just more ordinary evidence to weigh, with no special privilege and no special grip; the view that rationality forbids believing yourself irrational, so the higher-order defeater simply wins automatically; and the heretical view that akrasia — believing p while believing your belief in p is irrational — is sometimes the only rational option. Where two positions can't really be separated by case judgments, say so rather than inventing a question that only works because it smuggles in theory, and please include one combination of answers that corresponds to no defensible position, noting briefly why nobody should end up there. Two requests on epistemic hygiene: since my readers and I are amateurs, keep the jargon minimal and flag where you're using a label that professional philosophers would contest; and at the end of the quiz, include a short note on how confident you are in the mapping itself, singling out the part of the taxonomy you're least sure actually carves the debate at its joints.
