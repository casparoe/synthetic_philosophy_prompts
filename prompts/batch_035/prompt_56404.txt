I'm a sixth-form student who runs a small philosophy discussion group at school, and next term we're doing a session on whether insects might be sentient and what follows from the sheer number of them. I've been reading around the topic, and I've noticed that people who say "insects probably aren't conscious, but they might be, and there are something like ten quintillion of them" end up in wildly different places: some conclude we should care a lot, some barely at all, and some say the very question of how to combine a small probability with a vast number is where the real disagreement lives. I want to build a decision tree that sorts my classmates (and me) by what we actually believe, rather than by which label we'd pick for ourselves.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher specialising in animal ethics and practical reasoning under moral uncertainty, and think carefully before you begin — I'd like you to start with a short brainstorming section where you work out which questions genuinely split the field, and only then build the tree from those questions.

The deliverable is a decision tree with roughly eight to twelve branch nodes and around six to nine leaves. Each node must be phrased as a question about a concrete case or claim that someone could answer without knowing which position it leads to — for example, cases involving pest control at a school, a bee's reaction to a threatening stimulus versus what looks like simple avoidance learning, or a choice between donating a fixed sum to a human-focused charity and to an organisation working on insect welfare. Please include at least one node about whether sentience is an all-or-nothing matter or comes in degrees, and at least one about whether moral decisions under uncertainty should work like expected-value calculations or whether some threshold- or rights-based reasoning should block the multiplication of small probabilities by huge numbers.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position it corresponds to (or "unnamed" if nobody really defends it), plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. After the tree, please add three short notes: (1) any pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate, and why; (2) any combination of answers that leads to a genuinely unoccupied position, with your best guess as to whether it's unoccupied because it's incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored; and (3) which single node you think does the most work, in the sense that my classmates will be least able to answer it without already smuggling in their conclusion.

Please keep the register accessible to bright seventeen-year-olds — technical terms are fine but must be explained when first used — and please do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, since I'll be printing this as a handout and want it self-contained. Write in British English throughout.
