So at my school we have this committee with a few students and a few teachers called the Academic Standards Committee that votes on attendance policy and stuff, and I'm the junior rep this year. Our principal wants us to vote on a new rule next month: if you have more than 10 unexcused absences in a semester for any one class, you automatically fail that class, no exceptions, no appeals. She says the current system is too mushy because teachers all handle it differently and kids basically get lucky or unlucky depending on who their teacher is.

The weird thing is the argument that broke out at the last meeting. My history teacher (Mr. D.) basically said no fixed line can be justified: if 9 absences is okay and gets you a passing grade, how can one more day flip you to failing? One extra missed Tuesday can't literally change whether you learned the material. And you can run the same argument down the ladder — if 9 is fine, 8 must be fine, and so on until missing zero days and missing a whole semester look the same, which is obviously nuts. But he says that means the rule is arbitrary wherever we put it, so we should stick to teacher judgment instead. The vice principal countered that every rule has this problem (like, why is the driving age exactly 16 or whatever our state says and not 16 and a day) and you can't run a school on "use your judgment" because that's exactly the mushiness we're trying to fix, plus judgment is where bias sneaks in.

They kind of reached a stalemate and now it's on me to write something up before the next meeting since I missed the deadline for the last one lol. Can you draft the written opinion I'll circulate to the committee? It should give a clear recommendation we can actually vote on (adopt the rule as-is, reject it, or adopt some modified version — like maybe a grace period, or an appeals process, or a sliding scale of consequences instead of pass/fail), plus the reasons. Please take Mr. D's argument seriously rather than just waving it off, but also take seriously the vice principal's point that vague standards get applied unfairly. If you think there's a smarter version of the rule that keeps the fairness of a bright line while admitting the line is kind of arbitrary, I'd honestly love to hear it, because that's probly what I actually believe but I can't articulate it. Keep it short enough that people will actually read it before the meeting, maybe a page.
