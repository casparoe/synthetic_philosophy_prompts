My brother-in-law thinks convention-based obligations are all fake, and he ambushed me with an argument over Thanksgiving that I can't quite demolish even though I'm pretty sure it's wrong somewhere. It goes:

(1) The content of a convention is arbitrary: 15% vs 20%, driving on the right vs the left, first-come-first-served vs take-a-number — any of these would coordinate about as well as any other, so there was never a reason to pick the one we picked.
(2) You can't be obligated to do something when every alternative open to you would have done just as well. Being under an obligation means having a reason that rules something out, and where the options are all on a par, nothing gets ruled out.
(3) So nobody is ever obligated to comply with a convention as such. (You might be obligated for independent reasons — don't steal the waiter's wallet — but the convention itself generates nothing.)
(4) So nobody is obligated to tip or stand in line, and a visitor has no obligation at all to learn the local stuff when traveling, which he was very pleased about since he travels constantly and hates tipping.

When I pushed back that this would swallow promising too, he said fine, and laid out the parallel himself: (5) which noises or marks count as a promise is just as arbitrary as the tipping percentage — another country, another era, a completely different formula does the binding. (6) So by his own steps nobody is obligated to keep a promise, and he says he'll happily bite that bullet (promising is just a habit policed by reputation, nothing more). But when I extended it to money — what counts as a dollar, what a debt even is, convention all the way down — he started squirming, because now it looks like nobody owes anybody anything, ever. And honestly I squirmed too, because I can't see what, inside his argument, distinguishes promising from any other convention.

I want to reject something between (1) and (4), but whenever I try, he says the convention itself can't be the thing that makes the difference, because the convention is exactly what's authority is in question — pointing at the rule to justify the rule. Is that move actually as airtight as he thinks it is? Where does the argument go wrong (assuming it does)? Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? For what its worth I'd like to keep (2) if possible, since it also blocks a couple of other arguments I like, but tell me honestly if that's the one that has to go.
