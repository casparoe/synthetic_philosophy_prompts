So here's my situation: I'm a science-and-ideas reporter, and I've been circling a piece on how people decide to have children — the whole "you can't know what you're signing up for until you're living it" problem. The obvious philosophical hook is L.A. Paul's work on transformative experience: the idea that some choices (becoming a parent, going blind, becoming a vampire) can't be made rationally in the standard way, because you'd have to weigh the pros and cons of an experience you literally cannot imagine from where you stand, and the experience itself will change the very utilities you'd be weighing with. I rewatched Arrival last week for background, and it hit me that this movie is basically a laboratory for that exact problem, except it does something the philosophy papers can't. Spoiler alert for a movie everyone's seen, but: Louise learns the heptapod language, and instead of just letting her talk to aliens, it rewires her experience of time so she genuinely perceives her whole life at once — including her daughter's childhood and early death. And she still chooses to have her, and Ian, who presumably has the same information handed to him secondhand, experiences the choice as unforgivable.

Here's the bit I want help with, and it's a little in the weeds. It seems to me the film runs the Paul problem backwards, twice over. First, Louise's decision isn't made from the near side of a transformative experience — by the time she chooses, the language has already transformed her, so she's deciding with full epistemic access from the far side, which is precisely the position Paul says we can never occupy. Does that make her the one parent in fiction whose choice actually satisfies the standards of rational decision theory, or does the film quietly concede Paul's deeper point that even with all the information, the choice isn't the kind of thing that information settles? Second — and this is the angle I'm most excited about but least sure of — the famous Sapir-Whorf setup means the transformation is literally linguistic, and I wonder whether the film itself works as an analogous device for the audience: the twist restructures the audience's memory of everything we've watched, so we undergo a cheap, secondhand version of Louise's transformation, which seems to challenge the claim that transformative knowledge can't be communicated. Or is that self-flattering nonsense, since I've still never had a child and am just borrowing the emotional outline of one?

I'd love a reading that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously: which specific scenes actually do the philosophical work (the disclosure to Ian, the hospital scenes, the montage where we realize the "memories" were flash-forwards, the ending), where the film resists being an illustration of Paul's argument rather than a dramatization of it, and honestly whether the film smuggles in some tendentious assumption — e.g. that a life visibly containing devastating loss is still worth choosing — that the philosophical debate is supposed to leave open. Also tell me where the reading breaks: is the nonlinear-time element just sci-fi chrome that does no philosophical work, or is it load-bearing?
