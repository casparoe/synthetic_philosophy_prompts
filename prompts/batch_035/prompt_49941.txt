I'm writing a term paper for a seminar in moral psychology that pairs Kafka's The Trial with the philosophical and anthropological literature on guilt and shame, and I've hit a puzzle I can't resolve on my own. The framework I'm using is the familiar one: guilt is the emotion of a self that has done a bad act — it looks for a charge, a defense, apology, repair — while shame is the emotion of a self exposed as defective; it looks toward hiding or disappearance, and its trigger is the witnessing other, real or internalized. Classicists and anthropologists once sorted whole cultures this way, and philosophers like Bernard Williams and Gabriele Taylor have complicated the picture by arguing that shame need not be mere conformity to social expectation but can be a response to genuine failure. That's the literature. My problem is the novel.

Read through a guilt-structure, The Trial seems incoherent: Joseph K. is arrested without a charge, the court never names an offense, and every hearing is adjourned to a further hearing — guilt without a deed looks like a contradiction. Read through a shame-structure, however, a great deal snaps into focus. What the arrest actually costs K. is exposure before his colleagues at the bank rather than any sanction; the scenes that wound are the ones with audiences (the two warders being flogged in a storeroom at the bank where K. cannot stop watching, the client Block creeping on all fours in the lawyer's bedroom while Huld displays him, the priest's sermon in the dark cathedral addressed to K. alone); and the ending names shame outright. The final sentence has K. say "Like a dog!" and the narrator add that "it was as if the shame of it must outlive him." Yet the one thing missing from the whole shame-reading is shame in K. himself: he feels indignation, self-pity, and at the end something like complicity, but he never blushes, and to the last he demands the guilt-framework's goods — a charge, a defense, a possible acquittal — as though the court were a court.

So here is what I'd like from you. Give me a reading of the novel as a study in how shame can be administered institutionally: does the court need K. to feel anything at all, or does it run entirely on what others see in him, so that his insistence on guilt-logic — his treating the proceedings as a case he can win — is itself the mechanism of his humiliation? Work through individual scenes and tell me where they support the lens and where they strain it: the flogging, the painter Titorelli's account of the possible outcomes, the parable of the doorkeeper as the priest tells it. And be honest about where the reading breaks down: is the guilt/shame distinction simply too tidy for a book that also seems to be about law, theology, and bureaucracy, and if so, what in the text would you point to as evidence that Kafka is doing something the moral-psychology literature — which mostly asks which emotion has the better moral credentials and whether a culture can choose between them — does not capture? I'd like the paper to close by saying what the novel adds to that debate that the arguments by themselves don't.
