A philosophy podcast I listen to recently ran an episode on whether practical stakes affect knowledge, and I transcribed a passage that has been bothering me. Here is what the host said:

"Epistemologists discovered something surprising about the word 'knows.' It turns out whether you know something depends on the practical stakes. Imagine you're standing in line at a bank on a Friday afternoon, deciding whether to come back tomorrow, and you're pretty sure — you saw the schedule last week — that the bank is open on Saturdays. If nothing rides on it, you know the bank will be open tomorrow. But suppose you have a large payment due Monday and the lines are short; now you don't know, even though your evidence hasn't changed one bit. Philosophers call this pragmatic encroachment, or sometimes epistemic contextualism — two names for the same position, associated with philosophers like Keith DeRose and Jason Stanley. The view says that the semantic standards for 'knows' go up and down with the stakes, so the word itself changes its meaning from one conversation to the next. This was established by the famous bank cases and the airport cases, where everyone's intuitions confirm that knowledge rises and falls with how much the person cares about being right. What's really striking is that there's now a broad consensus behind this, because the old-fashioned view that knowledge depends only on evidence, truth, and the like — call it intellectualism — has been basically abandoned. And once you accept that stakes shift knowledge, the deeper lesson follows: whether anyone knows anything is relative to the conversational setting, so there's no fact of the matter about what a person knows, full stop."

Something about this feels off to me. I've heard that pragmatic encroachment and contextualism are actually distinct, even rival, positions, and the host's closing claim sounds stronger than what precedes it. But the episode is by people with real philosophy training, so I don't trust my own suspicions.
