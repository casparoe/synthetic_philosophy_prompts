i've basically bought the Aumann view: if two agents have common priors and each treats the other as rational and honest, then mere back-and-forth announcements of posteriors should force them to agreement, so genuine "agreeing to disagree" can't happen between them. And I take the further step a lot of people stop short of: I think this tells us something about real disagrements between smart people. If two comparably informed, comparably capable people walk away from a debate still split, at least one of them did something epistemically wrong -- wasn't actually updating, was being dishonest (maybe with themself), or never really had a common prior, which I count as a failure too. What would count against this? I don't want the standard shoulder-shrug about humans not being ideal Bayesians -- I want the specific observations, arguments, and technical results that should actually move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Empirical stuff on how real experts diverge, results on how long convergence actually takes, worries that the common prior assumption smuggles in the conclusion, problems with logical omniscience, whatever. Which of these would the view's defenders concede as counting against it, and which do they wave off? And be honest about whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability -- and if it has, whether that's a fatal flaw for a theorem-based view like this or just what you'd expect. Tell me how confident you are in the ranking and flag the single point you're least sure of.
