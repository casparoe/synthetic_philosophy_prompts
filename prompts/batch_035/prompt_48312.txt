I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and technology, and this term I want to open with a unit on what people in AI safety actually mean by "the alignment problem." My students are philosophers with no ML background, and I have noticed that the term is used in the literature to cover at least four things that need not come together: making a system do what some principal intends, making it pursue values that would survive idealized reflection, making it defer to or tolerate correction by its operators, and making the surrounding training-and-evaluation process produce systems that are safe in the relevant sense. Whether that list decomposes into one problem with several facets, or several problems misleadingly gathered under one banner, is itself disputed, and I want the seminar to start by mapping this territory rather than by assuming any particular carving of it.

For the first session, please design a decision tree for a handout that takes a student from the starting commitment "I accept that sufficiently capable AI systems could cause catastrophe unless we get something right in advance" to a named position on what alignment requires and where it resides. Each branch node should be a question a student could answer without knowing the labels, and where possible anchor the node in a concrete case: a system that satisfies its stated instructions while violating obvious intent; a system that is perfectly corrigible but useless; a system that behaves impeccably in evaluation and badly in deployment; a system whose internal goals are opaque but whose behavior is reliably prosocial. I would like the tree to build from the logical space rather than from a roster of authors: first the questions that genuinely split the positions, then the positions located in the resulting space. The splits I expect to matter include at least these, though I leave you to revise the list: whether there is a fact of the matter about what a system ought to be aligned to, or alignment is only ever relative to some chooser; who or what counts as the target when the target is a chooser (a single principal, stakeholders, humanity, an idealized process); whether alignment resides in the artifact's internal states, its input-output behavior, the process that produced it, or the institutional scaffolding around it; and whether corrigibility is an endpoint of alignment or merely an instrumental waystation. Aim for six to nine branch questions and perhaps ten to fourteen leaves, including the obvious candidates — intent-alignment views, ambitious value learning, deference or assistant-style positions, corrigibility-as-endpoint views, positions on which alignment is a property of the sociotechnical system rather than the model, and views that make alignment hostage to substantive moral conclusions.

I also want to impose two requirements. First, my own half-formed scaffold is that any answer to "what is the alignment problem?" must say three things: what the target of alignment is, what in the world could carry or realize alignment, and what counts as success. Please use this scaffold, revise it, or reject it outright — but be explicit about which you are doing, since the shape of the tree depends on that choice. Second, wherever the tree places a position at a leaf, flag pairs of leaves it cannot genuinely separate (I suspect, for instance, that interpretability-first and behaviorist evaluation views will not be pulled apart by the value questions alone, one being a method and the other a success criterion) and identify unoccupied combinations of branch answers, saying for each whether nobody holds the view because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Finally, a request about intellectual honesty. Throughout the handout, distinguish what is settled bookkeeping from what is genuinely contested among people who know the field: where a distinction is near-terminological, say so, and where a framing itself embodies a contested substantive commitment — including my own three-part scaffold and the very claim that "alignment" names one problem at all — mark it as contested rather than presenting it as consensus. My students should leave the first session knowing not only where they stand on the map but where the mapmakers disagree about the map. No citations, no bibliography; names of positions and short glosses are enough.
