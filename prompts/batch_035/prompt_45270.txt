"The feeling theory is the oldest view, going back to William James. On this view an emotion just is the feeling of bodily changes -- you don't cry because you're sad, you're sad because you cry, as James famously put it. The main problem is that the bodily profiles of different emotions overlap too much. Fear and anger both involve elevated heart rate, sweating, stomach tension, so if emotions were just feelings of bodily states we couldn't tell them apart. That seems like a fatal problem for James.

The judgment theory, developed by Robert Solomon and later by Martha Nussbaum, and also defended by Jesse Prinz with his idea of embodied appraisals, says that an emotion is an evaluative judgment about how things stand relative to one's concerns. Grief is the judgment that something of great value has been lost; shame is the judgment that one has failed in front of others. The view explains how emotions can be about the world and can be assessed as rational or irrational, which pure feelings can't be.

The perceptual theory says emotions are like perceptions of value or significance. But since judgments and perceptions both represent the world as being a certain way, the judgment theory and the perceptual theory are really the same view under two names, so choosing between them doesn't matter.

The strongest objection to the judgment theory is recalcitrant emotions, like being afraid of a dog while fully judging that it is harmless. But I don't think this objection actually works. If you genuinely judged the dog was dangerous, then... sorry, harmless -- you would not fear it. So the lingering fear just shows you haven't fully made the judgment that the dog is safe; some part of you still judges it dangerous. The objection assumes what it's trying to prove. So the judgment theory survives, and it's clearly the best of the three."
