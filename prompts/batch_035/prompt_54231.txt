I'm a sociology postdoc presenting at a Friday seminar in an economic history department, with several economic sociologists in the room, and I need a Q&A prep sheet for the talk.

The thesis I'm defending is a deliberately weakened version of Weber's Protestant ethic argument. I am not claiming that Calvinist anxiety about salvation motivated people to accumulate capital. My claim is about causal structure, not motivation: values enter economic history as filters rather than engines. On my telling, the doctrinal content of ascetic Protestantism did not supply the energy for capitalist behavior -- material interests and institutional constraints do that just fine -- but it changed the cost structure of exiting certain practices: literacy for Bible reading made numeracy cheaper, communal surveillance of sobriety and diligence made shirking expensive, and congregational membership tied reputational credit to behavioral regularities that banks and suppliers could then price. Once those regularities were priced, they could persist without the doctrine. So culture, on my view, is a cause only in the way a sieve is a cause of what ends up in the pan: it selects among equilibria that material forces would otherwise leave indifferently available. The upshot I want the room to accept is that "can culture be a cause?" has a defensible yes-answer once causes are read as selection pressures rather than pushes, and that this is enough to keep Weber interesting without committing to the strong thesis everybody enjoys refuting.

The audience: about fifteen economic historians who think in terms of identification, natural experiments, and measurement, plus three economic sociologists of an institutionalist bent who are sympathetic to culture talk but hostile to anything that smells like idealism, plus one rational-choice type who thinks all values reduce to preferences over constraints. They have all read Weber as an undergraduate and set him aside. They will not raise the standard philosophy-of-social-science objections about verstehen or the interpretive tradition; they will raise ones about evidence, mechanism, and whether my view does any distinctive work. Do not give me the objections a philosophy department would raise about reasons and causes; I will not get those.

Please prepare the sheet as follows: roughly eight objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage each would do if answered badly, each phrased the way an economic historian would actually put it, with a two-minute reply I can deliver standing up, and a note on where that reply is soft -- I want to know where I am conceding more than I am admitting. At least one objection should come from the sympathetic institutionalists, attacking from my own side (for instance, that institutional stickiness alone explains persistence, so the cultural filter is doing no work). Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me what makes it dangerous and whether my position can survive an honest answer or only a deflection. Finally, tell me what I should concede up front in the introduction so that it never has to be dragged out of me at the podium.
