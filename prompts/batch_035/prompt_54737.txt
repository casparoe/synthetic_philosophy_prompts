My friend thinks he can prove that every vague predicate has sharp borders we can never know, and I can't find where it slips:

(1) If there's no fact of the matter whether n grains of sand make a heap, then things could have gone either way: a way where n grains are a heap, and a way where n grains aren't.
(2) Whether n grains make a heap depends only on the grains themselves -- how many there are and how they sit. Fix that and youve fixed everything relevant.
(3) The number of grains is held fixed across the two "ways" from (1), so if both were genuinely possible, heap-hood would depend on something beyond the grains. By (2), it doesn't.
(4) So for every n, it's already settled whether n grains make a heap. Which means there's a sharp cutoff, down to the single grain, between non-heaps and heaps.
(5) And we could never know where that cutoff is, since every candidate looks exaclty as good as its neighbors.
(6) So vagueness is just ignorance of sharp borders.

Worse, he says you can rerun the whole thing one level up: if it's not settled whether it's *definite* that n grains are a heap, then "definitely a heap" could have gone either way, and by the same reasoning every "definitely" has a sharp border too, and so on up. He admits that part feels like proving too much, but says he'll take it.

I keep suspecting the trick is in what "could have gone" means somewhere, but every time I try to pin it down it wriggles loose. Which step is the one to give up on, and what am I paying for it?
