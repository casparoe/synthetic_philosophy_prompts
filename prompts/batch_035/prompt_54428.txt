I'm a software engineer, definitely not a philosopher, so bear with me. I've gotten pretty into democratic theory from podcasts and stuff, and the idea I keep coming back to is sortition — picking decision makers by lottery instead of elections. The pitch, as I understand it, is that randomly selected ordinary people don't have reelection incentives, aren't pre-filtered by party machines, and (statistically anyway) look more like the actual population than a parliament full of lawyers does, so they can deliberate on the merits. I want to test that pitch against a real case, and the one I can't stop thinking about is the French Citizens' Convention for the Climate, the CCC, which ran from late 2019 into 2020. Quick recap of the facts as I know them, correct me if I slip: after the yellow vest protests and a big national consultation, Macron's government had the Economic, Social and Environmental Council convene 150 randomly selected citizens, who spent months hearing experts and working out proposals to cut France's emissions. They came back with 149 of them, and Macron publicly promised they'd go to parliament or a referendum essentially unfiltered, except for three he set aside himself (the press called them his three jokers, and one was the plan to lower the motorway speed limit from 130 to 110 km/h, which was also the one the polls showed people hated). Then in the end: no referendum happened, a climate law went through parliament with a lot of the proposals watered down or dropped, and by the convention's own accounting only a fraction got implemented in anything like the original form. Some citizens publicly said they'd been used, that the whole thing was a PR exercise with a predetermined ceiling.
