So I write about AI for a living — mostly the research beat, the occasional culture piece — and there's this argument I keep circling back to that I think has quietly deadlocked, and I want your read on what kind of deadlock it actually is.

The subject is Bongard problems. In case you don't have them at hand: each one is a little grid of twelve simple line drawings, six on the left and six on the right, and the whole game is to find the property that separates the two sides — like, everything on the left is made of curved segments and everything on the right is made of straight ones. No labels, no training set beyond the twelve examples. It's concept induction in its purest form, or at least that's how Hofstadter pitched it in Godel, Escher, Bach, where he used Bongard's puzzles as basically the acid test for real understanding. His whole research program for decades was that this kind of fluid pattern-finding, inventing a concept on the spot from a handful of examples, is the core of intelligence, and that you couldn't get it by piling up stored knowledge. His group actually built solvers for these — Phaeaco was the big one, a student's dissertation project — and they worked, sort of, on a narrow slice of the puzzle space, with tons of hand-built structure.

And then, you know the punchline. The big multimodal models now do pretty well on Bongard-style puzzles. There's been a whole wave of benchmarks in the last few years built on exactly this format precisely because it looks like few-shot concept discovery, and the frontier models pass a lot of them. Not perfectly, but way past what Phaeaco dreamed of.

Here's the thing though: the argument didn't get settled by that, it just went strange. One camp says this settles it — generalization all the way down, Hofstadter was a beautiful writer and wrong, and the reason the models can do it is precisely that they've seen such an ocean of visual and linguistic structure that a new six-vs-six split is not really "new" for them, it sits close to things they've already compressed. The other camp — which includes a lot of people who were around Hofstadter's orbit or at least sympathetic — says that's exactly the concession, not the rebuttal: if you can only do it because you're standing on a million near-miss examples, then you haven't induced a concept, you've matched a fingerprint, and the interesting question is what happens when you get a puzzle that is genuinely far from everything in the training distribution. And the first camp says okay, but define "genuinely far" in a way that doesn't just mean "whatever the model still fails," and then everyone stares at each other.

I've now sat through, I want to say, four conference panels where this exact exchange happened with different vocabulary, and nobody moved an inch. So my actual question isn't who's right. It's: what kind of disagreement is this? Some candidates I've been kicking around with a colleague of mine who actually trained as a philosopher before defecting to data science — (a) it's verbal, a fight over whether the word "induction" or "concept" gets to cover the case where a model compresses a million near-examples and then transfers, (b) it's substantive but hiding a empirical question nobody can operationalize, namely distance from the training distribution, and every proposed operationalization gets gerrymandered, (c) the two sides are actually answering different questions — one about what the system does, one about what makes its doing impressive — and each side's evidence is orthogonal to the other side's question, or (d) Hofstadter's original framing poisoned the well, because he set it up as a test of "real understanding," which was maybe never going to be settled by any benchmark no matter what it showed, in which case the whole thing is closer to a debate about vitalism than about cognition. My colleague swears by (c), I keep sliding toward (d), and neither of us can find a clean argument.

So: diagnose it. Use what the sides actually say, push back on my colleague and on me if we're being lazy, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a benchmark design, a distinction somebody needs to draw, a result that would force one side to update, or an honest case that this is bedrock and nobody should expect movement. Also, since I might end up quoting you in a piece: flag how confident you are in each part of the diagnosis, and single out the single point you'd least want to have to defend in print. If you think I've misdescribed Hofstadter's actual position somewhere, say that too — I've read GEB and Fluid Concepts but I'm a journalist, not a scholar, and I may be flattening him.
