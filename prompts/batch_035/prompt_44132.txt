For the past two years I've been part of a metaphysics reading group that keeps circling the special composition question — van Inwagen's question of when some things compose something — and we have stalled completely. The group's positions have hardened into the usual ones: one committed mereological nihilist who insists that whenever there is no genuine unification there simply are no composite objects, only simples arranged chairwise; two universalists who think composition is unrestricted and that there is an object wherever there are some things, no matter how scattered; and a couple of people drawn to brutal composition, the view that there is a brute, unprincipled fact about which fusions exist, with no illuminating answer available even in principle. The nihilist and the universalists each think the other's position is a non-starter, yet their arguments never seem to land: the nihilist's challenge to say what unifies the scattered fusion of my nose and the Eiffel Tower gets met with the shrug that composition doesn't need to be interesting, and the universalist's complaint that nihilism is arbitrary in the other direction gets met with the insistence that arrangements are all we ever needed. Nobody has changed their mind in about eight meetings, and I've started to suspect we're not really disagreeing about anything.

My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, and I'd like it taken apart if it deserves it — is that the dispute is partly verbal: that the parties are using the existential quantifier with different restrictions, and that when the universalist says "there is an object composed of my nose and the Eiffel Tower" while the nihilist denies it, they are not contradicting each other so much as running two different candidate regimentations of ordinary talk. But I can also see the counterargument: the brute-composition people seem to be defending exactly the claim that there is a substantive fact of the matter that our vocabulary is tracking, and if they're right the dispute can't be merely verbal. So I want a diagnosis rather than a survey: is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one hiding behind shared vocabulary, or several distinct questions (one about semantics, one about what ontology is for) wrongly bundled under a single heading? What does each side's actual behaviour in the debate reveal about where the real disagreement sits, and is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, or just genuinely hard?

Please tell me, given your diagnosis, what would genuinely count as progress here — a distinction, a new kind of case, an argument that one position is unstable — and whether anything could actually end the debate rather than just redistribute the terrain. And be explicit about where your own reasoning is most likely to fail: which step in the diagnosis you'd give up first, and what consideration would push you to a different diagnosis altogether. Please write in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the sort of thing one could read aloud as a letter to the group — and in British English.
