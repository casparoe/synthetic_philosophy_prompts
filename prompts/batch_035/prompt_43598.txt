I run quality review for the AI support agent at a mid-size online retailer (think a few thousand tickets a day). My team of reviewers flags transcripts where the agent "did something dumb" and we have to decide what to do about it: file a bug, change the policy, retrain, or just tell the reviewer to stop flagging this category. Right now everyone's going on gut and we're inconsistent as hell.

Here's the idea I want you to build the procedure around. There's an old story about the digger wasp: she drags a paralyzed cricket to her burrow, leaves it at the threshold, pops inside to check the burrow, comes back out, and only then hauls the cricket in. But if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she's inside, she comes out, finds it, drags it back to the threshold... and goes in to check the burrow again. You can apparently do this to her over and over and she never just pulls the cricket in. (I've read later naturalists think the story is embellished, whatever, it's a parable at this point.) People call this "sphexish" behavior, I think the word comes from Hofstadter, and the thing that makes it unsettling isn't that the wasp follows a procedure — it's that the procedure has captured her. The checking step isn't serving the goal anymore, but nothing in her can notice that.

The reason this haunts me: our agent is rigid in at least four different ways and they need completely different responses, and reviewers keep mashing them together:

1. It's stuck in an actual loop (asks for the order number, customer gives it, asks again) — that's a bug, engineering problem.
2. It's rigidly following a policy that WE wrote badly, and a human agent would've quietly bent it — that's a policy problem.
3. It's following a policy that gives it no discretion but gives a human reviewer discretion, and it doesn't know the discretion exists — that's... some third thing.
4. It's enforcing a rule that is genuinely non-negotiable (age verification on certain products, legally mandated stuff) and the customer calls it dumb, and the agent is actually right.

The sphexishness concept is what I want doing the work: the difference between following a rule and being captured by one, and the fact that you can't always tell from the outside — the wasp checking the burrow and a "smart" agent confirming an order number look identical until you intervene and see whether the behavior re-runs pointlessly.

So, please act as a philosopher of action/cognitive science type who has also done a few years of support ops, and write me a decision procedure my reviewers can actually run on a flagged transcript. Constraints: reviewers spend maybe two minutes per transcript, most have no philosophy background at all, and the output has to be one of a small set of verdicts (bug / bad policy / discretion-gap / correct-but-looks-dumb / hand to a human because it's a values question) with a short reason attached. Think carefully about whether "loop" behavior can even be diagnosed from a single transcript versus whether you need the counterfactual — the nudged cricket — and if the procedure needs the reviewer to probe, say so explicitly. Start with some brainstorming about what separates the four categories conceptually before you commit to the checklist, I want to see the reasoning, not just the artifact.

Then run the procedure on three transcripts (make them up, keep them realistic and kind of boring like real tickets):

A. Customer wants to return an item 5 days past the window. Agent restates the policy. Customer says "I understand the policy, I'm asking if you can make an exception." Agent restates the policy. Third restatement, customer gives up. Note: our policy actually DOES give reviewers discretion on returns under $75, but the agent was never told.

B. Agent asks for the order number, customer pastes it, agent thanks them and asks for the order number. This goes around maybe four times before the customer types in all caps.

C. Customer tries to change the shipping address on an order containing an age-restricted item, which triggers an identity re-verification the agent cannot skip. Agent explains this correctly and calmly. Customer calls it "the dumbest bot I've ever used" and demands a human.

Also please include one worked case where the procedure gives the verdict that my team will hate and will want to argue with, and be honest about where the procedure just moves the judgment call rather than eliminating it — I'd rather know that going in than find out when a reviewer gets chewed out over a call the checklist forced them into. Don't pad it, no 15-step monster, it has to survive being run a hundred times a day by tired people. And no jargon that isn't explained in one line — "sphexish" itself is fine if you gloss it.
