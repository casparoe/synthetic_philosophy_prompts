I'm taking a third-year seminar on animal ethics, and this week we discussed what our course reader labels the charge of "speciesism." Near the end of the session, a classmate closed off a debate about the permissibility of medical research on animals with a slogan she'd picked up from animal-rights activism: "A rat is a pig is a dog is a boy." Her argument was that since there is no morally relevant difference among these beings, there is no defensible line between using a pig for experimentation and using a human of comparable cognitive capacity in the same way — so the research programme our university runs should be treated as equivalent to experimenting on cognitively disabled humans, and wound down accordingly. Our instructor said the slogan was doing "too much work," but declined to elaborate before the seminar ended, and I've been unable to work out precisely what she meant.

I'd like help taking that slogan apart. Could you lay out, as a structured list I can bring to the next seminar, the distinct precise theses "A rat is a pig is a dog is a boy" could be expressing — from the near-trivial to the genuinely radical? For each reading, I'd like a verdict on whether it is defensible, and, crucially, whether it would actually support my classmate's conclusion: that animal research of the kind carried out at universities is on a moral par with research on certain humans, and should be ended rather than regulated. I suspect some readings (e.g., that equal interests of different beings count equally in moral deliberation) are much weaker than the conclusion she drew from them, while others (e.g., that all these beings possess identical moral capacities, or deserve literally identical treatment regardless of their capacities) seem plainly false. Where possible, flag which reading people who repeat the slogan probably intend, and which reading would be needed for her argument to go through — so I can see whether the gap between the two is what our instructor had in mind.
