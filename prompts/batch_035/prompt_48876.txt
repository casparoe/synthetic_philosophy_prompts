I'm a high school senior, and I've gotten stuck on an argument my friend Dana has been building all semester. It started in physics class, when our teacher mentioned that the one-way speed of light can't be measured without assuming what you're trying to measure: to time light traveling from one synchronized clock to another, you first have to decide how to synchronize the clocks, and the standard method already assumes the trip takes the same time in both directions. She explained that the standard theory (light moves at the same speed in every direction) and a stranger rival (light moves at half the accepted speed in one direction and nearly instantaneously in the other) agree on every experiment anyone could ever perform, so the choice between them is a convention rather than a discovery. Dana has been generalizing from that ever since, and last week she presented what she calls her matching-rival argument. Each step sounds fine to me, but the conclusion seems to prove far too much. Here is her argument as she laid it out.

(1) For any theory T about how the world works, we can construct a rival T* that matches T on every observation anyone could ever make while disagreeing about what's behind the observations: keep every testable prediction and change only the hidden machinery — or, for claims about the past or about other minds, build a rival on which the records and behavior were produced some other way.

(2) If T and T* agree on every possible observation, then no possible evidence could ever favor T over T*.

(3) Rational belief must be responsive to evidence, so if no possible evidence could favor T over T*, then believing T while dismissing T* is arbitrary rather than rational.

(4) There is nothing special about the speed of light here: Dana claims her construction works for every belief anyone has ever held, from general relativity down to my belief that my grandmother remembers her childhood, since in each case her rivals reproduce all the appearances.

(5) So no one has ever been rational in believing any ordinary theory over its matching rival; scientific theories are conventions adopted for convenience rather than beliefs we have earned.

(6) Therefore the idea that evidence tells us what is true collapses, and science is just one internally consistent mythology among many.

Dana is delighted and now declares that she rationally believes nothing about physics beyond the raw data tables. What unsettles me is that the argument refuses to stop at physics. If (4) is right, then I am equally forbidden to believe that my grandmother was ever a child, that my cat is not a very well-made puppet, or that my memories of yesterday were not installed along with everything else five minutes ago. I want to keep (2) and (3), since they seem to be part of what believing on evidence means, and I do not want to keep (6). My vague suspicion is that something shifts between how Dana builds T* in (1) and what (3) requires T* to be, but I can't put my finger on it. So: which step should I reject, why did that step look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? If you think more than one step is shaky, tell me which rejection is cheapest and why. And if you think the argument is actually sound all the way through, say so plainly and explain what I'm paying by refusing to follow it.
