I was raised properly evangelical -- church three times a week, the whole thing -- and about two years ago I finally admitted to myself that I don't believe any of it. The interesting part isn't the leaving, it's what got left behind: a question my old church had an easy answer to and my new friends (all atheists, all very breezy about it) don't seem to feel the force of at all, which is whether moral seriousness survives the loss of the metaphysics, or whether once God's gone it's just vibes all the way down. At Christmas my gran, who is still very much a believer and knows exactly what happened with me, pressed her old copy of Middlemarch into my hands with a look, and honestly it's knocked me sideways. So what I want is a proper reading of Middlemarch as a novel about precisely my question: what happens to ardour, vocation, and the hunger to do some great good once the religious framework quietly drops out of the picture.

Dorothea seems like the obvious test case -- the Prelude frames her with Saint Theresa, the later-born Theresa who never finds her epic life, and then channels all that religious longing into marriage to a dried-up scholar of myth. And Casaubon's Key to All Mythologies keeps snagging on me: is that Eliot poking fun at religion studied from the outside, or at the kind of scholarship that thinks it can pin the sacred down in a comparative table, or is it a self-portrait of someone who lost faith the slow way and can't stop embalming it? I gather she translated Feuerbach's Essence of Christianity at some point, so presumably she knew the whole "religion of humanity" line of thought intimately, and I want the reading to take that seriously rather than just noting it as trivia. And then there's Lydgate, whose secular vocation fails for painfully ordinary reasons -- money and vanity -- which feels like part of the same argument about whether anything can carry the weight religion used to carry.

What I'd like is a reading that goes both ways and is honest about the fit: where the novel genuinely dramatises what the Feuerbach-style debate only asserts, where Dorothea's final renunciation and the little unhistoric acts at the end amount to an actual atheist ethics of sympathy, and where the novel resists being read as a solution at all -- whether Eliot is offering a replacement for faith or just elegising it, and whether the answer to that changes what we're meant to make of the fact that the narrative voice feels rather like anOld Testament prophet throughout. Don't soften the ending for me either -- tell me whether the book earns its hope or whether it's the sentimental residue of a girlhood spent believing. I'm not writing an essay, I just want to think about it properly, and ideally to have something to say at my book club beyond everyone's current take, which is that Dorothea was a silly girl who married the wrong man.
