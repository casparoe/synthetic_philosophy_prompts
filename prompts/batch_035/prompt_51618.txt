I've just gone back through some old Scientific American issues from the early 80s (my daughter found a box of them at an estate sale, of all things), and I fell into the Hofstadter rabbit hole. Back in '83 he had this column with a puzzle about an eccentric trillionaire who gathers 20 people and tells them: if exactly one of you sends me a telegram by noon tomorrow, that person gets a billion dollars, but if nobody sends one, or if two or more do, nobody gets anything at all. And then, if I'm remembering right, he actually ran a follow-up contest for readers where the prize was a million dollars divided by the total number of entries received, and you could claim as many entries as you wanted just by writing a bigger number on a single postcard. Apparently the whole thing collapsed in a hilarious way — people mailed in numbers like a googolplex and the prize shrank to some miniscule fraction of a cent. The idea underneath all of it, this way of reasoning where you take it as given that anyone thinking the way you think will land on the exact same answer, has been rattling around my head for weeks now. I spent thirty-odd years as an analytical chemist and I thought I understood what "rational" meant, but this has me properly confused, in a good way.

Heres the thing: since retiring Ive somehow become the guy who runs the monthly philosophy discusion group at my retirement community — 12 to 15 of us in the activities room, 90 minutes, mixed crowd (a couple of ex-engineers, a retired schoolteacher, a former nun, folks who've never heard the words "game theory" in their lives). Next month's session is mine to plan. What I want is a real activity, not me talking at people — a game with rules and small real stakes (I'm thinking a tin of my shortbread, or five-dollar coffee vouchers, nothing more than that) where they play something Platonia-shaped and run into the problem themselves: everyone individually wanting to jump, everyone knowing that everyone wants to jump, and the whole thing failing not because information is missing but because nobody can see a way to be the one. Ideally there'd be a second round where they get to invent their own way out, so they can discover for themselves how hard it is without communication, and whether reasoning about what others would do if they reason like you actually buys them anything or is just pretty talk.

Don't have me name the concept or Hofstadter until the debrief at the very end. The debrief should map what actually happened in the room onto the formal idea — where the everyday rational calculation breaks down, and what the "everyone reasons the same way" answer even amounts to. I vaguely recall the recommended move in the original puzzle was something probabilistic, like everyone sends the telegram but only with a small chance, and I'd like that laid out clearly enough that I could re-derive it on the whiteboard afterwards. Please tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I assume half the group will misread the rules and at least one person will try to game the mechanics), and be honest about which part of the idea no classroom version can reproduce and I'll simply have to assert in the debrief. Materials should be things I can print off my home computer or buy at a drugstore.
