I run a graduate reading group on decision theory, and for our final session — on counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail — I want a handout that works as a classification instrument: a decision tree titled, roughly, "Which decision theory do you actually hold?" The idea is to sort members by what they would actually do in the relevant cases, not by which theory they like the name of, so no node should ask anything like "do you accept causal decision theory?"

Please include the following elements:

1. Self-contained case descriptions. Each branching question should be a concrete case described in one or two sentences within the handout itself, so that someone who has done none of the reading can still answer it. The cases must include, in some form: Newcomb's problem; the opaque-coin counterfactual mugging (the coin was flipped long ago, you never learn how it landed, but a predictor who anticipated your decision in advance set up a large prize conditional on your paying when asked); XOR blackmail (an extortionist who only sends the letter if their investigation already found the incriminating information, so paying is good news about your situation even though refusing makes you better off); Parfit's hitchhiker, at the moment of deciding whether to pay the driver after already being delivered; and the smoking lesion. You may re-describe these in your own words and adjust the numbers, but the structural features that make each case do its work should be preserved.

2. Reasons, not just actions. A person can match the behavior of one theory while reasoning like another — an agent who bound itself by precommitment before the coin was flipped can pay the counterfactual mugging for causal reasons. So each node should force a choice among stated rationales (for example: "because my act doesn't cause the money to be there or not," "because my act is evidence about what the world is like," "because the procedure that produced this situation was tracking what I decide"), not merely between actions. The tree must be able to send a precommitment-style causal agent and a genuinely updateless agent down different branches.

3. A novel case. Include at least one case of your own invention, designed specifically to separate two leaves that the classic cases leave entangled — for instance, whatever you need to drive apart a causally-minded agent who escaped trouble by precommitting early from an agent who needs no precommitment because of how it evaluates its decision procedure itself. Explain in a short designer's note (separate from the handout) what each invented case is for and what it splits that the stock cases cannot.

4. Required leaves. Causal decision theory; evidential decision theory; the updateless or functional family (whatever you prefer to call the cluster around functional decision theory and timeless decision theory); a self-modifying causal agent that binds itself in advance; and at least one leaf for a respectable minority or unstable combination, if you think the case answers allow one (for example, someone who one-boxes in Newcomb but refuses the counterfactual mugging).

5. Coverage and gaps. Build the tree from the logical space first — the genuinely independent questions the cases pose — and only then locate the named positions in it. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say so on the handout and add one sentence diagnosing why: is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it has simply not been explored? Where two positions your tree cannot genuinely separate (for instance, if your cases do not distinguish two members of the updateless family from each other), flag that explicitly rather than pretending the tree draws the line.

Keep the whole instrument to a size a person could actually work through in twenty minutes — roughly eight to ten nodes — and phrase every node so it is answerable by someone who has never heard of any of the theories.
