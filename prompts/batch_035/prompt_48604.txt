My friend is the terminology editor at a small human-rights quarterly, and whenever the magazine has to decide whether to call something "genocide" or settle for weaker phrasing ("mass atrocities", "crimes against humanity"), she writes a short published ruling with reasons. I'm trying to predict her next one and want a second opinion. Her rulings so far: yes for Rwanda in 1994; yes for Srebrenica, where she was firm that killing "only" the men and boys of one town still counts, since the Convention's "in whole or in part" covers a region; yes for the Armenians in 1915, even though it predates the Convention, no court has ever ruled on it, and Turkey denies it to this day (a Turkish-owned advertiser complained and she didn't budge); yes for the Herero and Nama in German South West Africa, 1904–08. On the no side: the Bengal famine of 1943 (she called it the worst thing Churchill did, but negligence plus wartime extraction isn't intent to destroy a group); the Atlantic slave trade as an institution (the perpetrators needed the victims alive — monstrous, but not destruction); the dynamiting of the Bamiyan Buddhas and heritage destruction generally, where she also vetoed a colleague's push to adopt "cultural genocide" as the magazine's term of art, saying it dilutes the word; and the Stolen Generations in Australia, which she called a crime against humanity of forced assimilation but not genocide, because the policy wanted the children alive and absorbed, not the group destroyed.

The new case on her desk is the Fujimori-era sterilisation campaign in Peru, 1996–2000: roughly 300,000 tubal ligations, falling overwhelmingly on poor, rural, Quechua-speaking women, with doctors reportedly working to quotas, consent obtained under pressure or not at all, and a number of deaths (the Mestanza case is the famous one). The magazine runs a long feature next issue and the question is whether the standfirst says genocide. The annoying part is that everything she's ruled so far seems consistant with at least two different rules, and I can't tell which she's running: something like "genocide requires intent to destroy the group physically, which in her rulings has always meant killing", versus the Convention's Article II read literally, which brings sterilisation in through paragraph (d), "imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group" — though that route only works if the programme was aimed at indigenous women as such, and not just at the poor, who happen to be indigenous (and economic class isn't a protected group under the Convention anyway, which she's bound to notice). Her Srebrenica reasoning smells like Convention-literalism, but her Stolen Generations reasoning smells like the killing rule, and I genuinely can't tell which way those wash out for Peru.

So: what do you predict she'll rule, and why? I'm less interested in the answer itself than in which rule best fits her track record, what the other candidate rules are (including any cynical "she says genocide where it's diplomatically safe" reading, though I suspect Armenia kills that one), what each rule predicts for the Peru case, and which further case — the kind of thing that might plausibly land on her desk — would actually split the surviving rules apart. Also please tell me what would change your mind about the prediction, i.e. which bit of my setup you're least confident in, and write it all in British English.
