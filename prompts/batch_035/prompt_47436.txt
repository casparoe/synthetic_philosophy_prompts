I have been reading the rationalist blogs and forums since roughly the Overcoming Bias days, and Douglas Hofstadter's name surfaces there constantly — usually in connection with his long-running claim that analogy-making is the core of cognition, and in the recurring arguments about whether Gödel, Escher, Bach was really a book about AI. So I came to a popular cognitive-science article I read last week already inclined to be sympathetic to its subject. The article was about the Copycat project, and the gist of the relevant passage was as follows:

"In the 1980s, at Indiana University, Douglas Hofstadter and his student Melanie Mitchell built a program called Copycat to solve letter-string analogy problems of the form: if abc changes to abd, what does ijk change to? The program's answers were not stored anywhere; it found them through a stochastic process in which many small agents competed to build descriptions of the strings, with a global temperature parameter shifting attention between broad patterns and local details. When faced with the harder problem — if abc changes to abd, what does mrrjjj change to? — it discovered on its own that the rightmost letter could slip into the rightmost group of letters, and answered mrrkkk. This was the point of the famous Slipnet: a network of concepts whose connections were themselves fluid, so that in the right context successor could come to mean the next group up rather than the next letter up. Hofstadter regarded the smallness of the domain as a regrettable compromise forced by the limited computing power of the era, and he proposed that the same architecture, scaled up to richer domains, would yield something like general intelligence. He also read the program's success as a refutation of Fodor's picture of concepts as a fixed innate inventory, since the program demonstrated that concepts could be created on the fly. In the end the approach did not catch on, and nothing like it appears in modern machine learning."

Something about this sits oddly with me. My recollection from rationalist discussions is that Hofstadter was adamant that the microdomain was chosen deliberately for transparency — so that the mechanisms would actually be visible — and that he pushed back hard against any scaling-up reading of the project; but the details of the architecture are exactly where my memory is weakest, and the article sounds authoritative. So I would like an audit rather than a ruling on my impressions. Please go claim by claim through the passage and tell me: which claims would someone who worked on the project — Mitchell wrote the definitive thesis on it — accept as stated; which they would accept only after tightening the wording, and with what tightening; and which are straightforward misrepresentations, together with what the defenders would say. In particular I want your read on three points. First, the phrase "discovered on its own": how much of Copycat's knowledge was in fact built in by hand, and is the article's autonomy framing a fair compression or a real distortion? Second, the attributions to Hofstadter of a scaling ambition and of the hardware excuse — is either of these something he actually held, and if not, what did he say about why the domain was small? And third, the closing line about modern machine learning: does it follow from anything true earlier in the passage, or is it the sort of obsolescence claim that sounds like a consequence of the foregoing and is really a separate judgment standing on its own?
