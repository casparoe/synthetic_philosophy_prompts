At a series of workshops on aggregation in ethics, a committed act-utilitarian and a defender of the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism have been arguing past each other for two years. The utilitarian holds that the right act is the one that produces the greatest total sum of wellbeing, however that wellbeing is distributed across persons; the objector holds that this summative treatment fails to take seriously the fact that individuals are distinct centres of experience, each with a life of their own to lead, and that losses to one person cannot simply be cancelled out or outweighed by gains to another, however large. Please draft the joint statement that the two of them could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, on the assumption that neither has changed their underlying view but both are willing to make their remaining disagreement exact.

The statement should contain the following:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. These should be substantive rather than platitudinous: for example, claims about what the disagreement is and is not about (in particular, whether it concerns the structure of the moral theory as a whole or only its axiology; whether it applies to aggregation within a single life over time as well as across lives; and what each side takes the objection to be an objection to). Include whatever genuine common ground exists — e.g., about the distinction between intrapersonal and interpersonal trade-offs, or about the limits of what any single moral theory is expected to settle.

2. A disagreements table, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own row as a fair statement of its position. Where possible, distinguish within the objector's side between the claim that interpersonal aggregation is impermissible in principle and the weaker claim that it is permissible only under certain conditions or within certain limits; and within the utilitarian's side between the claim that total wellbeing is the sole morally relevant consideration and the weaker claim that it is the sole consideration at the level of axiology, with distributional matters handled elsewhere in the theory.

3. A list of cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. Candidates you should consider, though you need not accept all of them: whether the separateness-of-persons consideration has genuine moral content or merely restates a conclusion the objector already believes; whether the objection, if it works against utilitarian aggregation across persons, also works against aggregation within a single life over time, and if not, what explains the asymmetry; whether the dispute is partly verbal, turning on what "taking separateness seriously" amounts to, and if so, which part is verbal and how the residue should be reformulated; and whether the objection, if sound, tells against the summative structure of the value function itself or only against the use of that function as a decision procedure under conditions of moderate scarcity.

4. For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or thought experiment that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about how the case turns out or where the argument leads, recorded in advance so that neither can claim afterwards that the case was never probative. For instance, if a crux concerns the moral significance of numbers, both might agree that a case involving the choice between preventing a single severe harm to one person and preventing very many trivial harms to others bears on it — the two sides would then record, at various ratios, at which point (if any) each thinks preventing the many trivial harms becomes obligatory or at least permissible.

Throughout, write in British English. Do not include reading lists, bibliographies, or citations; the argument should stand on its own content. Conclude the joint statement with a short paragraph, signed by both parties in the statement's fiction, stating what each takes themselves to have gained from the collaboration and what they predict the other side would concede if the cruxes were resolved in the ways each expects.
