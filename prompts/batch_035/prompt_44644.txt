Six months ago my mom got diagnosed with early dementia, and last month a neighbor found her on the porch at 3am, three houses down, in her nightgown, confused about which door was hers. Before any of this — maybe four years ago, over dishes, the kind of conversation you don't think twice about — she made me promise I would never put her in a home. Her own mother spent her last years in a facility and she said she'd rather die than end up in one of those places. Now her care team is telling me she can't live alone anymore, I work full time, and there's no way we can afford 24-hour care. So I'm looking at an actual choice: break the promise and move her into memory care, or keep her at home knowing its genuinely unsafe.

My best friend, who did a philosophy undergrad and never lets anyone forget it, keeps telling me this isn't a real dilemma. His line is that hard choices still have a best answer, that if keeping her home isn't a live option anymore then I'm not obligated to do the impossible, and that whatever guilt I carry afterward is just grief wearing a costume — not evidence that I've actually wronged her. And I half beleive him. But there's another part of me that thinks the apology I'll owe her either way is tracking something real, that I'll have failed her as a matter of fact and not just through bad luck, whichever option I pick. I'm not a philosopher (I sell insurance, fyi), so forgive me if I'm framing this badly, but its been keeping me up and I want to actually understand where the disagreement lives.

So here's what I want from you: your credence that genuine moral dilemmas exist at all — situations where, no matter what you do, you do wrong and not just badly — and then specifically your credence that mine is one of them. Decompose it: how likely is it that ought implies can and what that does to promises you literally can't keep; how likely it is that morality is consistent in a way that guarantees some permissible option in every choice anyone ever faces; how likely it is that even a consistent morality could leave real, deserved residue — guilt and an apology you actually owe — without the choice counting as a genuine dilemma; and how likely it is that some apparent dilemmas are only epistemic, where one option really is right and we just can't ever know which. Give me a rough number and a sentence or two for each, then tell me how they combine, which one the final answer is most sensitive to, and what would actually move it. And then be straight with me about the thing I really want to know: is the guilt I'll feel either way part of the moral facts, or is it noise I should try to forgive myself for?
