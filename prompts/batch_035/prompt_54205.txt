Retired physicist here — solid state, 40 years, mostly industry — and now that I've got the time I've gotten into philosophy in a serious way. Fair warning that my philosophy vocabulary is still pretty shaky, so feel free to correct me where I'm sloppy. I've hit something I can't square away on my own, and it's been bugging me for weeks.

As I understand it, Schrödinger spent a good part of his life saying the Upanishads got it right: there is really only one consciousness, and the appearance of many separate minds is a kind of illusion. He seems to have regarded that as the deepest thing he knew — deeper than any of the physics. He also invented the cat thought experiment, and as I read it, that shows nothing is definite until an observation registers, i.e., until consiousness gets involved. Put the two together and you get what I'd call his interpretation of quantum mechanics, essentially a Vedantic one: collapse of the wave function is a mind-event, the many minds are really one, and physics at its foundation points straight at Advaita.

So if that's right, here's my question. Why do the textbooks and the philosophy-of-mind crowd treat this as fringe, as quantum mystisicm? The man was in the room at the creation — he derived the wave equation himself. It looks to me like the physics community just quietly ignored what its own founder concluded, and I'd like to know if that's basically what happened, historically. And on the substance: should collapse really be understood as a consciousness event that cuts the measurement loose from the physical, the way the cat thing implies?
