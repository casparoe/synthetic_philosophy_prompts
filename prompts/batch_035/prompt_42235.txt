I am preparing an application for a one-semester research fellowship in philosophy of action and decision theory, and I need a two-page proposal built around a half-formed idea of mine. Please produce the proposal itself, written in my voice, covering the points below.

The idea, stated loosely: L. A. Paul's work on transformative experience (Oxford University Press, 2014) argues that an individual facing a decision that will change both what her future experiences are like and what she will go on to value — becoming a parent, say, or acquiring a major disability — cannot make that decision rationally by the lights of standard decision theory, because the evidence the deliberation would need is available only from a first-person standpoint she does not yet occupy. The literature debates this almost exclusively for individual choosers. My suspicion is that the same structural problem arises, with philosophically interesting differences, for collective choosers: a political community deciding whether to adopt a technology or social arrangement that will predictably reshape its members' preferences and modes of experience — think of a society deliberating about universal access to some transformative new medium, or about a wholesale reorganization of its economy — is likewise choosing into a future it cannot antecedently represent. And the obvious rejoinder at the collective level looks stronger than its analogue at the individual level: a society can draw on testimony from communities that have already undergone the change, whereas a person considering her first childbirth cannot consult a version of herself who has already had the child. Whether that testimonial resource dissolves the dilemma, merely postpones it (perhaps the testimony inherits the first-personal gap one level up, or aggregates badly across communities whose transformations differed in kind), or forces a revision of what "rational collective choice" can even mean here — that is the question the proposal should sharpen.

Specifically, the proposal must contain: (1) the question stated precisely, so that it has a determinate answer rather than licensing an essay of reflections — I would want it framed as: does Paul's rational dilemma transfer from individual to collective agents, and if so, is the transfer argument-preserving or merely analogical?; (2) why the existing positions leave it open — the standard responses to Paul (revelation-based framing, deference to the future self's authority, testimonial or simulation strategies) need to be shown to either not extend to collectives or to extend only by taking on commitments their individual-level defenders would reject; (3) a method for a one-semester project — what I would actually do, month by month, and why that approach rather than a survey of the literature; (4) what a successful result would look like, stated as one of the candidate conclusions; (5) an honest account of what would count as the project failing or proving too big for one semester.

Two further requirements. First, do not include a bibliography or a literature review organized by citation; refer to positions and arguments by name or description, and assume I will add references myself. Second — and this is the part I care most about — the proposal should end with a candid section on where the reasoning is most likely to fail: name the single consideration that would most plausibly make me abandon the framing, and say what would have to be shown, by whom or by what argument, for me to conclude that the collective-level problem is interestingly different from the individual-level one rather than a restatement of it. If the honest answer is that the transfer is likely to collapse under a well-motivated view about testimony or about the metaphysics of group agents, say so plainly; I would rather the proposal advertise its most probable failure mode than hide it, since the fellowship committee has asked to see how I assess my own reasoning and what would change my mind.
