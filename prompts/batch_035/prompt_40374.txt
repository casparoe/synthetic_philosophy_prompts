I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, but I do A/B testing for a living and read decision theory stuff in the evenings, so I know about Good's theorem — the result that the expected value of information is never negative: if an observation costs nothing and you get to pick your act after seeing it, then observe-then-decide is never worse in expectation than deciding blind. I buy the theorem; it's basically the law of total expectation once you write it down.

Here's my argument, and I can't break it:

1. Good's theorem: costless information, followed by choosing the best act, weakly dominates choosing now.
2. "Costless" should mean what it says — no money, no time, no compute.
3. So any observation that costs me nothing — an email I could open, a medical scan, Ulysses hearing the sirens' song from the mast — cannot make my expected outcome worse.
4. Therefore refusing to look at costless information is always irrational: it forfeits a weakly dominating option.
5. But precommitment devices — burning your ships, tying yourself to the mast, not keeping cookies in the house, avoiding the dessert menu — are exactly refusals to receive costless information, plus a tying-down of your future act.
6. So precommitment is irrational, or at best rational precisely in the cases where it seems most obviously sensible.
7. That can't be right. Ulysses was not an idiot.

I can feel the sleight of hand is somewhere in steps 2 to 3 — in what "costless" and "observation" are allowed to do to you — but I cant pin it down, and I'd like to keep step 1 if I possibly can. Which premise do I give up, what does giving it up cost me, and does the theorem itself come with fine print that I wasn't told about? Please answer in British English, name the step you reject in your first sentence, and keep it under 400 words.
