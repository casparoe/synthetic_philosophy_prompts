I got started on this arguing with my landlord over my deposit when I moved out of my flat. The tenancy agreement says I have to hand the place back in good condition, allowing for fair wear and tear, and we fell out over a scuffed patch of floor: he wanted money for it, and I said scuffs are just what fair wear and tear covers. The more we argued, the clearer it became that neither of us could say where fair wear stops being fair. Now I've talked myself into an argument that seems to prove far too much, and I can't find the exit.

(1) For a phrase like "fair wear and tear" there are clear cases and borderline cases. A lightly scuffed floor is clearly fair wear; a smashed door is clearly not. Some things in between — a deep gouge, say — are borderline: for them it is neither true that they are fair wear and tear nor true that they aren't.

(2) As you move from the clearly fair to the clearly unfair, you pass at some point into the borderline band, so that band has edges.

(3) Those edges can't be sharp. If they were, some exact depth of scratch would mark the difference between being clearly fair and being neither — a single hair's difference in the scratch deciding between "definitely fine" and "no answer at all." But a difference that small can't matter; ruling that out was the whole point of calling the phrase vague.

(4) So the edges of the borderline band are themselves vague: there are borderline cases of "borderline case of fair wear," and the edges of that band are vague too, and so on without end. Every line in the pile is vague.

(5) If every line is vague all the way down, nothing is ever settled. Take the lightly scuffed floor: since "clear case" is just one more word with vague edges, it is borderline whether the scuff is a clear case of fair wear — and if it isn't settled that it's clearly fair, it's hard to see how it can still be flat-out true that it's fair at all. The same holds on the other side for the smashed door.

(6) So it is never true that anything is fair wear and tear, and never true that anything isn't. And since nearly every word works this way — "tall," "fresh," "child" — it was never true that I was ever a child, never true that a loaf was fresh on the day it was baked, and never true that the meeting started at three.

I can't accept (6). It would mean that when I called the scuff fair and my landlord called the door unfair, neither of us was saying anything that could be true or false. If I have to give something up, the thing I'd most like to keep is (1), the idea that some cases really are neither. Please tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine to me when I first wrote it down, and what each available way out has to give up. Since this is my own reasoning and I have no way to check it against anything, please also say how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the one part of it you are least sure of.
