My professor says this argument, which I defended in a seminar on conceptions of liberty, can't be right because it proves far too much — but he refuses to tell me where it breaks, and I've been unable to find the fault myself. It's meant to show that the republican (non-domination) conception of liberty is vacuous:

(1) On the republican view, you are unfree when you live under another person's arbitrary power: they can interfere with your choices at their pleasure, and any protection you enjoy is protection they could withdraw.
(2) What makes such power arbitrary is not how often it is exercised but whether it is constrained by rules the subject can invoke; a master's kindness is no constraint, because it can be revoked at will, and so is no better than a rule of his own making.
(3) Anyone who depends on another's ongoing goodwill for something important — with no rules to invoke, only the other's continuing good humor — lives under that other's arbitrary power.
(4) Nearly every social life involves such dependence: the employee on the manager, the tenant on the landlord, the friend on the friend, the spouse on the spouse.
(5) So nearly everyone, in every society including the best-ordered democracy, is unfree in the republican sense, and by (2) their situation is no better in kind than that of the slave with the kind master.
(6) A conception of liberty on which friendship, employment, and marriage are domination on a par with slavery cannot mark the distinctions it was invented to mark — between the slave and the citizen, interference and non-interference, constraint and independence.

Therefore, the argument concludes, either republican liberty is unattainable by any social being, or the analysis of unfreedom as subjection to arbitrary power must be abandoned. I'm reluctant to accept either horn: the analysis seemed to get the kindly-master case exactly right, which is what drew me to it in the first place. Which step should I give up, why did that step look so plausible, and what does rejecting it cost the republican picture? I would most like to keep (2), since it seems to be what does the real work against the kindly master — but I'm prepared to be told that keeping it is what generates the trouble.
