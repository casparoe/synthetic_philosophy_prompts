Please give an intuitive, non-technical explanation of Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. I can follow the formal statement — roughly, that if the probability of "If A, B" always equals the conditional probability of B given A, then conditionals cannot express propositions with nontrivial probabilities — but I lack a gut-level sense of why the collapse happens, and in particular of why it is the embedding of the conditional under negation or disjunction, rather than the probability equation on its own, that does the damage. Could you walk through the mechanism informally, and then assess the standard escape routes: denying that conditionals express propositions at all, keeping them as propositions but rejecting the probability equation for embedded occurrences, or treating "if" as a modifier of assertoric content rather than an operator that takes a truth value. I am especially interested in why the second and third routes are often treated so differently, given that both deny that "If A, B" is an ordinary constituent of complex sentences.
