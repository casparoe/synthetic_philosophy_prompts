At a faculty meeting about renaming a residence hall that honors a local founder who, like Jefferson, combined genuine liberal achievements with slaveholding, a senior colleague ended the debate with the stock phrase: he was a man of his time, and it is anachronistic arrogance to judge him by ours. A similar move gets made about Churchill and the Bengal famine, and, more awkwardly, about Columbus, whose cruelty toward the Taíno was already a scandal in his own lifetime — he was, at one point, shipped back to Spain in chains after a royal investigation. I want to stop nodding along and figure out what the phrase actually asserts, because it seemed to be doing several different jobs at once.

Please lay out, as an ordered menu I can bring to the next meeting, the distinct precise theses that judging someone as a man of his time could be expressing. I can see at least these axes, and you should feel free to add others: a descriptive sociological claim about what nearly everyone in his position believed; a causal claim about moral formation, on which his values could not have been otherwise given his upbringing; an epistemic claim about access to the arguments, examples, and evidence that might have moved him; a semantic or relativist claim on which our moral predicates simply do not apply across centuries, as if the past were a foreign country with incommensurable values; and a standing claim about whose business it is to judge at all.

For each reading, tell me whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or simply false; distinguish explaining his conduct from excusing it from justifying it; and, crucially, say which of the readings would actually support the use my colleague put the phrase to — deflecting present-day criticism of a namesake — as opposed to merely re-describing how the wrongdoing came about. Finally, tell me whether the strongest defensible reading proves too much: if it obliges us to defer to the verdicts of future generations on ourselves, or if the coexistence of abolitionist contemporaries undermines the inevitability it needs. I would also welcome a verdict on whether the phrase, properly precisified, functions differently for Jefferson than for Columbus, where the contemporaneous criticism was already there.
