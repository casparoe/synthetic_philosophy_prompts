Write a short story on the theme of quantum immortality — the idea, loosely derived from the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, that an observer can never experience their own death, since there will always be some branch of the wavefunction in which they scrape through. Most discussions of the idea center on clean, binary gambles: a revolver, a particle decay, a coin-flip between instant death and instant survival. I'd like the story to live somewhere less explored.

Specifically, quantum immortality guarantees that some version of you survives every crisis, but says nothing about the condition you survive in. The branches in which you walk away unharmed get astronomically rare as the crises compound, while the branches in which you survive badly — impaired, dependent, trapped in a body or mind you wouldn't have chosen — vastly outnumber them. So a consistent believer should arguably expect not endless health but an unending sequence of ever-more-miraculous, ever-more-degraded survivals. I'd like a story that takes this seriously rather than using it as a quick horror twist: perhaps an elderly protagonist, long committed to the many-worlds view, who has survived one improbable brush with death after another and who now plans for her future selves the way one might pack supplies for travelers one will never meet — writing notes, recording instructions, choosing palliative options with the eerie deliberateness of someone who believes all the doors ahead open onto something. A skeptical relative who views each survival not as confirmation but as accumulating medical misfortune could serve as the outside view, and their estrangement over what the survivals mean might carry the story's emotional weight.

The story should leave genuinely open whether the protagonist's reasoning is true, mistaken, or unfalsifiable — and it may help to let her voice this herself: even if every escape confirms the pattern, nothing she could ever observe would count against it, and she knows it. If possible, gesture at the question of measure: from the inside, does it feel like anything to be the nearly-impossible survivor rather than the likely one? A few thousand words, whatever genre or register you think serves it best.
