I'm on the steering committee of a research institute that has committed funding for five visiting fellows and a series of workshops over the next two years, all of it earmarked for conceptual work on the epistemic quality of collective decision-making — formal theory and philosophy, not empirical political science. The anchor point for the program is the Condorcet jury theorem and its neighborhood: the result that if voters independently judge better than chance between two alternatives, majority rule becomes almost certainly correct as the electorate grows, along with its better-known descendants (heterogeneous competence, correlated votes, qualified majorities, weighted voting).

I've sketched five candidate questions the program could concentrate on. What I want from you is not a survey of them but a defended agenda: which deserve the fellowships, in what order, and why.

1. Endogenous competence. The theorem takes each voter's competence as a fixed input, but in practice competence is partly produced by the very institutions being evaluated — deliberative processes, party primaries, the media environment, arguably even the existence of majority rule itself, which changes what it pays to learn. Is there a principled framework in which competence is endogenous, and if competence and the aggregation rule co-determine each other, does the theorem lose its normative force, gain it, or just change shape?

2. Correlated error. Everyone grants that independence fails in the wild — voters share media, priors, and informational ancestors. Which replacements for the independence assumption are tractable, and does any of them preserve a readable version of the "larger groups do better" conclusion, or does it flip sign?

3. Domain transfer. Whether the same aggregation logic does any work in institutional designs outside electoral politics: citizens' assemblies selected by lot, expert panels, forecast aggregation, even majority voting among the outputs of different models. What exactly breaks when the voters are unequal in standing, competence, or independence from one another?

4. Threshold rules across issue types. When one electorate votes on many issues on which its competence differs, does the theorem's machinery say anything about which issues should face simple majorities, which supermajorities, and which shouldn't be put to a vote at all?

5. The "so what" question. Whether the theorem's role in democratic theory is evidential or merely illustrative — whether actual practice depends on the premises being approximately true, or whether it should be understood as an idealization on the model of frictionless planes in mechanics: false, but indispensable for seeing what the real forces are.

For each question, tell me whether it sits upstream of the others, whether it's tractable with the tools of philosophers and formal theorists rather than requiring empirical social science, and what developments over the next couple of years — new formal results, better data on how errors correlate, institutional experiments — would move it up or down your ranking. If you think a question I haven't listed is more important than all five, say so, but justify it against the same criteria rather than by novelty alone.

Finally, a request about the epistemics of your answer: state how confident you are in each part of the ranking, and single out the one judgment in your reply that you are least sure of — I'd rather know where the ranking is load-bearing guesswork than receive it as settled.
