I never went to university, and philosophy is a hobby I came to late in life through secondhand books, so I apologize if I use the terms loosely. For the past year I have been thinking about failed long-run predictions — Malthus above all — and I have built an argument that my daughter tells me "proves too much." I cannot find the flaw, and I would rather know where it bends than win the argument.

The argument as I have it:

(1) A prediction about the long run is falsified by the passage of time only if it named a date, the date has passed, and the predicted event has not occurred.

(2) Predictions of scarcity and collapse, from Malthus onward, rarely name a date; and when they do, their defenders say the date marked the mechanism's arrival, not the theory's truth. Malthus is the standing example: population did keep growing as fast as he said it would, and his defenders say the Green Revolution merely postponed the reckoning — that he was right but early.

(3) Therefore no prediction of this kind has ever been falsified, or ever can be, by the simple failure of its event to arrive.

(4) A claim that can never be falsified by the failure of its event cannot be wrong in the ordinary sense; saying it "turned out wrong" means nothing for it.

(5) So when we say Malthus turned out wrong, we are saying nothing; and by the same reasoning, no prophet of collapse, however many times its deadline quietly moves, can ever be wrong.

(6) Yet in the famous metals bet, the pessimist side did name a date — 1990 — and did lose on it, and afterwards said, in effect, that they had been right but early about a longer timeline. If (5) holds, that rescue works; and if the rescue works, every pessimist who waits long enough becomes a prophet, and every failed prediction becomes a success on a postponed schedule.

Something here is too strong, because I know two things at once and they cannot both be whole: that Malthus was wrong about the standard of living — it rose while population exploded, which is what he said could not happen — and that some warnings really do arrive decades early. I have read of fisheries warnings dismissed in one generation and vindicated in the next, and that always struck me as a plain fact, not a rescue.

Which premise should I give up? I would most like to keep (6), or at least the part of me that believes both things at once, though I suspect that is exactly where the argument has me. If you tell me which step to reject, please also tell me what rejecting it costs: what kind of "wrong" remains available for Malthus if (5) fails, and what rule, if any, could separate a legitimate "right but early" from an endless series of moved deadlines.

End your reply with a line beginning "Verdict:" followed by the number of the premise you would reject, or a short phrase if the flaw lies elsewhere — for instance, in an ambiguity between two senses of some word that you can name.
