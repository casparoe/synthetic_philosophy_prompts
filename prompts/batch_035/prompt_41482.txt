I'm a PhD student in philosophy, working mostly in formal epistemology, and I'd like an outside adjudication of a disagreement from our Bayesian epistemology reading group that has now spanned two meetings without resolution. The subject was the Judy Benjamin problem, van Fraassen's puzzle about updating on a conditional. As we set it up: a soldier is dropped into unfamiliar territory divided by a front line into Blue-held and Red-held land, with each side divided into two regions, Blue-1, Blue-2, Red-1, and Red-2; antecedently she takes herself to be equally likely to be in each. A radio operator tells her that, if she is in Blue territory, she is twice as likely to be in Blue-1, the hillside, as in Blue-2, the swamp. What should her posterior be?

The exchange ran roughly as follows, as best I can reconstruct it.

Marta, who works on Bayesian foundations, opened with the orthodox position. Learning, she said, is conditionalization on an event in your algebra, or Jeffrey conditioning on a partition when the event is not fully observed, and there is no event here, because the message is a conditional, and a conditional is not in general a proposition in the agent's language. So the puzzle, as posed, is underspecified: the answer depends on the provenance of the message. If the operator broadcasts the conditional whenever the spotter has any information about the Blue side and stays silent otherwise, then merely hearing the broadcast is evidence she is in Blue; if he broadcasts at random moments regardless of where she is, it is not. Strip away the signaling arrangement and you have not specified an experiment, and there is no more a uniquely correct posterior than there is for someone told only that a coin has some bias or other.

Tomas accused her of dissolving the puzzle by refusing to state it. The interesting question, he said, is exactly what the constraint fixes: given only the requirement that her posterior make Blue-1 twice as probable as Blue-2 conditional on Blue, together with the demand that the rest of her opinions change as little as possible, the answer is the one associated with imaging: hold the Blue-versus-Red credence at one-half and rearrange within, landing on one-third for Blue-1, one-sixth for Blue-2, and one-quarter for each Red region. He contrasted this with the only standard conditionalization on offer, conditioning on the material conditional (either she is in Red territory or she is in Blue-1), which annihilates Blue-2 and pumps her credence in Red territory up to two-thirds, as though a remark about the composition of the Blue side were evidence that she is on the Red side. He added that the information-theoretic route does not rescue the intuition either, since the posterior that minimizes relative information subject to the constraint also shifts the Blue-versus-Red credence away from one-half, which he took to show that the formal notion of minimal change and the intuitive one have simply come apart.

Marta's reply had three parts. First, the imaging-style answer smuggles in a tacit assumption, namely that the Blue/Red partition is epistemically insulated from the message; but that is a fact about the source, not about the sentence, since an operator who only speaks up when he has spotter data about Blue territory is telling you something about your chances of being in Red territory precisely by speaking. Second, the orthodoxy has principled resources here: once you fill in the story, the operator's broadcasting policy, the spotter's coverage, the error rates, ordinary conditionalization on the full description of what she learned, including the fact that a message arrived, delivers a posterior, and for some fillings-in it will be Tomas's and for others not. The puzzle survives only as a question about which ceteris are paribus. Third, she found it suspicious that his favored posterior assigns one-third to Blue-1, exactly what conditioning on the material conditional delivers for that region, suggesting the numbers were doing rhetorical work the reasoning had not earned.

Tomas closed by turning her framework around. Jeffrey conditioning on a partition is the celebrated minimal-mutilation answer for constraints on unconditional probabilities, and nobody complains that it is silent about where the new partition probabilities came from; so the demand that imaging-style updating for constraints on conditional probabilities carry its own provenance story is a demand the orthodoxy itself cannot meet. And whatever one says about sources, there is a real normative question here that the dissolve-the-puzzle move refuses to engage: given only the constraint and the demand to be minimally committal, what should she believe? If the answer is that no update is determined, then the constraint was not expressible as anything she learned, and the epistemology of receiving conditionals in testimony is simply broken.

Neither will budge, and the rest of us split roughly down the middle, so I would be grateful for a move-by-move adjudication: for each substantive claim in the exchange, whether it is right, mistaken, or right-with-qualifications, and why. I would particularly like your view on (a) whether conditioning on the material conditional is as discredited as Tomas assumes, or whether it captures something the imaging answer misses; (b) whether the minimization he mentions really fails to preserve the Blue-versus-Red credence, as he claims — I confess nobody in the room could do the calculation on the spot; (c) whether the coincidence both of them flag, that Blue-1 comes out at one-third on both routes, is evidence for either side; (d) whether, in the end, Marta is right that the scenario is underspecified, or Tomas right that this underspecification is precisely what a theory of rational updating should be equipped to handle; and (e) what, if anything, the case shows about whether conditionals are the kind of thing that can be learned at all. Please say who is more right overall, and do not hesitate to identify points that both of them are missing.
