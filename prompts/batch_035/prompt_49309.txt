I was elected in January to the board of my housing cooperative — forty units around a shared courtyard, one vote per unit — and I've been assigned the job of rewriting the bylaws on what counts as a member's own business and what falls to the board. Drafting has forced me to write down my actual verdicts on the disputes we've had, and they cluster in a way I can't articulate. A member practicing trumpet in her unit at six in the morning: the board may stop her. A member keeping a five-foot plastic flamingo in her courtyard-facing window, which every other member loathes: her call, full stop. A member hanging a banner advertising his tutoring business from that same window: I think the board may make him take it down, though I cannot easily say why the flamingo is her business and the banner is ours. Cigarette smoke migrating through our ancient vents into two neighbors' bedrooms: the board may ban it. Fermented-fish cooking smells migrating the same way, which the upstairs neighbor finds genuinely nauseating: the cook's call. One member's intense distress that the man in 4B reads pornography in his own home: not the board's business, even though the distress is real. And a member keeping a large python in her unit, which sends a snake-phobic neighbor into panic attacks when she sees it carried through the hall: on that one I keep going back and forth.

At the same meeting we also adopted two procedural rules, both of which seemed obviously right when we voted. Rule One (spheres): for matters on the personal-sphere list — unit interiors, window boxes, scent devices kept within one's unit — the board's collective ranking defers to the affected member's preference between any two options that differ only in that member's matter. Rule Two (unanimity): whenever every member prefers one complete package of outcomes to another, the board ranks the first above the second. Our secretary then tried to apply both rules to a pair of live disputes and found something embarrassing. Alma's window box could hold live geraniums or plastic tulips; Bo's reed diffuser, which perfumes the stairwell, could run or not. Three packages were on the table: geraniums with the diffuser running; tulips with the diffuser running; tulips with the diffuser off. Alma ranks them: tulips-and-off, then geraniums-and-running, then tulips-and-running — she would rather keep tulips than smell the diffuser, but given the smell, she wants her geraniums. Bo ranks them: tulips-and-running, then tulips-and-off, then geraniums-and-running — he would give up the diffuser to be rid of the geraniums, which give him migraines. Every other member also ranks tulips-and-off above geraniums-and-running. Rule One makes geraniums-and-running beat tulips-and-running (Alma's box) and tulips-and-running beat tulips-and-off (Bo's diffuser). Rule Two makes tulips-and-off beat geraniums-and-running. The board's collective preference therefore goes in a circle, and whatever motion we might pass, one of our own rules pronounces something else strictly better. We adjourned without deciding anything.

A friend with graduate training in economics tells me this is no accident — that Amartya Sen proved a theorem around 1970 showing that no collective choice procedure can honor both a spheres condition and a unanimity condition, even for a society of two, and that the standard escape routes are either shrinking the spheres until they never conflict or restricting which preferences the unanimity rule is allowed to count. So here is what I want. First, a principle — or a small set of candidates — that generates my case verdicts on the trumpets, flamingos, banners, smoke, fish, pornography, and python, with the verdict it delivers on the python, where I have none of my own. Second, an answer on whether that principle can be made consistent with Rule Two, given that I also hold — flatly inconsistently, I suspect — both that 4B's reading is beyond the board's reach even by unanimous vote and that Rule Two was obviously right when we adopted it. Third, if something has to go, tell me which of my commitments is the weakest and precisely what I lose by dropping it. And I would like you to be exact about whether excluding meddlesome preferences from the unanimity rule — Prudence's distress at 4B's reading, the courtyard's offense at the flamingo — is a principled repair or an ad hoc one, and whether it can be done without smuggling in exactly the sphere-shrinking the spheres rule was supposed to make unnecessary.
