I do a column on science and ideas for a mid-size outlet, and my next piece is on the cogito — the "I think, therefore I am" thing from Descartes. The fun part, to me, is that nobody agrees on what it actually *proves*, so I want to end the piece with a little reader quiz: "what do you think the cogito proves?" But I don't want questions like "do you believe you're a thinking substance" — I want concrete cases, so readers get sorted by what they say about stuff, not by which label they like.

Cases I'm considering (use these plus whatever else fits): the drunk guy forming thoughts he'll later black out and forget — was there a subject there, or just some thoughts happening? A person under anesthesia — did they briefly stop existing? A split-brain patient whose two hemispheres can answer questions at the same time — how many "I"s does the cogito certify there? Lichtenberg's old jab that Descartes should've said it thinks, the way we say it rains — does the "I" in "I think" do any real work? Also worth asking: whether the certainty holds only while the thinking is actually going on (notice Descartes' Meditations wording, not the Discourse slogan, seems to make it true only each time it's thought), whether the cogito is an argument with premises or more like the self-verifying act of saying "I am speaking aloud," and whether it tells you anything about what you are, not just that you are.

So: write me ~8 questions, each with 2-3 options a normal reader can pick without knowing any philosophy jargon, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the main readings — the full-Cartesian one (a persisting thinking substance), the minimal event reading (thoughts exist, the "I" is just grammar), the performative reading, the Kantian one (the "I think" is a condition of experience, not a thing you get to know), and whatever combos come out unnamed. Also flag which pairs your quiz genuinely can't separate, don't paper over that.
