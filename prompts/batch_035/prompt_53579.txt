I am an infectious disease epidemiologist at a national public health agency — fifteen years in outbreak surveillance and field investigation — and I know the philosophy of moral progress only at the level of a well-read layperson. I want a particular thesis about moral progress translated into my professional vocabulary, as faithfully as a translation can be, followed by the residue report: what survives the translation, what is distorted by it, and what cannot be expressed in it at all.

The thesis is the one usually called the "expanding circle": roughly, that the scope of human moral concern has widened over recorded history — from kin and tribe outward to co-nationals, to all humans, and on some versions to non-human animals — that this widening is the substance of moral progress, and that the mechanism is something like contagion: people acquire a wider concern through contact with others who already have it, and then transmit it onward.

Translate this thesis into epidemiology. Treat the state of extending moral concern to some new class of beings as the condition under study, and render each element of the thesis in the terms I actually use: incidence and prevalence; clinical versus surveillance case definitions; attack rates and chains of transmission; the reproduction number; host susceptibility and immunity; endemicity, epidemic waves, and recrudescence; spillover from one host population into another; phylogenetic descent-with-modification of strains. Do it component by component, and tell me where the translation is doing honest work and where it is merely decorative.

Then the residue report — the part I care about most:

1. Directionality. In my vocabulary, spread is spread; an epidemic is not "better" or "worse" than endemicity except in burden of disease. The expanding circle thesis is not directionally agnostic: widening is supposed to be progress, and contraction regress. Where does that live in my vocabulary — is there any counterpart to the direction of moral progress, or does the translation end up treating every episode of spread as the same kind of event, regardless of what is spreading and why?

2. Truth. Nothing in my vocabulary marks whether a spreading condition tracks anything outside the host population; a delusion could spread with identical dynamics. Does the thesis say anything at all about the widened concern being warranted — about the newly included beings actually mattering — or is it, once translated, compatible with the whole historical episode being an epidemic of a false belief? If the latter, was the original thesis smuggling in its normativity under the word "progress," and was it therefore less clear than it looked?

3. The ratchet. I model reversal all the time: waning immunity, reintroduction, collapse of herd protection. The concentric-circle imagery — each containment breached and then held as the next is breached — suggests a ratchet my vocabulary has no reason to expect. What corresponds to immunity against contraction? Does the thesis contain anything that explains why the circle is not supposed to shrink back?

4. Who benefits. I suspect the analogy is not merely imperfect here but structurally backwards, and I want you to be straight with me about it. In my framework the pathogen benefits from transmission and the host bears the cost. In circle expansion, the person who acquires the wider concern is the "infected" one, but the beneficiary is the newly included outgroup. So which is the pathogen and which is the host? Is anything gained by forcing an answer, or does the impossibility of answering tell us that contagion is doing only metaphorical work in the thesis?

5. Ascertainment. We know apparent changes in disease incidence can be artifacts of surveillance — better case definitions, better reporting. Part of the historical widening could be an ascertainment artifact: people may always have had flickers of concern for the outgroup, and what changed is which attitudes could be recorded, and by whom. Can the thesis, as it stands, distinguish genuine incidence change from surveillance change in the history of moral concern, or would it need to be reformulated to admit that it cannot?

Finally, the standing question for this kind of exercise: name everything that did not survive the translation, and give your judgment on each item of residue — whether it is a defect of my vocabulary (something epidemiology cannot say but that is nonetheless true and important) or a defect of the original thesis (something that only sounded clear because it had never been forced into precise terms). Please do not assume any philosophy background beyond what I would pick up from general reading; when you need a philosophical distinction, introduce it in plain terms rather than by name alone.
