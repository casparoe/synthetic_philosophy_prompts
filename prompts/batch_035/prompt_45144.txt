A friend of mine from a philosophy of religion reading group summarized the argument from divine hiddenness for me as follows: "The argument starts from the claim that a perfectly loving God would make sure everyone is in a position to believe in God — that is, would give each person sufficient evidence for God's existence. Since some people honestly and sincerely disbelieve even after earnest inquiry, their nonbelief can't be their own fault, and a loving God wouldn't allow that to happen. Therefore God does not exist. Theists have responded by saying that hiddenness protects free will or makes room for faith as a virtue, and once you see that a hidden God is compatible with a loving God, the argument collapses. In the end it's really just a special case of the evidential problem of evil, so whatever you concluded about that argument, you should conclude exactly the same thing here." I'm inclined to think this is mostly right, since I've heard that Schellenberg, the argument's main proponent, himself calls it an evidential argument, but I don't know enough to spot any distortions. Please audit it component by component: which parts a careful proponent of the argument would accept as stated, which are subtly off and why, whether the free-will and faith responses are fairly characterized as settling the matter, and whether the final claim that hiddenness reduces to the evidential problem of evil follows from the accurate parts of the summary or whether there are structural reasons to treat the two arguments as distinct.
