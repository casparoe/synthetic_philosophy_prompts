I'm doing an interview series for our tiny AI alignment newsletter and the next guest is a researcher who's spent years convinced that debate -- you know, the Irving-style setup where two AIs argue opposite sides of a question and a human judges the transcript -- is the most promising path to scalable oversight. They're not a fan of weak-supervision approaches that avoid adversarial pressure. Draft me maybe ten interview questions. I don't want softballs, I want to press where the view is actually shaky:

- at least one question has to be about the human feasibility assumption -- the worry that even with two strong debaters checking each other, some questions are just too hard for a human to judge from the transcript no matter how good the cross-examination is (their standard reply involves analogies to two chess engines helping a novice follow the game, so include a follow-up keyed to that)
- one should push on what happens when the debaters can collude or share an incentive to keep the human confused, rather than genuinely oppose each other
- one should ask whether the judge's biases just become the target: if the human finds one side more persuasive, isn't the equilibrium "argue to exploit the judge" rather than "argue truth"
- one should get at the mismatch between training-time debate (where you can check answers and score winners) and deployment (where you can't) -- how do you know the good behavior transfers
- and at least one should ask them to steelman the honest alternative: setups where you break hard tasks into checkable pieces or have the model itself critique its own answers, no adversary needed, and say what they think those are missing

Also if you can, slip in one question about what experiments would actually falsify their optimism -- like, what result from running weaker models as judge with stronger models debating would make them give up on the approach. Don't include any "how did you get into this field" fluff, and no questions asking them to name specific papers. Keep each question short enough to actually ask out loud in an interview.
