My book club is working through Jonathan Haidt's The Righteous Mind, and we've gotten stuck on the analogy that organizes the whole book: the moral mind is like a tongue. On Haidt's telling, evolution gave humans a small set of innate moral "receptors" — care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation, and in his later version liberty/oppression — and cultures are like cuisines, differing not in which receptors they have but in how heavily each one is seasoned. His survey work then has liberals drawing mainly on care and fairness while conservatives spread their intuitions across all five or six, and this is supposed to explain why the two sides talk past each other.

At our last meeting one member pressed the analogy a step further than Haidt seems willing to go. If a conservative has all the receptors firing and a liberal has only two or three, she said, then liberals are like the partly colorblind: they are literally failing to perceive parts of the moral world, and the conservative palate is the more adequate one. The conclusion she wanted to license is that conservatism is the better-attuned moral outlook, or at minimum that liberal criticism of conservative positions is often like a colorblind person insisting there is no such thing as orange. Another member objected that a receptor firing is evidence something is in the environment, not evidence that it is bad — disgust fires at holes in skin and at harmless outgroups — so more detectors may just mean more false alarms. I'd like you to audit the analogy rather than adjudicate the politics. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the taste receptors, the cuisines, the supertaster, the flavor that one culture relishes and another finds inedible. Then lay out where the mapping breaks — I can already see some candidates: taste is paradigmatically subjective while moral judgment purports to answer to reasons; the number of distinct detectors a species has is a fact about its evolutionary history, not about how much truth is out there; and a detector can be adaptive to have without tracking anything good. Say which disanalogies actually matter for the normative conclusion (that the wider palate is the more adequate one) and which are incidental, and then tell me whether my friend's conclusion survives once the breaks are on the table — or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole way.

One request about how you frame all this: please keep separate the parts that are empirical, the parts that are interpretive, and the parts that are philosophical. Within the empirical part, distinguish what is broadly settled from what researchers themselves still contest — I gather the exact number and independence of the foundations, and how well the questionnaire evidence holds up outside Western samples, are live disputes, and I don't want those smoothed over. And since the leap from psychological description to who is actually right is a philosophical move that even people sympathetic to Haidt reject, please flag it as genuinely contested rather than resolving it by fiat. If you think a different analogy would do the work better than the tongue — something that keeps what's true in the taste picture without importing its subjectivity — I'd like to hear it.
