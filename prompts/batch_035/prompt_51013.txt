A friend of mine who thinks hard about decision theory has firm views on when an agent's preferences count as irrational, and she's been working through a running list of cases with me. Every case involves three options A, B, and C (think three itineraries — nothing turns on what they are), an agent who starts out holding one of them, and a trader who proposes swaps for cash. Her verdicts so far:

- Vera strictly prefers A to B, B to C, and — the awkward part — C to A. The trader offers her C in exchange for A plus $50 (an improvement by her lights at that moment), then B in exchange for C plus $50, then A in exchange for B plus $50. She accepts each trade and ends up holding A again, $150 poorer. My friend's verdict: irrational. "She was walking a circle and paying for the laps."
- Wes has exactly Vera's preferences, but no trader ever turns up; he never trades at all. My friend's verdict: still irrational. "The flaw is there whether or not anyone cashes in on it."
- Xu strictly prefers A to B and B to C, but is flatly indifferent between C and A. The trader starts with Xu holding C: $50 to upgrade to B, $50 more to upgrade to A, and then offers to take A back and return C with a $1 sweetener — since Xu doesn't care between A and C, the dollar tips it. Xu ends up holding C again, $99 poorer. My friend's verdict: irrational. "A tie doesn't break the circle; it just makes one link cheaper."
- Yolanda has Vera's preferences and is shown the entire script of all three offers in advance, yet takes each trade anyway, on the ground that each one is an improvement from where she stands when it is offered. My friend's verdict: irrational. "Knowing the script and walking it anyway is worse, not better."

Two new agents have now come up, and I'd like to predict her verdicts before we discuss them:

- Zelda has Vera's preferences, is fully informed, and has simply decided, once and for all, to refuse every swap the trader proposes. The trader runs the whole routine; she says no each time; she ends up holding A, out nothing.
- Tomas's preferences are transitive on every single day, but they rotate: Monday he ranks A over B over C; Tuesday B over C over A; Wednesday C over A over B; then the pattern repeats. The trader bides his time: starting on a Monday, he proposes the first swap on Tuesday (when B ranks above A), the second on Wednesday (when C ranks above B), and the third on Thursday (when the Monday-style ranking has returned and A ranks above C). By Thursday evening Tomas is holding A again, $150 poorer, though no day's ranking contains a circle, and each trade he accepted was an improvement by the ranking he held that day.

Will she say Zelda's preferences are irrational? And Tomas's? What I most want from you is not the yes/no but the rule she is implicitly running: the principles that fit her first four verdicts equally well, where those principles come apart on Zelda and on Tomas, which verdicts you would bet on and why, and — if her record plus these two still fails to pin her rule down — a further case that would settle it most cleanly.
