I'm on my school's debate team, and next month we're arguing in favor of a steeply progressive inheritance tax. Our coach drilled us on a single framing: society is a race, equal opportunity means everyone starts from the same line, and inherited wealth is a head start, so the inheritance tax is just the official pulling runners back to the starting line. It's intuitive, and it wins us rounds, but I've started to notice that whenever we use it, the other team answers with what I think is an equally intuitive picture: life isn't a sprint but a relay, where each generation runs one leg and hands the baton to the next, and parents running hard specifically so they can hand off a good position is not cheating but the whole point of the race. Taxing the handoff, they say, is penalizing the only move the relay actually rewards.

I want to understand both analogies rather than just deploy mine. First, for each one, what exactly corresponds to what — in the race picture, what plays the role of the track, the finish line, the runner, the starting-line official, and the stopwatch; in the relay picture, what plays the role of the team, the baton, and the exchange zone? Second, where does each mapping break down — for instance, does the single-race picture quietly assume there is one race with one finish line and one winner, when a society arguably isn't a competition for fixed places at all, and does the relay picture quietly assume the teams are the natural unit, when the runners on one team are also competitors in some deeper sense? Third, which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion each side wants — I don't need every imperfection, just the ones that decide whether the analogy can carry the weight of a policy argument — and which can be fixed with a small patch, like redefining the finish line as "a good life" rather than "first place." Finally, tell me whether my side's conclusion survives once the disanalogies in the starting-line picture are on the table, whether the relay picture supports anything stronger than a modest exemption, and whether there's a third analogy that would serve the debate better than both. Please be concrete enough that I can use this in rebuttal without sounding like I'm just swapping metaphors.
