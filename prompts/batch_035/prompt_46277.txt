I teach phil of mind and I've been trying to figure out whether I actually believe epiphenomenalism by writing down my gut verdicts on cases and seeing if they add up to anything. Here's the list. When I stub my toe and yelp, the pain caused the yelp — sure, obviously. When I solemnly tell my seminar "I am now experiencing a reddish patch in the upper-left of my visual field," my gut says that report is caused by the reddishness itself, not just by whatever neural state is doing the muscle-moving. A blindsight patient guesses that there's an X in his left visual field with no visual experience at all, and my gut says the causal story is complete as-is — nothing phenomenal missing, no complaint. Mary walks out of the black-and-white room and says "so that's what red looks like," and I feel like she got that belief through the experience, which sure sounds like the experience causing something. A perfect zombie, atom-for-atom identical to me, says "I am conscious and my consciousness is doing causal work," and my gut is that his utterance is produced in exactly the way mine is; and yet my gut also insists mine is genuinely about my experience and his isn't about anything — those two feel right together even though they probably shouldn't. And the one I keep going back and forth on: a locked-in patient with untreated pain who later says "I was in agony the whole time, that's why my heart rate was elevated." Something in me wants the agony to be part of the physiological explanation, even though I'd never say that about the zombie's agony.

So: is there a principle that saves all or most of this — maybe something like "phenomenal states determine what our judgments are about without causing anything," i.e. they fix content but not etiology — and can it be stated without collapsing into hand-waving about "aboutness"? What does the best version predict on a case I haven't listed, say someone whose pain is swapped for a phenomenally blank but neurally identical state, so every bodily response and every later report stays the same? And which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? Be honest with me — I'd rather drop one clean than keep them all with a principle nobody could actually use.
