I am a PhD student in cognitive science, and I sit as the postgraduate representative on my university's reasonable adjustments panel, which decides what learning and exam accommodations students with disabilities are entitled to. Increasingly the cases that come to us involve cognitive aids rather than the familiar extra-time-and-separate-room requests: a student with ADHD who has relied on a particular phone app for working-memory scaffolding since sixth form and now wants it in an invigilated closed-book exam; a student with acquired brain injury whose revision lives in an elaborate system of annotated flashcards she wants to bring in; a mathematics student who describes his personally compiled formula sheet as, in effect, his memory for the course. The panel debates these on pure intuition and the outcomes are inconsistent across cases, which leaves us exposed on appeal. I suspect the philosophy of mind actually has something usable here, specifically Clark and Chalmers's extended mind thesis: the thought experiment of Otto, whose notebook plays the role his biological memory would play in Inga, and the idea that a resource counts as part of a person's cognitive system when it is suitably coupled to it -- constantly available, reliably used, endorsed without re-checking, and deployed as automatically as inner recall -- so that consulting it is less like looking something up and more like remembering.

Please turn that thesis into a decision procedure the panel could actually run in the twenty minutes we get per case. It should specify the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in a clear yes or no, and which questions are irreducibly matters of values (academic-integrity judgements, fairness to other candidates) that must be handed back to the panel rather than settled by the framework. In building it, please be explicit about which philosophical distinctions genuinely have to survive the simplification -- for instance the difference between an aid that is constitutively part of the student's cognition and one that is merely a useful external tool -- and which debates the panel can safely ignore, such as the row over cognitive bloat or Rupert's objection that extended processes are just too different from inner ones to count as memory proper; say whether the procedure needs to take a side on that or can bracket it. Then run the procedure on three cases: the sixth-form phone app in a closed-book exam; a student with a documented memory impairment requesting her annotated flashcards in a closed-book history exam where the rubric tests recall of dates; and a case where the procedure delivers a verdict the panel will find unwelcome -- for example, if it classifies a formula sheet as extended memory, we have an awkward conflict with the department's rule that no materials may be brought in, so show us how the procedure handles that collision and whether it hands the final call back to us. Finish by stating honestly what the procedure cannot do, including cases where two students use the identical aid to different cognitive effect. Please answer in British English.
