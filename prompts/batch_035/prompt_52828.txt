So me and two friends have been having this argument for literally months now (since a philosophy club thing at school) and it has completely stalled, and I want to know what kind of disagreement it actually is, if that makes sense?

The question we started with: if my friend has seen a film and tells me it's a masterpiece, can I come to *know* it's a masterpiece just from her telling me, without seeing it myself? One of us says obviously not — you can know scientific facts from testimony (I've never seen an atom but I know they exist), but aesthetic stuff is different, you kind of have to experience the thing yourself, otherwise you've got something second-hand and hollow. Another of us says that's snobbery dressed up as philosophy — if I know my friend's taste is basically identical to mine, we agree on basically every film we've both seen, then her telling me this one is great gives me better grounds than most of my own judgements. And the third of us (me tbh) keeps trying to find a compromise and failing.

The weird thing is the argument never moves. When we do examples it always splits the same way. Like we invented this case: imagine a critic who you've agreed with on 200 films in a row, never once wrong for you, and she says "the new one is great but you have to go in blind." Third friend says you know it's great before you walk in. First friend says you know it's *probably* great, which is different, and that the cinema ticket is still doing all the work. And then we just go in circles. Also when we push on WHY testimony is fine for atoms but not for art, the first friend wavers — sometimes she says it's because aesthetic judgements need "acquaintance" (she read this somewhere), sometimes she says it's because you can't understand an aesthetic reason without already seeing what it's a reason for, and none of us can tell if those are the same point or two different points.

My working theory, and tell me if it's rubbish: we're not actually disagreeing about one thing. Maybe one side is answering "can you KNOW it's good" and the other is answering "can you APPRECIATE it / have the experience that makes aesthetic life worthwhile", and because in normal life those go together we think it's one question. Like maybe the anti-testimony friend is really defending something about the value of experiencing art yourself, which is true, but she keeps stating it as a claim about knowledge, which is maybe not true. If that's right then the whole argument is a bit verbal and we should just split the question in two.

So: is my diagnosis right, is it partly right, or is there a real substantive disagreement hiding in here? If it's substantive, what is it actually about — and what would even count as one of us winning? Would some clever example settle it, or a distinction, or nothing? Please write the answer in British English by the way (we're in the UK and I get marked down for American spellings, no offense). Also if it helps, tell me whether you think grown-up philosophers have this same argument and got stuck in the same place, or whether they found a way through that we're missing.
