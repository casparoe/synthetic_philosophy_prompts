Write me a satirical piece — an internal corporate memo, deadpan all the way through. The setting is the headquarters of a mind: the Cartesian Theater, and the memo's author is the Managing Director, i.e. the homunculus who sits in the audience and watches the show. He's just recieved the restructuring proposal that would abolish the Theater and replace it with something like Dennett's multiple drafts model, and the memo is his case to the Board for rejecting the reorganization. So the target of the satire is the Cartesian theater picture itself: the Director should be funny because he keeps making exactly the moves that show why that picture is hopeless. He insists there must be one place where it all comes together for consciousness. He demands to know the precise moment some content "became conscious" and flatly refuses to accept "there is no fact of the matter about that" as an answer. He cannot picture a theater doing all the work with no audience, and it never once occurs to him that hiring an audience just relocates the problem one level up.

Meanwhile the restructuring proposal he's fighting should reflect the real Dennettian positions, but in paraphrase only — don't put anything in quotation marks: multiple parallel editorial processes with no definitive final edition, the charge that looking for a place in the brain where consciousness happens amounts to Cartesian materialism, the worry about infinite regress. And somewhere in the memo there should be an "incident report" about a screening where the second slide changed color and the audience swore they'd seen it change from the start, with the Director misdescribing the whole thing as a projection-timing dispute and demanding to know whether the record was retroactively rewritten or the showing was delayed — while insisting both must be distinct possibilities with a determinate answer. That's the color phi / Orwell-vs-Stalinesque bit, but he shouldn't get it right.

It should still read as a memo though, not a lecture: numbered agenda items, a staffing section (the specialists allegedly needing no supervision), something petty about his pension. He never doubts for a second that the Theater exists, and he ends by asking the Board to confirm that the Theater has always held a lease on the premises — which, he notes with unease, no one has been able to locate. No winking at the reader; the irony has to stay legible without it. Keep it under 700 words total.
