I'm the deputy director for prioritization at a regional health authority, and I chair the panel that decides which of roughly ten to fifteen interventions gets funded in each quarterly cycle — new drugs, service expansions, disinvestment proposals — from a budget that never covers everything worth doing. The panel is a mix of clinicians, health economists, and lay members, and whatever we decide has to be defensible in a published rationale, so I need a procedure rather than a philosophy seminar: a sequence of steps the panel can actually run in a two-hour meeting, with something like a checklist, that a clinician with no ethics training can follow and a journalist can read afterward.

I want the procedure grounded in prioritarianism rather than straight cost-effectiveness maximization: the view that a benefit to someone who is worse off counts for more than the same benefit to someone better off, not because inequality is bad in itself but because of the diminishing moral weight of benefits as a person's position improves. Three things need to be built into the design. First, the leveling-down objection: one of our economists insists that any view that weights distribution this way must, in some case, endorse making better-off people worse off with no compensating gain to anyone, and that a procedure with that implication is unusable in a public body; I want the procedure to include an explicit test for whether it ever yields that verdict, and if it can, for the procedure to say so plainly rather than hide it. Second, the definition of "worse off": worse off right now (the patient with terminal illness) or over a lifetime (the young person whose whole life will be constrained) — these come apart constantly in our decisions, and the procedure needs a rule or an honest pointer to where the panel must choose. Third, a colleague has proposed a concrete operationalization — any health gain to a person below a fixed threshold of health-related quality of life counts double — and I want the procedure to evaluate that specific weighting rule rather than gesturing at weights in the abstract.

Please structure the response in four sections. (1) "The procedure," roughly six hundred words: the numbered steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in a no, and where it must hand the question to the panel as a judgment call rather than a fact. (2) "Where judgment cannot be eliminated," roughly two hundred words. (3) "Three worked cases," roughly five hundred words total: a choice between expanding intensive care for the severely ill and a mental-health program preventing mild-to-moderate depression in working-age adults; a disinvestment proposal to withdraw low-value physiotherapy from moderately ill patients in order to fund palliative home care for the severely ill, where the total health-years lost slightly exceed those gained; and at least one case where the procedure yields an answer I will find uncomfortable, stated without softening. (4) "What the procedure cannot do," roughly two hundred words, including a straight answer on whether my colleague's doubling rule survives the leveling-down test.
