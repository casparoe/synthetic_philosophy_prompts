At a dinner a couple of weeks ago, two friends of mine got into a long argument about whether it is misleading to say that the Classic Maya 'collapsed'. I'll report the exchange roughly as it happened, and I'd like you to adjudicate it: say who is right at each step, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. They asked me to referee at the time and I honestly couldn't.

(1) Dan opened by objecting to the word 'collapse' itself. He said the Terminal Classic period saw the dynastic kingdoms of the southern lowlands stop erecting monuments and lose most of their population, but the Maya did not disappear: cities in the north, such as Chichen Itza, rose and flourished afterwards; Maya languages kept being spoken, and writing continued in the region; millions of Maya live there today. So what ended was a particular political arrangement, and saying 'the civilisation collapsed' implies that something died which in fact carried on. 'Transformation' is the more accurate word, and 'collapse' is a leftover from an old habit of treating societies as organisms with lifespans.

(2) Emma replied that this proves too much: by that standard nothing ever collapses, because something always continues. The western Roman empire is said to have collapsed even though Byzantium carried on Roman law and administration, and cities and people persisted throughout the former provinces. If continuity anywhere in the tradition is enough to downgrade every collapse to a transformation, the word loses its use entirely — and it has a use. There is a real difference between a society that downsizes in an orderly, deliberate way and one in which the coordinating institutions — the ones that could maintain large-scale water systems, keep records, and sustain long-distance exchange — come apart faster than they can be replaced. The southern lowlands went from sustaining very large urban populations to being largely emptied within a few generations; that difference of scale and speed is what the word 'collapse' tracks.

(3) Dan said 'collapse' also smuggles in a value judgement, and always from above. It is written from the standpoint of the people who profited from the old order: kings, priests, scribes. For an ordinary farmer, the end of the monument-building cycle might have meant the end of decades of conscripted labour on temples and of recurrent dynastic wars, and we have no way of knowing that the average person's life got worse rather than better. Calling it a collapse enshrines the elite ledger as the ledger. Emma's coordinating-institutions test in (2) is an elite test too: those institutions existed to serve cities, and most people never lived the way urban elites did.

(4) Emma granted that we cannot survey peasant welfare, but said the point cuts both ways: if we cannot know that ordinary life improved, neither can Dan assert it, and meanwhile some facts are on the table that nobody could call elite preoccupations. The population of the lowlands fell dramatically — whole regions were largely depopulated. Whatever the mechanism behind it, that is not a political reorganisation; it is mass death, flight, or both, and the people living through it did not experience it as a change of regime. She added that Dan's (3) would license calling anything short of extinction a 'change', and she suspected his real motive is a fashionable distaste for doom narratives rather than a discovery about the Maya.

(5) Dan called that last remark cheap, but conceded part of (4): if demographic decline on that scale counts as evidence, then their dispute is not really about the word 'collapse' — it is about which facts are in play, and had they agreed on the facts they would have agreed on the word. That, he said, shows the terminological debate is shallow, and those who philosophise about collapse should get out of the way of the archaeologists. Emma said the exchange showed exactly the opposite: two people could agree on all the facts and still disagree, because 'collapse' is not a report of a fact-pattern but a choice of comparison class — cities of one century against cities of an earlier one, the lowlands against the north, the region against its own past — and choosing a comparison class is a judgement that no amount of excavation can make for you.

Please go through the exchange move by move, saying who is right about what at each step — not merely who wins overall. Then, separately, address Dan's closing claim and Emma's answer to it: is their disagreement one that further facts would settle, or one that facts cannot settle by themselves? If you can, sketch what a definition of 'societal collapse' would have to look like for disagreements of this kind to dissolve rather than merely be postponed. Please end your reply with a final line of the form

Verdict: <a short phrase>

where the phrase names, in your judgement, the single most mistaken move in the exchange.
