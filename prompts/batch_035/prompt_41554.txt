I sit on a parliamentary select committee charged with reorganizing the nation's trade and governing arrangements in the aftermath of the late war, and my colleagues and I have agreed to take as our framework the verdicts Adam Smith delivered in The Wealth of Nations of 1776. A new proposal has come before us, and it falls to me to draft the committee's recommendation. Before doing so, I want to be clear about what our framework actually dictates. I set out below the verdicts Smith delivered on earlier cases, followed by the new case.

His earlier verdicts, as we have compiled them:

1. The monopoly of the colony trade. Merchants and manufacturers at home enjoyed a monopoly over commerce with the colonies. Smith's verdict: a plain loss to the nation. He held that the arrangement transferred wealth from the many to a handful of merchants, drew capital away from its natural employments, and could never have been enacted had the legislature been guided by the interest of the whole rather than by the merchants' importunity.

2. The Navigation Acts. These also restrained trade, reserving the carrying trade to and from the colonies to English ships and mariners, and on strict commercial principles they resemble case 1. Yet Smith approved of them. His ground was that such regulations favour the growth of English shipping, that shipping is the nursery of naval power, and that where free trade and national defence conflict, defence must win — he expresses the point to the effect that defence is of far more importance than opulence — and he indicated that this was more or less the single case in which a restraint of trade could be justified on public grounds.

3. The East India Company. A chartered company held the exclusive trade with the East Indies and, by consequence of its charter, governed extensive territories there. Smith's verdict: scathing. He judged the government of an exclusive company of merchants to be, in effect, the worst of all governments for any country whatever, and found it absurd that a body whose proper business is to buy and sell should also be sovereigns. At the same time he was pessimistic that direct administration from London would do much better, given the distance.

4. The American crisis. Parliament taxed the colonies while denying them representation. Smith's verdict: Britain must either grant the colonies seats in Parliament in proportion to the taxes they bear, or voluntarily relinquish all authority over them; and he predicted that Britain would do neither and would instead lose the colonies by force, which he regarded as the foolish outcome.

The new case: a joint-stock company has petitioned for the exclusive right to trade with, and the government of, a group of islands acquired from Spain in the late war. The petitioners argue that only a company assured of monopoly profits could afford the forts, garrisons, and convoys needed to hold the islands against France and Spain; that the expense of defence would exceed any revenue an open trade could yield; and that, under company administration, the islands would be governed by men with a direct interest in their prosperity, as Bengal was once said to be. Nobody proposes representation for the islanders in Parliament.

My questions are these. First, predict Smith's verdict on the new case. Second, identify the candidate rules his track record is consistent with — the priority of the system of natural liberty, the defence exception that licensed his approval of the Navigation Acts, his views on company government and on governing at a distance, and the representation-or-relinquishment principle of case 4 — and say which of them diverge on this case. Third, name the closest analog among the earlier cases, and say whether the analogy runs in favor of approval (because the company invokes the same warrant the Navigation Acts had) or of rejection (as in the East India Company's rule). Fourth, give your best guess at the verdict, with your reasons for preferring it. Fifth, tell me what further case, should our committee face one, would most cleanly separate the candidate rules. And finally, if you think the precedents genuinely underdetermine the recommendation, say plainly what principle we would be adopting with each of the ways we could go.
