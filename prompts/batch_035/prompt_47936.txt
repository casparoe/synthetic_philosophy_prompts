quick background: I'm a novelist, most of a draft done now, set almost entirely in one room — a three-member tribunal deciding whether a surgeon keeps her operating privileges. The hospital charter says she only loses them if the tribunal finds BOTH that she deviated from standard practice and that the deviation caused the patient's death. I've set up the votes so the discursive dilemma actually bites: the chairwoman says yes-deviation/yes-cause, the second member says yes-deviation/no-cause, the third says no-deviation/yes-cause. So voting premise by premise she gets striped of privileges 2-1 on each question, but on a straight up-or-down verdict vote she keeps them 1-2, and the whole last act is the fight over which procedure they use. My problem: I want the premise-voting side to win because it's actually right, not because the plot needs them to, and a couple early readers said the premise vote reads like a lawyer's gotcha. So please argue that premise-based voting is genuinely the more rational and fairer procedure for this tribunal — I want to believe it when the chairwoman wins — while being honest about the strongest thing I can put in the dissenting member's mouth, since he gets the best lines and I don't want him fighting a strawman. In particular tell me whether the worry that whoever controls which premises go on the list controls the outcome is a real crack in the argument or actually fuel for the dissent.
