At our history and philosophy of science reading group, a colleague who styles himself a thoroughgoing Lakatosian has been issuing verdicts on research programmes. His record so far: the mature Ptolemaic programme was degenerating, each anomaly patched with further epicycles carrying no independent testable content; the Copernican programme was progressive from the start, since it implied the phases of Venus well before Galileo observed them, notwithstanding its ad hoc expedients about stellar distances; Newtonian celestial mechanics remained progressive through the Uranus crisis because postulated Neptune was a bold, independently testable prediction; Lorentz's ether programme was degenerating, since the FitzGerald contraction was invented solely to neutralize the Michelson–Morley result; and the 1913 Bohr atom was progressive, on the strength of both its derivation of the known Balmer formula and its reinterpretation of the puzzling Pickering lines as belonging to ionized helium. Next month the group turns to the dark matter programme. Please predict his verdict: does the postulation of unseen mass to rescue Newtonian dynamics from rotation-curve anomalies parallel his Neptune ruling or his FitzGerald ruling, and does his assessment change across the programme's history — Zwicky and Rubin, gravitational lensing, the Bullet Cluster, the cosmic microwave background, and the decades of null results in direct WIMP searches? I genuinely cannot tell whether his Bohr verdict turned on the novel helium prediction or on the derivation of the already-known spectral formula, so I do not know which rule he is running. Please set out the candidate rules that fit his record, say what each predicts about the dark matter case, give your best guess at his verdict with reasons, and name the further case, historical or contemporary, that would best discriminate between those rules.
