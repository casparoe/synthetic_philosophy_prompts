i'm a backend engineer and i help run our company's donation matching committee — small pot, like $80k a year, we pick a handful of nonprofits to feature each cycle. last month a coworker (big effective altruism guy) pitched us a framework for choosing causes, importance/tractability/neglectedness, and he leaned HARD on an investing analogy the whole time. his version: importance is market size, tractability is your edge, and neglectedness is market inefficiency — the signal that there's money on the table. the punchline: once a cause gets popular, it's like a trading anomaly that's been published and arbitraged away, the alpha decays, so by the time something is a household name (climate, global health basics) it's basically big-cap tech — efficient, priced in, skip it. fund the weird unloved stuff instead, that's where the excess returns are. he even claimed that if some intervention gets famous, we should expect its measured effect size to shrink over time, the same way quant strategies stop working once everyone knows about them.

some of us found this pretty persuasive but a couple things bugged me and i can't put my finger on exacly why. for one, donors don't seem to work like market participants — nobody "competes away" the good of a deworming program by also funding it, and there's no price mechanism. for another, sometimes more funding makes a cause MORE attractive, not less (fixed costs getting covered, advocacy crossing a threshold). and i suspect crowdedness can be evidence that a cause is important rather than evidence the returns are gone.

can you audit the analogy? give me the correspondence table (what plays the asset, what plays the price, who's the competition, who's the arbitrageur), then say which breaks actually matter for how we'd use this to pick causes with an $80k pot and which ones are just technical nitpicks, whether his "famous = effect size decays" claim follows from the analogy at all or is something he's smuggling in from elsewhere, and whether the punchline — skip the popular causes, fund the weird stuff — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. also want to know what the analogy quietly imports that we don't have (zero-sum competition? prices? profit motive?), since that seems like where the damage is. and if there's a better analogy for what a small donor committee is actually doing, i'd take that too.

lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning. no preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
