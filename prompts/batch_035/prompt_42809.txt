Write a parody of a software-as-a-service terms-of-service agreement, complete with a tiered pricing table, for a startup that sells conspiracy theories by subscription. The company, the tiers, and the product names are yours to invent. About 700 words, formatted as numbered clauses with section headings.

The humor must come from the actual epistemology of conspiratorial belief, not from mockery of believers. In particular, the fine print should quietly enact the standard mechanisms: a clause governing what happens when evidence surfaces against the theory (the service "upgrades" rather than cancels — ad hoc rescue and the self-sealing structure of the belief); an auto-renewal clause under which refutation renews the subscription, with debunking handled as a billable feature; a liability clause distinguishing what the company is "just asking questions" about from what the customer is entitled to conclude, so the motte can be defended in court while the bailey is what's actually sold; a "minimum conspirator headcount" disclosure in which the company, with visible reluctance, acknowledges that larger plots leak faster, and indemnifies itself against the arithmetic; and tier differences that turn on how much of the customer's evidence base is replaced with distrust — the premium plans selling not more content but fewer trusted sources, so that at the top tier no possible observation is billable as disconfirmation.

Add one clause that is funny only if you get the statistics right: something like a warranty on the leak rate, or a refund policy tied to the theory's explanatory scope (it explains everything, so it guarantees nothing).

Tone: deadpan corporate legalese throughout. A social epistemologist should recognize unfalsifiability, gerrymandered trust, the asymmetry between explanatory scope and prior probability, and the recursive absorption of disconfirming events without any of those terms appearing in the text. No winking at the reader, no editorial asides, no named real-world conspiracy theories or living people — the product line should be invented but plausible. The piece should be readable and funny to someone with no epistemology background, and fair-minded enough that a thoughtful conspiracy-inclined reader would find the company, not themselves, being satirized.
