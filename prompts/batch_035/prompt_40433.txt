A friend of mine who works on lichen systematics and I spent most of last weekend arguing, in increasingly sharp terms, about whether a lineage that reproduces only clonally but forms a stable, morphologically coherent cluster should count as a species. By the end we had discovered something more interesting than the answer: we kept each flip-flopping between positions depending on the case on the table — one of us was an isolationist about lichens, a confirmed splitter about cryptic moths, and something close to a nominalist about the fossil record, without noticing the pattern. We clearly hold views; we just can't name them, and the standard textbook tour of species concepts never gets at that instability because it asks which concept we like in the abstract rather than what we say about cases.

I would like you to write a diagnostic quiz, titled something like "Which species concept do you actually hold?", suitable for posting on a small philosophy-of-biology blog I help run. The requirements:

Ten questions. Each question is a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so that someone with a good biology background but no philosophy of biology could answer it without ever guessing which school of thought their answer feeds. The following cases must appear somewhere: a ring species whose terminal forms cannot interbreed; an ancient asexual lineage that forms a tight phenotypic cluster but exchanges genes with no one; a bacterium that routinely takes up DNA from distantly related lineages; a pair of hybridizing oak populations that exchange genes along a stable zone without merging; a chronospecies in a fossil sequence that grades continuously from one morphology to another; cryptic sibling species distinguishable only by song or pheromone; and a case where splitting a widespread organism into five species would change its conservation listing from "least concern" to "endangered" for four of them. No question may ask which concept of species the reader endorses, or use any of the standard names for the positions.

The scoring key should be built from the logical space first — the questions that actually divide the positions — and then locate the positions in that space, not the reverse. The leaves must include isolation-based, diagnosability-based, and monophyly-based versions of the phylogenetic position as separate outcomes where the cases permit it, plus the evolutionary lineage view, the ecological view, a phenetic cluster view, and outright species nominalism. Where two leaves cannot genuinely be separated by any case I have given you, say so plainly rather than inventing a fake distinction.

Please structure the response as follows, in this order:

1. "Preamble" — roughly 100 words of framing for the quiz-taker, warning them that their answers may not be consistent.
2. "The ten cases" — each 50 to 75 words, options of one or two sentences apiece.
3. "The scoring key" — about 600 words, mapping answer patterns to the positions above.
4. "Unoccupied combinations" — about 200 words on answer patterns no listed position can accommodate, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why nobody stands there.
5. "What this quiz cannot sort" — about 150 words, honest about whether the cases can distinguish viewing species as natural kinds from viewing them as individuals with spatiotemporal extension, or whether that question runs orthogonal to everything I have asked.
