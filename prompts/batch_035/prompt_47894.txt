Write the joint statement that two historians of the French Revolution could sign after a year-long good-faith adversarial collaboration. The first is a committed adherent of the classic social interpretation, in the line of Georges Lefebvre and Albert Soboul: the Revolution of 1789 was, at bottom, a class struggle in which a rising bourgeoisie, held back by feudal privilege and noble monopoly of office, broke the aristocracy's grip and cleared the way for a capitalist social order. The second is a committed revisionist, in the spirit of François Furet and the Tocquevillian tradition: the Revolution is best understood as a political and ideological event, driven by a new democratic political culture and by struggles for power within a post-aristocratic elite whose bourgeois and noble members were not, by 1789, economically opposed classes at all.

The document should have four parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. Include the institutional record both should be able to sign: the abolition of feudal dues and seigneurial rights, the sale of church lands as biens nationaux, legal equality, the Civil Constitution of the Clergy and its consequences, the abolition of monarchy, and the eventual stabilization of a regime of property-owning male citizens under Napoleon. They should also agree on what the Terror and Thermidor did to the revolutionary leadership — Danton, Hébert, and the Girondins all dying by the revolutionary tribunal itself — and agree on a precise formulation of the question of whether revolutions devour their children, stated neutrally enough that both would accept it as a genuine question rather than a rhetorical one.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should state a disagreement in wording its own side would accept — for example, on whether the bourgeoisie was a coherent class with distinct economic interests, on whether the events of 1793–94 were a continuation of 1789 or a betrayal or deviation from it, and on how much explanatory weight to give the ideology of republican virtue versus the pressures of foreign war and the Vendée. Mark each disagreement as empirical, conceptual, or partly verbal; where a dispute is partly verbal — for instance, over what a bourgeois revolution would have to be for the category to do any work — say so and explain what would remain a real disagreement once the words are fixed.

Third, a cruxes section. Identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party, and for each crux specify a concrete piece of evidence, argument, or case that both sides would accept as bearing on it: for example, the pattern of purchases of church lands and émigré property (did they go mainly to bourgeois buyers, and does that support the social interpretation of the Revolution's outcome?), the degree of social interpenetration of nobles and bourgeois elites before 1789 (does blurring of the elite undermine the notion of a bourgeois class acting as a class?), and the counterfactual of the war from 1792 (would a Revolution that avoided war have produced a Terror?).

Fourth, for each crux, record each side's prediction in advance — what each expects the evidence or argument would show if their interpretation is correct — so that the document could in principle be scored later. Close with a short section on whether the two historians agree that anything currently known already decisively bears on any crux, and if either concedes that some of their own side's traditional rhetoric would not survive this scrutiny, they should say so in their own voice within the joint statement.
