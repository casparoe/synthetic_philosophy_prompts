I'm a fifth-year PhD student in metaphysics, and in three weeks I'm giving a job talk at a department where I'll be defending counterpart theory against transworld identity. My thesis, in brief: de re modal claims about individuals should be analyzed in terms of counterpart relations — relations of relevant similarity holding between individuals inhabiting different worlds — rather than in terms of a single individual's literally being in multiple worlds; and the standard objections to this, from Kaplan's Humphrey-style complaint that it deprives me of being counterpart to rather than being the one who wins the election, to the complaint that counterpart theory cannot preserve the necessity of identity, either fail or can be met once we allow that there are many counterpart relations, with context determining which one matters for a given modal claim. Crucially, I want to remain neutral on modal realism itself: I claim the counterpart-theoretic machinery is available whether one's worlds are Lewis's concrete ones or ersatz constructions, and I'd rather not have the talk read as a defense of concrete possible worlds. My sketch of the argument leans on Chisholm's-paradox-style cases (where transworld identity seems to require arbitrariness about how much of an individual's history carries over) as the positive case for counterparts, and on treating the Humphrey worry as trading on a failure to distinguish what I do according to a world from what my counterparts do.

The audience is the difficulty. The department is mostly metaphysicians of a post-Kripkean temperament — people for whom rigid designation and the necessity of identity are background commitments rather than discussion topics, several of whom think haecceitism about individuals is where the dialectic ended up rather than something needing argument. There are two genuinely Lewisian metaphysicians who will hold me to the details of the counterpart-theoretic translation of quantified modal logic and who, I suspect, will think my contextualist move about multiple counterpart relations sells out the reductive ambition that made counterpart theory worth having in the first place. And there is a logician in the back who, I am told, asks every metaphysics speaker which inference patterns of quantified modal logic survive the translation into counterpart theory, and follows up until he gets a real answer. Nobody in this room will be polite out of kindness.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the ten or so objections I will actually get from this audience — not the ones I would get from a general philosophy audience — ordered roughly by how much damage each would do to the talk, each phrased the way this particular audience would phrase it (the Kripkeans' versions and the Lewisians' versions will sound different, and I want both), with a two-minute reply to each and a frank note on where the reply is soft. Include the objections the Lewisians will raise about my neutrality on modal realism, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Throughout, please separate what is settled among people who actually work on this from what is genuinely contested — especially where my replies depend on a particular reading of Lewis or of the necessity-of-identity arguments that others in the literature reject — so that I do not present a contested interpretation as consensus and get caught doing it by someone who knows the literature better than I do. Please write the prep sheet in American English.
