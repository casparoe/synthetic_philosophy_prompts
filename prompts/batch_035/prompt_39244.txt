I'm part of a small reading group that has been working through Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach since last autumn — mostly computer science people, plus one bemused medievalist — and we keep getting stuck on the idea of "jumping out of the system" (Hofstadter's JOOTSing). Rather than argue about it in the abstract, I've been collecting my own gut verdicts on concrete cases, and I'd like to know whether they add up to a principle, and if so which.

Here are the verdicts. First, the MU-puzzle: you start with the string MI and may apply four rules — append U to a string ending in I; replace any string of the form Mx with Mxx (doubling everything after the M); replace any occurrence of III with U; and delete any occurrence of UU. The goal is to derive MU, which is impossible. My verdict: no amount of searching inside the system could ever establish this, since failing to find a derivation is not evidence that none exists; the actual solution — noticing that the number of I's is never a multiple of three, since the rules can neither create nor destroy that residue — feels like a genuine jump out, bringing to bear something the system's own transformations cannot see. Second, the Tortoise who accepts each axiom and each inference rule as an explicit premise and still refuses to draw the conclusion: my verdict is that this shows a real difference between operating within a system and merely possessing its rules as objects, and that at the bottom of any formal system there is always something outside it that actually applies the rules. Third, Gödel's construction of a sentence of arithmetic that, read from outside, says of itself that it is unprovable: here my verdict is the opposite — no jump occurred, because the outside viewpoint, the semantics, got fully coded back inside the system; everything that mattered was internalized. Fourth, a speedcuber who flails with memorized move sequences for months, then learns some group theory and starts seeing why the sequences work: my verdict is that this is not a jump out, just a move to a richer system that still contains her — though I'm less sure of this one. Fifth, a chess player who, after thousands of games, starts to sense when a position is quiet without calculating any lines: verdict, no jump, mere pattern-matching within — but this one sits oddly beside my verdict on the MU-puzzle, since the mod-3 invariant also just gets "sensed" by someone standing far enough back.

So my difficulty is this: the MU verdict and the Gödel verdict pull in opposite directions (in one case the outside view is unreachable from inside; in the other it is folded back inside), and the chess verdict is hard to square with treating the invariant-spotter as having jumped out rather than merely having gotten better at within-system pattern recognition. Is there one principle — perhaps something about when a question about a system can be settled by resources the system's own rules can express, versus when it needs a vantage point the rules cannot reconstruct — that delivers all five verdicts, or close? If not, which verdicts would you overturn, and at what cost? Please also tell me what the resulting principle predicts about the case I keep going back and forth on: whether a sufficiently clever program (or a person) could jump out of itself entirely, or only ever out of subsystems while remaining inside the whole — and what it says about a further case I haven't listed, namely whether a program could discover the mod-three invariant on its own, without a human supplying the outside view.
