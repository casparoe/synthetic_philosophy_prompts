I am a novelist, roughly two-thirds of the way through a manuscript whose pivotal scene turns on a metaphysical wrinkle I want to get right rather than hand-wave. My protagonist is a sculptor who, late in life, melts down a bronze figure she cast decades earlier in order to reuse the metal for a war memorial commissioned by the town that has come to regard the old figure as a public artwork. The local paper accuses her of having "destroyed a sculpture"; her lawyer argues that nothing was destroyed, only the material re-allocated, since the bronze persists. I intend to stage the argument between the two of them as a genuine philosophical dispute, and to that end I have been reading around the material constitution literature — the statue and the clay, the lump and the mass. The trouble is that every summary I find gives me the labels without the mechanics, and my dialogue needs mechanics.

Here is what I want explained, in terms a determined non-philosopher can follow but without dumbing anything down. First, the Leibniz's Law argument for coincidence: I understand that the statue can survive minor dents while the clay can survive being flattened, and that this is supposed to show they are distinct; but I have also read that versions like "the statue is valuable, the lump is not" fail because they trade on relational or intentional properties rather than intrinsic ones — I would like a careful account of which properties do the real work in these arguments and which are red herrings. Second, the grounding problem: if the statue and the clay share all their microphysical parts and their entire history, what is supposed to explain why they differ in when they came into existence and what it takes for each to cease to exist? I have seen it claimed that there is no informative answer available to anyone who takes coincidence seriously, and that this is the argument's Achilles heel — I would like to know whether that is right, and what the most promising response looks like (for instance, tracing the difference to the sortals under which the objects fall, or to conventions about when we start counting artworks). Third, the deflationary escape: the view that we have one object here, referred to in two ways, with "statue" and "lump" merely tracking different interests or counting conventions rather than different entities. I want a serious statement of whether that reply actually dissolves the problem or only relocates it — in particular, whether it can accommodate the intuitive difference in survival conditions without smuggling in two objects under another name.

Finally, since my novel needs a verdict and not just a taxonomy: state, with reasons, which of these treatments you find most defensible, and then tell me what that treatment implies for my scene — when the bronze went into the crucible, was an artwork destroyed, was one object swapped for another, or was a single persisting lump merely redescribed? I should add that I am not looking for a survey of names attached to positions; positions interest me only insofar as you can show me the reasoning that does the work, and I would rather have two or three lines of argument reconstructed carefully than a dozen catalogued. If it helps to invent small variations on my sculptor's case — her reshaping rather than melting, or her assembling the original figure from someone else's earlier cast — to isolate where the philosophical pressure falls, please do; my interest is in the structure of the problem, not in quotations from the literature.
