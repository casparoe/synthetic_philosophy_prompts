I am teaching a graduate seminar on scientific realism this term, and in preparing a unit on structural realism I put together an argument of the sort the literature calls Newman's objection to Russell. I intended it as an anti-structuralist exhibit for discussion, but on reflection it seems to prove far more than I bargained for, and I would like help locating exactly where it goes astray. The argument runs as follows. (1) Epistemic structural realism says that our knowledge of the unobservable realm extends only to its structure — the network of relations into which things enter — and not to the intrinsic natures of the relata themselves. (2) A theory's structural content can be specified in abstraction from any facts about the intrinsic character of the relata: roughly, it consists in the number of objects it posits and the pattern of relations it lays down over them. (3) A purely combinatorial fact now comes into play: given any collection of objects at least as large as the theory requires, and any pattern of relations whatever, there exist relations over that collection exhibiting exactly that pattern — the relations can simply be defined to order. (4) Hence for any theory of the unobservable, and any world with enough objects in it, we can define relations over that world's objects that make the theory's structural content true of it. (5) So any theory of the unobservable is structurally true of any sufficiently populous world. (6) Therefore whatever knowledge of unobservable structure structural realism grants us is trivial: at the level of structure, nothing distinguishes our best theories from any consistent rival.

Here is what troubles me. If the argument is sound, it ought not to stop at the unobservable. Russell himself granted that our knowledge of the ordinary observed world is structural through and through — the desk, the photons, the sense-data, all of it known only by its relational profile. So the same reasoning that trivializes our structural knowledge of electrons trivializes our structural knowledge of tables, and my handout has quietly argued us into a skepticism about everything. Either that, or something in the chain from (2) through (5) is weaker than it appears. My suspicion is that the slide happens somewhere between (3) and (5), in the passage from there existing some relations that realize the theory's pattern to the world's relations realizing it — a scope shift, perhaps, or an unnoticed assumption that all relations are on a par when surely the physically distinguished ones are not. But I have not been able to make that suspicion precise without merely stipulating that the relevant relations are "natural," which looks like smuggling intrinsic natures back in through the side door — the very thing structural realism was supposed to do without.

Tell me which step of the argument I should reject, why that step looked acceptable in the first place, and what rejecting it costs — including, if you think the argument is simply valid, what it would mean to accept its conclusion and whether the resulting position is coherent or just skepticism wearing structuralist clothing. For my part, I would very much like to keep the thought that structure is a substantial and not a trivial part of what we know, and I would like to keep it without conceding that the rescue requires a doctrine of natural relations rich enough to undermine the structuralist's official austerity. But do not let those preferences constrain you; I want a diagnosis, not a comfort.
