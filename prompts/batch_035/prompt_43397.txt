So I've been going to our local rationalist meetup for like 12 years and somehow nobody has ever done a proper session on David Lewis's modal realism, which is wild given how much energy that crowd has spent on possible worlds as made-up bookkeeping devices. I voluntered to do it next month. Audience: ~15 people, programmers plus a couple philosophy grad students, all comfortable with basic logic and decision theory, several have seen Kripke-style semantics used in logic/CS contexts, but basically nobody has read Lewis beyond maybe skimming On the Plurality of Worlds.

I don't want you to explain modal realism to me — I know the view: the worlds are concrete, spatiotemporally isolated from each other, all equally real, and "actual" works like "here", an indexical that picks out whichever world you happen to be in. What I want is a teaching plan.

Specifically:

1. Ordering: I'm torn between (a) starting with possible worlds as a semantic bookkeeping device — how logicians use them for counterfactuals and modal claims — and only then escalating to Lewis's ontological claim that the worlds really exist, vs. (b) starting straight from a concrete counterfactual and building up to the ontology. Which dependencies actually matter, and how do I sequence it so the ontological move doesn't feel like a bait and switch? That's my biggest worry, that people will feel they got sold one thing and delivered another.

2. Carrying example: what single case should carry the whole session? It has to be concrete enough that people feel the pull of quantifying over ways-things-could-have-been before anyone says the word "ontology". I'm toying with chess move evaluation ("Qf7 loses because in the nearby worlds where you play it, Black has a forced mate in three") — good idea or is there something better? And what should the example deliberately NOT involve, so it doesn't smuggle in confusions later? Half the room is going to arrive primed with Everett many-worlds from physics, I just know it.

3. Misconceptions: my predictions are (i) "Lewis is just claiming the quantum multiverse is real", (ii) "if all worlds are equally real then nothing I do matters, nihilism", (iii) "if every world exists, nothing is impossible, so modal distinctions collapse". Where in the sequence should each one get headed off? And am I missing any? I have a nagging feeling there's a predictable screwup around counterpart theory vs. transworld identity — like people will think it's a substantive metaphysical claim about persons when it's really a device for making de re modal claims come out right — but I can't articulate the failure mode cleanly, so help me out there.

4. There's one guy who will 100% bring up parsimony: "Lewis multiplied entities by a factor of infinity and calls it parsimonious, lol". I want to handle the objection head on (qualitative vs. quantitative parsimony, the idea that his ontology is rich in kinds, not in number of kinds) rather than dodge it — but can I do that in a way that doesn't drag the whole session into the ersatz worlds literature? Or should I just name-drop ersatzism as "the competition" and move on?

5. Check for understanding: what one question at the end would actually test whether they got the indexicality of "actual", as opposed to just nodding along? I want something where the wrong answer reveals the exact residual confusion.

6. What do I openly refuse to cover in 90 minutes? Presumably the formal machinery of counterpart theory is out, the full Lewis-van Inwagen correspondence stuff probably out — what else, and should I announce the omissions at the start or when they come up?

Side question: do I even bring up the old Eliezer line that possible worlds are just made-up constructs, as a foil at the end, or does that just muddy things? A chunk of the room treats the Sequences as scripture and I don't want the session to turn into a referendum on that.
