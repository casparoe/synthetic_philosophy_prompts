One idea I keep circling back to is surrogate goals: an agent publicly commits to treating an attack on some cheap decoy target as if it were an attack on the thing it actually cares about, so that would-be threateners find nothing left that's cheap to threaten. The standard pitch is that this deterrence is basically free, because no rational threatener should ever bother attacking the decoy, so the commitment never gets called in practice. But every time I try to explain it in the article I hit the same doubt: deterrence that seems to good to be true usually has a failure mode hiding somewhere.

The backfiring has to come out of the mechanism of surrogate goals itself (what a visible precommitment signals to observers, or what happens once the commitment actually gets called), not from the agent picking an obviously stupid decoy — that would be cheating, like a trolley case where the villain just happens to be tied to the other track by stipulation. And the scenario itself should be a single paragraph with stakes an ordinary reader can picture.
