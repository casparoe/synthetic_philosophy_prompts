For the past two years I've been writing about mechanistic interpretability for a general audience of science readers, and I keep running into a dispute that never appears in the papers themselves but dominates the conversations around them. I'd like your help diagnosing it, because I'm writing a feature and I can't tell whether I'm watching a genuine scientific disagreement or two research programs talking past each other.

The dispute is over whether neural networks "really" have representations. One camp — call them the realists — takes features to be genuine objects in the network. On their picture, a trained model compresses more features than it has neurons by storing them in overlapping, partially interfering directions (the superposition picture), which is why individual neurons look polysemantic and meaningless; the point of tools like sparse autoencoders is to recover the underlying sparse features that were there all along, the way a good microscope recovers structure that was in the specimen, not in the lens. The causal tests are supposed to seal it: if you can ablate one direction and the model loses the ability to do exactly the thing you predicted, you've found something real.

The other camp — the deflationists — say the microscope analogy is doing all the work. Decompositions of a model's activations into "features" are radically underdetermined: dictionary-learning methods will happily produce many incompatible but equally sparse, equally causally potent decompositions of the same model, so which one is "the model's own"? What looks like a dog-detecting feature may just be tracking whatever co-varied with dogs in the training distribution, and the interpreter's choice of vocabulary — the fact that a direction is *labeled* with a human concept — is doing the semantic lifting, not the network. On this view, talk of "the model's concept of dogs" is projection: elegant, useful, and about us.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two camps are using different criteria for what makes something a representation. The realists quietly rely on a causal counterfactual criterion — it's a feature if intervening on it changes behavior in the right way. The deflationists rely on a semantic criterion — it's a representation of dogs only if it corresponds to the concept *dog* at the right grain, and networks have no obligation to carve the world along our seams. Since a causal structure can exist without matching our concepts, and a concept-match can be illusory without any matching causal structure, the two camps may be answering different questions under one word. But a second hypothesis competes with it in my notes: that this is simply the old debate about scientific realism about unobservables replayed at small scale, with features standing in for electrons, and the deflationists playing the instrumentalist who is happy to use the posits without believing in them.

Please structure your response in four sections, in this order:

1. **The two camps, stated fairly** (~200 words): each position in its strongest form, without caricature.
2. **Diagnosis** (~450 words): what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, substantive, two different questions under one heading, or a hidden methodological dispute about what counts as a discovery — supported by what each side actually claims, and an assessment of my two hypotheses.
3. **What would count as settling it** (~250 words): given your diagnosis, what specific development — an experimental result, a formal result about decomposition uniqueness, a conceptual distinction, a change of vocabulary — would move the debate, or why nothing should be expected to.
4. **Where my framing goes wrong** (~150 words): anything I've misunderstood or begged, stated plainly.
