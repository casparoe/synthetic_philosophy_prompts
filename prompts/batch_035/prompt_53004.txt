Analyze Hofstadter's two superrationality experiments -- the Platonia dilemma and the actual Luring Lottery he ran in Scientific American in 1983 -- as a real-world test of superrationality as a decision theory, and tell me what the outcome actually shows about the theory. Quick facts so we're on the same page: the Platonia dilemma was his thought experiment where a trillionaire tells 20 people that exactly one telegram (reverse charges, by noon the next day) wins a billion dollars, zero or multiple telegrams means nobody gets anything; the superrational move is to send one with probability 1/20. The Luring Lottery was the real thing: readers could send a postcard with any positive integer on it, the prize was a million dollars divided by total entries, winner drawn at random; the superrational advice was roughly to send "1" (or use a mixed strategy based on expected number of responders), but in practice people submitted numbers like a googolplex or expressions designed to be as huge as possible, so the prize became a microscopic fraction of a cent and the magazine couldn't even tell who won. Map it properly: who the players were, what the actual payoff structure was, what superrationality prescribed and why, and how the prescription depends on the assumption that everyone reasons the same way. Then run it in both directions: does the fiasco refute superrationality, or was the experiment stacked against it from the start (readers knew they were playing against a mostly-ordinary population, not a group of perfect logicians -- does superrationality even say anything about games with mixed populations, or is it silent there)? And is the runaway-entry outcome just standard game-theoretic rationality playing out, or something weirder -- people submitting googolplexes get essentially nothing either way, so was the actual behavior even Nash-rational? Finish by saying what, if anything, the episode teaches: a confirmation that superrationality is a normative curiosity with no descriptive grip, evidence the theory was misapplied as a test, or a case the theory never anticipated.
