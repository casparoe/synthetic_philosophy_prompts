Our philosophy of mind reading group has spent the last month on the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and we have hit a wall that feels structural rather than temporary. The setup: the epiphenomenalist in the group holds that phenomenal properties are non-physical and causally inert, generated by but not acting back on physical processes. The rest of us press the obvious objection: if qualia do no causal work, then my sincere utterance "this pain is throbbing" is produced entirely by physical states of my brain, and the phenomenal character itself plays no role in bringing that report about. So the match between what I say and what I feel is not something my saying tracks; a zombie duplicate of me, running on pure physics, utters exactly the same words with no pain there at all. His stock replies are that psychophysical laws could guarantee that judgments and experiences systematically co-occur — a kind of pre-established harmony — and that demanding causal tracking between experience and report just assumes a causal theory of justification that he rejects. When someone in the group compared the harmony reply to reading the time off a watch that is perfectly synchronized with another watch (you learn what the second watch says without it doing anything to you), he seized on it happily, and the rest of us thought it changed nothing, and around we go.

Here is why I think we are stuck, offered as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion. A friend outside the group remarked that the whole dispute looks structurally identical to two debates that also never resolve: Benacerraf-style access worries about how our causally isolated beliefs could track abstract mathematical objects, and evolutionary debunking worries about whether moral realists can explain reliable moral judgment when the causal genealogy of those judgments never touches moral facts. In both of those, there is a standard "third-factor" style response — some further fact guaranteed to co-vary with both the facts and the beliefs — and there is a standing complaint that such responses are just restating the coincidence at a higher level. If the parallels are real, then our stalemate is not really about consciousness at all: the two sides are disagreeing about what it takes for a belief to be justified or for a correlation to be explanatory, and they would split exactly the same way on mathematics and on ethics. But maybe the parallel is fake, because in the phenomenal case the co-occurring judgment is literally about the causally idle item itself, not about a distinct object it merely tracks.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on epiphenomenalism. Is the dispute substantive, or is it partly verbal — say, equivocation on whether a report needs to be caused by its object in order to be about it, or on whether "knowledge" requires a causal or merely a lawlike connection? Are the two sides perhaps answering different questions, one about the etiology of our judgments and one about their warrant? Test my structural-parallel hypothesis along the way: does the watch analogy, or the third-factor strategy borrowed from the mathematical and moral cases, actually transfer to the phenomenal case, or is there a disanalogy that makes this dispute genuinely its own? And tell me what would count as progress here — a sharper distinction between causal and non-causal theories of warrant, a worked-out epistemology of acquaintance, some testable prediction, or nothing short of abandoning one of the starting positions — because at the moment we are just re-deriving our own convictions once a meeting.
