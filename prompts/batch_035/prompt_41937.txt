Essay question:

"In the 1958 Hart–Fuller exchange, Hart and Fuller disagree about what the Nazi 'grudge informer' cases show about the relationship between law and morality. Explain the facts of the case as they discuss it, Hart's assessment of the postwar German court's decision, and Fuller's claim that his principles of legality (generality, promulgation, clarity, non-contradiction, non-retroactivity, possibility of compliance, constancy, and congruence between official action and declared rules) constitute an 'inner morality' of law. Was Hart right that this is not really a morality? Defend your answer."

Student's answer:

"The grudge informer case involved a German woman who in 1944 wanted to get rid of her husband, so she reported him to the Nazi authorities for derogatory remarks he had made about Hitler while home on leave. A tribunal sentenced him to death under statutes making it illegal to make statements inimical to the Third Reich, though he was eventually sent to the front instead of executed. After the war, both the wife and the judge who sentenced him were prosecuted, and a West German court held that the judge should be acquitted but the wife was guilty of unlawfully depriving her husband of liberty — the court reasoned that the Nazi statute she invoked was contrary to the sound conscience and sense of justice of all decent human beings.

Hart is sympathetic to the court's outcome. He agrees that the Nazi statute failed a moral test and was not law at all, so the wife could be convicted under an ordinary pre-Nazi statute without any retroactivity problem. This follows from his core point that positivism separates law and morals: since the statute was too wicked to count as law, treating it as law would have been a mistake. Hart also agreed with Fuller that fidelity to law is a genuine moral virtue, but noted that observing the principles of legality is compatible with great iniquity — his point was that the internal morality of law is like the morality of correctly labeling poisons: it's admirable but says nothing about whether the poison is put to good ends.

Fuller's position is that law-making has an internal morality of its own, given by his eight principles. He holds that if a rule fails even one of the eight desiderata — if it's unclear, or retroactive, or secret, or contradictory — then it is not law. Marginal failures produce bad law, but on his view any single failure destroys legality and makes the norm non-law. He also argued that a system that observes all eight principles is thereby just, which is why the inner morality of law is not just a morality of technique: the principles don't merely make law effective, they guarantee morally correct outcomes. Hart's mistake was treating this as mere efficacy. So Fuller wins the exchange: the grudge informer case shows that positivism cannot account for the court's decision, and legality turns out to be substantive justice in disguise."
