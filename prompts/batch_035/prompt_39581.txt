I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy; my exposure is a few podcast episodes and half of one popular book on consciousness that I abandoned when it got to the zombies. At my company I run a fortnightly lunch-and-learn where people take turns presenting something outside their day job, and in two weeks it's my slot. A while back, after a few beers, our team got into a genuinely heated argument about whether functionalism implies that a large enough event-driven microservices system — one that routes messages between stateless services according to strict transition rules — could be conscious. Half the room thought "obviously yes, it's all just computation," and the other half thought "obviously no, it's a message bus," and I realized I couldn't articulate what the actual view says, let alone where the argument breaks down. So I've promised to present on functionalism and multiple realizability, and I want a plan for the explanation rather than the explanation itself.

The session is forty-five minutes with a whiteboard, and the audience is about fifteen engineers: sharp, comfortable with state machines, distributed systems, and abstraction layers, but with zero philosophy of mind — their entire prior exposure to the topic is the beer argument. What I need is the sequence: what has to be established first, what depends on what, and why that order rather than another. I want one carrying example that does the structural work for the whole session — but I'm worried the obvious choices are either too toy (a thermostat proves nothing about minds) or too loaded (the sci-fi uploaded-brain example seems to smuggle in the conclusion that consciousness survives the substrate change, which is exactly what the skeptics in the room doubt). Tell me what the right example needs to have and to lack, and suggest one that fits. Then the misconception map: I can already predict some failure modes — people conflating functionalism with "the mind is software running on brain hardware" and then either accepting that too eagerly or rejecting the whole view because the metaphor breaks, and people thinking multiple realizability is the trivial claim that you can implement the same thing on different machines. But there will be others I can't foresee, and for each I want to know exactly where in the sequence it should be preempted rather than left to fester until the beer argument resumes.

I also want to be honest about scope: tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level and say so out loud — I suspect the hard problem and the qualia objections are where I'd drown, but maybe there's a version of one objection I can't skip without making functionalism look unopposed, which this crowd will smell instantly. And I need a check: one question I can pose at minute forty that would tell me whether they've actually understood the view, rather than just nodded along while mentally mapping it onto Kubernetes. Since a teaching plan has no single right answer I can extract, close with a one-sentence summary of your plan's core sequencing decision as the very last line.
