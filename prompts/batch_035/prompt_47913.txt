I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I've lately been reporting on the long afterlife of expected utility theory — specifically on how the field metabolized the Allais paradox. The descriptive finding is settled enough: people reliably prefer a sure large gain over a gamble that stochastically dominates it in one choice pair, and then reverse that preference when the same gamble is embedded in a different pair, which is precisely what the independence axiom forbids. The interesting question, the one economists argue about at dinner, is normative: is an agent who exhibits this pattern making an error, or expressing a value the axiom was never entitled to rule out? I've come to believe the question isn't uniform across agents, and that's the wrinkle I want your help thinking through.

Here is the case that got me wondering. An aid official is allocating a relief budget for a single drought-stricken district, and the outcomes are other people's lives. In the first pair she must choose, (A) a program that will certainly save 1,000 lives, or (B) a gamble that saves 1,000 lives with probability 0.89, 5,000 with probability 0.10, and nobody with probability 0.01 — a logistics failure. In the second pair, she chooses between (C) a program that saves 1,000 lives with probability 0.11 and nobody otherwise, or (D) one that saves 5,000 with probability 0.10 and nobody otherwise. Many people, choosing for others, take A over B and D over C — the classic Allais pattern. Now, the usual defenses of that pattern lean on things that seem inadmissible in a fiduciary context: the chooser's own dread at having "caused" the disaster, the special solace of certainty, regret about the choice itself. When the stakes are someone else's lives and the chooser's feelings are not part of what's being optimized, I can't tell whether the pattern becomes a clear error or whether something structural survives — some legitimate way in which the total prospect, not the way prospects nest inside one another, is all that matters.

So give me a number with the machinery visible. Specifically, your credence that the independence axiom is a genuine normative requirement for an ideal agent in this fiduciary role — such that an allocator exhibiting the A-over-B, D-over-C pattern is making a mistake, not merely expressing a permissible value — decomposed into terms like: the probability that rational preference over prospects must be consequentialist about final outcomes alone, with no weight on how one prospect is nested inside another; the probability that there is a defensible self/other asymmetry, so that regret and the value of certainty might be legitimate for personal choice while being inadmissible when allocating others' welfare; the probability that the Allais pattern, however explained, can be rationalized without abandoning independence at all; and any further terms you think belong. Give each a rough probability with a line of justification, show how they combine into your final number, and tell me which term the final credence is most sensitive to. For calibration: the economists I interview tend to put this around 0.95, a behavioral scientist I trust puts it around 0.35, and I hover near 0.6 — tell me where you think the disagreement between the 0.95 and the 0.35 camps most likely lives. Close with a final line in the form \answer{p}, where p is your combined credence to two decimal places, and make that the very last thing in your reply.
