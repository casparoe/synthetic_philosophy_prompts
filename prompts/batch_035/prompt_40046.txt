I need a poem for a small, private occasion, and there's something specific about how it should be built, which I'll try to explain as clearly as I can, since I have no training in this and don't know the technical words for any of it.

At my thirtieth college reunion last month, I spent most of the evening talking to a woman I took to be a stranger. We were seated together, we traded the standard updates, and I liked her enormously — sharp, funny, easy to be with. Near the end of the night, someone across the table used her name, and I realized she was the girl who had lived three doors down from me in the dorms freshman year, someone I hadn't thought about in decades. Nothing about the conversation changed. Every sentence she'd said was still true, still meant what it meant. But the whole evening turned inside out in a second, and I've been chewing on why ever since.

Here's what I keep noticing. If someone at that table had said to me, "the woman you're talking to is the woman you're talking to," I'd have wondered whether they were feeling all right. Yet "the woman you're talking to is the girl from down the hall" landed on me like news — even though, if it's true, it was true all evening, before I knew it, and the sentence tells me nothing that wasn't already the case. It tells me nothing and everything at once. The facts didn't change; something else did, and I can't put my finger on what.

I want a poem about that evening, and I want the poem's construction to carry this rather than state it. Specifically:

- Sixteen to twenty lines, no fixed form, in two movements: everything before the recognition, then the recognition itself and what it did to the hour I had just lived.
- The recognition should arrive in the plainest possible words — one flat sentence of the form "she is the girl from down the hall" — with no metaphor anywhere near it. I've noticed that the flatter the sentence, the harder it hits, and I'd like the poem to bet on that.
- The opening line and the closing line must be identical, word for word. The first time, it should read as an empty truism, something a reader skims past. The last time, the very same words should read as almost too much to say. Nothing between the two should change except what I learned.
- Nowhere should the point be explained or named — no "little did I know," no commentary, no summing up. Someone who has never thought about this should simply feel it. Someone who has thought about it should recognize exactly what is being done to them and why it works, without the poem ever saying so.

Two cautions. First, I'd rather the poem not get misty about reunions in general — no rocking chairs, no "the years," no soft-focus nostalgia. The event was startling, not sweet, and the poem should keep that temperature. Second, I've considered sending it to her, since we exchanged addresses and I hope we stay in touch. So it should be a poem she can read cold, without any of what was going through my head, and still have it land.

If you can, tell me afterwards which line you chose to repeat, and why the second occurrence of it doesn't just restate the first. If you can't say that clearly, I suspect the poem hasn't done its job, and I'd rather know.
