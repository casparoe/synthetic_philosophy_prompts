I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've become curious about a dispute I keep bumping into in what philosophers call metaontology: whether questions like "Do numbers exist?" or "Is there a single object composed of my chair and my laptop, over and above the chair and the laptop themselves?" are genuine questions about the world, or artifacts of how we talk. As I understand the lay of the land, Carnap held that such questions are internal to a linguistic framework — within the framework of arithmetic, "numbers exist" is trivially settled, and asking it outside any framework is a pseudo-question. Quine rejected any principled line between framework-relative and substantive questions, treating existence claims as answerable by the same ordinary methods as any scientific claim. The disagreement is alive today: Eli Hirsch argues that many classic existence disputes are merely verbal, invoking his doctrine of "quantifier variance" — the idea that the quantifier "there are" means slightly different things in different linguistic communities, so the disputants talk past each other — while Theodore Sider replies that some ways of using existence vocabulary carve nature at its joints and are objectively better, so the disputes are substantive after all.

Restate this debate in the vocabulary I actually use: schemas and type systems, namespaces, module boundaries and what a module exports, overloading and polymorphism, refactoring legacy code, and the difference between a design decision and a fact about the runtime. On this rendering, Carnap's internal questions would become questions answerable inside a given schema; external questions would look like choices about which schema to adopt; Hirsch's quantifier variance would look like overloading of an operator across dialects, so the disputants get different referents from the same surface syntax; Sider's rejoinder would be a claim that some schemas are not merely conventions — that the runtime has a structure one schema tracks better than another. Translate as faithfully as you can, resisting the temptation to smuggle in my vocabulary's assumptions and call them translation.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want. Which parts of the philosophers' debate map cleanly onto mine? Which parts had to be distorted to fit? And is there anything in their dispute with no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — and if so, does that point to something my vocabulary is missing, or to something less substantive in the original than it looked? My engineering instinct is that once you see it in terms of schema choice, Sider has to be wrong: a schema can be more convenient, but not more correct. Please tell me honestly whether that instinct survives your translation.
