Hilary Mantel's Wolf Hall keeps Thomas Cromwell at the center of every sentence — famous to the point of parody for its bare "he," so that readers sometimes lose track of whether "he" is Cromwell or the rival hovering in the previous clause — and it does all of this in the present tense, from inside Cromwell's perception, with no authorial narrator stepping back to judge him or survey the field. I want to read that technique as a move in the historiography debate between Ranke's ambition to show the past "as it actually was" and the scepticism of Carr and Hayden White: Carr's line that you must study the historian before you study the history, and White's claim that historical writing inevitably emplots the past as tragedy, comedy, romance, or satire.

My tentative thesis, which I'd like you to test rather than flatter, is that Mantel's device is a two-front intervention: against Ranke, it concedes that there is no view from nowhere and plants the camera squarely in one skull; but against Carr and White, it refuses to let the author's presence show, dissolving the historian into the historical agent instead of vice versa. So is it a refutation of White by example — history written from so close that no emplotment gets a grip — or is that just emplotment concealed, with Cromwell quietly written as the tragic self-made man whose fall is being saved up? And is a novel that never claims to be history entitled to answer these questions at all, or is historical fiction precisely where Ranke's program survives by changing clothes?

Please take both the novels and the Ranke–Carr–White debate seriously. Ground the reading in concrete choices: the opening chapters with the young Thomas and his father; how the More–Cromwell contrast is handled without letting either be the villain; the decision to end Wolf Hall and Bring Up the Bodies where they do, with Anne Boleyn's fall but not Cromwell's; and the fact that Mantel's paratextual notes coax readers toward exactly the kind of close, source-suspicious attention historians practice. Say plainly where my thesis fails — where the novels smuggle in a God's-eye view anyway, or where the analogy with White's modes is a stretch — and finish with what the novels add to the objectivity debate that the philosophical arguments, working on their own, cannot give us.

Open with your verdict in the first sentence — does Wolf Hall ultimately side with Ranke or with his critics, or dissolve the terms — and give the full argument after it, with no preamble and no restatement of this brief.
