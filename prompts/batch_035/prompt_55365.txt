I teach a seminar on chance and deference, and every year I bring in the morning forecast as my Exhibit A — but the more I look at what meteorological agencies actually do, the less sure I am what theory of chance, if any, the whole practice is committed to. Perhaps your department can sort this out for me.

Consider the practice concretely. A national weather service issues a probability of precipitation for a given location and time window — a number like 0.3 — generated by running an ensemble of simulation runs from slightly perturbed estimates of current conditions and reporting the fraction of runs in which rain occurs. A separate verification office then grades the service on calibration: across all the occasions on which 0.3 was announced, rain should have fallen about three times in ten, and the service is praised or dinged accordingly. The public, meanwhile, uses the numbers almost unmediatedly: a commuter takes an umbrella when the number crosses one half; a farmer delays spraying; a festival organizer rents extra tents. Sophisticated users know the service recalibrates its past record, and some quietly shade the announced figures before acting on them.

The natural gloss on all this is deference to chance in the style of the Principal Principle: if you know the objective chance of rain is p, rationality requires that your credence in rain be p, and the public seems to be executing precisely that rule — announced number to degree of belief to decision. But two features of the practice sit badly with that gloss, and I want to know whether the tension is real or merely apparent. First, the announced number comes out of a deterministic physics: each ensemble run, given its initial conditions, either has rain or does not, and the spread reflects our ignorance of the initial conditions rather than any indeterminism in the weather itself. In what sense, then, is 0.3 the chance of anything? Is it a chance in the world, a measure of our ignorance, or a best-system summary that is neither purely objective nor purely subjective? Second, verification is a long-run frequency exercise, yet the public is being told to act on the number for a single, unrepeatable occasion. Does the practice quietly assume that single-case chances exist, or only that credences calibrated over the long run are what decision-making needs — and if the latter, is the deference involved really deference to chance at all, rather than deference to a track record?

Please reconstruct the theory of chance, and the deference principle, that this practice strictly presupposes; separate that from what it merely sits comfortably with; tell me whether the deterministic-generation story genuinely undermines the deference reading or whether several rival metaphysics of chance would equally license the practice as it stands; and say what in the practice — the announced numbers, the calibration standard, the recalibration habit — would have to change if we insisted that chance be understood on one of those rival views rather than another.
