I have settled on the higher-order theory of consciousness — in its monitoring versions, the view that a first-order mental state is conscious only in virtue of there being some higher-order state, or higher-order aspect of that state, that makes it available to the subject as a state of theirs. I find the inference from the transitivity of consciousness (if I am conscious of the table, I am conscious of something; so being conscious of red must involve being conscious of something) close to compelling. Set out for me what would genuinely count against this family of views, ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward.

Cover at least the following, and anything else you judge material: the misrepresentation problem (if the higher-order state mischaracterises the first-order one, what is it like for the subject — and can the answer avoid collapsing the view into first-order or adverbial alternatives?); the problem of creatures, such as infants and many non-human animals, whose possession of higher-order states is doubtful but whose responsiveness to noxious stimuli seems richly organised; and the epistemic embarrassment that the theory apparently renders consciousness constitutively tied to cognitive sophistication rather than sensory differentiation. Then assess a hypothetical case: suppose a subject's first-order states were functionally integrated exactly as ours are — guiding discrimination, learning, report, and affective regulation — while a causally isolated module computed the higher-order states, such that switching it off changed nothing in behaviour or information processing. It seems the theory must say phenomenal consciousness flickers with the switch. Should that be treated as a bold prediction, a mere bullet to bite, or a sign that the view has substituted stipulation for explanation?

Finally, tell me whether the successive retreats within the family — from actual higher-order thought to dispositional, perceptual, and self-representational variants, in which the higher-order element is made intrinsic to the first-order state itself — have bought immunity to these objections at the cost of insulating the view against any possible finding, and whether a constitutive rather than causal reading of the higher-order relation leaves the theory with any distinctive explanatory work left to do. Distinguish what committed defenders of the view would themselves concede is a defeater from what they would dismiss as a mere confusion of consciousness with its functional accompaniments. Please write in British English.
