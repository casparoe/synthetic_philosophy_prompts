I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on moral uncertainty, and in our department's animal-ethics reading group a conservation-biology PhD student presented an argument that has been bothering me for weeks, because it seems to license far more than she intended and neither of us can locate the joint at which it bends. Her argument runs as follows. (1) On any broadly aggregative view, the rational way to allocate our moral effort across domains is by the expected total welfare at stake in each. (2) Where we are uncertain whether the members of some class of beings are sentient, the expected welfare at stake in that class is the number of beings, multiplied by our credence that they are sentient, multiplied by the welfare at stake per sentient being. (3) The evidence on insect sentience is thin but not empty — decentralized nociception, trade-offs against competing motivations, self-medication-like behavior in some species — so a defensible credence falls somewhere between one in ten thousand and one in ten. (4) Even at the bottom of that range, the roughly 10^18 insects alive at any moment yield an expected number of sentient beings that dwarfs all vertebrates put together, so insect welfare should dominate the allocation of animal-welfare resources. (5) But the reasoning is perfectly general, and she concedes this: bacteria outnumber insects by more than a trillion to one, so a credence of even one in a trillion that bacterial valenced experience exists — not demonstrably zero, she insists — puts the expected stakes in bacteria ahead of those in insects by the same logic that put insects ahead of chickens. (6) Iterate the point across fungi, protists, and any merely possible sentient systems, and the expected totals grow without bound as populations grow and our credences, while never exactly zero, track nothing we can act on. (7) So the same premises that told her to take insect welfare seriously tell us to direct essentially all moral effort toward beings whose inner lives, even if real, we have no idea how to influence — a conclusion she will not sign and I cannot either.

She suspects the fault lies in (2), something about the multiplication silently ignoring the difference between welfare at stake and welfare one can actually affect; I keep feeling that the deeper problem is in the slide connecting (3) to (5) and (6), where a credence that is "strictly between zero and one" gets treated as a stable quantity that can be set arbitrarily close to zero while the population term balloons to compensate — but I can't make that complaint precise without it sounding like I just dislike the conclusion. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I would be genuinely sad to abandon (1), since I'm temperamentally drawn to aggregative views, and a diagnosis that amounts to capping our credences wherever the arithmetic gets uncomfortable looks like motivated reasoning rather than philosophy — but I'd rather pay that price than sign (7).
