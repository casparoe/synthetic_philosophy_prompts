I'm taking an aesthetics seminar, and we've just finished Hume's "Of the Standard of Taste," which I thought was going to be a historic curiosity and instead has turned out to be the paper the whole rest of the course orbits around. As I understand it, Hume's move is to split the difference in the old debate: beauty is not a property of objects the way extension is, but neither is it mere sentiment with no standard, because over time the verdicts of qualified critics — those with delicate sensibility, refined by practice and comparison and cleared of prejudice — converge, and that convergence is the standard. My professor used the Milton-versus-Ogilby comparison, and it seems obviously right: nobody with any exposure to poetry ranks them as equals, any more than anyone disputes that two is less than three, and Hume takes cases like that to show that the disagreement between taste and geometry is one of degree, not kind.

Here's where I'm stuck. My seminar-mates keep insisting that Hume faces a devastating circularity problem — something about how we identify the qualified judges by their liking the works that the qualified judges like — and therefore that the paper fails and the objectivity question is as open after Hume as before him. I half-see the point when it's spelled out like that, but to me it looks like a technicality. We identify experts in every domain by their track records, and nobody concludes that wine-tasting or diagnostic medicine therefore has no standards. Since Hume showed that critical convergence is as much objectivity as beauty could coherently have, and since that convergence is empirically verifiable, hasn't he simply answered the question, with a modestly deflated notion of objectivity that we should just accept? What I'd like to know is whether the question is settled in something like the way I think, and if not, what exactly remains genuinely open. Please answer in at most 300 words.
