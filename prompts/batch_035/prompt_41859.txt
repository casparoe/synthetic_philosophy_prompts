I'm preparing a position statement for a graduate seminar in philosophy of language; this week's topic is Moore's paradox. Before I submit it, I'd like an audit of whether my commitments actually hang together, because I have a suspicion that at least two of them are quietly at war, though I can't for the life of me see which. Here is the set, roughly as it will appear in the paper:

(1) Moorean sentences are logically consistent. There are possible situations in which it is raining in Bristol while I fail to believe that it is; a sentence can be true at a world while remaining, in some way, absurd.

(2) The absurdity is a pragmatic phenomenon, grounded in the norms of assertion. Someone who asserts that p thereby presents themselves as believing that p, so a speaker who asserts "It's raining in Bristol, but I don't believe that it is" represents themselves, in one breath, as both believing and not believing the same thing.

(3) The absurdity survives even when nothing is asserted. If I merely entertain the thought that it is raining though I don't believe it — sitting alone, asserting nothing to anyone — the thought still strikes me as absurd, indeed hardly intelligible as something to think.

(4) Sincere assertion is the expression of belief: to assert that p sincerely is, in part, to manifest belief that p.

(5) Present-tense, first-person avowals of belief enjoy a special authority. When I say "I believe that it's raining", I am not reporting on an inner state on the basis of evidence; rather, the avowal functions as an indirect assertion that it's raining, which is why I can hardly be mistaken, in the present tense, about whether I believe something.

(6) Belief is ultimately dispositional. What it is to believe that p is to be disposed, in the appropriate circumstances, to act, infer and feel as though p — and this entails that self-deception must be possible: a subject can be wrong about their own current beliefs, sincerely avowing a belief they lack or disavowing one they have.

What I'd like to know is which of these are genuinely in tension, which merely look to be, and what the cheapest route back to consistency would be. I have no attachment to any particular one of them giving way, and I'd rather not discover afterwards that I had quietly smuggled in a seventh commitment to paper over a crack. I'll admit that (5) and (6) each feel right to me when I consider Moorean cases on the one hand and self-deception cases on the other, so if they do clash, I want to know how badly, not merely that they do.

Please take on the role of a careful philosopher of language with expertise in assertion, Moore's paradox and the philosophy of mind, and think the whole set through before writing rather than flagging every pair that could conceivably interact.
