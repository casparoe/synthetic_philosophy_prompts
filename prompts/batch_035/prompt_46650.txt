Been reading about how EA grantmakers decide where money goes — cost-per-life-saved math for the global health stuff, and for the weirder grants they'll justify reducing extinction risk by writing down some gigantic-but-finite number of future people, multiplying by the percent reduction in risk, and comparing that expected number of lives against other uses of the same money. Sometimes they'll actually attach a number, like 10^38 potential descendants or whatever, but it's always finite, always something you can put in a spreadsheet.

Here's what bugs me. A lot of the same people say the universe might literally be infinite in extent, and that the future could go on forever. And if theres infinitely much value out there, the arithmetic seems to collapse — infinity plus a billion saved lives is still infinity, so every option with any positive effect ties at the same infinite value, and every charity looks equally good. It's not like they believe the universe is definately finite; if anything they say we don't know. So they're doing finite-number expected value math on top of a view that they themselves admit might be true.

So what does the practice actually presuppose? That you should truncate and only count value you can causally reach, like whatever's inside the little bubble of stuff we could ever affect? That infinities are a technicality to set aside when the grant decision is due? Or maybe that expected value only makes sense over finite chunks and they're quietly doing something else entirely? Does the spreadsheet need any particular answer here, or would several different views about infinite value license the same ranking anyway? And if they took the infinite case seriously, what would have to change in how these justifications get written — could they keep using expected value at all, or would they need a totally different decision rule, and what would that even look like?
