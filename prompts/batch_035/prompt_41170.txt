A popular book on ancient philosophy I picked up gives this account of the ring of Gyges episode in Plato's Republic: 'In Book II, the sophist Thrasymachus tells the story of a shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible. With its power, he seduces the queen, murders the king, and seizes the throne. The moral Glaucon draws is that no one is willingly just: people behave justly only because they fear punishment, and anyone immune to detection would act as the shepherd did. So Socrates must spend the rest of the Republic proving that the just life is more rewarding than the unjust one, which he does by showing that just people end up happier, healthier, and better rewarded by their communities.' The story details ring true to me, but something about the frame feels compressed, and I dimly recall that the dialogue's climax involves the just man being tortured with his reputation in ruins, which sits oddly with that last sentence. How much of this summary is fair? Please go through it claim by claim, telling me what a scholar of Plato would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong — and say whether the book's picture of what Socrates is trying to prove survives your corrections.
