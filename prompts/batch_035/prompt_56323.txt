My roommate is deep into Hofstadter and has an argument he swears shows that defecting in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma is straight up irrational, not just "technically dominant." It runs like this. Payoffs: if we both cooperate we each get $300; both defect, $100 each; if one cooperates and the other defects, the defector gets $500 and the sucker gets nothing. One shot, no communication, and both of us are rational.

(1) My co-player and I each decide by reasoning it out, and we go about it in the same way — same information, same payoff structure as described to each of us, both rational.
(2) So whatever considerations would move me to a conclusion move him too. Same reasoning on the same inputs, same output.
(3) So there are only two outcomes on the table: both cooperate or both defect. The mixed ones are off the table.
(4) Both-cooperate pays me $300, both-defect pays me $100.
(5) So cooperating is the rational move and defecting is the irrational one, even though defecting dominates.

I'll admit when its just the two of us hypothetically in a room, this feels pretty convincing. What worries me are the extensions he insists follow. He says the timing is irrelevant — the argument never used the fact that we deliberate at the same moment. So take the version where his choice is already sealed in an envelope: he wrote it down last week, after doing (he tells me) exactly the reasoning I'm doing now, and I've been truthfully told the envelope says defect. My roommate says I should still cooperate. My reasoning matches his, so if I land on cooperate, he landed on cooperate, and if I land on defect, he landed on defect — the envelope cant really say defect, or if it does, that just shows he didn't reason like me after all, in which case step (2) was a good but falsifiable expectation and I got unlucky. Either way cooperate was the right call, he says. That one makes my teeth itch. It looks like I'm being told to hand the bank $100 on the way out the door, and I don't see how an argument that concludes that is an argument I should trust anywhere.

And he has a second extension. Since rationality was never defined as caring about my payoffs in particular, he says the same template also covers the case where the co-player's payoffs are flipped — he gets the $500 exactly when I get the $0, so he rationally wants the mixed outcome where he defects and I cooperate, and he's fully rational in pursuing it. My roommate says the argument still goes through: we reason alike, so we land alike, so I should cooperate for nothing. That one has to be wrong — no argument should get me to cooperate for $0 against someone reasoning his way to defecting on me — but I can't point at the step that breaks it.

So: which premise do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? For what it's worth, step (2) is the one that looks airtight to me in the original setup, where we're described as literally running the same reasoning, and I'd rather not have to deny that. Also, if the whole thing turns on a slide somewhere between "his choice will correlate with mine" and "my choice settles his," I want that named plainly, because my roommate thinks that slide is the whole point and not a bug.
