A postdoc in my lab and another grad student got into it after our reading group on Molyneux's problem, and I was the only one who stayed to the end, so refereeing fell to me and I couldnt do it. Context in case you need it: Molyneux's question is whether someone blind from birth, who knows a cube and a sphere by touch, could on first gaining sight tell which is which by vision alone, before any experience has a chance to connect the two modalities. Locke put it in the Essay and everyone from Diderot on had an opinion, with the empiricist answer being no. What restarted the fight was the modern stuff: there are studies from the last 15-odd years where surgeons restored sight to adult patients blind from birth, congenital cataracts I think, in India, and right after surgery the patients reportedly couldn't match an object they had just held to one they were now looking at. Jake (the postdoc) said this settles it: the answer is no, the question has an empirical answer and we now have it, and the rest is philosophers not liking the result. Maya (the grad student) went at him in three rounds.

Round 1: Maya said those patients aren't Molyneux's man. In the first days they don't just lack visual experience, they seem to see badly at a pretty basic level — eyes darting around, trouble telling motion from stillness, some can barely fixate. If your visual system is that scrambled at the moment of testing, failing the matching task says nothing about whether touch-derived shape concepts could be taken up by vision, because maybe not enough is getting in to tell. Jake said that's question-begging: any actual candidate for Molyneux's man is someone who has never seen, so every real one will have some version of these defects, and if you discount every actual subject on those grounds, you're admitting the question can never be tested, which is the opposite of what she was supposed to be arguing.

Round 2: Maya said even granting the patients see fine, two questions are being run together. One is philosophical: whether the information touch delivers about shape is the same kind of information vision delivers, i.e. whether the concepts are modality-specific. The other is psychological: whether a person naive to vision can deploy a concept through an unfamiliar channel on day one. The studies answer the second, and the answer — no, not immediately — is no shock, since sighted people take ages to learn to use any new sensory channel (cochlear implant users go through exactly this). So failing at hour one doesn't settle whether the concepts are shared. Jake said this move makes the philosophical question untestable by construction, and a question about whether shape information is "the same" across modalities that refuses to be operationalized is too vague to overrule an actual finding — a question you won't pin down doesn't get a veto over one you can.

Round 3: Maya said there's a test that doesn't need newly sighted people at all: devices that convert camera images into tactile grids or soundscapes for blind users. If the modalities share concepts, cross-modal translation should be learnable at a reasonable rate, and reportedly it is — some long-term users get eerily good at recognizing objects from a distance, so the concepts do transfer eventually. Jake said those users trained for months, so they show fast learning, not immediate identification, and immediate is exactly what Molyneux asked about, so her own favorite evidence answers a question she just spent round 2 insisting was different.

They finished with Jake saying the honest verdict is "no, and the lingering yes's were mostly motivated by wanting the mind to come with innate structure," and Maya saying the honest verdict is "it was two questions all along and the data answers the boring one."
