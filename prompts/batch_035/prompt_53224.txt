I teach a section of an intro sociology course with a philosophy-of-social-science unit attached, and I'm tired of the generational debate in seminars amounting to students trading stereotypes about Gen Z and then discovering, if we're lucky, the age–period–cohort identification problem an hour too late. I want a diagnostic instrument that sorts them by what they actually believe about whether 'generations' are real kinds, before they know any of the positions or their names.

Please write me a sorting quiz of eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. Build it in the following order: first lay out the logical space your questions will partition — the load-bearing questions on which the candidate positions genuinely come apart — and then locate the positions as combinations of answers, rather than working down a list of famous names. The distinctions I want the space built from are at least these: (1) whether birth-year cohorts share causally efficacious properties beyond age and period effects, or whether apparent cohort traits are age and period effects in disguise; (2) whether the boundaries of a generation are discovered or drawn — whether there is a fact of the matter about where one cohort ends and the next begins, or whether any cutoff is stipulation; (3) whether the label 'millennial' or 'Gen Z' does something to the people it applies to, creating or entrenching the very traits it purports to describe; (4) whether a kind's being real requires that it be identifiable in principle by measurement, or whether it can be real yet statistically inaccessible.

Cases the questions should draw on, or cases like them: two people born in 1996 and 1997, raised identically, whom a standard cutoff places in different generations; the observation that smartphone adoption changed the daily lives of forty-year-olds as well as teenagers, so that rising teen anxiety attributed to a cohort might be a period effect interacting with age; the fact that 'generations' are often named and dated by demographers and marketers before anyone has measured a cohort effect, and that people then self-identify with the label; a purported microgeneration of a handful of birth years between two accepted generations; and the point that every living cohort is always simultaneously aging, so that what looks like 'the young are always like this' versus 'this generation is different' cannot be settled from a single time slice. Every question must be answerable by someone who has never heard of the positions; no question may simply ask whether generations are 'real' or 'socially constructed'.

The scoring key should map onto at least these leaves: a robust realist who takes cohorts to be historical or lineage-like kinds with real causal powers; a thin realist who holds that cohort effects exist but only as statistical residue identified under strong assumptions; a constructivist who holds that generations are real but maintained by the practice of labeling them; and an eliminativist who regards generational talk as age stereotyping dressed up. Include the unoccupied or unstable answer combinations your space allows, and say for each whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive in a way the quiz should expose, or merely because nobody has bothered to defend it. Finally, flag explicitly any pair of positions your questions cannot separate — for instance, whether the thin realist and the constructivist who grants statistical cohort effects are distinguishable at all by cases rather than by vocabulary — rather than letting the scoring key paper over the gap.
