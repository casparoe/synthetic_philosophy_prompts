I spent thirty-five years as an epidemiologist before retiring, and in the years since I have been working my way through political philosophy as an enthusiastic amateur. I have become stuck on the position I gather is called luck egalitarianism — the thought that inequalities are unjust when they trace to brute luck but not when they trace to the agent's own choices. As a scientist I find myself pulled in two directions: professionally I thought in terms of exposures and probabilities, and would have judged two people with the same exposure as identically situated, whatever happened to either of them; but as a citizen my moral reactions track realized outcomes. I have written down my own verdicts in the hope that someone can tell me whether a single principle lies behind them, and I would be grateful for a careful attempt.

The cases concern what a fair, publicly funded health and compensation scheme would do. Denying emergency care is, I take it, off the table in every case; my verdicts concern who should bear the extra cost — premiums, surcharges, deductibles, or loss of priority in the queue.

1. A child born with a severe genetic condition: full public support, no questions asked. This one seems to me beyond argument.
2. A man who smoked for forty years, aware throughout of the risks, now with lung cancer: I think it is fair that he bears more of the cost than the child in the first case — through premiums or deductibles, not denial of care. He gambled and lost.
3. A healthy man who took up rock climbing with full knowledge of the risks, now paralyzed after a fall: I find I do not think he should bear extra cost at all. I cannot say what distinguishes him from the smoker, except that I know many climbers and they strike me as reasonable people — which I suspect is not an argument.
4. Two drivers, both over the limit on the same road on the same night; one strikes a pedestrian, the other gets home uneventfully. The one who caused the injury seems to me to owe something the other does not — yet from my old professional standpoint the exposures were identical, and the difference between them is precisely the sort of thing I spent a career calling chance. I record the verdict but distrust it, and I would like to know whether any principled scheme can keep it.
5. A woman who declined a free, reliable genetic screening that would have flagged a predisposition to a treatable condition, and who then developed that condition: I lean toward treating her like the smoker, on the ground that the ignorance was chosen. A former colleague insists that since the information was never in her head, the case is one of brute luck throughout. I genuinely do not know.
6. A man with a severe gambling disorder — the disorder having, on the evidence I have seen, a substantial heritable component — who has ruined himself with bets he could not resist: I think the state owes him more help, the more severe the disorder. Yet nicotine dependence is also substantially heritable and is also a disorder, and in the smoker's case I was happy to hold responsibility fixed. These two verdicts cannot both be right, and I do not know which one to surrender.

I have a tentative principle of my own: responsibility attaches when the agent had the relevant information, a reasonably affordable alternative was available, and the choice was not itself a symptom of a disorder. The third clause, I can already see, will be the trouble — it appears to rescue the gambler at the price of the smoker, since a forty-year smoker's choices are hardly made free of dependence. As stated, the principle gets the first, second, third, and fifth cases right (or nearly), and the sixth wrong; or it gets the sixth right and the second wrong.

What I would like from you:

- A statement, as precise as you can make it, of the principle or principles that best capture these verdicts. Please treat my tentative principle as a candidate to be tested and repaired, not as an assumption.
- An explicit answer to the question I keep circling: should such a scheme judge people ex ante, by the risks they knowingly chose to run (which is how I would have analyzed the world in my working life), or ex post, by what actually befell them (which is how my verdicts in the second and fourth cases seem to run)? If the honest answer is that my verdicts mix the two, please say which verdicts must be surrendered, and to which side.
- Your accounting of which of my verdicts no defensible principle can keep, and why.
- Predictions for cases I have not listed — for instance, a motorcycle rider injured after a decade of unhelmeted riding, and a worker who took a dangerous but well-paid job on the reasoning that the extra wage was compensation for the risk. Does the market's having paid him for the risk change what the state owes him when the risk materializes?

For what it is worth, I have read enough to know the labels brute luck and option luck, and to know that the literature is uneasy about holding people responsible for their choices. But I am not after a survey of who said what. I want the principle, if there is one, stated plainly enough that a scientist could implement it — or a clear statement that my verdicts are an incoherent residue of prejudice, which I am prepared to hear.
