Here's something that's been bugging me about how we use prediction markets. Everyone quotes them like oracles — "the market has the incumbent at 62%" — and news people treat that number like the single best estimate of the probability, better than any one poll. Fine. But the market only works because people disagree with the price. Every trade needs a buyer and a seller, and a rational person only buys "yes" at 62 cents if they think the true chance is above 62 — meaning they think everyone else currently quoting that price is wrong. And there's this old result in economics, the no-trade theorem stuff, building on Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree thing: if traders are rational, start from the same priors, and each knows the others are rational, none of them should ever want to trade on private information alone, because "this guy is willing to take the other side of my bet" is itself evidence that cancels out whatever edge I thought I had. So the market needs traders who violate exactly the assumptions under which the price would cleanly aggregate everyone's information. That's the tension I can't get straight.

So my question: what does the practice of quoting market prices as probabilities actually presuppose? To treat the price as THE probability you seem to need it to be some kind of consensus — but the market only exists at all because there's no consensus, just people on both sides of every trade. Does the practice presuppose that the smart money outweighs the dumb money, i.e. that irrational "noise" traders supply the liquidity and slowly lose their stakes to informed traders? Or that a bet reveals a belief? Something Hayekian about dispersed information flowing into the price? Or is the whole thing just underdetermined, with several incompatible pictures of what a price even IS all licensing the same practice? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit epistemology, say which rival views the practice quietly rules out (if any), and say where it's genuinely compatible with multiple theories. And then tell me what would have to change if we swapped in the no-trade picture: if markets really were full of common-prior rational agents, would there be any trades left to set a price, and what would remain of the habit of treating prices as forecasts?

One related thing, don't know if it belongs in the same answer or not: the same pundit will cite a poll and a market price as two separate pieces of evidence for a forecast. But the traders in that market are reading the very same polls. What does counting them as independent lines of evidence presuppose?
