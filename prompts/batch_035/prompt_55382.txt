I'm an undergraduate taking an upper-level epistemology course, and for the final seminar meeting each of us must circulate a written position profile — the set of views we actually hold on the regress problem and its immediate surroundings — which the rest of the class will then spend an hour attacking. Mine is drafted below, and before I submit it I want an audit: do these commitments hang together, and where do they pull against each other?

1. Foundationalism about perception. Some perceptual beliefs — that there is a desk in front of me, that it is daylight — are justified without needing support from any further belief. The regress stops because some beliefs simply don't need premises.

2. Internalism about justification. Whatever justifies a belief must in principle be accessible to the believer by reflection alone; facts about my cognitive architecture that I could never uncover by thinking can't be what justifies my beliefs.

3. A requirement on inference. An inference confers justification on its conclusion only if the believer is also justified in believing the method of inference is truth-conducive — that deduction preserves truth, that the inductive practices are reliable. Otherwise inference merely shuffles beliefs around rather than transmitting anything.

4. Coherentism is false. Mutual support is circular, and circular support can redistribute whatever justification is already in the system but can never generate it; something has to come in from outside the circle.

5. Epistemic circularity is benign. It is legitimate to use perception to confirm that perception is reliable, and memory to check memory, even though the procedure presupposes its own conclusion. Everyone actually does this, and I think it works.

6. Infinitism is a non-starter, because no finite mind can possess infinitely many reasons. Though I also hold that a reason need only be available to me — stored, retrievable on reflection — rather than occurrently entertained in order to do justificatory work; no one can be required to rehearse their entire defense on demand.

Don't explain the regress problem back to me or survey the three positions; I've had a semester of that and the class will be bored. What I need is a consistency check on my own set. Please organize your response as follows:

"Restatement" (about 150 words): what each of the six commits me to, in terms of what follows from accepting it — not the labels, but the substance.

"Genuine tensions, ranked" (about 450 words): the pairs that genuinely conflict, most severe first, each with what would have to give if I keep the others. Be honest about severity differences rather than flagging everything equally.

"Apparent tensions" (about 200 words): pairs that look like conflicts but dissolve on closer inspection, with the dissolution spelled out.

"The cheapest single revision" (about 150 words): if I could change exactly one commitment, which change repairs the most, and what it costs me philosophically to make it.

I'd rather know now where the set breaks than find out in front of twelve classmates.
