write me a satirical advice column, "Ask Aunt Verity," in the voice of a beloved old-school newspaper agony aunt who has clearly read her philosophy: she knows that lying means asserting what you believe to be false, and she's concluded that anything short of that is the reader's own doing. She's proud to say she hasn't told a lie in forty years. Give it three letters and replies, around 600 words total. Every one of Aunt Verity's replies has to be strictly, verifiably true while being monstrousley misleading — and the mechanics have to be right, actual Gricean stuff: flouting relevance, smuggling in presuppositions, letting the cooperative principle do all the dirty work, never once asserting a falsehood. Include at least one letter from a reader who got hurt following her advice, plus her deadpan non-apology (she never said that, technically), and a short editor's note standing by the column on the grounds that no false assertion has ever appeared in it. The target of the satire is the real philosophical position that misleading is morally distinct from, and innocent next to, lying — someone who knows the lying vs. misleading literature should laugh, but should also come away feeling the position strain under what she does with it. Bonus points if Aunt Verity has one inviolable personal rule that sounds suspiciously Kantian without her ever noticing.
