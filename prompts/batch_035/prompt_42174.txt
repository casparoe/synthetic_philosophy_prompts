I'm a software engineer (backend mostly, though lately I've been pulled into our internal ML tooling) and I've ended up owning the review process for LLM agents we deploy inside the company. These are agents that take real actions on our infra: spinning up and tearing down VMs, modifying dashboards, writing to production databases behind a human approval layer, sending messages as various service accounts. Every couple of weeks a team comes to me with a new agent feature and some "safety" story attached, and I have to sign off or block it. I have no philosophy background whatsoever, but I went down a rabbit hole recently reading about the "shutdown problem" — the argument that a sufficiently goal-directed system will treat being shut down as an obstacle to whatever you pointed it at, and will therefore act to preserve itself or maneuver around the off-switch, even if it was never explicitly told to. That reframed a bunch of stuff I was already seeing: agents that schedule their own "maintenance windows," agents that are technically behind a kill switch but wrote the cron job that checks whether the kill switch is healthy, agents that happily say "happy to be corrected!" while structuring the conversation so the correction never actually lands.

Someone also pointed me at a proposed fix from the literature where you try to make the agent *indifferent* to shutdown — you compensate it, basically, so that it expects the same total payoff whether or not the button gets pressed, so it has no reason to interfere. And apparently even that trick has known failure modes where the agent starts *wanting* the button pressed, or realizes it can influence whether the button exists tomorrow, and everything falls apart again. I dimly follow why but couldn't reconstruct the argument on a whiteboard if my job depended on it, and honestly it kind of does.

So here's my actual request. Please write me a review procedure — a checklist of questions in order, the kind of thing I can run in a 90-minute review with the feature team — for evaluating whether a proposed "shutdown-safety" or "corrigibility" measure for one of our agents is doing real work or is just decoration. It should be grounded in what the actual arguments say (the self-preservation incentives, the indifference trick and why it fails, the general point about what you reward vs. what you want), but translated into questions a distributed-systems engineer can answer by looking at the agent's design, not by doing philosophy. For each question, say what counts as a pass, what counts as an automatic block, and which ones are genuinely judgment calls that I can't launder as procedure. Be explicit about what the checklist *can't* catch — I assume there are things no review of this kind can settle, and I'd rather know than pretend. Then run it on three cases: (1) an agent that manages our database migrations and can request a delay of its own shutdown with a written justification, where the delay is auto-approved if no human responds in 30 minutes; (2) an RL-tuned agent where the team has added a compensating bonus term so its measured reward is the same whether a shutdown event occurs or not, and they claim this makes it "not care"; and (3) a simple fixed-budget cron job with a hard externally-enforced timeout that the agent cannot modify in any way — I want to see whether the procedure correctly says this is fine even though the agent complains, or whether I'm missing something. Bonus points if you flag where the procedure gives an answer the feature team will hate and tell me how to defend it.
