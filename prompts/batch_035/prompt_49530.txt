I'm a teaching assistant for an intermediate decision theory course, and every year the two-envelope paradox costs me a week of office hours. Students arrive certain of the switching argument — whatever amount A is in your envelope, the other holds either 2A or A/2, each with probability one half, so switching gains A/4 in expectation — then equally certain of the symmetry reply, and they leave having learned only that infinities invalidate calculations, which sounds to them like a referee's excuse rather than a diagnosis. For next term I want a sharper exhibit. Construct me a case, built to explicit specifications, in an ordinary setting — a solicitor's letter about an eccentric benefactor, a bank's promotional game, whatever you like — one paragraph of scenario, no demons, no perfect predictors, no envelope ever containing an infinite amount, and no appeal to anything beyond ordinary countable probability.

The specifications. Arrange the distribution of envelope pairs so that (a) for every specific amount you might find when you open your envelope, the conditional expected gain from switching is strictly positive; (b) the symmetry argument is not refuted but explained: the unconditional expected gain from switching does not exist as a number, because the expected gain and the expected loss are both infinite; and (c) the naive switcher's fifty-fifty premise comes out false even though the conclusion it was meant to support is, at the conditional level, true — the case should punish the lazy dismissal and the lazy derivation equally. A natural route uses pairs (2^n, 2^{n+1}) with weights that decay from one pair to the next by a factor strictly between one and two; check for yourself that the tempting choice of an exact factor of two yields a conditional expected gain of exactly zero for every amount, a beautiful but useless boundary case, and that any factor of two or more forfeits specification (a).

Then demonstrate that the case meets each specification: walk through the symmetry argument, the pointwise switching calculation, and the comparison of strategies, and identify precisely which inference from the pointwise verdicts to the overall verdict fails, and which standard condition licenses that inference when it obtains. Note that the pointwise conditional gains, summed against the distribution of what you will actually see, form a series of strictly positive terms that diverges; say carefully what that does and does not establish, because my sharpest student will try to argue that the expected gain from switching is therefore plus infinity and the paradox returns with sharpened teeth. Next, truncate the distribution at a finite cap and report exactly where the conditional argument breaks — I predict the damage is confined to the topmost amount, which the player is almost never holding — and draw the moral that a player who has not yet opened the envelope cannot distinguish the truncated world from the untruncated one. Then give the strongest reply available to someone who still wants to defend the naive switcher, on the grounds that conditioning on the observed amount is precisely what a rational agent does, so that if the paradox lives at the conditional level, so be it. Finally, tell me honestly what the exhibit cannot reproduce — in particular the humbler version of the paradox in which the total expectation is finite and the switching argument is simply a probability error — and how you would deploy the case across a fifty-minute session: what students should compute before the reveal, and where the demonstration's limits lie.
