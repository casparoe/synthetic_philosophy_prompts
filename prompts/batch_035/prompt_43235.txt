Quick background so you know where this is coming from: I'm a retired physical chemist. Thirty-some years of reaction kinetics and spectroscopy, then retirement, and somewhere in there I picked up a philosophy habit that's gotten out of hand. The thing that has me in its grip lately is panpsychism — and not the fun cocktail-party version either. If experience goes all the way down to the fundamental level, then what about the *quality* of that experience? Is there something it's like to be an electron, and could that something be bad? Apparently people call this "pansufferism" and some of them take it seriously enough to do expected-value math on it. Meanwhile the other camp thinks that's basically a reductio of the whole view.

My nephew (a philosophy grad student, insufferable in the way only philosophy grad students can be) tells me the real fight here is between two camps: the panpsychist who genuinely worries there's suffering at the bottom of physics, and the functionalist who thinks suffering requires specific machinery — nociception, learning, something like a self-model — and who suspects that once you admit suffering could exist without any of that, you've signed up for something crazy. They talk past each other constantly. I've read enough of both to confirm it.

So here's the job. Draft the joint statement these two would produce after a serious adversarial collaboration — the document both could put their names on at the end. What I want in it:

1. A numbered section of claims both sides would actually sign. Real ones, with teeth, not "consciousness is mysterious." For example, I'd want to know whether they can jointly agree on what the pain-asymbolia cases show (patients who report the raw sensation of pain but say they don't mind it) — both camps think those cases matter, and I suspect there's shared ground there about nociception and suffering coming apart. Also whether they can jointly state the expected-value problem: even a small credence in micro-suffering, multiplied by the number of fundamental entities in a single liter of water, is an astronomically large number. Whatever they disagree about, they should be able to agree on *stating* the arithmetic. Both signing is the test — if a claim is worded so a functionalist would wince, it doesn't go in the agreed section.

2. A disagreements table. Each row needs to be phrased so the side it describes would accept the wording of its own view. The panpsychist should not come out looking like "believes rocks feel pain" unless an actual panpsychist would endorse that sentence; the functionalist should not come out looking like they "deny insects suffer" unless they'd really say that.

3. The cruxes — the subquestions such that settling one would actually move at least one of the parties. I have guesses I want checked against what you draft: (a) whether valence requires the capacity for motivation or behavioral plasticity, or whether it's just a qualitative character an experience can have all by itself; (b) whether tiny credences times astronomical numbers of sufferers should dominate our moral calculus, or whether there's a principled way to discount what I can only describe as Pascal's mugging with better manners; (c) what the combination problem does to the *location* of suffering — if micro-experiences don't combine into macro-experiences, can micro-suffering still add up to anything in a rock, a star, an ocean, that we should care about? If I've mis-identified a crux, or missed the load-bearing one, say so and fix it.

4. For each crux, some case, argument, or piece of evidence both parties would agree bears on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. This is the part I care about most as an old scientist — I want them making bets, not speeches. One obvious place to look: both camps have opinions about where suffering shows up as you go down the animal kingdom (nematodes, insects, jellyfish), and both think the trend line reflects on their view. But I'm open to other tests. What I don't want is "more research is needed" hedging — each side commits to what they'd expect, and the other side agrees it would count.

5. Finally, tell me whether any of this dispute is verbal rather than real. My suspicion, from my outsider's seat, is that both camps use "suffering" to mean two different things — a functional role on one side, a phenomenal character on the other — and that some fraction of the fight dissolves once you force them to say which they mean. If that's right, identify which parts dissolve and which parts survive as genuine disagreement.

The honest motivation, so you know what this is for: I ran a few thousand reactions in my career, and I'd like to know whether I have something to feel bad about, or whether the worry is an artifact of a theory that was never intended to carry moral weight in the first place. A document that ends with "this specific question, if answered, would tell us whether the moral stakes at the fundamental level are zero" would do more for me than another round of both sides asserting their intuitions at each other.

Format: numbered agreed-claims section first, then the disagreements table, then cruxes with tests and predictions, then the verbal-vs-real section at the end. Don't have either side win. The whole point is that the residual disagreement comes out *exact* — sharp enough that someone could, in principle, go settle it.
