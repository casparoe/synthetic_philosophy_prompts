My flatmate is doing a master's in international history and "who's to blame for which war" is basically her dinner-table topic. After a semester that started with the Fischer controversy she's developed pretty firm verdicts, and I've been keeping a list. Her rulings so far: WWI is mainly Germany's fault — everyone was reckless, but the German leadership made the choice that locked a continental war in, so not sole blame, but primary. WWII in Europe: overwhelmingly Germany. The US Civil War: the South — it seceded and fired first rather than accept losing an election. Korea: the North, obviously. The Seven Years' War: mainly Frederick, for invading neutral Saxony, even though Austria was gunning for revenge. And the Thirty Years' War: no primary culprit at all — the Empire's constitution plus the confessional deadlock made the whole thing pretty much overdetermined, and once it started everyone just kept choosing to continue it.

Next week her seminar moves on to other wars and I want to predict her rulings before she gives them, mostly to win an arguement. The candidates: (a) the Franco-Prussian War — France declared the war, but only after Bismarck edited the Ems telegram specifically to make sure they would; (b) the Russo-Japanese War — Japan struck first at Port Arthur, but only after Russia had spent months stalling a diplomacy it could easily have conceded on; and (c) the Peloponnesian War — does she pin it on rising expansionist Athens, on Sparta's decision that the rise had to be checked, or does she refuse primary blame the way she did for the Thirty Years' War?

Act as a diplomatic historian who moonlights writing on collective moral responsibility, and think it through before answering — maybe brainstorm out loud first. What I actually want is the inference problem, not a history lecture: lay out the candidate rules that could fit her whole track record (blame whoever first used force; blame whoever deliberately chose war as policy while a tolerable peace was still on the table; blame whichever great power had the real capacity to stop the escalation; when the structure overdetermines the war, primary blame dissolves), say which of those her record actually supports and where her rulings fail to separate them, then give your best guess with reasons for each of (a), (b), and (c). Finally, name one further war — not from my list — whose ruling would best split whatever rules are still tied after her Peloponnesian answer. And please don't get the underlying history wrong, even though that's not the main point.
