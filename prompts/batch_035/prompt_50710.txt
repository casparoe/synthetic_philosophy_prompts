I'm a retired entomologist -- 30-some years doing taxonomic work at a state agriculture department, mostly weevils and bark beetles, so I've spent my whole career deciding what is and isn't a species without ever once thinking about whether "species" means anything coherent. Since retiring I've gotten into philosophy of biology and discovered there's this whole species problem literature, and honestly it's been humbling. The biological species concept I grew up on doesn't work for half the beetles I described (plenty of them don't have sex, or we have no idea if they do), and the phylogenetic people seem to have a different problem for every solution.

Anyway, now it's not academic for me. Our state wildlife commission asked me to sit on the scientific subcommittee that reviews petitions under our endangered species law. Most of the petitions lately are pure taxonomy fights dressed up as law: a conservation group submits a genetic study splitting a common salamander into three "species" and asks us to list the rare one; a developers' association submits a different study lumping two rare plants back together and asks us to delist both. Same tools, opposite incentives, and we have to give a yes/no recommendation in writing within 90 days. We get maybe 6-8 petitions a year. The other committee members are a herpetologist, a land-use lawyer, and two retired wildlife biologists, and none of them want a philosophy lecture -- they want something we can actually run in a two-hour meeting with a packet of papers in front of us.

So what I want from you is a decision procedure. Something like: the questions we ask of a petition in order, what counts as enough of an answer to move to the next question, where it ends in a "recommend delist/deny" versus "recommend list" versus "insufficient evidence," and -- this is the part I care most about -- where the procedure has to stop and hand it back to us as a judgment call, because I've read enough now to know some of this is values, not facts. I'd like it to be honest that we're not really discovering whether these things are species, we're deciding what to treat as one for legal purposes, and the procedure should at least make that explicit rather than pretending it away.

Then run it on three petitions we've actually gotten variants of: (1) a DNA barcoding paper splitting a common streamside salamander into four, with the petition to list the one that happens to live where a highway extension is planned; (2) new genomic work suggesting two endangered freshwater mussels everyone's protected for decades are actually one gene-swapping species that's doing fine, so the petition argues we should delist both; and (3) an all-female parthenogenetic stick insect lineage someone wants listed, where I know the interbreeding criterion is just silent. I suspect case 2 is going to come out of the procedure as "delist," and I hate that answer, so show me where I'd have to break my own procedure to avoid it -- or whether the procedure has a principled way to surprise me.
