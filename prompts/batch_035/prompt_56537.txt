I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am building an interactive feature for our website: a diagnostic quiz, provisionally titled What Do You Actually Think a Belief Is?, organized around Daniel Dennett's distinction between the physical, design, and intentional stances and his claim, developed in his 1991 essay Real Patterns, that beliefs and desires are real in roughly the way biological species or economic recessions are real — as robust, predictively useful patterns in the physical grain of things. The editorial conceit is that almost no reader applies one stance consistently. The same person will describe their dog as wanting to be let out, their laptop as designed to sleep when the lid closes, and a hurricane as nothing but weather, without noticing that these are three different explanatory registers. The quiz's job is to expose that hidden structure: it should sort readers by how they react to cases, not by which philosophical label they would claim for themselves, and it must be fully answerable by an educated reader who has never heard of Dennett.

Please design the quiz under the following constraints:

1. Eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options. No question may use the words stance, intentional, functionalism, or any other term of art; technical vocabulary may appear only in the scoring key, where each term must be defined in one sentence.

2. The cases must between them force the distinctions that matter. At minimum, include: something like a chess engine or a language model, where readers will be tempted to treat a designed artefact as having beliefs; something like a bacterium swimming up a glucose gradient, which behaves purposively but is routinely denied beliefs; something like a thermostat, which sits on the border; a question in the spirit of Leibniz's mill, asking whether, if you could be shrunk down and walk inside the system watching its parts interact, you would expect to find the belief as one more visible component — and if not, what follows about whether it is there at all; and a question about predictive loss, asking whether a hypothetical superintelligence that could compute the particle-level physics of your friend would, in describing her that way, be missing something, or merely using more digits than necessary.

3. At least one question must concern a borderline attribution, where ordinary talk is genuinely undecided — for instance whether a person who sincerely says recycling matters while never sorting their rubbish believes it, or which of two things an ambiguous remark was really a belief about. The point is to discover whether the reader thinks there is a determinate fact here or that the question is itself ill-posed.

4. At least one question should test whether the reader's confidence in mental attributions tracks predictability or something else. The design stance is supposed to be invoked when prediction from physics is intractable but the thing was built for a purpose, and I want to know which of those two considerations my readers actually lean on when they come apart.

5. The scoring key must map answer patterns onto a genuine taxonomy, with each leaf carrying a name where one exists (real-pattern realism about the intentional stance; strict physicalism shading into eliminativism; design-stance deflationism, on which talk of belief is shorthand for talk of function; outright instrumentalism, on which it is a convenient fiction; and any others you think the space requires) and, for unnamed leaves, a note that nobody defends them, plus one sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment — the consequence its holders find hardest to swallow.

6. Two kinds of reader interest me most, and the key should make them visible as distinct leaves even though neither has a name in the literature: the reader who freely grants beliefs to animals but refuses them to machines, and the reader who does the reverse. If your question set cannot separate these two from each other, or cannot separate real-pattern realism from straightforward functionalism, say so plainly rather than papering over it — part of the feature's message to readers is that some of these positions are genuinely hard to tell apart from the inside.

7. Bear the medium in mind: readers will answer alone, on a phone, in a few minutes, and will share their leaf with friends. Each question should be answerable in under thirty seconds, and each leaf name should be short enough to repeat in conversation — but do not let this push the quiz toward flattery; an honest result that lands as slightly uncomfortable is a better share than a flattering one.

For the accompanying article, assume the working thesis is that the interesting question is not whether beliefs exist but where the patterns stop — and that most people's intuitions have no consistent answer to that, which is precisely why the quiz is worth running.
