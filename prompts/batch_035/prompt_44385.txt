I'm taking an upper-level seminar on Renaissance political thought, and we spent this week on Machiavelli's treatment of fortune and virtù. I've read Chapter 25 of The Prince several times now (in the W. K. Marriott translation), and I keep getting stuck on what exactly the argument is supposed to be. I'd be grateful for help unpacking it.

The chapter opens with Machiavelli conceding that many people believe fortune and God govern human affairs so completely that human wisdom counts for nothing, and that he is "in some degree inclined to their opinion." But then he writes:

"Nevertheless, not to extinguish our free-will, I hold it to be true that fortune is the arbiter of one half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less. I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings, bearing away the soil from place to place; everything flies before it, all yield to its violence, without being able in any way to withstand it; and yet, though its nature be such, it does not follow therefore that men, when the weather becomes fair, shall not make provision, both with defences and barriers, in such a manner that, rising again, the waters may pass away by canal, and their force be neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous."

Here is what confuses me, and I'd like your help working through it carefully:

1. The river analogy is clearly doing the heavy lifting, but I can't tell whether it actually supports the claim it's attached to. Dikes and canals seem to reduce fortune's power over everyone in the floodplain, all the time, which sounds like more than "half" of our actions. Is the half-versus-half division a serious quantitative claim, or should I read it more like a rhetorical gesture whose only real content is that fortune's influence is neither zero nor total? And if the analogy is the real argument, does it perhaps suggest that the proportion isn't fixed at all — that it depends on how much has been invested in the "defences and barriers," which for a prince means institutions and military preparation?

2. Later in the chapter, Machiavelli introduces what looks like a second, quite different mechanism: the successful ruler is the one "who directs his actions according to the spirit of the times," and men fail when their fixed natural disposition (cautious or impetuous) happens to fall out of step with what the times reward, since they cannot persuade themselves to abandon a method that has always worked. This adaptability story doesn't seem to be about pre-emptive engineering at all — Pope Julius II succeeded by impetuousness, not by building dikes. So are there two distinct conceptions of virtù running through the chapter (advance preparation versus flexible conformity to circumstances), and if so, do they pull in different directions? It seems to me that the Julius II example quietly undermines the river analogy: if what matters is whether your temperament happens to match the times, then when times change, no amount of preparation saves the man who "cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to."

3. A more philosophical worry my professor raised in lecture, which I only half understood: on the second mechanism, virtù starts to look suspiciously like another name for luck. If a prince succeeds because his nature happened to fit the times he lived in — and Machiavelli explicitly says the cautious man is ruined "when it is time to turn adventurous" — then was the virtù ever his achievement? How might Machiavelli respond to the charge that the chapter, read this way, concedes more to fortune than its opening half-and-half formula admits?

Could you walk me through the structure of the chapter and tell me whether these three puzzles are genuine tensions in the text or artifacts of my misreading? I'm less interested in the historical background than in the argument itself — though if you think something about the circumstances in which The Prince was written bears on how the chapter should be read, I'd want to know that too.
