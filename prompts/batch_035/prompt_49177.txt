My book club is reading a popular history of the Bloomsbury Group, and it contains a summary of Moore's ethics that has stayed with me: "Moore's Principia Ethica (1903) proved that good is a simple, non-natural property, and used this result to show that the only things worth having for their own sake are beauty and friendship — beautiful objects are good in themselves, and personal affection is good in itself. The value of a friendship is simply the value of the beloved added to the pleasure the affection brings. And since these are the only intrinsic goods, Moore's reckoning is that a life spent contemplating art among friends is better than a life devoted to relieving suffering." The book goes on to credit Moore, through this argument, with justifying the Bloomsbury set's devotion to art and private relationships over public service.

I teach mostly political philosophy and haven't worked through Principia since graduate school, so while something feels off — I half-remember Moore insisting that the value of a whole is not always the sum of the values of its parts — I can't locate the errors with any confidence. Please audit the summary claim by claim: which parts a careful Moorean would accept as correct, which are conflations or overstatements (I suspect the summary runs together claims Moore kept distinct, but I'd like to know which), and whether the closing comparison between the contemplative life and the life of relieving suffering follows from anything Moore actually held or merely from the misstatements. I want a committed verdict: identify the single worst error, explain what the correct position is, and tell me whether the book's conclusion about Moore's influence on Bloomsbury survives the corrections or has to be abandoned. Please take a definite position rather than laying out the interpretive options and leaving me to choose.
