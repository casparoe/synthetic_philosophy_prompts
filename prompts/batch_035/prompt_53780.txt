I'm taking a philosophy of psychiatry course and have got stuck on something I suspect is really a philosophy of biology problem in disguise. Our textbook insists that whatever else a mental disorder is, it has to involve some kind of dysfunction — a failure of a psychological or biological mechanism to do what it's supposed to do — and this is supposed to explain why ordinary suffering isn't automatically pathological: intense grief after a death, terror during a mugging, and misery in an unlivable marriage are painful but, the thought goes, the mechanisms producing them are working fine. At the same time, the psychiatric literature I'm reading happily deploys evolutionary functional explanations: anxiety as an evolved threat-detection alarm, low mood as an adaptive response to unattainable goals, attachment distress as a system designed to keep infants near caregivers. So the same field relies on function-talk to explain why symptoms exist and on dysfunction-talk to explain why they're disorders.

Here's what I can't sort out. As I understand it, there are two competing accounts of what a biological function even is. On a causal-role account, the function of a part is the contribution it makes to the activities of some containing system — the heart's function is pumping blood because pumping is what the heart does that keeps the circulatory system going. On an etiological or selected-effect account, a trait's function is what it was selected for in the trait's evolutionary history — the heart pumps blood because ancestors whose hearts pumped blood left more offspring. These come apart in psychiatry in uncomfortable ways. Consider a person with severe social anxiety in a modern city: if the threat-detection system is responding to its inputs exactly as selection calibrated it, and the problem is that the ancestral calibration meets an environment full of strangers and cameras, then in the selected-effect sense the mechanism is functioning properly while the person is debilitated. Conversely, causal-role function seems to classify almost anything that impairs the person's overall psychological performance as a dysfunction, which threatens to drag ordinary grief back into the category of disorder. And there's a further wrinkle: fever and pain are paradigm cases of systems doing exactly what they were selected for, and medicine treats them as treatable conditions but presumably not as dysfunctions of anything — so is "dysfunction" even doing the work psychiatry wants it to, or is it a placeholder for a value judgment about harm?

What I'd like is a reconstruction of what the dysfunction criterion implicitly presupposes about biological function. Does psychiatric diagnosis need one account rather than the other, is it genuinely compatible with both, or do different parts of psychiatric practice quietly presuppose different ones (e.g., evolutionary-psychiatric explanations of symptoms presupposing selected-effect functions while the diagnosis of those symptoms as disordered presupposes causal-role ones)? Please also work through the mismatch cases honestly: if we took the selected-effect view strictly, would much of what the DSM classifies as anxiety or depressive disorder fail to count as dysfunction at all, and would we then have to say either that those conditions aren't disorders or that being a disorder doesn't actually require dysfunction? And tell me where the practice is underdetermined — cases where the criterion doesn't decide between the accounts, or where both would deliver the same verdicts for different reasons — rather than forcing a tidy answer. I'd also like to know what would actually change in how we classify conditions if psychiatry openly committed to one account over the other.
