Write an essay on nudging and paternalism, but from an angle I haven't seen argued much, so don't just rehash the usual Thaler/Sunstein vs. critics stuff. Here's the setup I want you to engage with: suppose a city passes a rule that every government-set default has to come with a small label, like a nutrition label, saying that the default was chosen to steer people, plus one line on which direction it steers. So the organ donor box is pre-checked, AND right next to it it says "pre-checked in order to raise donation rates; uncheck to opt out." Call these labeled nudges. One side says that once the steering is disclosed like that, it stops being a nudge and becomes just an ordinary recommendation. The other side says the disclosure is basically irrelevant, because the default effect runs on inertia, not on ignorance — people stick with whatever's pre-selected regardless — so the paternalism is exactly the same, just more honest about itself.

The core question: is a fully labeled nudge still paternalistic in the bad sense? Work through which of these is supposed to be the bad thing about paternalism — (a) the deception or non-transparency, (b) the bypassing of deliberation, or (c) the substitution of the nudger's judgment for the nudgee's — because each gives a different verdict on labeled nudges. Also deal with the "someone has to pick a default anyway" objection: if the city has to pre-check or not pre-check something no matter what, and random coin-flipping is silly, in what sense is any default paternalistic at all? And does your answer to that undermine or rescue the case for the labels?

Don't end on a wishy-washy note — defend a clear verdict on whether labeled nudges are acceptable, and ideally your verdict should explain whether labeling makes nudges meaningfully better than unlabeled ones or only cosmetically so. At the end, add a short paragraph saying what would change your mind: which premise or argument in your own reasoning is most likely to fail, or what consideration would flip you to the opposite verdict. Keep the whole thing under 600 words. Casual tone is fine, I'm not after a formal paper, but do be rigorous where it counts.
