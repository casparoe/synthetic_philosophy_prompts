As a science journalist who has spent much of the past decade writing about pain — profiles of pain researchers, explainers on the 2020 revision of the IASP's pain definition, features on chronic pain patients — I have accumulated a set of gut verdicts about when pain is bad for the person in it, and I can no longer tell whether they add up to anything coherent. Here are the verdicts.

A child born with congenital insensitivity to pain who bites through her own tongue and loses fingertips to unnoticed injuries: her painlessness is a catastrophe, and I find myself saying the pain she never feels is a good her life is missing, not merely a signal her body is missing. A patient with pain asymbolia, who after a stroke or cingulotomy reports that pinpricks still hurt but seems almost amused by them, ducks nothing, and shows no distress: I cannot make myself think his pain is very bad for him, even though the intensity he reports is normal. A postsurgical patient on morphine who says the pain is still there, fully felt, but that it no longer matters to her: somewhere in between — worse than the asymbolic patient's pain, much less bad than the same pain before the drug. A burn victim whose excruciating days actually do their jobs — pulling his hand off the stove, keeping him from reinjuring the grafts: the pain is bad, but I want to say it is partly redeemed by its usefulness, and I notice the asymbolia verdict already sits uneasily with that one, since the asymbolic patient is precisely the one whose pain does no job at all. Two people with the same intensity and duration of post-surgical pain, one of whom is terrified and the other calm: I judge the terrified one's pain worse, which suggests the badness tracks the fear rather than the hurting. A person with a low-grade daily headache for a year versus a person with one severe headache for an afternoon, the two matched for total misery-hours if you multiply intensity by time: I think the chronic one is worse, though I can't say why linear addition feels wrong to me. And the case I keep going back and forth on: a patient given midazolam before a procedure so that the procedure is painful at the time but the memory is entirely erased afterward — the pain was fully aversive while it lasted, and it now touches nothing, no memory, no dread, no aftermath, and I cannot settle whether that makes it barely bad after all or as bad as ever.

My tentative principle is that the badness of pain just is its aversiveness — the bothering, not the hurting — which would make the asymbolic patient's pain nearly costless and the calm patient's pain less bad than the frightened one's. But the principle seems to imply that the midazolam patient's pain is barely bad only if badness is somehow retrospective, which feels wrong, and it leaves me no way to say what the CIP child is missing, since what she lacks is not distress but the whole package.

Can you state a principle, or a small set of rival candidates, that captures these verdicts — each clause motivated by the case that drives it — tell me what the best candidate predicts for cases I haven't listed, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot save? I would particularly like to know whether the asymbolia verdict or the calm-versus-terrified verdict has to go, because I suspect they cannot both survive contact with a single principle about what makes pain bad.
