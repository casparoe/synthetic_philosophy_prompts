I'm teaching an evening class on philosophy of mind next term, and one session is on whether self-deception is even possible. The standard setup is the paradox: to deceive yourself you'd have to know the truth while believing its opposite, all in one mind, and it's never quite clear how that's supposed to work — it looks like a card sharp marking his own deck. Then there are the escape routes: the Sartrean one, where the self-deceiver doesn't exactly know the truth but is aware of it in a way he refuses to avow (the famous waiter, playing at being a waiter), and the deflationary one, where there's no deception going on at all, just motivated reasoning — a lawyer in your head who never lies outright but only cross-examines one side, and Davidson's version where the contradictory beliefs get held in separate compartments that somehow don't talk to each other. For the session I want to pair the theory with Arthur Miller's Death of a Salesman, because Willy Loman looks like the textbook case, but I keep flip-floping on whether he really is one.

The scenes pulling me in different directions: the flashbacks, where the Boston affair and the Woman are right there on stage while Willy talks to her in the past and to Linda and Biff in the present — the audience can see him knowing and not-knowing at once, which sounds like the partition the theories want, except Miller splits it across time rather than across the mind, so maybe that's cheating. The rubber pipe Linda finds: hiding it seems to require knowing what it's for, yet he never admits to himself what it's for. And the restaurant scene, where Biff is finally telling him the plain truth and Willy keeps drifting into the past, refusing the one piece of evidence that can't be spun. But there's a complication I have to face honestly: plenty of critics read Willy as simply ill — exhausted, half-breakdown, not really responsible for his beliefs — and if that's right then the philosophical question dissolves and my session is about a tragedy, not a paradox. I don't want to do that to the play, but I also don't want to pretend the reading isn't there.

So what I'd like is a reading that takes both the play and the debate seriously: say where Willy fits the bad-faith picture, where he fits the deflationary one, where the play resists being an illustration of either, and whether it dramatises somthing the arguments themselves can't get at — maybe about how long a person can go on not-avowing something without ever quite believing its opposite. Which scenes would you send the class to, and at the end, which is the honest verdict: is Willy self-deceived, deluded, or both at different moments? Please answer in British English.
