I teach a sophmore seminar on democratic theory — 17 students, one 75-minute block — and in two weeks we hit the Condorcet jury theorem. I don't want to lecture it and have them nod along. I want a game where they first feel the theorem working, and then feel it break, and only afterwards get the name and the math. Design it for me.

The shape I have in mind: repeated rounds where the class votes on binary questions with real but unknowable-in-advance answers (something like which of two jars has more marbles, or which of two cities is farther north — questions where they genuinely have to guess, nobody just knows). The trick is that I control the information. In some rounds each student gets a private slip that points at the right answer with a fixed probability like 0.65, so the majority should start outperforming any individual and they can watch that happen. Then you sneak in rounds where I've rigged the dependence: e.g. a "tip sheet" I hand to everyone before they vote that's usually right but occasionally wrong, or one slip that five students all share. Individual accuracy stays exactly the same, but the votes are now correlated, and the majority goes to garbage. That's the moment I want them to hit — same voters, same competence, majority stops working, and they don't know why yet.

Also include a round or mechanic around heterogeneity: I want three students to secretly recieve much better slips, and then at some point the class gets to vote on changing the voting rule — one person one vote vs. giving the (supposedly) better-informed students double votes — ideally before they know whether they themselves are in the expert group. I want them arguing about that.

Please give me: the full round structure with timing, materials, the payoff scheme (points, not money, and it should make them care about getting majorities right rather than individually right), the debrief mapping each round onto competence and independence and what the correlation round says about electorates that share a media environment, and the last ten minutes where I finally state the theorem and they see what they were inside. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students comparing slips, someone actually knowing an answer, ties — and be honest about what 17 voters just cannot show: I assume the asymptotics are mine to supply on the whiteboard, tell me if there's anything else the game structurally can't reproduce.
