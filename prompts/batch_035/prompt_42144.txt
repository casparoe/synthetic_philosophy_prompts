I'm preparing a term paper for a history of economic thought seminar on Einstein's political writings, and I've hit a question his own texts can't settle. In his 1949 essay for the inaugural issue of Monthly Review, he argued that capitalist production for profit rather than use generates cycles of boom and depression, that an oligarchy of private capital dominates both the legislature and the sources of public information, and that the economic anarchy of competition is the real source of capitalism's evils. His remedy was a planned economy oriented toward social goals, with the means of production owned by society. Yet he closed on a cautionary note: a planned economy is not yet socialism, since it may simply install an all-powerful bureaucracy and complete enslavement of the individual, and the grave problem for socialism is how to secure a democratic counterweight to that bureaucracy. Nowhere in the essay does he confront the economic objection that had already been on the table for decades — the Mises-Hayek claim that rational calculation without market prices is impossible.

Suppose Einstein could be shown what has happened since 1955. Computing power has grown by many orders of magnitude; enormous firms like Amazon plan continental-scale logistics internally, without internal price signals, in a way mid-century planners could only dream of; and machine-learning systems now forecast demand and optimize supply chains in real time. A recurring line of argument among economists and computer scientists — with roots in mid-century cybernetics, its brief real-world trial in Allende's Chile, and its periodic revivals ever since — holds that the calculation problem was a limitation of mid-century information technology rather than a permanent feature of socialism. What would Einstein make of this? Would he treat algorithmic planning as the missing economic warrant for the planned economy he called for, or would he insist that his essay's reservations were never about computational capacity in the first place — that if he were to concede anything to Hayek, it would be that the relevant knowledge is dispersed, tacit, and never encoded in any dataset, so that no demand-forecasting algorithm answers the objection? And given that his complaint targeted an anarchy he thought private ownership made inevitable, would he distinguish between planning by a democratic state and planning by a private logistics giant — or would the latter look to him like the oligarchy of capital he feared, now with a planning apparatus of its own?

Please reconstruct his likely position as carefully as the evidence allows, grounding it in what he actually wrote in the essay and in his broader, well-documented political commitments, and flag clearly where you're moving from reconstruction to extrapolation. I'd also like you to address the awkward historical detail that the calculation debate predates his essay: whether his silence on it reflects unfamiliarity, indifference, or a considered view that the objection was answerable. End by taking a stance on the question I actually care about: would Einstein see contemporary claims that big data and AI make socialism technically feasible as the fulfillment of his hopes, or as precisely the technocratic future his closing warning was written to guard against?
