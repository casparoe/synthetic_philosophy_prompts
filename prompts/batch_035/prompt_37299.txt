My mother was diagnosed two years ago with frontotemporal dementia, and I am her healthcare proxy. Long before any symptoms, she signed an advance directive refusing artificial nutrition and hydration in advanced dementia, and her care facility has now begun asking me, quite formally, whether I intend to honor it — this while she sits contentedly in the common room, greets me warmly, and tells anyone who asks that she wants to keep living. In trying to sort out what I owe her, I've noticed that the whole practice of honoring advance directives in the face of a patient's current, contrary wishes seems to rest on some view of what makes a person the same person over time, and I'd like help reconstructing it. The earlier self issues an instruction that binds a later self who no longer endorses it, and the institution treats this as not merely permissible but obligatory. What theory of personal identity — or of what matters in survival, if that's different from identity — does that practice presuppose? Does it require that my mother then and the woman in the common room now are numerically the same person, or would some weaker relation, like a promise owed to a predecessor, carry the same weight? And it seems to presuppose it selectively: the same legal system that lets the past self bind the present one here also holds that a contract signed at twenty-five binds the signer at sixty, punishes the sixty-year-old for what the twenty-five-year-old did, and in general treats a person's life as one continuous subject of rights and duties — so I can't tell whether the directive practice is an application of that same assumption or a quiet exception to it, or whether it rests on something else entirely, like a judgment that the earlier, competent self's autonomous choice deserves protection precisely because the later self is compromised.

I've also been reading about Parfit's teletransporter, where a machine on Earth scans and destroys you while a replica is constructed on Mars, and his suggestion that identity may not be what we actually care about in survival — that psychological connectedness and continuity are what matter. That complicates things, because if what matters is psychological continuity, then the directive's authority seems strongest when there is still substantial connectedness between the author and the current patient (she echoes her old values in fragments) and to lapse as connectedness frays — meaning there might be some point at which honoring it stops being what she would want and starts being something else. Whereas on a view where the person simply persists as a biological organism, or where past autonomous choice binds regardless, the directive retains its full force right to the end, and the woman in the common room never gets a vote. I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with — I suspect several rival views of identity would license it, and I'd like to know where it's genuinely underdetermined. Then tell me what the practice would have to look like if the presupposed view were swapped out: what does a directive regime look like if built on the view that what matters is psychological connectedness rather than identity, or on a narrative view of persons, or on the view that the demented patient is best understood as a successor rather than a continuation? Finally, and this matters to me practically, please flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail: which premise in your reconstruction is the weakest, and what consideration — about the practice, about my mother's situation, or about the underlying view of survival — would most plausibly flip your verdict about what the practice commits me to.
