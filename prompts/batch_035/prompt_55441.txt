I'm a novelist with three published books, and I'm applying for a nine-month residential fellowship at a humanities center that funds writers doing substantive research alongside a draft. My working subject is consciousness and narration, and my half-formed idea is this: the omniscient narrator — the convention that a novel can deliver a single, settled account of what a character experienced, moment by moment — is a fictional embodiment of the very thing Dennett called the Cartesian theater. And if the multiple drafts model is right that there is no canonical inner account, no final edit screened for a single inner audience, then most techniques of literary interiority, including the experimental ones, quietly reinstall the theater: stream of consciousness still flows through one authoritative channel, and free indirect discourse still resolves into one report of what happened in a mind. The novel I want to draft would try to narrate a mind without that canonical resolution — competing drafts of the same moments, none privileged — and I want the fellowship to fund the research and drafting of a substantial section of it.

Please turn this into a proposal. Specifically: state the research question precisely, so that it has a determinate answer rather than dissolving into "can fiction do weird things," which of course it can. Explain why the existing positions leave it open — my sense is that the philosophy uses literary metaphors freely while treating them as illustrations rather than constraints, and that the fiction that looks like it rejects a unified self mostly rejects unity of self, not unity of narrative record, but you should sharpen or correct that. Set out the method month by month: what I would actually do, including the technical writing exercises I'd run (my current thought is a sequence of scenes each drafted three ways, then a study of whether anything like an implied final version emerges for a reader no matter what I do). Say what a result would look like, including the negative result I take seriously: that the demand is incoherent or sterile, that narrative requires a canonical record the way it requires time, and that the interesting finding is why. Say what would count as the project failing, and be honest about scope — nine months buys research, exercises, and maybe eighty pages, not a novel.

Two requests beyond the drafting. First, since my grip on the philosophy is an interested amateur's, the proposal should flag — perhaps in a closing note — wherever it leans on a contested or charitable reading rather than a settled one, so I don't present more confidence than I have. Second, and more important: if you think the premise is confused — that narrative authority and the Cartesian theater are only loosely analogous, that I'm proposing a philosophy paper in a novelist's coat, or that the question as I've posed it can't survive being made precise — say so plainly instead of writing the polished proposal I asked for. A fellowship built on a muddled analogy would cost me nine months, and I'd rather hear it now. You can write the proposal and the objection, if both are warranted, but don't soften the objection to spare me.
