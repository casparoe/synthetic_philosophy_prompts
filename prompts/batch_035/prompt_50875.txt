I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and one of my regular gigs is a back-page feature in a general-interest magazine: spoof documents that are funny to specialists and readable to everyone else. The next installment takes on economic methodology — the debate over unrealistic assumptions in scientific models that Milton Friedman's 1953 essay set off, and the defense of such assumptions now known as the F-twist. Paraphrased, the F-twist says roughly this: a theory's assumptions should not be judged by their descriptive realism but by the accuracy of the theory's predictions, and the more significant a theory is, the more unrealistic its assumptions are entitled to be.

Please write, in the deadpan administrative register of a genuine consumer-product safety recall, a notice of roughly 600 words (headline included) issued by an invented body — call it something like the Consortium of Model Builders — announcing the partial recall of several batches of idealizing assumptions: perfectly rational agents, frictionless planes, perfectly competitive markets, infinitely large populations, instantaneous adjustment. The comedy must come from the real distinctions philosophers of science actually use, not invented ones. In particular, structure the recall around the three kinds of assumption that Alan Musgrave distinguished when answering Friedman: negligibility assumptions, where a factor is ignored on the grounds that it hardly matters (no defect, nothing to recall); domain assumptions, where the model is claimed to hold only within a stated range (defective only when taken outside that range, so the recall covers solely customers who misused the product); and heuristic assumptions, which are knowingly false and deployed deliberately, as scaffolding (these cannot be recalled, since they were never sold as true in the first place). Give each batch its own section — affected units, recall status, remedy, refund policy — and make the allocation of assumptions to categories do at least some of the joking.

Somewhere in the notice, include a paragraph in which the Consortium declines, on explicitly Friedmanian grounds, to recall its flagship theory — precisely because that theory's assumptions are the most unrealistic, and its predictions the best. Let the reader feel the genuine pull of that defense even while laughing at it. Close with a disclaimer that quietly performs the same sleight of hand the notice satirizes.

A few ground rules. Take on the role of a philosopher of science with a comic bent: before drafting, think through briefly which classic idealizations plausibly fall under which of Musgrave's categories and where the joke in each lies, so that the parody stays fair to the actual methodological positions rather than caricaturing them. Do not quote any real text verbatim — paraphrase every position in your own words. And keep the irony legible: a specialist in economic methodology should finish the piece feeling that the author understood the debate, not that the author sneered at it.
