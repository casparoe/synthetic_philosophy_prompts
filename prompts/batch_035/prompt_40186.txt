Seed items — take a position on each (expand, prune, or rebut):

1. Blindsight-type cases: unconscious perceptual states that appear first-order indistinguishable from conscious ones, so first-order sameness cannot entail sameness of phenomenology. Genuine desideratum, or does including it beg the question against first-order rivals before the contest starts?
2. Targetless higher-order states: a higher-order representation with no first-order state beneath it, as in certain confabulations or imaginative episodes. Must a theory allow these at all, and if so, what must it say the experience is like?

Misalignment: when the higher-order representation misclassifies the first-order state — takes a G-ish state for an F-ish one — the theory must say which phenomenology the subject has, and the answer cannot simply be "whatever the first-order state is," on pain of collapsing into a first-order theory.
4. Infants and non-human animals: if phenomenally conscious states essentially involve conceptual higher-order thought, the theory owes us either a story about which concepts infants command or a bullet to bite. Say which you find acceptable.
5. No self-stultification: whatever makes a state conscious on the theory must apply to the higher-order states themselves, without launching a regress the theory cannot close — assess the dispositional, teleological, and same-order escape routes, and say which, if any, you would accept.
