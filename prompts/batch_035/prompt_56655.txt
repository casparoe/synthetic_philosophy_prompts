Each December, the magazine I write for — I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet — runs an issue in which journalists are paired with a philosophical idea and asked to contribute a short piece of fiction that makes the idea felt rather than explained. This year I have drawn Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," and specifically its long opening argument that no one has ever managed to say what makes a sentence analytic — true by meaning alone, come what may — without quietly appealing to some further notion (definition, synonymy, interchangeability, a semantical rule) that itself cannot be specified without assuming the very distinction at issue. I find the idea stubbornly resistant to dramatization, and I would rather not fall back on the cheap device of two professors arguing over coffee. The story needs to make a reader run into the circularity Quine describes, in the gut, before they could name it.

Here is the setup I have been circling, which you should feel free to modify or replace entirely if you see a better one. A fictional national standards bureau maintains the Official Dictionary: a court-admissible record of what words mean, consulted in citizenship hearings, contract disputes, and inheritance cases. The bureau's founding charter promises that its definitions are analytic — true in virtue of meaning alone, revisable only when the language itself has drifted, never in response to how the world happens to turn out. My protagonist is a career lexicographer who inherits the entry for "bachelor," defined per the charter as "unmarried adult man," and begins receiving rulings from the field: a widower whose grandchildren insist the word cannot apply to him; a monk who is technically an unmarried adult man but whom no magistrate would call a bachelor; a man in a marriage the state refuses to recognize, so that the law says unmarried and common sense says married. For each case she must decide whether it is a failed application (the world's fault, the definition stands) or a failed definition (the dictionary's fault, the entry must change) — and I want the story to show that this very line between the two kinds of failure is what she cannot draw, since ruling that the monk is "not really a bachelor" just is revising the meaning while claiming to have discovered it.

A few requirements for the piece:

1. Length of roughly 1,500–2,500 words, realistic and near-contemporary in setting, single point of view, and no genre devices — the pressure should come from paperwork, deadlines, and precedent, not from sci-fi trappings.
2. The philosophy must arrive through the character's choices and casework, not through exposition. No character may name Quine or use the words "analytic" and "synthetic" before the final page, if at all.
3. At least one scene should dramatize Quine's image of our statements facing the tribunal of experience as a corporate body: a proposed fix to "bachelor" that would force revisions rippling through "marriage," "man," and "adult," so that defending one entry means destabilizing others.
4. Choose the ending deliberately — whether she quietly rewrites the entry and backdates the paperwork, resigns, writes a definition with a built-in escape clause ("any man whom a court would describe as..."), or something else entirely — and make that choice say something definite about whether the reader should come away thinking the distinction is false, or merely indefensible, or salvageable at the price of becoming unrecognizable.
5. After the story, include an author's note of about 300 words mapping each plot device onto the specific stretch of Quine's argument it stands in for, and candidly flagging where the fiction does something Quine would not endorse — for instance, the bureau's charter presupposes that definitions have genuine institutional force, which is precisely the kind of thing Quine's argument is meant to make suspicious.

If you think the dictionary bureau is the wrong vehicle and that a better story lurks elsewhere — a border checkpoint where officials must apply a definition of "refugee" or "device," an insurance adjuster litigating "flood," a translation office certifying that two phrasings in different languages mean the same thing — I am open to that, provided the substitute generates the same structure: someone who must treat a statement as unrevisable in principle, discovers they can only keep it upright by revising something, and cannot say which they are doing.
