The exam question was:

"Explain the distinction between weak and strong emergence. Then explain how multiple realizability bears on the prospects for Nagel-style intertheoretic reduction. If multiple realizability is as widespread as Fodor suggested in his classic paper on the special sciences, does that show that the higher-level sciences are strongly emergent, or does it support only a weaker kind of autonomy?"

The student's answer reads:

"Weak emergence is when a system's higher-level behavior is unexpected or surprising but is still fully deducible from the lower-level facts in principle — given enough computing power, we could simulate a system like the Game of Life and predict the gliders that appear. Strong emergence is when higher-level facts are not deducible even in principle and the higher-level properties have genuine causal powers of their own, over and above the lower-level ones. Nonlinearity is a good example of strong emergence: chaotic systems like the weather are so sensitive to their initial conditions that their behavior simply cannot be predicted from the microlevel, so it isn't deducible from the lower-level facts at all. Turning to reduction: Nagel's model requires bridge laws, which state strict identities between the terms of the two theories, for example 'temperature = mean molecular kinetic energy.' The classic objection, due to Fodor, is multiple realizability: a single higher-level property, like a given economic property or a psychological state, can be realized by countless different physical configurations, so there is no one physical kind for it to be identical to. This shows that the identity theory is false, and token events aren't physical either, since a given pain event could have been realized by a nonphysical substrate if the laws of nature had been different. The special sciences are therefore autonomous: their generalizations cannot be reduced away, and since the higher-level facts supervene on the lower-level facts, the lower level fully explains them. So multiple realizability gives us strong emergence — the higher-level properties cause things that no purely physical description can capture."
