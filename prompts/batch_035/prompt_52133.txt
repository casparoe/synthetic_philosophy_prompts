As a retired physicist who has recently taken up philosophy in earnest, I have been working through the literature on the doomsday argument and have hit a snag I cannot resolve unaided. I understand the Carter–Leslie version well enough: if I treat my birth rank as a random sample from all humans who will ever live, a Bayesian update on my comparatively early rank shifts probability mass towards earlier extinction. I also understand Gott's delta t version, which reaches a similar conclusion from nothing but the Copernican premise that the present moment is unlikely to fall in either tail of the phenomenon's duration. What I cannot see is whether these are genuinely the same argument in two notations, or two arguments that merely converge on the same conclusion — and, relatedly, whether the self-indication assumption, which is usually presented as a rebuttal to the Carter–Leslie reasoning by boosting the prior probability of worlds containing more observers, does anything at all against Gott's formulation, which never mentions observers, only durations.

Please take on the role of an expert in anthropic reasoning and Bayesian philosophy of science, and think carefully before answering — I would suggest you begin with a short brainstorm of the possible positions on whether the two arguments are equivalent, then set out each argument formally enough that any disanalogy becomes visible, and only then assess whether the standard SIA rebuttal is a scalpel for the one and a blunt instrument for the other, or whether I have manufactured a distinction where none exists. I should confess that I find the SIA rebuttal suspicious on independent grounds, as it seems to confer a kind of blessing on populous worlds that has nothing to do with evidence; if you think that suspicion is muddying my question, please say so plainly rather than humouring me.
