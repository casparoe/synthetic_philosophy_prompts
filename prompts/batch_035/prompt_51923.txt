So my friend Dev and I just wrapped up a four-hour argument (over coffee that went cold, then pizza) about expected value fanaticism, and we've decided the only productive thing left is to do one of those adversarial collaboration writeups people talk about. I want you to draft it for us.

Where we each stand: Dev is basically the fanatic. He thinks that if you're choosing between (a) a certain outcome of a few thousand units of well-being, say saving a handful of people, and (b) a one-in-10^50 shot at a future containing trillions of trillions of flourishing lives, you're obligated to pick (b), because expected value just dominates, and anything else is a kind of temporal provincialism — privileging the people you can see. I'm the moderate. I don't dispute the arithmetic, but I think something is off about letting vanishingly small probabilities of astronomically large payoffs swamp everything else, and I have a pile of half-formed worries: that our "utility" numbers stop meaning anything across that many orders of magnitude, that we're clueless enough about the far future that tiny-probability estimates are basically noise, and that a bounded value function is not the ad hoc cop-out Dev says it is.

Here's what I want the document to contain:

Dev thinks the deepest one is whether unbounded aggregation of value is coherent; I think it might be whether credences of 1-in-10^50 can carry any normative weight at all given where they come from (back-of-envelope estimates stacked on back-of-envelope estimates). Include at least one crux neither of us has thought of if you can find a real one.

4. For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or thought experiment we'd both agree bears on it — ideally something like: the repeatable version of the choice (facing the same gamble a million times vs. once), or the "many independent tiny chances" scenario where the fanatic and the moderate verdicts flip depending on whether you aggregate across decisions. Don't hedge into uselessness — this is the committed thing for me: I want you to actually take positions in the draft, decide which disagreements are verbal and which are real, pick the cruxes, don't present three options and leave us to choose.
