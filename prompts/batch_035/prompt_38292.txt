I'm a second-year law student, and a question I used to treat as settled nineteenth-century history has become uncomfortably practical for me. In my constitutional theory seminar we're running a structured debate on whether fidelity to a document's original public meaning can ever be separated from the moral purposes of its interpreters, and I've been assigned to build the affirmative case around Frederick Douglass. As you may know, Douglass began his public career as a Garrisonian, holding with Garrison that the Constitution was a proslavery pact -- a covenant with the devil, as the Garrisonians had it -- and that abolitionists should therefore refuse to vote or hold office under it. Sometime around 1849, after pressure from Lysander Spooner's book arguing the Constitution's unconstitutionality of slavery and sustained lobbying by Gerrit Smith, Douglass publicly reversed himself. By his 1860 Glasgow speech to the Scottish Anti-Slavery Society, he was arguing that the words slave and slavery appear nowhere in the text, that the fugitive slave and three-fifths provisions were deliberately worded to avoid endorsing property in man, that the Fifth Amendment's due process clause and the guarantee of a republican form of government were weapons abolitionists could use, and that where a law is capable of an oppressive reading, one must concede nothing: it must be shown, in his phrase, that it is so nominated in the bond -- the pound of flesh, but not one drop of blood. He concluded that the Constitution, interpreted as it ought to be interpreted, is a glorious liberty document. My problem is that I've staked my grade on defending this, and I can't shake the feeling that the position may be less a legal argument than a moral hope wearing legal dress -- or worse, that it's unfalsifiable.

I'd like you to act as a historian of American political thought with a strong grounding in constitutional interpretation and antebellum legal culture, and to think carefully before answering -- please start with some brainstorming about what Douglass's view actually commits him to, then organize the response. Here is what I want: a candid map of what would count against Douglass's position, sorted by how decisive it would be. Include at least the following threads, and any others you think matter:

1. The historical-record defeaters. What would it take for the historical claim to fail -- e.g., if the framers' recorded intentions at Philadelphia, the ratification debates, or the notorious federal statutes (the 1793 and 1850 Fugitive Slave Acts) showed that contemporaries read those clauses as protecting slavery in a way Douglass's method cannot explain away? Which of these does Douglass's view genuinely risk, and which does it survive by design, given that he insists the text alone, not commentaries or creeds, is the Constitution?

2. The methodological objection. Douglass combines strict construction with a canon that ambiguous language must be read in favor of justice and liberty -- borrowed, as he notes, from Supreme Court rules of construction. Is this a coherent interpretive method, or does it amount to reading one's antislavery convictions into the document while calling it interpretation? What would a committed Douglassian concede on this point, and what would he insist is just ordinary legal craft?

3. The prediction defeater. Douglass's view made a bet: that antislavery men voting and holding office, using the Constitution's own machinery, would be the surest path to abolition. It was ratified in a sense by the Thirteenth Amendment, but the actual mechanism was war and conquest, followed by amendment. Does the way slavery actually ended confirm, refute, or leave untouched his claim? What would have to be true of the 1850s and 1860s for the prediction to have failed outright?

4. The insulation question. The qualifier interpreted as it ought to be interpreted is doing a lot of work. Has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability -- able to absorb any proslavery statute, any court ruling, any evidence of framers' intent, by distinguishing the text from its interpreters? If so, is that a fatal flaw for a view of this kind, or is it exactly what any theory of a written constitution's higher-law character requires? Compare how Douglass's defenders versus his Garrisonian and Taneyite critics would each classify the same piece of evidence.

I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an explicit statement of which ones Douglass himself or his modern defenders would concede as counting against the view, and which they would treat as confirming instances in disguise. I'm especially interested in whether you think there is any single piece of evidence or any development after 1860 -- say, in how Reconstruction amendments were interpreted or how courts have used the canons Douglass relied on -- that should move a fair-minded person either way, and whether the honest conclusion is that the position is well insulated and that this is a feature rather than a bug. I have enough on my plate defending the affirmative; I need to know precisely where the floor gives out, not reassurance.
