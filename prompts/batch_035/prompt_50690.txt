I'm building teaching material on escalation of commitment — dollar auction stuff, Shubik's game where the runner-up also has to pay. The standard line is that escalation is a bias: locally tempting, globally dumb, and a good Bayesian should cut losses. What I want for my students is the opposite case, to show that "one more dollar" reasoning can be locally airtight and still sink you.

Construct a thought experiment in which an escalating agent is fully rational at every single decision point — each bid is justified by expected value given everything the agent knows at that moment — and yet ends up worse off than a naive agent who never bids past their opening. Constraints: realistic setting (two firms bidding on a lease or licence, two departments fighting over a budget line — no casinos, no demons, no lab setup), the scenario itself no longer than one paragraph, and the rationality of escalating must not come from psychology — no spite, no face-saving, no status anxiety — only from how the evidence and payoffs are structured. The trick I have in mind (improve on it if you can) is common-value uncertainty: the prize's true worth isn't known to either party, and the rival's continued willingness to outbid is itself evidence the prize is worth more than you thought, so each update pulls your valuation upward. Each bidder's escalation is evidentially sound, and jointly they wreck themselves.

After the scenario, walk through the reasoning bid by bid — three bids should be enough — so the local rationality is explicit rather than asserted. Then tell me which structural feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if you remove it (my hunch is it collapses back into an ordinary dollar auction, where escalating really is a bias — but check that). Finally, give the strongest reply on behalf of someone who insists escalation of commitment is a bias even here. I suspect they'd accuse the bidders of double-counting the rival's bids, since those reflect sunk exposure as much as prize value, but you tell me. Begin with the scenario itself — no preamble, no meta-commentary, and no restating this ask.
