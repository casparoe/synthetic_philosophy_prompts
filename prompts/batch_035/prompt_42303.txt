Longtime reader of the rationality blogosphere here, trying to settle a running argument from my local meetup. For years I took the phrase about categories that carve reality at its joints as obviously meaningful: there is a way the world is structured, and better and worse taxonomies track that structure. My friend thinks this is mostly residual essentialism dressed up in predictive language -- that what we call a natural kind is just whatever grouping has proven useful for our current explanatory projects, and that the appearance of joint-carving dissolves once you look at actual scientific practice. Rather than trade intuitions, I'd like to see your credence, properly decomposed.

Here is the proposition, stated as precisely as I can manage: at least some categories used in the successful sciences (chemistry's elements, but also trickier cases like biological species, psychiatric diagnoses, or economic constructs like inflation) correspond to real, interest-independent structure in the world, in a sense strong enough that a hypothetical predictor with unlimited data and no stake in human convenience would still find it natural to use categories resembling ours. My credence is around 0.8; my friend's is around 0.3.

Please give me your own number, but the number is the least important part. Decompose it into the sub-questions the answer hangs on, with a rough probability and a line of reasoning for each. Some candidates, though I'm happy for you to add or replace terms: whether the cluster-structure picture (kinds as homeostatic property clusters rather than essences) is enough to make sense of joint-carving, or whether it quietly reintroduces conventionalism; whether there is a principled difference between kinds where the clustering is held together by a common underlying mechanism (gold, or water) and kinds whose unity is maintained by selection, social practice, or historical continuity (species, and arguably psychiatric categories); whether the argument from "real patterns" -- that a pattern is real if it supports compressed prediction better than alternatives -- secures mind-independence or merely relabels usefulness; and whether the whole question is confused because any equivalence-classing of the world involves some choice of what to count as a difference, so that "joints" can only ever be defined relative to a measure.

After the decomposition and the combination, tell me three more things. First, which term you are least confident in and what observation or argument would move it most -- ideally something that could actually turn up, like a category in some science that is wildly successful for prediction while being flagrantly gerrymandered, or a case where two predictive-optimal taxonomies have no natural way to be compared. Second, whether you think my 0.8 and my friend's 0.3 are really disagreeing about ontology at all, or about the weaker proposition that most scientific categories, taken one by one, are joint-carving -- since I'd grant that plenty of them aren't without giving up the strong claim. Third, whether the answer matters practically: if the conventionalist side is right, does anything change for how we should treat contested categories in medicine or taxonomy, or does that debate proceed on independent grounds either way? I'd rather see a defended decomposition that disagrees with my framing than a number that flatters it.
