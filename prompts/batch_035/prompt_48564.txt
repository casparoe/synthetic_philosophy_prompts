At my uncle's funeral a few weeks back, the celebrant tried to comfort us by saying that the eternity after his death will be for him just what the eternity before his birth was: a stretch of time he isn't in, and was never going to be in, and which couldn't have bothered him before he existed — a time when there was no him yet to have anything taken from. Since then I've noticed the same comparison all over the place, from grief books to a podcast host brushing off fear of death: if the billions of years before your birth caused you no distress, why should the billions after it? I've been chewing on it alone for a month and I can't settle it, and I'd like a careful audit rather than another round of condolences.

Here is where I've got. The comparison is being used to do two jobs at once. First, the celebrant's: to say death isn't bad for the one who dies, so our grief is about our loss, not his. Second, a harder one my friend pushes: that if endless nonexistence after forty is nothing bad, then dying at forty rather than eighty can't be bad either — the missing decades are just more of the void, and the void is harmless. I can see that if the symmetry holds, the second follows from the first. But this seems to prove far too much, and that makes me suspect the symmetry itself is shaky.

The asymmetry I keep circling is this: before my uncle existed, there was no particular person being shorted — of all the people who could have been born in 1890, none was ever anyone, so nothing was taken from anyone. After his death, there is a particular person it happened to, and it is his future, with his grandkids in it, that got cut off. My friend says this is word games: if nobody is around afterwards to miss anything, what work can being a particular person do? I honestly don't know. The only other difference I can find feels flimsier — that we tell lives as stories running forward, so a missing ending reads as damage and a missing beginning reads as nothing. But if the comfort rests on a habit of narration, it isn't much comfort.

So: take the before-birth / after-death comparison seriously as an argument rather than a condolence. Give me the mapping explicitly — the void before, the void after, the subject on each side, the harm if any, the thing that differs in each case — and then say where the mapping breaks. Tell me whether the difference I keep gesturing at, that after death there is someone it happened to and before birth there isn't, is a real disanalogy that does load-bearing work, or just my preference for the forward-running story. Then the question I actually care about: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the celebrant's conclusion — death is not bad for the one who dies — survive, and does my friend's harsher one survive with it? If the symmetry can't carry either, is there a better comparison that can, or does the whole style of reasoning from what the time before birth was like have to be abandoned?
