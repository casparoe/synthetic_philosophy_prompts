Last month something happened in my circle of friends that has turned into a running philosophical dispute at our dinners, and I have to admit I cannot referee it, though I badly want it settled. My closest friend Daniel, whom I have known for fifteen years, agreed to handle the renewal application for the small cooperative we all belong to, and he let it lapse — no reminders, no warning, just reassurances right up to the deadline that it was in hand. The consequence was a season of lost funding and a good deal of collateral damage to other people's plans. It then emerged that Daniel had been going through a severe depressive episode and had concealed it from all of us; he has since apologized with what struck everyone as complete sincerity, taken up treatment, and shouldered the practical cleanup. At dinner a week later, two of my friends, Maya and Ben, fell into an argument about whether the rest of us get to remain angry with him.

Maya opened by insisting that the wrong was real and that his suffering explains it rather than erasing it: on Strawson's picture from "Freedom and Resentment," she argued, to set aside blame because of his condition is to adopt the objective attitude — regarding him as a case to be managed rather than a participant to be addressed — and that would be a worse injury to him than the resentment itself. Ben replied that Strawson's own framework marks severe incapacity as an exemption, which removes the person as a target of the reactive attitudes entirely, so there is nothing for Maya's blame to fasten on; and he went further, claiming that blame is never a verdict issued from nowhere — the reactive attitudes are internal to relationships, so partiality in how we look at those close to us is constitutive of friendship rather than a bias to be corrected, and it cuts both ways, intensifying expectations even as it disposes us to interpret charitably. He added that since Daniel has apologized sincerely and taken responsibility, the exchange resentment exists to elicit has already occurred, so resentment that persists past that point has stopped being a moral response and become grudge-keeping. Maya would have none of it: if the rule is that the more someone matters to you, the less you may blame them, then warranted blame has become a function of sentiment with a philosophical cover — an ethics of favoritism — and there is no longer any fact of the matter about what Daniel deserves; either he deserves resentment or he does not, and friendship should not get a vote. Ben's last word was that her demand presupposes a scale of deserts that simply does not exist outside particular relationships with particular histories, and that the very sting Maya feels marks how much we had expected of Daniel, which was set by the relationship, not by any impersonal standard.

I want you to adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each step in the argument, say who is right about that specific point, briefly justify the rating, and flag where either of them overreached — for instance, whether Maya is right that Ben's position reduces to favoritism, and whether Ben is right that partiality can both amplify and dampen blame without anything arbitrary following from that. Then tell me what the disagreement is ultimately about — a factual question about what Daniel did and could do, or a deeper question about the point of blaming at all — and whether Strawson's distinction between excuses and exemptions, or anything else in his framework, actually settles the case or merely relocates the dispute. Finally, say who, if either, is more right overall, and what both of them may be missing about the situation with Daniel.
