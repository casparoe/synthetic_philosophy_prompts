My friends and I have been arguing about mind uploading ever since a sci-fi movie night got out of hand, and I've been trying to figure out what I actually believe by going case by case, on gut feeling alone. Here are my verdicts. Replace my neurons one at a time with silicon implants that do exactly the same job, spread over a couple of years while I live my life normally: I survive that, no question. Same procedure but done all in one surgery in a single afternoon, with general anesthesia: I *feel* like I survive that too, though the speed difference bothers me less than I would've guessed. Now the same neuron-by-neuron procedure, except each artificial neuron doesn't sit in my skull but wirelessly connects to a datacenter in Nevada where the actual processing happens, with signal latency kept low enough that my body keeps functioning fine: that feels like death, or at least like something much worse than the in-skull version, and I can't fully defend why. Moravec-style transfer where a robot surgeon copies each neuron then disassembles it, so at the end there's a complete functional duplicate and my old brain is gone, but the transition is smooth and conscious throughout: feels like survival when I think about it, but feels like death when I stop thinking about it, if that makes sense. Teleport-style scan-and-destroy where I'm dematerialized here and rebuilt from new atoms there, no gradual phase at all: definitely death, the duplicate is just somebody else with my memories. And the one I keep going back and forth on: replace each neuron with silicon that runs a thousand times *slower* than the biological one, with the whole system slowed down to match (so I experience everything at one-thousandth speed and can't notice from inside) -- did I survive, and is the answer different if I *can* notice, because my new neurons occasionally drop signals and I feel a kind of lag?

So: is there a single principle -- some view about personal identity, whether continuity of function, continuity of the physical stuff, continuity of consciousness, or whatever -- that captures all of these verdicts, or close to all? I have a tentative principle of my own that I'd like you to beat up on: "I survive whenever the replacement is incremental enough that there's no last moment at which the old me is gone and no first moment at which the new me is present" -- a kind of no-joint-in-the-process view. It handles the gradual vs. one-surgery cases and maybe the slowdown one, but I honestly don't know what it says about the Nevada datacenter version, since that's also perfectly gradual and my gut still screams death. And the scan-and-destroy case is a problem in the other direction: my gut says death, but I can't find the joint there either, unless the joint is the gap in physical continuity, which then threatens the one-surgery version I said I survive. Please tell me what principle (or maybe a couple of rival candidates) best fits my verdicts, where it forces me to give one up, and what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: my neurons replaced one at a time, but with the biology killed by a poison that's neutralized only at each replacement site -- so every neuron still alive at any moment is scheduled to die, and the replacement is always just barely ahead of the poison. Gut says survival, but I want to know if your principle agrees.
