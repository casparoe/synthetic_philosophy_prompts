Hey, so I need a philosophical audit and I'm hoping you can play philosopher of biology for me. A bit of background: I'm a retired physiologist (kidneys and salt balance, thirty-odd years at the bench), and since retiring I've fallen hard for philosophy of biology, Gould, Dawkins, Sober, the whole fight. I'm drafting a short piece for an online discussion group laying out where I actually stand on adaptation and its critics, and before I embarass myself in front of people who know the literature, I want to know whether my position is even internally consistant or whether I've cobbled together views that secretly hate each other. Here's where I've landed:

(1) At the phenotypic level I'm a pretty unrepentant adaptationist. In my whole career I never once came across a structure that didn't do its job well once you measured carefully enough; organs are near-optimal given the tradeoffs, and I take "this works well for something" as the default expectation for any trait worth explaining.

(2) At the same time, I think the Gould–Lewontin spandrels paper is one of the best things written in twentieth-century biology. Selection has to work with what development hands it, you can't rebuild the vertebrate body plan from scratch, and plenty of features are just structural byproducts of other features rather than adaptations in their own right.

(3) When a trait looks badly designed — the vertebrate retina built backwards, the recurrent laryngeal nerve taking the scenic route — my instinct is always to reach for developmental constraint as the explanation, not to hunt for some hidden adaptive function we haven't thought of yet.

(4) I'm fully sold on the neutral theory at the molecular level: most substitutions at synonymous sites and most of what's in noncoding DNA is drift, not selection.

(5) I think the gene's-eye view is basically right: adaptations are for the benefit of the genes that build them, organisms are vehicles, and Williams and Dawkins settled most of that argument decades ago.

(6) And, perhaps awkwardly, I have a soft spot for evolutionary psychology. The mind must have been shaped by selection in the Pleistocene just as much as the kidney was, and the reverse-engineering approach — look at a cognitive trait, ask what problem it solved — seems to me like the same legitimate strategy we use in anatomy and physiology, and I've said so in my draft.

So here's what I want from you. Don't lecture me about what each of these views is, I know the words, I want the audit. Go through the set and tell me which pairs or triples are genuinely in tension, which only look like they're, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than waving at all of them equally. For each genuine one, tell me what would have to give and roughly what it costs me to give it up. And then tell me, if I only revise one commitment, which single change cleans up the most mess. Please actually think it through before answering — maybe sketch out for yourself what each commitment commits me to elsewhere — because my instinct is that the problem, if there is one, is buried somewhere I'm not looking, and I'd rather hear the bad news now than in the comment section. One formatting request: write it as continuous prose, like a long email, no headings or bullet points or bold text, just paragraphs.
