I sit on the content committee of a regional history museum, and we are designing a permanent gallery that traces our city from its founding charter to the present day. Our lead curator wants the throughline to be organic. Societies, he argues, grow the way an organism does: the founding settlement is the seed, the early parish and guild structure the childhood, the industrial city the adolescence, and the present metropolis — with its universities, public health, and rights — the mature adult form. On his picture, earlier arrangements were not wrong so much as immature stages of what we became, and he invokes the old idea of the childhood of the human race to make it vivid. From this he draws three concrete curatorial conclusions: the gallery should be narrated as a story of maturation; the dead ends (the canal era that went nowhere, the failed cooperatives) should get only a paragraph each, since they were growth that never took; and the final room should celebrate the present as the organism's completed form. A second committee member keeps objecting that this is Whig history — though she is hazy about what exactly that charge amounts to — and wants instead an exhibit that treats each era as complete in itself, judging no one by what came after them.

You are a philosopher of history retained as a consultant to our committee. Before you write anything, think carefully and brainstorm: what exactly does the organic-growth analogy map onto what? Then produce a memo the committee can act on, with the following elements. First, make the correspondence table explicit — seed, childhood, adolescence, maturity, branching growth, the adult form as endpoint — and say what each item is doing in the historical story. Second, audit where the mapping breaks: organisms have a genome that fixes the mature form in advance, whereas nothing in a city's founding charter fixes its present shape; an organism's non-developmental branches simply never existed, whereas the canal era and the cooperatives existed and involved real people; maturity in organisms is a fact about life-stage, while immaturity as applied to a society smuggles in an evaluation; and the adult is terminal for an organism but would be just one more stage for a city that expects to outlive the exhibit. Crucially, separate the disanalogies that matter for the curatorial question from the ones that don't — some breaks in the analogy are irrelevant to whether his three conclusions follow, and you should say which. Third, give your considered verdict on whether each of the three curatorial conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some weaker story of progress survives even if the organic one does not. Fourth, assess the rival: is the treat-each-era-as-complete-in-itself approach a genuine corrective or just the same mistake run in reverse — pretending the exhibit's own vantage point is the one that doesn't exist? Finally, if neither analogy should carry the gallery, propose a better one — say, something Darwinian rather than developmental, with selection and path dependence rather than maturation — and spell out what narrative choices that replacement analogy would and would not license. Write for an intelligent committee with no philosophy background: no jargon left unexplained, and no name-dropping in place of an argument.
