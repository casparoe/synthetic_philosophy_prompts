I'm in an upper-level seminar on political obligation, and we've just finished pairing the principle of fair play with Rawls's natural duty of justice as the two live candidates for grounding a duty to obey the law. My seminar partner keeps insisting that although the two accounts disagree in the abstract, they're extensionally equivalent in practice: any case where one generates an obligation, the other does too, so the choice between them is idle. I want to prove her wrong with a constructed case, but the obvious constructions all run in one direction, so I'm looking for the harder one.

Construct a thought experiment in which the fair-play principle and the natural duty of justice deliver opposite verdicts on a single act of civil disobedience — and, specifically, the direction that cuts against type: fair play, which is usually thought to be the more demanding theory about compliance, should end up permitting or at least not condemning the disobedience, while the natural duty of justice should condemn it. Constraints: the setting should be realistic and contemporary (a tax, a registration scheme, a licensing regime, something like that — nothing science-fictional); the scenario itself should be one paragraph; the act must count as civil disobedience in the classic sense — conscientious, public, nonviolent, with the agent prepared to accept the legal penalty — so the disagreement is about obligation and not about the act being secretly sabotage or ordinary crime; and the society should be close to just, so the natural duty has genuine traction rather than the case being settled trivially by setting it in a tyranny. Note, in constructing it, that you can't simply stipulate the verdicts: the facts described have to actually generate them once each theory is applied.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specification. Walk through the reasoning of each theory to its verdict, step by step; identify the single structural feature of the case that is doing the work; and say what happens to each verdict if that feature is varied or removed (for instance, if some compliance rate or distributional fact in your scenario crossed a threshold). Then give the strongest available reply on behalf of whichever theory comes out worse — for the natural-duty theorist, it's worth considering whether the particularity problem (the question of why a general duty to support just institutions binds me to obey this state's laws in particular) can be deployed to blunt your case rather than sharpen it. Finally, tell me where a consent theorist would come down in your scenario, and whether that pushes toward my seminar partner's equivalence thesis or away from it.
