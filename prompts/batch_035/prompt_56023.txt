I am a novelist who has spent four years on a book in which a single sentence — "The lamp stood on the desk between the inkwell and the revolver" — is meant to bear the entire weight of the plot, and somewhere in the revising I became obsessed with a question I later discovered has a name in metaphysics: what makes that sentence the report of one fact rather than a heap of words? I host a small conversation series that pairs fiction writers with analytic philosophers, and our next guest is a metaphysician who defends an ontology of states of affairs: the world genuinely comes pre-divided into facts, each fact has constituents — particulars, universals, relations — and he holds that Bradley's regress is stopped because the tie binding a universal to its particulars is not itself a constituent standing in need of further binding. Please draft eight to ten interview questions for him, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give. I want at least one question pressing whether the exemption of the tie from constituent treatment is principled or merely what the theory needs to survive, with a follow-up keyed to his probable answer that the tie is the universal's manner of occurring in the fact rather than an item over and above it; one question on whether the regress simply reappears one level up when facts are combined — what, after all, unites the constituents of a conjunctive fact; one on the unity of the false, since if unity is conferred by facts, it is unclear what holds together the constituents of a sentence describing a way things merely might have been; one confronting him with the Fregean alternative that combination is explained by the incompleteness of concepts rather than by metaphysical ties, and asking what would decide between the two pictures; one putting to him the dissolving move that unity belongs to sentences and not to the world at all, so that my lamp-sentence is one thing because I wrote it with a verb, not because reality is glued together; and one question of my own devising: must the world's carving into facts match the sentences available to a novelist, and what should he say about a pair of my sentences — say, one reporting that she forgave him and one that he was forgiven by her — that seem to carve the same stretch of events differently, or about two sentences that carve it identically while telling the reader quite different things? Where he is inclined to invoke Bradley's own idealist conclusion as a reductio, press on whether he is actually entitled to it. Keep the questions literate but technically exact: he is a patient explainer, and I have no interest in handing him softballs.
