I write for a general-interest magazine, mostly on a science and ideas beat, and something I keep tripping over in my own copy is how we talk about things lasting through time — and I want to know whether philosophy has a name for the specific wrinkle. Here's the wrinkle. Last year I did a piece about a cathedral that burned and was rebuilt, and without thinking I wrote both that the cathedral has stood on that site for 900 years and, two paragraphs later, that nothing of the original building survives above the foundations. Both sentences felt fine to my editor, both felt fine to me, and their in tension in a way I can't quite pin down: if nothing original survives, in what sense has the *same* cathedral stood there? Once you start looking, the same switch shows up everywhere. A profile will say a company traces its history to 1904 right after describing how three mergers replaced its management, workforce and product line — and a source quoted in the very same story will say the outfit he joined doesn't exist anymore, which also seems true. Obituaries do it with people: "she was never the same after the stroke," said as a literal claim and not just a turn of phrase.

The part I'm actually curious about is not the Ship of Theseus bit — I know that label, everyone knows that label, and it's usually told as a puzzle about how much replacement one object can absorb. What I keep noticing is different: it's that we seem to silently switch between two ways of picking the thing out, depending on which features the sentence needs. Sometimes the thing is whatever thread connects the stages — the corporation chartered in 1904, the building on that plot, the body with that name — and sometimes it's a cluster of characteristics, the architecture, the research culture, the personality, and ordinary language lets us call both of them "the same thing" without ever flagging that a switch happened. So: is there an established concept or literature for this two-track way of talking about persistence? If the nearest thing out there is just the Theseus puzzle again, tell me that straight, and explain how the two come apart. And if there's no standard name, coin one and define it precisely enough that I can look at a given sentence in a piece and say which track the writer is on — because right now I'm printing sentences that are true on one reading and false on the other, and I'd like to at least know which is which.
