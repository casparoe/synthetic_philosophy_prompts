I teach the game theory unit in our epistemic logic course, and my seminar students have cooked up an argument I can't get out of my head. It seems to show that common knowledge of rationality destroys itself in any sequential game with a known end. Setup: an ordinary centipede game — two players alternate, at each node the mover can either take (ending the game, taker gets the larger slice) or pass (the pot doubles), and the tree has a last node. Payoffs and the whole structure are common knowledge, and it's common knowledge that both players are rational in the plain expected-utility sense. Their argument:

(1) At the last node, the rational move is take — there's no future to invest in — and this is common knowledge.
(2) So at the second-to-last node, passing just hands the pot to an opponent who will certainly take; so take. Repeat up the tree: at every node, take.
(3) So, with common knowledge of rationality, player 1 takes at the first node.
(4) But now suppose we somehow observe player 1 pass at node 1. Player 2 learns something: either player 1 slipped, or player 1 is gambling that a pass resets the game. On either reading, player 2 can now rationally pass at node 2 — banking on continued passing toward the much bigger pot — and both walk away better off than take-at-node-1 ever gave them.
(5) Player 1 can run all of that at node 1: passing beats taking in expectation.
(6) So common knowledge of rationality entails that the backward-induction move is irrational — the assumption refutes itself. Hence no centipede game between rational players can ever actually start with common knowlege of rationality.

Step (6) is where they land, and it smells wrong to me — surely two players who really are rational and really do know it of each other can sit down at this game. But I keep staring at (4) and (5) and can't name the exact slippage, and one of my students insists the whole thing is valid and the real lesson is that common knowledge of rationality can't survive the first off-path move, whatever that's supposed to mean. Don't cite literature at me, just argue it through.

Give me this in sections, in this order: "The guilty step" (short — name the step and the exact slide or equivocation involved, or say the argument is valid and a premise has to go), "Why it looked fine" (one paragraph), "The price" (one paragraph — what rejecting that step costs, e.g. for backward induction in general or for what players are entitled to infer off the equilibrium path), and "Seminar notes" (a plain-language version I can actually say out loud to the students, about three sentences).
