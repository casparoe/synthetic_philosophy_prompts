I've been keeping a list of my gut verdicts on these Omega-style decision problems, and I want to know whether they add up to a coherent decision theory or whether some of them have to go. One-box in Newcomb's problem: obviously. Pay in Parfit's hitchhiker, after being safely delivered to town: yes, I'd hand over the money. Pay Omega the $100 in counterfactual mugging, where the coin landed tails and she now asks for $100, having promised on heads to pay me $10,000 if and only if she predicted I'd pay in the tails branch: yes, though with less confidence than in the hitchhiker case. Refuse in XOR blackmail, where the blackmailer's letter reaches me if and only if either I have a stain on my shirt or I would refuse to pay, so that receiving the letter is strong evidence I'm clean: refuse, and this feels clear-cut to me. But now the harder ones. If Omega's coin is heavily biased so the tails branch occurs 999 times out of 1000, paying still feels right, though less so; if the coin is biased 999 out of 1000 toward heads, so that paying almost certainly sacrifices $100 for the sake of a nearly nonexistent windfall branch, my gut says don't pay — and I notice I can't say exactly where along that spectrum my verdict flips, or whether flipping at all is consistent with what I said about the fair coin. And if Omega is only a 60%-reliable predictor rather than a perfect one, paying in the mugging starts to feel like a mistake even with a fair coin, though I'd still pay the hitchhiker. What I'd like is a principle — or a few candidates — stated precisely enough to give verdicts on all of these, including an explanation of why refusing XOR blackmail and paying in counterfactual mugging, which look structurally similar since both involve honoring a policy in a branch where the payoff is already gone, come out differently in my intuitions. Please tell me what the best principle says about the biased-coin cases and the unreliable-predictor case, and be honest about which of my verdicts it has to overturn.
