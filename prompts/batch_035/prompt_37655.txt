Not sure if this has a name, but it keeps showing up and its bugging me. I'm a programmer, took a bit of logic in undergrad, so forgive me if I'm describing something everyone learns in week one and calling it a discovery.

Here's the shape. Take some checker — something that verifies other systems. It can verify systems weaker than itself, that's routine, happens all day. But it cannot verify anything as strong as itself. Not just stronger systems, which would be obvious — I mean it can't verify its own equal, not even an exact copy of itself with different guts. And the failure isn't "too expensive" or "nobody's written the tool yet," it looks structural: to certify the equal-strength system you'd have to show that its rules only ever output true things, and since those rules include your rules, you'd be showing that about yourself, and there's apparently a theorem that a system of this kind can never establish that about itself. The workaround everyone uses is to bring in a stronger system from outside, but then nothing checks THAT one, so you've just moved the wall rather than shown the wall is special to you.

Where I've hit it: proof assistants, which will happily check proofs in logics weaker than what they use internally but where you can't prove, inside one, that it itself is OK — you end up just trusting it as a starting point; a person re-checking their own arithmetic with the same habits that produced the error in the first place; and, the one that got me thinking, designs for AI systems that rewrite themselves, where the improved version is supposed to be verified by the original, and everyone quietly assumes that works.

There's a fourth thing, and honestly this is the part I most want explained. I once half-read that if a system COULD prove "whatever my equal proves is true," the upshot wouldn't be that it gets to lean on the equal's work — the upshot would be that it already believes everything the equal could ever prove, so the certificate never actually extends its reach. If that's a real theorem and not something I dreamed, it seems like the deepest part of the wall, and I can't reconstruct the argument at all.

So: does this configuration have a name, is there actual literature, and can you tell me which of my four observations are the same theorem and which are seperate facts that just happen to live near each other? A bare name won't satisfy me — I want to know exactly what gets proved, what's merely postulated when people "trust" the equal system anyway, and where the wall stops (wether it also applies to checkers that work probabilistically, or only to ones that prove things).
