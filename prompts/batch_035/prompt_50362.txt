I'm a PhD student in metaphysics, and something from our seminar on persistence has been bugging me for weeks. The endurantist slogan, you know the one, that objects are 'wholly present' whenever they exist, got thrown around constantly. My supervisor used it to state the position, a visiting speaker used it as if it were a substantive thesis that perdurantists must deny, and then at some point someone in the discussion claimed it's basically a definition of endurantism, so of course perdurantists deny it, it's true by stipulation. So the same phrase was doing descriptive, argumentative, and stipulative work within about ninety minutes, and nobody seemed to notice the slide.

Here's roughly where I want help. A friend who works on mereology swears the phrase is ambiguous between at least two things: something mereological, like no proper part of the thing is missing at any time it exists, versus something about location or occupation, like the thing doesn't have other parts located elsewhere (at other times). And those come apart, don't they? A perdurantist can happily say my temporal parts' spatial parts are all present at each time, so in the mereological reading restricted to a time they might even agree with the slogan, whereas on the locational reading the whole point of temporal parts is that you do have parts elsewhere. There's also, I suspect, a deflationary reading on which 'wholly present' just means 'not identical to anything having you as a proper temporal part', or something that makes the slogan a mere label for the position rather than a claim inside it. And I vaguely remember someone arguing the slogan, once precisified, is either false or trivial, which would be embarrassing for how much work it's made to do.

What I'd like is a careful disambiguation. Lay out the distinct precise claims that could be hiding in 'wholly present', from the platitudinous to the genuinely substantive, and for each say whether it's true, defensible but contested, or plain false, and crucially whether the perdurantist actually denies it — I want to know which readings make the endurantism/perdurantism clash a real disagreement and which make it look worryingly verbal. Along the way, please tell me which reading the endurantist needs when they reply to the argument from temporary intrinsics (the one where the bent and straight statuses of a wire, or my sitting and standing, seem to require relativising something or other), because I have a suspicion the slogan only earns its keep there if it's read in one specific way, and the deflationary readings leave the endurantist without a reply. Write it all as continuous prose, like a long email to a confused grad student, no headings or bullet points or anything, and please use British English throughout. Don't bother with citations or telling me who said what in which paper, I can chase the literature myself afterwards; what I need is the logical geography.
