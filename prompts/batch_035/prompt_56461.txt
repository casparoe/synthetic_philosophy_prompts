After a long argument with my friend, a palliative care nurse, I need to draft something for work, and I want to get the framing right. Her position in our argument: an advance directive written by a healthy sixty-year-old should not bind the woman she has become with advanced dementia — a woman who is, in every observable way, content and wants to keep living — because the person the directive was meant to protect no longer exists to be protected. My position: if she's right, then the entire practice of advance directives is a fraud we perpetrate on patients — we take their signature while they're competent and discount it the moment they aren't. We ran out of wine before either of us could say where the line actually falls.

The task: I sit on the ethics consultation service at a mid-sized hospital, and we're getting more consults of exactly this shape — a directive that conflicts with the patient's current, imperfectly-capacitated wishes. Please write a decision procedure our consult team can actually run in a single thirty-minute case discussion: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure stops with "honor the directive" or "honor the current wish," and the points where it must hand the question back to humans because what remains is a value judgment, not a factual one — for instance, how much of the earlier self must survive for her choices to remain hers rather than ours, or what weight the family's interests deserve. Ground it in the actual philosophical distinctions rather than in legal compliance: precedent autonomy, the difference between a directive that expresses enduring values and one that merely records a bet about circumstances, and what follows when the directive's author could not have anticipated the situation she's now in. Then run the procedure on three cases: a contented woman with moderate dementia whose decades-old directive refuses antibiotics for any life-threatening infection; a man whose directive refuses dialysis, written during a depressive episode that has since been successfully treated; and a still-competent patient who tells her family she has changed her mind about refusing surgery but won't update the document because revising it feels like tempting fate. At least one of these should come out the way my friend would call monstrous or I would call a betrayal — say which, and why the procedure yields it anyway. Close by stating plainly what the procedure cannot settle, so the team knows the difference between applying it and hiding behind it.
