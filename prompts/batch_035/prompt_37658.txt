I run onboarding for new hires at an AI safety organization, and next week I'm leading a two-hour working session for a mixed group — a couple of philosophers, several ML engineers, a policy person or two — on what it would actually take to define honesty and deception for AI systems. Everyone will arrive with the folk definition inherited from the human case: deception basically means lying, lying means asserting something false, and it presupposes an intentional deceiver. I don't want you to give me the session's content; I want a plan for it.

Specifically: (1) What has to be put in place before any definition can even be discussed — e.g., the point that current models' outputs may not be the same kind of speech act as a human assertion, and the choice between defining honesty as a property of individual outputs versus a disposition of the system across contexts — and in what order, since some of these presuppose each other? (2) Which single carrying example should anchor the whole session — something like a model that gives technically true but engineered-to-mislead answers, or a sycophantic model that tells users what they seem to want to hear — and what properties the right example must have (it should split the naive definitions apart rather than merely violate one) and which it must lack (e.g., it shouldn't turn on exotic capability claims we can't verify)? (3) Which misconceptions should I expect, in roughly the order the audience will raise them — I anticipate "deception requires intent, so applying it to current models is a category error" from the philosophers and "honesty just means never outputting anything false" from the engineers, but tell me what else is likely — and at exactly which point in the sequence each should be headed off? (4) What should I openly refuse to settle at this level — the metaphysics of machine intent, whether models have beliefs, whether training counts as a liar's intent — and how to fence those off without the session dissolving into them? (5) And how should I close with a check that distinguishes people who can apply the working definition to a genuinely fresh case (say, a model that understates its confidence to avoid disappointing a user, or one that games an eval it has inferred is running) from people who can merely recite it back to me?
