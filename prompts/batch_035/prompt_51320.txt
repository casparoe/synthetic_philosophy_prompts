I'm taking a philosophy of mind course and we just covered Dennett's heterophenomenology from Consciousness Explained, and I keep thinking it would've been handy around 1900 when experimental introspection imploded. Since you can't look things up, here's the episode as I understand it, in case the details are fuzzy for you: Wundt's Leipzig lab made trained introspection of one's own conscious states the core method of psychology, and Titchener at Cornell pushed it furthest, trying to catalogue the basic elements of experience. Then Külpe's Würzburg school reported that some thinking seems to have no sensory or imaginal content at all -- 'imageless thought' -- and Titchener shot back that they were just failing to notice faint images, which he claimed to have resolved the Würzburg findings into sensations, feelings and images in his own introspective studies. Wundt himself attacked the Würzburgers' retrospective reports as untrustworthy. Neither side could see inside the other's head, the dispute could not be settled by the very method it was about, and confidence in introspection collapsed, which paved the way for Watson's behaviourism, which just banned the question.

What I want is an analysis of the imageless thought controversy as a case study for heterophenomenology. Run it in both directions, please. First: what does the method say the two camps were actually doing when they introspected and disagreed? On Dennett's picture, subjects' reports are just more data -- texts to be interpreted, subject to all the ordinary distorting machinery of expectation, training and memory -- so does the method dissolve the dispute (both labs were producing reports shaped by their theories, and the reports are data about the observers, not windows), or merely relocate it? Second: what does the episode show about the theory? You could argue it vindicates it -- this is precisely the mess heterophenomenology was built for, and notice that Titchener's actual reply to the Würzburgers (you missed faint images because your training didn't equip you to catch them) already sounds like a heterophenomenological hypothesis about how expectations shape reports. But there's a wrinkle I cant get straight: if trained observers' reports can be explained away wholesale like that, what exactly is left for the method to study, and wasn't the behaviourists' move -- declare the whole data source hopeless -- in some tension with Dennett's insistence that the reports remain usable evidence?

So tell me where the theory genuinely illuminates this episode, where it's silent and I'd be forcing it to speak, and whether the outcome of the whole affair is best read as a confirmation of heterophenomenology avant la lettre, an anomaly for it, or something it never anticipated. For what its worth, my lecturer thinks the controversy shows introspective reports can be theory-laden past the point of usefulness, but I suspect Dennett would say theory-laden data is just... data. Feel free to weigh in on that too.
