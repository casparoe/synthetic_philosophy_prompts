As a journalist who writes a regular column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, I've become fascinated by a debate in moral philosophy that my readers would recognize instantly from ordinary life, even though they've never heard the technical terms for it: whether lying and merely misleading are morally different. Telling someone something false outright, versus saying something true that you fully expect them to take the wrong way — most people have a strong intuition that these differ, and an equally strong disagreement about which one is worse. For our next interactive feature, I'd like you to write a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz of eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete case with two or three answer options, followed by a scoring key.

The cases should be varied and recognizably real-world: the household employee who truthfully answers "he is not at home" while the boss hides upstairs; the lawyer or press officer who issues a carefully drafted non-denial that reads as a denial; the doctor who, out of compassion, lets a family draw an overly hopeful conclusion from accurate facts; the friend asked about a surprise party who says something literally true to keep the secret; a wartime or emergency case where outright falsehood would save a life but evasion would not. Crucially, no question may just ask "do you think misleading is as bad as lying?" — the reader must be sorted by what they say about cases, not by which label they find congenial. The answer options should be phrased as reactions to the case ("acceptable," "worse than a lie," "only wrong if X," and so on), not as position names.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto the main positions in this literature: the Kantian-inflected view that lying is categorically worse because it asserts what one believes false, while misleading lets the hearer reach the false belief through their own inference; the moral-equivalence view on which what matters is intended deception, not the mechanism; the Gricean-flavored view that conversation operates on shared norms which misleading corrodes in a sneakier way; and the consequentialist view that collapses the distinction except instrumentally. Please note any combination of answers that corresponds to a position nobody really defends, with one sentence on whether that's because the view is incoherent or merely unexplored. Finally, be honest about what the quiz cannot separate: for instance, whether certain answer patterns distinguish "lying is worse" from "misleading about one's own beliefs is a special aggravated case," and flag that ambiguity rather than papering over it. Two to three paragraphs of framing notes for me as the editor, on what the quiz reveals and where it is genuinely inconclusive, would round the piece out nicely.
