I've been reading about the Nordic model on and off for a couple years, and I'm trying to figure out what I actually believe about whether it can be exported, going by my gut reactions to specific cases. When a rich country with low social trust (I'm thinking France, say, or the US) tries to adopt Nordic-style high taxes and generous universal welfare, I'm pessimistic -- it seems to slide into either backlash or fiscal trouble. When a poor country with high social trust tries it, like post-independence Tanzania's villages or Kerala's welfare state, I'm way more sympathetic even though they stayed poor, and I don't count their poverty as evidence the model failed. When Japan adopted bits of Danish-style "flexicurity" -- less job protection, stronger unemployment insurance and retraining -- it seemed to work about as well as you'd expect, which is to say decently, even though Japan isn't culturally Nordic at all. When oil states like Norway use a sovereign wealth fund it looks great, but when Venezuela does functionally the same thing (resource revenue funding universal programs) it's a disaster, and my gut says the difference isn't just oil prices or management. And when a small homogeneous US state like Vermont manages Nordic-ish policies locally, I count it as a success of the model, but when someone says "see, that proves it's about smallness and homogeneity, not institutions," I resist that conclusion.

The tension I keep hitting: my verdicts seem to track something like "social trust is the precondition, and you can't import trust," but then Japan case should have failed, and it didn't, and also Kerala-style cases where the trust was there but prosperity never came make me unsure whether trust is doing the work or whether it's really something else entirely, like state capacity or just not having oil. And there's a deeper puzzle: if the precondition for the Nordic model is basically "already be like the Nordics," then saying the model "can be exported" is nearly incoherent, but my gut keeps saying some exports work.

Is there a single principle behind all these verdicts -- maybe something about what exactly needs to be in place before high-tax universalism doesn't collapse, or maybe a distinction between exporting the institutions vs. exporting the political bargain behind them? What does your best candidate principle say about a case I haven't mentioned, like South Korea or Poland trying to move in that direction now? And be honest about which of my verdicts the principle can't save -- I suspect the Vermont one and maybe the Venezuela one are the shaky ones, but I'd like you to tell me which ones really have to go if I want a clean story.
