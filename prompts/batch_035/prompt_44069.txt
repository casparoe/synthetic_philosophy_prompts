I am a novelist, and my current book turns on a scene I have been circling for two years: a small museum committee, charged with redisplaying its collection, must draft a label for a portrait of a founder whose record contains both genuine civic achievement and conduct we now find abominable — think Jefferson's slaveholding, or Churchill and the Bengal famine, though my character is fictional and the committee is split between two members who have argued past each other for the whole novel. I want the document those two committee members ought to have produced, so that the scene can dramatize the moment they finally speak precisely instead of loudly.

Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps: the defender of the "man of his time" defence — who holds that judging historical figures by standards unavailable in their era is a kind of moral anachronism, and that condemnation should be tempered accordingly — and the critic, who holds that some acts were wrong by standards the figures themselves had access to, and that the defence generalizes far beyond the cases where it could possibly work. I want it to cover the judging of real figures of the Jefferson/Churchill/Columbus sort, not my fictional one.

The statement should contain, in this order: a numbered section of claims both camps can sign, including claims about what we owe the dead, what commemoration is, and what the historical record supports about the moral disagreement these figures themselves lived with; then a disagreements table in which each remaining dispute is stated twice, once in wording the defender would accept and once in wording the critic would accept, with each side's formulations placed side by side so the reader can see whether they disagree or merely talk differently; then the cruxes — I suspect one is whether a person's own-era sincere belief functions as an excusing condition or merely as an explaining condition, another is whether honoring a person and remembering a person are separable practices, and a third is what to do with figures who demonstrably knew better, like a slaveholder who wrote against slavery — and for each crux, at least one concrete historical case, test, or argument both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case written down in advance, so that the novel can show later who was right. If any part of their dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, the statement should say which part and why, and the drafting should let both committee members keep their dignity.
