I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, and for the past two terms our chance-and-laws reading group has watched two of its members argue past each other: one a committed Lewisian Humean, who holds that objective chance is nothing over and above what figures in the best systematisation of the total history of events; the other a propensity theorist, who holds that chances are single-case dispositions of experimental set-ups, which can exist and take a value even in worlds whose actual outcomes run wildly against them. We have finally persuaded them to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and I have been tasked with drafting the joint statement. Please write that draft for me.

Assume the following shared background. Both parties accept the Principal Principle in some form — roughly, the injunction that a rational agent's credence in an outcome, conditional on the hypothesis that its objective chance is p (together with admissible further information), should be p. Both also concede that the principle generates pressure for their own side. The Humean faces the undermining problem: on a best-systems view, a hypothesis about the chances just is a hypothesis about the frequency-pattern of the very events one is betting on, so conditionalising on it seems to demand credences that the pattern itself would not support — Lewis conceded this was a bug, and his attempted repair (a reformulated principle tying credence to the chance-predicting patterns rather than to chance itself) strikes the propensitist as an ad hoc rescue. The propensity theorist, meanwhile, faces Humphreys' paradox (propensities, as causal tendencies, do not appear to obey the symmetry between a probability and its inverse) and the charge that single-case propensities are epiphenomenal: nothing in a rational agent's epistemology strictly needs them.

The deliverable should have the following sections, in this order:

1. Shared commitments (roughly 400 words): a numbered list of claims about what any theory of objective chance owes us — its evidential role, its explanatory ambitions, how it should behave in deterministic worlds, and what the Principal Principle gets right — that both parties can sign without flinching.

2. The disagreement, stated twice (roughly 350 words, presented as a table): the substantive points where they part ways, each stated twice — once in wording the Humean would accept as faithful to their view, once in wording the propensitist would accept as faithful to theirs. Cover at least: what objective chances are; the epistemic status of the Principal Principle; the undermining problem; Humphreys' paradox; and what each side would count as fatal to its own position.

3. Cruxes and registered predictions (roughly 600 words): identify three or four cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. For each, describe a case, argument, or thought experiment that both parties agree bears on it (feel free to construct new toy cases, e.g. worlds with small finite histories, or unrepeatable trials), and record in advance what each side predicts about it, phrased so that the prediction is risky — i.e. it could come out wrong for them.

4. Verbal or substantive (roughly 250 words): a jointly signed assessment of whether any part of the residual dispute is merely verbal — e.g. whether the two camps mean the same thing by chance at all — and if so, which part and why, without either side conceding more than the assessment warrants.

The whole document should be roughly 1,500–1,800 words and written in a register two professional philosophers could genuinely put their names to: precise, unslanted, and free of rhetorical victories for either side. Please write it in British English throughout.
