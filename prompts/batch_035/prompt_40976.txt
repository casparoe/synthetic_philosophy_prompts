I've followed the LessWrong-adjacent discussions of akrasia for years, and they keep gesturing at George Ainslie's picoeconomics without ever laying out the actual mechanism, so I'd like a careful explanation of it. Specifically: why does hyperbolic (as opposed to exponential) discounting turn a single person's choices over time into something like a repeated prisoner's dilemma among successive selves, so that "one person with inconsistent preferences" gets redescribed as "many partial agents in a bargaining problem"? What role do personal rules play — why does Ainslie think a rule needs a bright line rather than a mere intention to be moderate, and why is a single lapse supposed to be so much worse than its local cost, given that it functions as evidence about one's own future choices? And finally, where does all this leave Socrates' argument in the Protagoras that akrasia is impossible and being "overcome by pleasure" is really ignorance? It strikes me that Ainslie's picture could be read either way: as vindicating Socrates, since a lapse is a failure of a kind of self-knowledge (a mistaken prediction about what one's future self will do), or as refuting him, since the failure is now one of coordination rather than cognition. Please explain the mechanism and then adjudicate that reading.
