Been making a list of when I defer to stated chances and when I don't, and I'd like to know whether there's a principle under my gut calls, because from here it looks like I'm just being inconsistent.

The cases and my verdicts:

- A mint-made coin. The mint's specs say it's evenly weighted. My credence that the next flip is heads: 50%. Defer completely.
- Same coin, but I watched it land heads two minutes ago. The chance of heads on that flip was still 50%, yet my credence that it landed heads is now basically 1. Obviously. So "defer to chance" can't be unconditional.
- A bookmaker posts even odds on a coin flip. I know bookmakers shade their numbers to build in a margin. I don't take the posted odds as the chance, but I do treat them as evidence about the chance.
- Weather forecaster says 30% chance of rain. I've noticed this particular forecaster slightly overpredicts rain, so I put my credence a bit under 30% — not by much. Partial deference.
- Roulette in a casino I have no reason to distrust: posted chance of any particular number is 1 in 38. Defer.
- Then I read a credible report that this casino has rigged wheels in the past, but the report doesn't say which wheels or in which direction. I can't work out what to do. Raising my credence in every number can't be right (they'd sum past 1), and lowering it in every number can't be right either, so maybe full deference to the posted 1/38 is correct after all? This is the one I keep going back and forth on.
- A physicist tells me the chance a given atom of some isotope decays within the hour is such-and-such. I defer flat-out, even though I can't check the calculation myself. Meanwhile, when my brother-in-law — decent amateur record with dice, for what it's worth — tells me the die at the craps table is biased toward sixes, I nudge my credence up but nowhere near where he puts it.

So the tension I can see: with the physicist I defer fully to unverifiable testimony, with the brother-in-law I discount heavily, with the rain forecaster I discount slightly. Is the difference just track record, or is there something structural about the kind of claim? And the rigged-casino case seems to cut against the physicist case in some way I can't put my finger on.

My own stab at a principle: defer to a stated chance unless your evidence also bears on whether the chance really is as stated, or on how the event turned out. But that gets the physicist case wrong, since his say-so obviously bears on the chance in some sense, and I still defer completely. I gather there's a named thing for this kind of deference — the Principal Principle — with some clause about which evidence is "admissible," but I've never seen a clean statement of what makes evidence admissible or not, and my cases feel like they're circling exactly that hole.

So, questions: is there a principle that captures all or most of my verdicts, and can it be stated without the admissibility clause just listing the cases? What does it say about a case I haven't given — for instance, a fully deterministic universe where somebody hands me "chance" numbers anyway: defer or not? And which of my verdicts would you overturn? Please don't rescue all of them by bolting on clauses, one per case — if my pattern is just incoherent, or if two of my verdicts can't be had together at any price, say that straight. Same if the whole framing assumes chances are a real thing out there to defer to, and that assumption is doing more work than it should — I'd want to hear that rather than a polite principle built on sand.
