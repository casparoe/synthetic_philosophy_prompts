I've been trying to work out when an act has moral worth — done for the right reason, not just the right thing done by accident or for dodgy reasons. I've collected my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and I'd like to know if theres a principle that captures them, or whether some of them just have to go.

My verdicts:

1. A shopkeeper gives a tourist the correct change when the tourist could never have noticed being short-changed, and he does it purely because he thinks honesty is required — no pleasure in it at all. Moral worth: clearly yes.

2. Same shopkeeper, but the only reason is that there's a camera over the till. No moral worth. (These two are basically fixed for me; I'm treating them as the anchor.)

3. A passer-by jumps into a canal to save a drowning kid before any thought crosses her mind — pure alarm, no moment of "this is my duty". I still want to say her act has moral worth.

4. Someone donates to disaster relief because the appeal filled him with pity; he'd never phrase it in terms of obligation. Moral worth: yes, I think, and not a watered-down version either.

5. Someone else donates the same amount because she works out that this is what morality requires, while feeling nothing and quietly resenting it. Moral worth: yes — and yet Kant, as I understand him, wants to say this case has the *most* worth of all, and I can't make myself go that far. But I also can't see why it would have less worth than the pity case. So 4 and 5 sit awkwardly together for me.

6. A doctor treats a patient with meticulous care, but only because the illness is rare and she finds it a fascinating puzzle — the moment the case turns routine she'd lose all interest and hand it off. She never does wrong by the patient, but I'm reluctant to say her treating him has moral worth. Something is missing there, even though the pity donor in 4 also never explicitly thought about the value at stake...

7. A man tells an awkward truth about a colleague's performance in a meeting when a soft answer was available. But his reason is that he enjoys the colleague's discomfort — truth-telling as a weapon. No moral worth, even though honesty is exactly what he did. This one and 4 are the pair I can't reconcile: both are inclination-driven, neither involves any explicit thought of duty, but my gut splits them.

8. The one I keep going back and forth on: a woman lies to officials to stop someone being deported, while sincerely believing that lying is wrong and that she's doing wrong — she feels guilty, does it anyway out of a plain inability to watch the person be taken, and says afterwards that she did wrong. Right act (I'll grant that), done while believing it's wrong, from something that isn't conviction. Moral worth or none?

My own first stab at a principle was: an act has moral worth when it's done for a reason that tracks whatever actually makes the act right — so spite fails because it doesnt track why honesty is right, and pity succeeds because it tracks the suffering, which is part of what makes relief-giving right. But I can already see trouble: does alarm in case 3 track the drowning? And does the duty-calculator in case 5 track anything, or is she just complying? Test that stab against my cases, repair it or replace it, and tell me: exactly what the resulting principle says, which clause each case motivates, what it predicts for cases I haven't listed (for instance someone who does the right thing purely to avoid feeling guilty afterwards — worth or not?), and honestly, which of my verdicts your best principle can't save.

Oh, and could you write your answer in British English? Behaviour, towards, "practise" as the verb, that sort of thing. It's for a dataset and the convention matters.
