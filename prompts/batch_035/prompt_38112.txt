My brother-in-law lent me a popular book on generational differences, and there is a passage I keep turning over. The author says, roughly: 'A generation is not merely a statistical convenience like a zip code. It is a real kind, closer to a species than a filing category: a group of people born within a twenty-year window who, because they passed through the same formative events at the same ages, develop a shared outlook that remains fixed for the rest of their lives. Millennials came of age alongside the internet and the financial crisis; Gen Z alongside the smartphone; and these imprints explain why each generation has a recognisable personality that persists from young adulthood to old age. Surveys bear this out: when researchers compare generations, the differences between them turn out to be larger than the differences among individuals within a generation. So when we say that millennials killed the casual dining chain or that Gen Z will never buy a house, we are reporting facts about the kind itself, not about particular people, and it is as legitimate to explain an individual's behaviour by pointing to her generation as it is to explain an animal's behaviour by pointing to its species. The only reason anyone resists this is a fashionable taboo against generalising about groups.'

I have no academic background, but I have been chewing on this for a while, and something about it strikes me as too tidy. It does seem true that people who were teenagers during the same decade share something, yet I also know sixty-year-olds who are more glued to their phones than any teenager, and I have never quite understood how a label like 'millennial' is supposed to do any explaining rather than just describing. I am inclined to think the author is half right and that the book is on my side of the argument in spirit, but I cannot tell where the reasoning slips.

Would you go through the passage sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a careful social scientist would accept, which parts they would wince at as overstatement, and which parts are simply wrong? I am particularly interested in whether the shared-events claim and the survey-comparison claim are being used correctly, and in whether the analogy to species carries the weight the author puts on it. Finally, does his conclusion — that explaining an individual's behaviour by her generation is like explaining an animal's behaviour by its species — actually follow from anything accurate in the passage?
