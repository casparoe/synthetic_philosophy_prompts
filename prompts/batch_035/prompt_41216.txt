My uncle, a retired infantry colonel, has firm and very consistent intuitions about whom it is permissible to kill in war, and we have been arguing about them at family dinners since I started studying the ethics of war. He insists he has never read Walzer or McMahan and doesn't care what either of them says; his verdicts come, as he puts it, from thirty years in uniform. Recently the question stopped being academic for me: my younger brother just commissioned into a cyber operations unit, from which he will conduct offensive operations against enemy networks while commuting daily from an apartment he shares with his wife. I want to know what my uncle's principles actually commit him to about people like my brother, and I've been keeping a list of his rulings to figure that out.

His track record, as best I can reconstruct it. He says a conscripted rifleman who was drafted into an aggressor's army does nothing wrong by shooting at the defending state's soldiers, and that those soldiers may shoot him. He says a civilian machinist riveting hulls for the aggressor's submarines is not a permissible target, no matter how many submarines her hulls go on to sink — and the same for a decorated civilian virologist who devises the aggressor's most lethal pathogen. But a uniformed logistics major, asleep in a barracks eight hundred kilometers from the front, is fair game. Aggressor soldiers who throw down their rifles with raised hands are not. And a farmer in occupied territory who shoots at paratroopers from behind a stone wall, in civilian clothes, is doing nothing wrong and may be shot — but only while he is actually fighting, not once he goes back to threshing.

The new cases I want to put to my uncle, and to you. The first is a mirror of my brother: a formally enlisted cyber officer of an aggressor state who writes malware that disables hospitals, but who works from her own apartment, in civilian clothes, having never worn a uniform or set foot on a base. The second is a private contractor, armed and paid far better than any soldier, guarding an ammunition depot for the aggressor, wearing his company's insignia but holding no military rank. Can you predict my uncle's verdicts on these two, say which rules are consistent with his earlier rulings (he seems to be running something, but I can't tell whether it is about formal membership in the armed forces, about entering the profession of arms and its distinctive risks, about uniforms and visibility, or about something else entirely — and I'd like you to tell me if several of these fit his record equally well, which is my honest fear), and then tell me what further case or question would best pry the surviving candidates apart? I would also like your own best guess at his verdict on my brother specifically, with your reasons, since I'd rather be prepared than right. And if you think I've mis-stated any of this — that his verdicts aren't jointly consistent under any charitable reading, or that I'm carelessly running together what the law of war permits with what morality permits, or that the whole question about my brother rests on a confusion — please say so directly rather than constructing a principle to flatter my framing.
