I'm taking a seminar on Nietzsche and we've just moved onto the Genealogy of Morality. I generally follow the Second Essay's story about guilt coming out of the creditor-debtor relation, but then we hit this passage in §2 about the "sovereign individual", and honestly the whole thing threw me. Our tutor said this bit is one of the most argued-over paragraphs in the book, and I can see why. Here's the passage (Samuel's 1913 translation, so the English is a bit creaky — sorry about that):

"If, however, we place ourselves at the end of this colossal process, at the point where the tree finally matures its fruits, when society and its morality of custom finally bring to light that to which it was only the means, then do we find as the ripest fruit on its tree the sovereign individual, that resembles only himself, that has got loose from the morality of custom, the autonomous 'super-moral' individual (for 'autonomous' and 'moral' are mutually exclusive terms),—in short, the man of the personal, long, and independent will, competent to promise,—and we find in him a proud consciousness (vibrating in every fibre), of what has been at last achieved and become vivified in him, a genuine consciousness of power and freedom, a feeling of human perfection in general. And this man who has grown to freedom, who is really competent to promise, this lord of the free will, this sovereign—how is it possible for him not to know how great is his superiority over everything incapable of binding itself by promises, or of being its own security, how great is the trust, the awe, the reverence that he awakes—he 'deserves' all three—not to know that with this mastery over himself he is necessarily also given the mastery over circumstances, over nature, over all creatures with shorter wills, less reliable characters?"

There are a few things I can't square. First, the obvious one: Nietzsche spends the rest of the Genealogy (and Beyond Good and Evil) trashing the idea of free will as a fiction invented by theologians to make people punishable — so why is he suddenly singing the praises of a "lord of the free will" and calling this person "autonomous"? Is he using "free" in some weaker, compatibilist sense here (something like: reliable, self-governed, calculable), or is there a genuine tension? Second, that parenthetical that "autonomous" and "moral" are mutually exclusive — I keep rereading it and I'm not sure what work it's doing. If the morality of custom is what made the sovereign individual possible, why is the result described as free of morality, and does that mean the genealogy of morality ends up excusing it somehow (the cruel, stupid process was "worth it")? Third, someone in my seminar claimed this paragraph doesn't fit the rest of the book at all and that Nietzsche should've cut it — is that fair, or is there a reading where the sovereign individual makes sense as the fruit of the process and not just a weird digression? Could you walk me through the passage slowly, keeping it in the weeds of what "autonomy," "morality of custom," and "competent to promise" are doing here? Please write your answer in British English.
