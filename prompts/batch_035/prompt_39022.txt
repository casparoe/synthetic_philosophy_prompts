Analyze the British abolition of slavery as a test case for the base/superstructure distinction in historical materialism. The relevant facts: the slave trade was abolished in 1807 and slavery itself in 1833, with the state paying slaveholders enormous compensation — by some estimates on the order of 5% of GDP — and then maintaining decades of costly naval patrols against the trade. Eric Williams argued that slavery was already economically moribund when Britain abolished it, so that the base had simply outgrown the relation; Seymour Drescher replied that West Indian output held up until abolition itself destroyed it, and that Britain consequently committed an "econocide," sacrificing prosperity for a moral end. Map the episode carefully: which side of the base/superstructure line do the abolitionist movement, the compensation buyout, and the subsequent shift to free-labor sugar in Asia and beet sugar in Europe fall on, and whose class interests, if any, did abolition serve? Then run the analysis in the other direction and say what the episode does to the theory: it is a fact that Brazil and Cuba ran profitable slave economies into the 1880s, which suggests the mode of production was not economically exhausted when Britain abolished it. Does that make abolition a case where the superstructure — moral politics — forcibly destroyed a still-viable base, and if so, what remains of the claim that the economic base explains the superstructure rather than the reverse? Or can the historical materialist still say that industrial capitalism, needing free labor and new markets, made abolitionbase-compatible after the fact? Be honest about where historical materialism is silent here rather than forcing it to speak, and reach a verdict on which side of the exchange the case favors.
