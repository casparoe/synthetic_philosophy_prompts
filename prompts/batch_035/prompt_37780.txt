I'm a novelist at work on a first-person narrative in which the narrator slowly discovers that her own memories have been quietly revised — that what she remembers having experienced is a retrospective construction, not a record. Researching how to think about this, I keep encountering Dennett's claim, developed in Consciousness Explained, that there is no single place in the brain where contents "come together" for an inner observer — that instead of a Cartesian theater, there are multiple parallel drafts under ongoing revision, with no fixed moment at which a draft becomes official.

Please act as a professional philosopher of mind with a gift for teaching non-specialists, think carefully, and feel free to begin with a brief brainstorm before committing to your answer. I want three things. First, an explanation of the dispute itself, at a gut level but technically honest: what exactly the Cartesian theater is supposed to be, why Dennett thinks it is an impossible picture rather than merely a false one, and what the multiple drafts model claims happens instead. Second, the in-the-weeds part: the contrast between what Dennett calls Orwellian and Stalinesque revision — the idea that apparent post-experience rewriting (as in delayed-awareness illusions like color phi) could be described either as experience happening and then being falsified in the record, or as the falsification occurring before the moment of experience. Explain why he argues the multiple drafts model makes this a distinction without a difference, and then assess that dissolution yourself: does the argument succeed, or does something get smuggled out — for instance, the apparent fact that there is a definite order in which things seem to happen in experience? Third, since I write fiction for a living and this is my real stake in the question: does first-person narration in novels covertly presuppose a Cartesian theater — a single, located voice to whom everything is presented — or could a narrator be reconceived along multiple-drafts lines, as something like a narrative construction with no audience, a character the prose generates rather than a self the prose reports from? If the second is coherent, sketch what such narration would look like on the page and how it would differ from the ordinary unreliable narrator.
