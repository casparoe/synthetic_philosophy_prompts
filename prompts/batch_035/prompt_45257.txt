I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but my work increasingly raises questions I've come to realize are causal rather than merely statistical: interpreting A/B experiments, reasoning about the root causes of production incidents, and, lately, studying causal inference for machine-learning systems. To get oriented, I've been reading what I can find at the popular level, including Judea Pearl's The Book of Why, and I recently came across a blog post aimed at engineers that summarized the philosophical debate between counterfactual and interventionist theories of causation. The post's summary, condensed, was this:

"Philosophers have two rival theories of causation. The counterfactual theory, due to the philosopher David Lewis, identifies causation with a pattern of counterfactual dependence: A causes B exactly when, if A had not occurred, B would not have occurred. The interventionist theory, developed by Judea Pearl and the philosopher James Woodward, rejects this: on their view, A causes B when an intervention that sets A changes B. Pearl showed why the counterfactual approach fails for practical purposes: counterfactuals describe imaginary worlds that never happened, so claims about them can never be tested, whereas interventions can actually be carried out — this is what his do-operator formalizes, and it is why the do-operator is the gold standard for causal claims. Woodward pushes interventionism further into a manipulationist view: a variable can be a cause only if it is something we can literally manipulate, which is why interventionists deny that there can be causes among quantities no one can manipulate, such as the constants of nature. For engineers the lesson is simple: when you are doing root-cause analysis or interpreting an experiment, counterfactual questions — 'would the outage have happened if we had rolled back an hour earlier?' — are strictly unanswerable, and you should restrict yourself to questions you can express as interventions."

I have no way to tell how much of this is right. Some of it fits what I remember from Pearl's book — he certainly emphasizes interventions and the do-operator — but the post's framing bothers me: it presents Pearl and Lewis as rivals, and I recall Pearl himself using counterfactual language freely, which sits oddly with the claim that he showed counterfactual claims can never be tested. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me: (1) which parts a careful philosopher of science would accept as accurate, at least as a first approximation; (2) which parts are subtly wrong — conflations of distinct positions, overstatements of a modest thesis into a strong one, or attributions to a theorist of a claim stronger than the one he actually holds; (3) what a defender of each camp — the Lewis-style counterfactual theorist, the Woodward-style interventionist, and the Pearl-style structural modeler — would object to in this summary; and (4) most importantly for me, whether the practical lesson in the final sentence — that counterfactual questions in engineering are strictly unanswerable and should be replaced by interventionist ones — actually follows from whatever parts of the summary turn out to be true. If it does not follow, I would like to understand what the real relationship between the two theories is, expressed in terms an engineer can evaluate: is the disagreement about semantics (what causation is), about epistemology (how causal claims can be tested), or about which questions a validated model is licensed to answer? In your final assessment, please say plainly whether I should keep or discard the blog post's lesson for my day-to-day work.
