I'm writing my term paper for an epistemology seminar and I've got stuck on the closure business. Where I am so far: I was pretty sold on Nozick's tracking theory, because it just seems to handle Gettier cases and lottery cases in a way that JTB-plus-a-fourth-condition views keep fumbling. But my study group keeps needling me about closure. If I know the animal in the pen is a zebra (it's a zoo, it looks exactly like a zebra), and I know that zebras are not cleverly disguised mules, then surely I can know it's not a disguised mule — yet tracking says I can't, since if it were a mule things would look the same and I'd still believe it wasn't. And having to say I know it's a zebra but don't know it's not a disguised mule just sounds insane out loud, which my friend tells me people call the abominable conjunction problem. I suspect closure is more negotiable than they think but I can't quite say why yet.

So before I write anything I want the spec first. Write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of knowledge has to deliver — which cases it must get right (Gettier, fake barn country, the lottery, boring everyday perceptual knowledge), which principles or distinctions it must either respect or be explicitly allowed to give up (closure under known entailment, the difference between knowing p and being in a position to know p, whether a theory can let you know ordinary things while denying you know skeptical hypotheses are false), and how it should treat Moorean moves like here's a hand, therefore there's an external world. Give the motivating case behind each requirement, mark which you take to be genuinely non-negotiable and which are just camp prejudices dressed up as requirements, and — this is the part I actually need for the paper — tell me whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. If you can't have tracking-style anti-skeptical results, closure, and everyday knowledge all at once, say so and show me the contradiction, because if the list itself is a result that's my thesis right there.

Then just a quick scorecard at the end: Nozick's tracking, a safety- or reliability-flavored theory, and contextualism, against your list. Keep the scorecard to a few lines each — the meat is the requirements, not the scoring. A table is fine if you want but don't let it carry the arguement.
