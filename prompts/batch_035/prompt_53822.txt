I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of epistemology and ethics, and I've become fascinated by what seems to me a deep structural parallel between two arguments that are normally kept in entirely separate literatures. On the one hand, there is Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against ultimate moral responsibility: roughly, that to be truly responsible for one's actions, one would have to be responsible for the mental states that produce them (one's character, motives, intentions), and to be responsible for those, one would have to have deliberately brought them about in a way that was in turn caused by earlier mental states for which one is responsible — leading to either an infinite regress or the demand that one be causa sui, which is impossible. On the other hand, there is the ancient regress problem in epistemology, nowadays usually called the Agrippan trilemma: any belief, if it is to be justified, seems to need a reason, which needs a further reason, so that justification terminates either in an infinite chain, an arbitrary stopping point, or a circle. The obvious response on the epistemic side is coherentism — the claim that circular justification at the system level is not vicious but benign, since mutually supporting beliefs can form a self-grounding web. And this is what I want to see worked out carefully: can an analogous "responsibility coherentism" be mounted against the Basic Argument?

Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in both free will debates and formal epistemology. Begin by briefly brainstorming how the two arguments really map onto each other — where the analogy is tight, and where it might break down (for instance, it is often said that responsibility is a historical, diachronic property of agents while justification is a synchronic property of belief systems, and it's not obvious that this difference is harmless). Then write a comparison of Strawson's Basic Argument and the Agrippan trilemma with three specific goals: (1) reconstruct the shared regress structure and identify any relevant disanalogies; (2) assess whether a coherentist reply transfers — could someone argue that responsibility is grounded in a web of self-forming actions and self-endorsed character traits, none individually self-created, but collectively self-grounding? and (3) evaluate what a Strawsonian should say in response. Feel free to bring in nearby ideas, such as the thought that divine voluntarism merely relocates the problem, or that some philosophers treat certain belief-forming processes as cognitively immanent and thus exempt from the regress. Reach a definite verdict on whether the coherentist escape from the Basic Argument is more or less promising than the coherentist escape from the epistemic regress, and justify it — don't leave the comparison hanging as merely an interesting parallel.
