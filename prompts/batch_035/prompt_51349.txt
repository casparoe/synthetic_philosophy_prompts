I am the research integrity officer at a large research university, and several times a year I have to adjudicate disputes about the ownership of ideas — not text plagiarism, which our software and policies handle, but the murkier category where one researcher claims another used an idea they originated: a graduate student who sketched a research direction at a lab meeting and later saw it as the centerpiece of the supervisor's grant; a postdoc who heard a talk, decided the speaker had abandoned the idea, and published first; two collaborators who each insist the key move in a joint paper was theirs. My sanctions committee consists of scientists and an administrator, none of them trained in philosophy, and they want something they can actually apply rather than hand-wave about academic norms.

I want you to build me a decision procedure grounded in what the philosophy of intellectual property actually says, translated into something my committee can run. I take it the relevant raw material includes the labor-desert view that effort confers a claim, the personality view that ideas are expressions of the self, the utilitarian view that idea-ownership is justified only by incentives, the fact that ideas are non-rival in a way land and objects are not, and the Lockean proviso that appropriation is legitimate only if it leaves enough and as good for others — but you should decide what belongs in the procedure and what to leave in the seminar room. What interests me especially, and where I suspect the philosophy has real work to do, are the distinctions the disputes actually turn on: between a problem, an approach, and a worked result — I want to know whether claiming a problem is different in kind from claiming a route into it; between originating an idea and being merely its first audience; and between cases where the hearer would have got there independently anyway and cases where they took something they could not have supplied themselves. If the utilitarian and labor-desert strands of the philosophy pull in different directions on any of these, the procedure should say so rather than papering over it.

Please write the procedure with these constraints: it must be usable by a non-philosopher committee over the course of a two-hour meeting plus a few days of document review; it should pose its questions in a fixed order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, mark the points where the procedure must stop and hand the question to human judgment because it is a question of values rather than facts, and be honest about where it is philosophy dressed up as a flowchart. Then run it on three cases: first, a graduate student who raised an unformed idea in a lab meeting and eight months later found it as the central aim of the supervisor's grant application, with no acknowledgment; second, two researchers in different countries who published near-identical results within months of each other, where there is no evidence of contact but one of them did attend a conference the other spoke at; third, a journal referee who declined a manuscript and then published a paper using its framing, citing the rejected manuscript only in a footnote. Include at least one case where the procedure delivers an answer I am not going to like, and say so plainly. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the classes of dispute it should never be applied to, and the places where I should just accept that what looks like theft of an idea is the ordinary, ungovernable traffic of a research community.
