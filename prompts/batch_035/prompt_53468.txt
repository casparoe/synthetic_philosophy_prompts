Two friends and I have been running a little philosophy reading group for about a year now, and it has basically devolved into the same fight every fortnight: one of them, Daniel, is a committed Epicurean about death — his line is that death cannot be bad for the person who dies, because a harm needs a subject, and once you're dead there's no you left for anything to be bad for, so all the apparent badness is really badness for the survivors or a confusion born of misjudging death while alive. The other, Priya, holds the deprivation view: death is bad for you when it cuts off a life that would otherwise have contained goods, so the badness isn't located in any experience but in the gap between your actual welfare and the welfare of the counterfactual self who kept living. They've done this so many times that they're not even disagreeing about the same things any more — Daniel keeps answering an experiential claim Priya says she never made, and Priya keeps talking about value comparisons Daniel says never get off the ground because there's no bearer for the value. I want to force them to produce the document a proper adversarial collaboration would end with.

Please draft their joint statement. I want a numbered section of claims both could sign — presumably things like: death is not an experience; dying can be bad; the badness of death for others is real but a separate question — then a disagreements section where each point is worded so its own side would accept the phrasing of their own view (this is the crucial bit, no slanted formulations). Then the cruxes. I suspect at least some of these: the timing puzzle (if death is bad for you, *when* is it bad for you — before, during, after?); whether a harm must have a subject at a time or only a subject simpliciter; the Lucretius symmetry point about us not regarding our prenatal nonexistence as a misfortune, and whether "identity hasn't been fixed yet" is really an adequate reply; and which counterfactual continuation the deprivation theorist even means (the nearest possible world where you dodge the bus? a best-case continuation?), since picking different ones seems to give different verdicts. For each crux, give a concrete case both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about the case written down in advance. Some cases to include: our friend's grandmother, who died at 94 after a full life, versus a 40-year-old killed in an accident the day before starting something they'd dreamed about; someone terminally ill whose remaining months would have held only pain and confusion; and the weird one Daniel invented, the annihilation switch — a device that, the moment you fall asleep, painlessly and without any experiential trace whatsoever swaps your future for nonexistence, versus a matched device that swaps your future for ten years of ordinary contentment. Does the first device harm you, and if so when, and compared to what?

Also, both of them have said separately that they suspect some of the dispute is verbal — maybe about what "bad for" means — and each blames the other for the verbal part. If you think part of the fight is genuinely verbal, say which part and why; if you think it's substantive all the way down, say that too. And be honest about which side has more of its position riding on a single crux. Please write the whole thing in British English, and keep it signed-off-able — Daniel in particular will nitpick any sentence that quietly assumes his view is wrong.
