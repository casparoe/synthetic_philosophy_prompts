I teach a graduate seminar in epistemology, and this year I want to stop grading my students on which norm of assertion they say they like — they simply recite the position defended by whichever paper they read first — and instead sort them by how they react to cases. Please write a diagnostic quiz, titled something like "Which norm of assertion do you actually accept?", aimed at students who know their Gettier and their Moore but have not yet memorized the assertion literature. Take the role of a professional epistemologist who has thought hard about the structure of this debate; brainstorm the logical space and candidate cases before writing anything down, since I want the instrument to do real sorting work.

The quiz should contain eight to ten items. Each item presents a short concrete scenario followed by two or three answer options, and each option must be phrased as a verdict about the speaker in the scenario — fine as uttered, said something false, said something true but spoke out of line, must retract now that the defeater arrived, deserves blame regardless of truth — never as a claim about a norm or thesis, so students cannot pattern-match to labels. The items must include the load-bearing cases: the stopped watch that reads the right time by sheer accident; the lottery-ticket holder who asserts that her ticket will lose; the Gettiered subject with a justified true belief that falls short of knowledge; the selfless assertion, made sincerely for the audience's benefit despite the speaker's own disbelief; the Moore-paradoxical utterance in which the speaker asserts a content while denying that she believes it; and at least one case turning on whether an assertion that was acceptable when made must be retracted once later evidence defeats it. Beyond these, invent at least two novel cases of your own — I would be glad to see, for example, an assertion made by a forgetful but superbly placed expert who has temporarily lost track of what she is entitled to say, or an unhedged weather forecast asserted about a chancy outcome — and make sure at least one item concerns the propriety of blaming the speaker rather than the truth of what was said, since blame is where I suspect the candidate norms come apart most sharply.

After the quiz, provide a scoring key built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first set out the questions that generate the space (must the asserted content be true? must the speaker believe it? must the speaker be justified? must the speaker know? is the norm constitutive of assertion or merely a rule of good conversational practice?), then locate as leaves the knowledge norm, the simple truth norm, the justified-belief norm, the belief norm, and the deflationary option on which assertion has no constitutive norm at all. Flag any combination of answers your key maps to no position, and say in one sentence each whether nobody occupies that combination because it is unstable, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag also any pair of leaves your instrument cannot genuinely separate — I expect the truth and justified-belief norms to be hard to pry apart with cases alone, and if you discover a case that does separate them, feature it prominently as a quiz item. Where an item could be read as supporting two different norms, sharpen the scenario until it forces a choice.

Please keep the register formal throughout, and end your response with a machine-readable list of the leaf positions your key distinguishes, in the form \answer{...} containing the labels separated by semicolons, so I can check the key against my roster of positions before handing it out.
