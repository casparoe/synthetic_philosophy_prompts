For an ethics course I am preparing a unit on Glaucon's challenge in Book II of Plato's Republic: the claim that anyone granted a ring making them undetectable would commit injustices freely and be better off for it, since what we call justice is merely a compromise adopted by the weak. Every counterexample I have found cheats in one of three ways: the wrongdoer is eventually caught, or he is tormented by guilt, or the case quietly imports Plato's later theory of the disordered soul from Book IV. Construct for me a thought experiment in which the ring works perfectly and its bearer never regrets a thing, yet is nonetheless worse off by his own lights — where the costs are ones he would count as costs even before putting the ring on, not ones only a moralist could love. Constraints: his injustices must succeed completely in delivering the worldly goods Glaucon says the unjust life is really for — wealth, power, gratified appetites; the setting must be an ordinary modern life, with no gods, no karmic machinery, and no appeal to the tripartite soul; and the scenario itself should be a single paragraph. After setting out the case, demonstrate that it meets the specification: show step by step how the cost arises without stipulating it, identify which single feature of the scenario is doing the work, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed. Close by letting Glaucon fight back — state his strongest objection, namely that the case builds in a cost his ring-bearer never agreed to count, and assess whether that objection succeeds.
