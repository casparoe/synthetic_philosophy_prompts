I'm a philosophy professor who usually teaches ethics of war, but this term I got handed a second-year module on philosophy of history that a colleague built years ago, and the midterm marking has landed on me at the worst possible time. The exam was closed-book, 90 minutes, one essay question, which I set as follows:

"Ranke is often taken to define the historian's task with his dictum 'wie es eigentlich gewesen'. Explain what Ranke's ideal of historical objectivity involves, and explain how E. H. Carr and Hayden White, in their different ways, challenge that ideal. Does either Carr or White show that there is no fact of the matter about the past? Defend your answer."

There are forty scripts and I'm behind, so I'd like a careful second pass on one answer before I commit to a mark. Here it is, verbatim:

"Ranke is the founder of modern source-based history. He introduced the seminar method, insisted on archival work and primary sources, and wanted the past judged on its own terms rather than used to extract moral lessons for the present — each age is equally immediate to God, so no age should be ranked by another's standards. His dictum wie es eigentlich gewesen means the historian's job is to show the past as it actually was, and to do this the historian must get inside the minds of past agents, re-enacting their thought from within, since what actually happened depends on what the agents themselves thought they were doing.

Carr attacked this ideal in What Is History?. His famous image is that facts are like fish swimming in a vast ocean, and what the historian catches depends on where he chooses to fish. So for Carr there are no objective facts in history at all: the historian's selection creates the facts rather than the facts constraining the historian. History is written from the standpoint of the present, an unending dialogue between the present and the past, so Ranke's dream of a neutral, purely past-directed history is incoherent.

White goes further. In Metahistory (1973) he argued that historical writing is not fundamentally different from literature: the historian takes a body of events and emplots it as romance, tragedy, comedy or satire, and these plot structures are imposed by the historian rather than discovered in the evidence. Since the same events can be given any of these forms, there is no fact of the matter about the past — one emplotment is as true as any other, and the choice between them is aesthetic or political rather than evidential.

So Carr and White agree that history is a branch of literature, and both show that the Rankean ideal is dead: the past does not impose a story on us, we impose it on the past."
