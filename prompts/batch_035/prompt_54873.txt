After a career as an ornithologist with a state wildlife agency, I have retired into philosophy of science and into volunteering as a reviewer for our county breeding bird atlas. The atlas asks, for each species, something of the form "every bird of species S breeding in the county this decade showed trait T" — every raven record a black bird, every golden-winged warbler record from the northern tier — and I have come to see that our verification problem is Hempel's raven paradox in work clothes. Our reviewers are volunteers with no background in statistics or philosophy, each handling perhaps thirty records a week at ten minutes a record, and contested cases go to the chair. Please distill confirmation theory — Nicod's criterion, the equivalence condition, Hempel's observation that a non-black non-raven confirms the claim that all ravens are black, and whatever role background knowledge plays in that observation — into a decision procedure a reviewer can actually run on an incoming record: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with accept or reject, and where it hands the record to the chair because the issue is the weight rather than the mere existence of confirmation. Then run it on three records: a sharp photograph of a leucistic, near-white raven; a reported gray raven that the accompanying photo shows to be a gray heron, no raven involved; and a raven record in which the observer never noted plumage color. I suspect the procedure will tell me the heron counts as confirmation in some sense; I would like it to be honest about how weak that sense is, about where the procedure will misfire, and about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as checklist items.
