I am a novelist, and I have hit a question of craft that turns out to be a question of philosophy of language. My current book is narrated by a woman remembering her father, a virulently prejudiced man in a 1970s fishing town, and I must decide whether to put his actual language on the page or to have the narrator refuse it — to write, in effect, that he used a word she will not reproduce. To think this through, I read a popular book on language aimed at writers of fiction, and it summarised the philosophy of slurs for me roughly as follows: the power of a slur to wound is not part of what the word means at all, but lives in the speaker's attitude and the social setting; this, the book said, is why the same term functions as a weapon from an outsider and as camaraderie within the group, and why an author who puts it in a character's mouth is merely mentioning it rather than using it, and so endorses nothing. It went on to say that the alternative view, on which contempt is built into the word's dictionary content, has largely been abandoned, because it cannot explain reclamation — how a group can take a slur over and make it affectionate. All that remains controversial, the book concluded, is how far the non-semantic factors should be socially regulated.

I am not equipped to audit this, but two things make me suspicious. It moves rather smoothly from "offensiveness depends on context" to "mention is harmless," as though the second followed from the first, and it dismisses the dictionary-content view on the strength of a single objection, which feels like how popular books tidy up live debates. So I would be grateful for a sentence-by-sentence verdict: which claims a careful philosopher of language would accept, which are conflations (for instance, between a word's offensiveness and its content, or between the pragmatics of use and the use/mention distinction), which is an overstatement dressed as a consensus, and whether my narrator's choice is actually licensed by anything true in the summary.

I should say that I am not looking for a survey that lays out the semantic, pragmatic, and expressivist options and leaves the verdict to me; I have enough of those. Please take a definite position: say which picture of how slurs work you actually endorse, tell me plainly whether the book's claims survive your corrections, and then, on the strength of that, give me a committed recommendation about the page — whether the narrator should reproduce her father's language or refuse it, and why, in terms a working novelist can act on.
