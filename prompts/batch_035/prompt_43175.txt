Welfare states mostly cut checks. Unemployment benefits, child allowances, pensions -- the money lands in your account and its yours to spend, even if you spend it badly. But when someone is disabled, the same states usually don't just hand over a bigger check to cover the expected extra costs of living with the disability. Instead they provide specific things: wheelchairs, home ramps, personal assistants, job coaches, subsidized transit. So for one group of badly-off people the state hands over general-purpose resources and lets the person decide what to do with them, and for another group it targets particular functionings directly. What does that split presuppose about what we're actually trying to equalize? Is the cash half presupposing that what matters is resources (or even welfare, trusting people to spend on whatever makes them best off), while the in-kind half presupposes something like capabilities -- making sure people can actually do and be certain things regardless of their own choices? Can those two halves really sit in the same system, or is there a hidden inconsistency? And there's a further wrinkle: some places have moved toward disability payments and personal budgets in cash that the recipient directs themselves. Does a reform like that quietly change the underlying theory of equality, or is it just swapping delivery mechanisms while keeping the old commitments? I'd also want to know whether the split is philosophically required at all, or whether its just covert paternalism about disabled people's choices dressed up as a theory.
