I teach an upper-level environmental ethics seminar (15 students, one 80-minute session, movable seating). I want a structured decision-making game that makes them feel the wedge between two framings they all hold simultaneously without noticing: that what is good for an ecosystem and what is good for the individual animals inside it can come apart, and that our intuitions track whichever framing is locally convenient. Ideally they should run into this themselves, through decisions they have to make under one set of incentives and then discover were being scored under another.

Here is the shape I have in mind, which you should refine, extend, or correct. The class is split into four boards of trustees of a mid-sized nature reserve (roughly 30 square kilometers, a large free-ranging population of a generic deer-like ungulate — I would rather not name a real species and let students import real-world controversy about actual wildlife agencies). The facilitator presents a scripted sequence of three decision rounds, and each board must publicly commit to one of three or four options per round. Consequences are pre-scripted by the facilitator and revealed after each round, so no wildlife expertise is needed and the game moves fast. Round one should be the classic trap: a harsh winter is coming, and the boards choose between emergency feeding, a cull of 20 percent of the herd, or doing nothing. Feeding saves animals this winter but pushes the population above carrying capacity, so a worse crash arrives two rounds later; the cull is ugly but stabilizes; doing nothing means a third of the animals die slowly of starvation but the population and the vegetation recover. Round two introduces a slow-spreading wasting disease (options: mass cull of the affected watershed, expensive vaccination covering a minority of the herd, or letting it run its course). Round three should be the reversal: predators recolonize on their own, and now the boards must decide whether to tolerate them, remove them, or leave the situation alone — and here the supposedly "natural" option is the one that involves the most visible individual deaths, so intuitions that were pointing one way in rounds one and two should start pointing the other way.

The scoring mechanic is where the conceptual payload lives, so design it carefully. Each board draws a sealed charter at the start stating what it is accountable for, and the charters differ: one board is scored on the number of animals alive at the end of the ten-year game horizon; one on minimizing slow deaths, however many deaths there are overall; one on the reserve's ecological integrity, including vegetation and predator presence; one on minimizing deaths caused directly by the boards' own interventions. The charters stay sealed from the other boards, though each board knows its own. Because the boards must announce and defend their choices publicly after each round, they will be unable to fathom how other boards could decide as they do — and at the end, the reveal shows that boards with identical factual information and incompatible charters sometimes made identical decisions for opposite reasons, and sometimes opposite decisions that score identically. That is the moment I want to engineer: factual agreement does not settle anything, because the dispute was never about the facts.

Two further mechanics I would like your help with. First, each board holds a one-time "that's natural" card that lets it defend a decision by appeal to naturalness alone, immune to challenge. Keep a tally: I predict it will be played on opposite sides in different rounds, and I want a debrief question that makes the class confront the fact that the appeal cut in both directions. Second, I want to work in the ecological idea sometimes called the doomed surplus — the thought that a predator's kill is not always an additional death, because the animal taken might have starved or frozen anyway within the season, so predation redistributes deaths in time and manner rather than adding to their number. I want a round or a consequence where the boards are asked explicitly whether this makes predation better, worse, or irrelevant for the animal taken, and I want the debrief to force the distinction between deaths being non-additional at the population level and being any less bad for the individual — which is exactly where the two framings collide.

For the debrief, map moments onto concepts rather than lecturing: which round made aggregate thinking feel humane and which made it feel monstrous; whether anyone could articulate what the ecosystem- integrity charter was for without using the word "natural"; whether the slow-deaths charter, which seemed most compassionate, ever demanded an action the players found hardest to carry out; and the identification question — would they accept the same trade-offs if the reserve held fifteen hundred dogs instead of fifteen hundred deer, and if not, what exactly changed. Close with the question I do not want answered too quickly: whether "leave nature alone" is a coherent position about the animals at all, or only about us.

Finally, be honest about failure modes and limits. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect boards to try to reverse-engineer the facilitator's script, a student to refuse a charter as against their conscience (tell me whether to let that stand or require everyone to play their card), the first round's feeding debate to eat forty minutes, and complaints that some charters are easier than others, which I suspect is itself the lesson rather than a bug. And say plainly which feature of the real problem the activity cannot reproduce: the scale of suffering in actual wild populations, the asymmetry between statistical deaths and the one animal you watch die, and the fact that real reserves do not get clean information between rounds. Tell me what I must supply in the debrief because no game mechanic will generate it.
