I'm not a philosopher — I work in insurance and do most of my thinking on the train — but a few years ago I read Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations on my own, and it changed how I think about words, and I've been quietly assembling a set of views ever since. I've tried to check them against each other and got nowhere, which I suspect means I can't see the problem from the inside. Here is the set:

(1) Wittgenstein was right that there is no hidden thing common to everything we call a game — only overlapping similarities running in different directions. I've since decided this is true of nearly all our ordinary concepts: sport, art, religion, furniture.

(2) Meaning is use. A word means whatever the community uses it to do; a dictionary can lag behind or misreport usage, but it cannot legislate.

(3) Where a concept has fuzzy edges, that isn't ignorance on my part. There is no hidden fact about whether some newly invented activity is a game which I merely fail to know — the fuzziness is in the concept, not behind it. There is no line to be drawn, not even one I can't see.

(4) And yet there are border cases where I hold confident views that I don't think of as claims about my own usage. Chess is simply not a sport, and someone who insists it is one is wrong about something, not just speaking differently from me. The same for professional wrestling, given that the outcome is fixed in advance.

(5) The rule-following discussion convinced me as well: whatever rule you lay down, a difference in interpretation is always possible, so no rule determines its own future applications — even the instruction to add two can be read in such a way that the series goes on 1004, 1008 after a thousand. What carries a rule is the ongoing practice, not its content.

(6) But when a chess engine plays, what it does seems fixed down to the last detail by its programme: no interpretation gap, nothing left open. And it seems obvious to me that the engine follows the rules of chess — indeed follows them better than any human, never misreading a rule.

(7) I have a private name for a particular feeling I get on Sunday evenings — I've called it the drop in my head for years — and I'm certain I re-identify the very same feeling week after week, even though I could never describe it well enough for anyone else to pick it out. I can't see how a word whose whole life is inside one head counts as meaning anything on view (2), but I can't bring myself to give up either half.

(8) Two things I'm unsure whether even to worry about. First, mathematical concepts: prime number seems to me perfectly exact, with no fuzzy edge, and I feel no threat to (1) — though I'd be hard pressed to say why not. Second, from nature documentaries I've taken on board that species is a mess in roughly the family-resemblance way — ring species and so forth — yet I also believe species are real divisions in nature, so that there is a genuine fact about whether two populations are the same species even if nobody yet knows it. The messiness bothers me for biology in a way it never has for game.

So: which of these are genuinely in tension, which only look that way, and what is the cheapest fix for each real one? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, tell me which single revision would tidy up the most, and be specific about which commitment has to go — I honestly can't tell, and I would rather be told than protected.
