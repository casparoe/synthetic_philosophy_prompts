I'm a teaching assistant for a survey course on classical Indian philosophy, and one recurring problem is that students arrive at the school labels (Cārvāka, the Buddhist logicians, Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, the two Mīmāṃsā lineages, Advaita Vedānta) by memorizing doctrine lists rather than by noticing where the schools actually disagree. I want a one-page decision tree that works the other way around: a student who knows nothing about the school names starts at a question they can answer from ordinary experience, and the branch questions sort them into the position their answers imply. So the branch nodes must be concrete cases, not requests for labels: nobody should be asked "do you accept anumāna as a pramāṇa?" — instead they should be asked, say, whether seeing smoke rising from a distant hill, where the fire itself is out of view, gives them knowledge that there is fire there, or only a strong expectation; or whether a trustworthy person's report about a place they have never visited gives them knowledge; or whether someone who has never seen a gavaya, having been told only that it resembles a cow, and who later spots an unfamiliar animal and concludes it must be the gavaya, has thereby gained knowledge and of what kind; or whether the fact that a robust man never eats during the day supports the conclusion that he eats at night; or whether failing to find a jar in a carefully checked, well-lit room is itself something they learned, rather than just the absence of learning.

Please build the tree from the logical space first: enumerate the branch questions, work out which combinations of answers the questions make available, and only then locate the named schools in that space, rather than starting from a list of schools and reverse-engineering questions for them. Include the leaves the combinations actually support, and explicitly flag two things rather than papering over them: (1) any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate — I suspect the pramāṇa inventory alone won't distinguish some schools whose real disagreement lies elsewhere, and if a pair needs a doctrinal question the tree doesn't contain, say so instead of forcing a bogus split; and (2) any combination of answers that no school occupies, with one sentence diagnosing whether that is because the view looks incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Keep it to around six branch questions and no more than ten leaves, and make sure every node is answerable by someone who has never heard the Sanskrit terminology.

Finally, since the tree will be handed to students as authoritative, I want the response to be candid about its own weaknesses: end with a short note on which branch placements you are least confident in, what further distinction or case would force you to redesign the tree (for instance, where a school's internal debate means one leaf is really two), and what kind of consideration would change your mind about the overall structure. If any placement rests on a contested or minority reading of a school, mark it as such rather than presenting it as settled.
