My friend Priya has these very specific takes on hard choices and I've been keeping a list of them because I'm convinced there's a system in there somewhere. When she was 22 she chose between becoming an ER nurse and becoming a jazz bassist, and her verdict on that pair was: neither is better, but it's also not a tie — she got genuinely annoyed when I said "so they're equal, just flip a coin." Her line was that it's not that she *can't tell* which is better, it's that better-than kind of runs out on choices like this. Offer her the bassist life plus a five-thousand-dollar signing bonus and she says still neither better; same answer at fifty thousand. The bonus is a real improvement, but it doesn't put one life *above* the other, it just makes one option better than it was. Compare two identical slices of cake: there she's totally indifferent and will happily let the waiter pick, and if you make one slice slightly bigger it's straightfowardly the better slice, no fuss. Then there's the friendship case — I asked whether any amount of money would make selling out a friend the *better* choice, and she said no, a trillion dollars wouldn't do it, but she insisted this is a different thing from the nurse/bassist case, though she had a hard time saying exactly how. One more data point: for the career-type choices she says the fact that neither is better is precisely why the choice is *hers* to make — choosing isn't discovering which option is best, it's more like authoring the person who looks back on it.

Now her nephew is deciding between taking a year to care for his aging mother and taking a prestigious fellowship abroad, and everyone wants Priya's opinion. Predict her verdicts on the sub-questions: will she say one option is better, that they're tied, or the third thing? If the fellowship comes with more funding and a slightly better title, does that flip it for her, and is there any amount of prestige that would make it decisively better? If the mother's condition turned out to be slightly less demanding than feared, does that flip the caregiving year? Would she tell him it's fine to flip a coin, fine only if he's exhausted by agonizing, or wrong even then? And here's the meta part, which is what I actually care about: what rule is she running? The obvious candidate is that parity thing she picked up from some philosopher — comparable but neither better, worse, nor equal — but at least two other rules fit her record just as well: one where it's really indifference all the way down plus a story about regret and identity to explain why she won't flip coins on careers but will on cake, and one with some lexical threshold structure that treats the friendship case as the paradigm and reinterprets the career cases as something else. Lay out what each rule predicts for the nephew, give me your best guess with your reasons, and tell me which further case — the single question you'd slip into dinner conversation with Priya — would most cleanly tell the rules apart.
