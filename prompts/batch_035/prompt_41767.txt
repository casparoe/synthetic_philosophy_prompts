I advise a small philanthropic funder that can support roughly eight researchers for three years on conceptual work about value alignment through preference idealization — the family of ideas that runs from coherent extrapolated volition and ideal-advisor theories to the more academic literature on ideal observer theory and "what we would want if fully informed and perfectly rational." To be clear about scope: this is philosophy, decision theory, and formal epistemology, not empirical ML, and the grantees would mostly be people trained in those fields rather than in machine learning. The funder has to commit to a portfolio in the next few months, and I've been asked to recommend which open questions the portfolio should center on. The difficulty is that everyone in this space has a pet worry, and I don't trust my own sense of which worries are load-bearing.

To give you a sense of the candidate pool, here are the questions I keep hearing about, roughly as they come up. Whether the coherence of idealized preferences is a substantive bet or just a mathematical hope — i.e., whether we should expect idealization to actually converge rather than merely assuming a coherent aggregate exists. Whose preferences go in and how to aggregate when idealization doesn't converge across persons — the difference between treating the target as a single coherent volition standing above individual agents, versus treating it as a fair compromise among persons who remain distinct, which looks like it already takes sides between utilitarian and contractualist readings. Whether the extrapolation procedure itself smuggles in a moral theory — the choice of conditions like "if we knew more, thought faster, and had grown up further together" seems innocuous but arguably encodes substantive commitments. What happens to actual people's complaints when their current preferences are overridden by their idealized ones — the question of whether idealized preferences have genuine normative authority over the people whose preferences they're supposed to be. And finally whether idealization should be a one-time snapshot fixed at deployment or an ongoing process that remains answerable to actual human beings as they change. That list is presumably not exhaustive, and I'd want you to flag anything important that's missing from it.

What I want from you is a defended ordering, not a survey and not a reading list. For each item you prioritize, tell me why it's upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers working a priori as opposed to questions that would really need empirical ML people, and how neglected it is relative to its importance — including whether it's neglected because it's genuinely hard or because it's unfashionable. Then tell me the trade-offs: where you'd sacrifice tractability for importance, and where you wouldn't. I'd also like two scenarios sketched: one development over the next couple of years that would move something up the list, and one that would move something down. And because funders love clean stories, please identify which of these questions is genuinely overrated — which one, if it were settled tomorrow, wouldn't actually change much downstream — and say whether you'd drop it from the portfolio entirely. If you think the whole idealization program rests on a crux none of my candidates captures, name that crux and say where it would slot into the ranking.
