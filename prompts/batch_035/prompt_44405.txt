So I finally got round to reading Popper's Poverty of Historicism, and I keep thinking about Fukuyama's end of history thesis, and I want someone to actually work through the combination instead of the usual hot take. Here's roughly what I want.

First, the mapping. Fukuyama's claim (the 1989 essay, then the 1992 book) wasn't a dated prophecy -- it was more like: liberal democracy plus market capitalism is the only system left with a claim to legitimacy, so the direction of history is settled even if the timetable isn't. Is that the kind of unconditional large-scale prediction Popper says is impossible in principle, or does the hedge about the timetable make it something weaker, a conditional prediction, or just trend extrapolation, which Popper allows? Popper's core argument, if I have it right, is that a law of history would have to anticipate the growth of knowledge, and that's unpredictable, because if you could predict the growth of knowledge you'd already have the knowledge. Does Fukuyama's thesis smuggle in an assumption about no future ideas? Like, the thesis only works if nothing genuinely new gets thought up, and Popper says precisely that we can't rule that out.

Second, the Oedipus effect thing. Popper's point that predictions about society can change society's behavior (self-fulfilling, self-refuting). Does announcing "the end of history" itself do social work? I can see it cutting both ways: it could demobilize challengers who figure the argument is already lost, or it could goad people into becoming historical actors precisely to prove history isn't over. After 2008 and the China decade alot of people said Fukuyama had been refuted; but is "refuted" even the right category by Popper's lights, since the thesis isn't framed as a testable law? What would honestly count as a falsification of it, and if the answer is nothing, is the whole thing more like a metaphysical program, which Popper would treat as neither science nor hopeless, just non-falsifiable?

Third, the part I actually care about: is Popper's own position historicism in reverse? He says the future is open, there's no fixed destiny, we can repair institutions piecemeal. But doesn't "the future is open" function in his rhetoric exactly like "the future is determined" functions for Marx, a grand claim about the shape of history as a whole? And piecemeal engineering presupposes that liberal-democratic institutions are the right chassis to be tinkering with in the first place, which looks like a substantive bet about history too. So does the Fukuyama episode show that Popper's line between prophecy and prediction is harder to hold than he made it look, or just that Fukuyama misapplied a line that's fine?

Don't be diplomatic and tell me everyone's partly right. If one of them is doing something illegitimate by the other's own standards, say so plainly. And no reading list, I've already got one.
