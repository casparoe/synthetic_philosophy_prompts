A friend and my brother got into it at dinner about the classic Frankfurt-style case, and I promised I'd ask someone who actually knows this stuff. The case they used: Sam is deciding whether to report a coworker for fudging expenses. Unbeknownst to Sam, he's under surveillance, and the people watching have some way of intervening — they stipulated this part, it's hypothetical — that would have stopped him from deciding not to report, if he'd ever leaned that way. He never leaned: he went straight to HR and reported her. Both of them agreed Sam is responsible for it. The fight was over why, and I lost the thread a couple of times.

Dan's opening: Sam couldn't have done otherwise — the setup guaranteed it — yet he's plainly responsible, so alternative possibilities aren't needed for responsibility.

My brother's reply: the case only works because the watchers can tell in advance which way Sam will go. Either that forecast is infallible because Sam's decision is already fixed by his character, in which case Dan has smuggled determinism in the back door and shown nothing about alternatives; or the forecast could be wrong, and then there's a possible world where Sam wavers and the watchers intervene — and in that world Sam had an alternative, of a sort.

Dan: that alternative is worthless — it's the ability to do otherwise at the price of being zapped, a flicker nobody should care about.

My brother: fine, call it a flicker, but flickers are all the principle of alternative possibilities ever needed. If any residual difference-making ability counts, Frankfurt cases don't refute the principle, just a puffed-up version of it.

Dan then switched cases: forget the predictor. Suppose the intervention already happened an hour earlier — the watchers tweaked Sam's brain so reporting was the only thing he could do — and he reported. My brother said that version is worse for Dan: if the tweak removed his capacity to do otherwise before he even started deliberating, then either it also removed whatever makes him responsible, or it never touched his reasons and his character is doing all the work — in which case Dan isn't explaining responsibility via the absence of alternatives, he's just redescribing it.

Can you go through their exchange move by move — who's right at each step, not just who wins overall — and tell me whether either of them ends up with a stable position on whether Sam could have done otherwise?
