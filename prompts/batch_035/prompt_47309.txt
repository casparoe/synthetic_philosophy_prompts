At a meeting of my university's space-policy society, the case for prioritizing human settlement of Mars over all other space spending was made with the proverb "don't put all your eggs in one basket": humanity currently keeps all its eggs in Earth's basket, and one asteroid, engineered pandemic, or nuclear exchange could break it, so we should get some eggs into a second basket as fast as possible. The speaker then drew the concrete conclusion that near-term settlement funding should outrank asteroid detection and deflection, since a deflected asteroid protects only this basket while a settlement protects the eggs. I found this rhetorically powerful but suspiciously elastic, and I'd like to know what I was actually agreeing to. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims the eggs-and-baskets line could be expressing in this context — for instance: a bare probability claim that two independent baskets make species extinction less likely; a claim about what kind of "basket" a Mars settlement is (a self-sufficient civilization, a seed population, or something that remains dependent on Earth for centuries, and so shares Earth's fate for a long class of risks); a claim about whose survival is being secured, given that in the proverb the eggs in the broken basket are never saved — the second basket only ensures that *some* eggs remain, not the ones you know; and a priority claim that spreading eggs across baskets beats any amount of hardening the first basket. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, substantive but defensible, or false, and — most important — whether it actually supports the speaker's conclusion that settlement funding should come at the expense of asteroid deflection. I'd like this as a menu I can bring to the next meeting, since I suspect the speaker moved freely between readings depending on the objection he was facing.
