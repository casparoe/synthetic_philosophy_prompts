I'm a graduate TA grading the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the ethics of war and peace, and I'd appreciate a careful second read of one answer before I assign it a mark. The exam question read as follows: "Gregory Kavka argued that nuclear deterrence gives rise to a paradox — in fact, he thought there were two distinct paradoxes, one about rationality and one about morality. (a) State each paradox as precisely as you can, explaining why the paradox is supposed to be special to deterrence rather than a feature of threats generally. (b) Explain why the paradoxes do not arise, or arise only in weakened form, for ordinary coercive threats such as extortion. (c) Assess one proposed resolution — for example, Schelling's 'threat that leaves something to chance' — and say whether it fully succeeds."

The student wrote: "Kavka's paradox starts from the fact that nuclear retaliation, once deterrence has failed, cannot achieve anything. If the attack has already happened, retaliating cannot deter that attack — the harm deterrence was supposed to prevent has already occurred — and it cannot deter future attacks any better than a smaller response would. So if the threat ever has to be carried out, carrying it out serves no purpose. Yet deterrence only works if the adversary believes we would carry it out anyway. This creates two problems. The rationality problem is that it seems irrational to form an intention we would have every reason not to act on, and intentions we would not act on are hard to make credible. The moral problem is that since massive retaliation would be disproportionate and pointless, it would be wrong to do it, and it is equally wrong to intend to do what it would be wrong to do. From this Kavka draws his conclusion: nuclear deterrence is morally impermissible, since it requires intending a great wrong, and the only defensible policy is to make the weapons unusable so the wrongful intention is never formed. As for why the paradox doesn't arise for ordinary threats: ordinary threats are made for gain, whereas deterrent threats are made only to avoid a loss. The extortionist threatens to hurt me unless I pay; he would rather I paid, but he is happy to hurt me if I don't, since hurting me costs him nothing and builds his reputation with future victims. The deterrer, by contrast, gets nothing out of carrying out the threat — which is exactly why the intention is paradoxical and the extortionist's is not. Finally, Schelling's solution is the threat that leaves something to chance: instead of deliberately intending retaliation, we set up alert levels, launch procedures, and deployments such that war may break out through accident or escalation even though no one ever decides to retaliate. Because nobody intends the retaliation, the moral problem disappears entirely, and because the risk is real, the threat stays credible. This is the same idea Kavka had in mind when he coined the term brinkmanship — manipulating the shared risk of war rather than issuing a flat threat — and if it works, the paradox is dissolved without the wrongful intention, so Kavka's disarmament conclusion is avoided. The residual worry is that imposing a risk of accidental war on everyone, including ourselves, may simply be a new wrong traded for the old one."
