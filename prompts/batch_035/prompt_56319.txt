I move in circles where game theory meets decision theory — a reading group that has been meeting for three years, plus an online forum or two — and there is one argument that keeps recurring there and never resolves: whether rational, purely self-interested agents can cooperate in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma. One side holds the classical line: in a genuinely one-shot game, with no repetition, no reputation, no altruism, and no external enforcement, defection is the unique rational choice, and every apparent counterexample turns out to have smuggled one of those elements back in. The other side points to settings where the agents' decision procedures are mutually transparent before they act — two software agents that can read each other's source code before choosing, say. There, each agent's choice is evidence about the other's, and strategies of the form "cooperate if and only if you can verify that the other party will cooperate iff you do" become available; the game-theory literature on this calls it program equilibrium, and there was an earlier, more informal version of the same intuition in Douglas Hofstadter's writings on what he termed superrationality. The transparency camp takes this to show that one-shot cooperation among the self-interested is achievable and rational. The classical camp's reply never varies: you have changed the game — an agent whose procedure is inspectable effectively has more than two moves, the dilemma has been transformed into a different game, and the result says nothing about the original question. The transparency camp's reply never varies either: the original question was never well-posed, because opacity of procedures is not a law of nature but an engineering choice, and it is precisely the tacit decision to hold it fixed that makes defection come out rational. I have watched competent people cycle through these exact moves in person, not just in print, and nobody has budged in years.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about rationality but a disagreement about what may be held fixed in an analysis: the classical side takes the game as given and asks for the rational action within it, the transparency side takes the agent as given and asks for the rational design across games, and each side regards the other's fixed point as illegitimate to fix. But I am not confident in this, because at times it also looks merely verbal — each side may be using 'rational' for a different property (a property of an action given a game, versus a property of an agent given a distribution of games), and I cannot tell how much of the impasse that alone would explain, or whether there is a hidden formal question underneath, about which counterfactuals are legitimate to consider when asking what an agent "could have done." Diagnose the disagreement for me: is it verbal, is it a hidden formal question about the object of choice, are the two sides answering different questions under a single heading, or is it a substantive dispute that is simply hard? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says, including the bits that sit awkwardly with my hypothesis, and be willing to tell me my hypothesis is wrong or incomplete.
