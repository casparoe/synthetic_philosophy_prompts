I'm writing a term paper for a graduate seminar in social choice on what Harsanyi's aggregation theorem actually establishes, and before I commit to a thesis I keep getting pulled in opposite directions by people I trust. My advisor treats the theorem as the strongest formal argument utilitarianism has ever had: if individual and social preferences both satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and a Pareto condition holds, the social ordering must be a weighted sum of individual utilities — done. A postdoc in my group insists the whole thing is a theorem about functional form with no ethical content whatsoever until you supply an interpersonal comparability assumption the theorem cannot itself generate, since vNM utility only represents each person's own risk preferences and is unique merely up to positive affine transformation. I want to adjudicate, and I've realized I can't do that until someone writes me the specification both sides are implicitly arguing against.

So that's the request: not a defense of the theorem, but the desiderata — the list of things any adequate derivation of a social evaluation rule from individual inputs has to deliver. Which cases must it get right and which must it get wrong on principle: the case where a coin flip between two people seems better than a certainty of giving the good to one of them, because ex ante fairness matters and a weighted sum with fixed weights can't see the difference; the cases where individual attitudes before and after uncertainty resolves point in different directions; the dictatorship cases it obviously must exclude. Which distinctions it must respect: between what a vNM utility function is as a representation of preferences over gambles and what utilitarianism needs it to be as a quantity summable across persons; between ex ante and ex post evaluation; between the impartial-observer route and the social aggregation route, in case they place different demands on the list. Which possible axioms and results it can't contradict — including, importantly, whether an equity condition of the Hammond type is compatible at all with ex ante Pareto, and whether the appearance of conflict there is a real joint unsatisfiability in my desiderata or a sign that one of them was a camp preference dressed as a necessity. For each requirement, give the motivating case, mark it as non-negotiable or as something one school insists on and another wouldn't miss, and tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories — that asymmetry is what I actually need for the paper, because if some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, that is itself the result, and no cleverer proof repairs it.

One more thing I'd like woven in rather than tacked on. A friend of mine claims the machinery proves too much: if you replace welfare with any quantity individuals happen to hold vNM-consistent preferences over — dollars, hours of leisure, it wouldn't matter if it were cholesterol — the derivation still coughs out a weighted sum, so either utilitarianism is true of cholesterol or the word welfare never did any work inside the proof. I want to know whether that objection is a confusion or a genuine constraint, and if it's genuine, where it belongs in the specification and what it does to the non-negotiables. If you want to close with two sentences on how the two Harsanyi routes do against your own list, fine, but keep the weight on the criteria — I'll do the scoring in the paper.
