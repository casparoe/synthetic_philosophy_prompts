I write novels, and for some years now my fiction has been circling a pattern of behavior I keep having to stage on the page without ever having a name for it — my editor has started pointing out that the pattern, not the people, seems to be my real subject. The situations vary but the structure is the same. Two parties are locked in a standoff, and the outcome turns not on which of them is stronger or better informed, but on which of them can convincingly close off their own options before the other does. In one manuscript, a partner in a failing firm, on learning his colleague is about to walk out, publicly resigns first and in the most theatrical way possible — the move is ruinous in itself, but it hands him the shape of the aftermath. In another, a woman who suspects her husband is preparing to leave preemptively announces to both families, at a dinner she has engineered for the purpose, that she will never take him back if he goes; the announcement only works because she is constitutionally incapable of walking anything back, and everyone knows it. What interests me most is the temporal structure: the commitments are made under pressure, before the parties could possibly have thought the consequences through, and the person who pauses to deliberate is at a competitive disadvantage against the one who has already — sincerely, irrevocably — stopped deliberating. I've written scenes where both parties, each terrified of being preempted by the other, bind themselves into a jointly catastrophic outcome neither would have chosen with an hour's calm reflection; and scenes where a third character, understanding the mechanism, quietly engineers the race and picks up what's left.

I know Schelling has a body of work on commitment, threats, and burning one's bridges behind one, and I know about brinkmanship as a description of nuclear-era standoffs. But as I read those, they concern one party's clever exploitation of commitment, whereas what I'm describing is something like the race itself — the dynamic in which the availability of commitment mechanisms, plus mutual fear of being beaten to the punch, systematically corrupts the deliberation that ought to go into deciding what to commit to. It has a frankly perverse feature I find very hard to write honestly and would love a cleaner handle on: the mechanism rewards characters for credibly degrading or disabling their own future ability to reconsider, and the more sincerely they have destroyed their capacity to think again, the better their position. It's as if rationality punishes itself in these situations, and I don't know whether that's a real feature of them or an artifact of my staging.

So my question: does this dynamic — the time-pressured race to irrevocable commitment, in which speed beats deliberation and both parties can lose to their own haste — have an established name in game theory, decision theory, or anywhere in the philosophical literature? If it does, I'd like to know what it's called, and more importantly how it relates to and differs from Schelling-style commitment, brinkmanship, and the game of chicken, which seem like near neighbors but not the same thing. If it doesn't, I'd be grateful if you could coin a term and define it carefully enough that I could tell whether a given scene counts as an instance — including the boundary cases that vex me most, such as a commitment made after real deliberation but presented as if spontaneous, or a race in which one party only pretended to commit and got away with it. A bare label won't help me; what I need is the carving — what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
