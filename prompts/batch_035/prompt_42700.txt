I'm a postdoc who reviews for a mid-tier cognitive/psych journal, and the editors keep assigning me papers with evolutionary claims basically because I'm the stats person on the file, not because I know anything about evolutionary biology. Lately it's been nonstop: a paper arguing pregnancy nausea is an adaptation for avoiding toxins right when the fetus is most vulnerable, one explaining the sex difference in appetite for casual sex through parental investment, one on the so-called survival processing effect (people remember words rated for survival relevance better than words rated for moving house, which the authors chalk up to ancestral selection pressures on memory). My default move is muttering "just-so story" and writing a grumpy review, but honestly I can't articulate what separates a testable adaptation claim from a story that happens to fit whatever data it's pointed at, and I'm tired of reviews that are just vibes with citations to the spandrels paper. I need something better than vibes and worse than a degree in evolutionary theory.

Write me a decision procedure I can actually run during the ~4 hours I get per submission. It should be an ordered list of questions: what the authors need to show for the adaptive claim to be doing real work, what counts as a sufficient answer to each (e.g., do they need cross-species or cross-cultural comparison, or is a plausible failure mode table enough?), where the procedure tells me to say "fine paper, drop the adaptation framing" versus "reject," and where it hands the decision back to the editors because the issue is policy or taste, not evidence. It has to handle the byproduct/learning/cultural-transmission rival explanations specifically — how much does it cost the authors that they never mention them? And it should be honest about which steps are secretly judgment calls dressed as checklist items, because if I'm going to be making calls anyway I'd like to know which ones they are. I can't demand new data collection, and I can't re-run their analyses, so build to that constraint.

Then run the procedure on the three submissions above. For what it's worth, my priors going in are that the morning sickness one is the classic suspect, the casual sex one is probably fine, and the memory one is solid experimental work — so if the procedure disagrees with any of those, say so plainly, including where it's forced to shrug. I want to see at least one case where the procedure tells me the paper survives with a caveat I don't enjoy writing, and one where it gives me a firm answer on a case where I genuinely couldn't tell. Also flag where the procedure would misfire — e.g., on a claim that's genuinely novel and so fails my "did anyone predict this in advance" test for reasons that aren't the authors' fault.
