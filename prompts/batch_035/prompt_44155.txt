I've been circling a reductio of all frequency-based inference, generalized out of the reference class problem, and I can't decide whether it has a real flaw or whether the flaw is one I'm attached to refusing.

The argument runs as follows. (1) To assign a probability to a single event on the basis of observed frequencies, I must place that event in a reference class — the class of cases relevantly similar to it — and my probability is a function of the class I choose. This is as true of an actuary pricing a life-insurance policy for a forty-year-old man as it is of anything else: the mortality figure changes depending on whether the class is all forty-year-olds, all forty-year-old men in his country, all forty-year-old men with his blood pressure and smoking history, and so on down toward the single case, where the class becomes the man himself and frequency talk loses its grip. (2) There is no non-arbitrary principle that selects the correct class. Reichenbach's own recommendation — use the narrowest class for which the statistics are still adequate — is a pragmatic directive, not an identification of a fact of the matter, and it is hostage to what counts as adequate statistics. Broadening the class adds irrelevant cases; narrowing it shrinks the sample; and every similarity relation that could privilege a class (same species, same disease, same hospital) is itself just another respect of resemblance among infinitely many available ones, with no reason given why that one is the evidentially right one. (3) If no class is the correct one, then there is no fact of the matter which probability the event has. (4) If there is no fact of the matter which probability the event has, then no particular credence about the event is warranted — not merely less than certain, but unwarranted, since a warranted credence would have to answer to something. (5) By (1), every application of frequency-based reasoning involves a single event placed in a class; so (3) and (4) apply to every frequency-based credence without exception. (6) Therefore actuarial science, clinical trials, weather forecasting, quality control, and indeed all of statistics yield no warranted credences whatsoever.

I take (6) to be intolerable, so something before it is wrong, but I genuinely cannot see which step is the culprit. (1) looks like a truism about how frequencies get applied. (2) strikes me as well supported: I know of no account of the correct reference class that doesn't either stipulate a class on non-evidential grounds or collapse into choosing the single case, where frequency goes trivial. (3) and (4) each feel like bookkeeping rather than substantive claims — 'no correct class, so no fact of the matter, so no warranted credence' — and yet the route from (1) to (6) somehow turns two plausibilities and some bookkeeping into the destruction of empirical inference.

Please tell me exactly which step to reject, why that step looked fine to me (it looked fine to me in my seminar too), and what the rejection costs — what I give up or have to replace with something more complicated. If you think the flaw is a single equivocation that runs across several steps, say which word or phrase is doing it and where its meaning shifts. Two further constraints on the answer: I would very much like to keep (1) and (6), so if one of them must go, be explicit about exactly how bad that is. And if your diagnosis leaves the Doomsday argument and other anthropic cases exactly where it leaves the actuary — in the same boat, or in importantly different boats — say which, and why.
