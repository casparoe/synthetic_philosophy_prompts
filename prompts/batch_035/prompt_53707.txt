A philosopher friend has talked me into an argument I can't refute but can't accept, and I'd like a careful diagnosis of it. The subject is pest control, though by the end it isn't really about pest control at all.

His argument:

(1) Suffering is bad wherever it occurs, and one instance counts against the world no less for being small or for belonging to a small creature; disvalue aggregates across individuals.
(2) The average insect life is very likely worse than nothing: most individuals die within days of hatching, of starvation, desiccation, injury, or predation, and there is no stage of insect life that goes well enough, for long enough, to plausibly outweigh this.
(3) Insect populations are so vast that total wild insect suffering plausibly dwarfs all human and farmed-animal suffering combined.
(4) If a state of affairs contains vast amounts of disvalue, we have a pro tanto duty to replace it with a better state of affairs whenever we can do so at reasonable cost.
(5) Death is not much of a harm to an insect. It has no long-term projects to be deprived of, and even on a deprivation account the loss is small — a few more days of what premise (2) says awaits it anyway.
(6) Modern insecticides can eliminate insect populations quickly, cheaply, and at enormous scale.
(7) Therefore we have a pro tanto duty to destroy insect populations wherever it is cheap to do so — including in wilderness areas that serve no human interest — and conserving insect biodiversity is, other things equal, a way of perpetuating a vast pool of suffering.

He insists each premise is more plausible than its denial: (1) is just anti-speciesism plus arithmetic; (2) is the standard starting point of welfare biology; (4) is the same principle that generates our duties to distant strangers; and he points out that a few people in the wild-animal-suffering literature have bitten something close to this bullet, one of them suggesting we may have reason to welcome habitat loss. But (7) strikes me as monstrous, and not merely because of side effects. Suppose the spraying were ecologically surgical — no food-web collapse, no groundwater contamination, no cascade onto birds or fish. It would still seem wrong to seek out a thriving insect ecosystem and end it, and I can't believe that the reason it's wrong is just that we'd be bad at it.

I can see three escape hatches and distrust all of them. I could reject the aggregation in (1), holding that tiny sufferings don't sum into overwhelming reasons — but I can't state a version of that which doesn't look ad hoc, and it threatens my reasons for caring about factory farming. I could reject (2) as epistemically overreaching — we simply don't know that insect lives are net-negative — but if there's a serious probability they are, my friend can rerun the argument in expected-value terms. Or I could reject (4) as stated, denying a duty to reduce suffering we didn't cause — but that principle is doing a lot of quiet work elsewhere in my ethics.

So tell me: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the flaw isn't a false premise but a slide between steps — two things being treated as one somewhere in the chain — name the slide and say exactly where it happens and why it looked harmless. Also tell me whether the argument's reach really stops at insects, or whether whatever blocks it must also block conclusions about any numerous species whose members plausibly have bad lives.

As you answer, please be explicit about your confidence: distinguish the parts of your diagnosis you would defend in print from the parts that are hunches, and single out the one step or claim in the argument that you suspect everything turns on but that you are least sure how to evaluate.
