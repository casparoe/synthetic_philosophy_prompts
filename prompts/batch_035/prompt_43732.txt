I want to get serious about sphexishness — Hofstadter's coinage for the thing the digger wasp does: drag the paralysed prey to the edge of the burrow, pop inside to check it, and if you nudge the prey a few centimetres away while she's checking, she comes out, finds it, drags it back to the exact same spot, and checks the burrow again — apparently you can loop her forever. People reach for the wasp as a mascot for behaviour that looks intelligent but is just a rigid routine with no re-planning. Before I go read anyone's actual theory of what separates sphexish behaviour from genuinely flexible behaviour, I want the spec such a theory has to meet. Write me the desiderata for an adequate account of the sphexish/non-sphexish divide: which cases it has to classify correctly (the wasp, obviously, but also a chess engine that falls into the same opening trap three times in one game and then stops falling for it; a human making coffee on autopilot who still copes when the milk's gone off; a cleaning robot that re-vacuums a patch when you shift the furniture mid-cycle; a student who rehearsed a presentation and genuinely cannot improvise when interupted); the distinctions it can't afford to collapse (can't adapt versus can't be bothered; rigid because the sensors are limited versus rigid because the policy is); the empirical stuff about animals it can't contradict (lots of allegedly dumb creatures re-route fine when the world changes, and clever ones freeze in odd conditions); and whether it owes us anything about free will or should just stay out of that. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration behind it, in a sentence. Mark which you think are non-negotiable and which are one camp's prejudice dressed up as a requirement. And flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — I have a hunch that "the distinction must be gradable, not binary" and "it must predict a fairly sharp cut-off somewhere among animals" are going to fight, but check that properly rather than just agreeing with me. Then, two or three sentences each, say how an internal-model story (the creature represents the situation, the wasp doesn't) and a hierarchical-control story (routines stacked on routines, with an override level that notices when a routine is misfiring) do against your list — that's the part I'll want to extend later, so be honest about where the list is thin. Keep it under 650 words total; the criteria matter more than the scorecard.
