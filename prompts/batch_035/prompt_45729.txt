The exam question was:

"Consider the sentence: 'It is raining, but I don't believe that it is.' (a) Explain why this sentence is not a logical contradiction -- that is, why it could be true. (b) Explain why asserting it nevertheless strikes us as absurd. (c) Is the past-tense version -- 'It was raining, but I didn't believe that it was' -- equally absurd to assert? Defend your answer."

And here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Moore's paradox shows that there are sentences that are formally consistent yet cannot be sensibly asserted. 'It is raining, but I don't believe that it is' is not a logical contradiction: there is nothing impossible about it raining while I fail to believe that it is, since I might be asleep, distracted, or simply stubborn about a forecast. The contradiction appears only at the level of assertion. When I assert that p, I express my belief that p; so in uttering this sentence I commit myself to believing that it is raining while simultaneously saying that I don't. The sentence is thus a cousin of the liar paradox: like the liar, it is a sentence that undermines itself by talking about the speaker's own mental state. Indeed, the same absurdity attaches to the second form, 'It is raining, but I believe that it is not raining' -- and this is really just the same statement over again, since not believing that p and believing that not-p amount to the same thing. As for the past-tense version, since the structure of the sentence is exactly the same, merely with the tense changed, the absurdity persists just as much: I would still be asserting that something was so while denying that I beleive it."
