I've spent my career in ethics and moral psychology, teaching the personal identity material every year without ever committing my own views to paper. That changes next month: I've been invited to give a colloquium on what matters in survival, and I want to present my actual position rather than a survey. Before I send off the abstract, I need to know whether the position hangs together. Here it is, stated as plainly as I can.

(1) What matters in survival is psychological continuity — overlapping chains of strong connectedness in memory, intention, and character — and it comes in degrees; numerical identity is not what matters. (2) Identity cannot be what I care about when I care about my future, because identity is one-one while what I care about can be preserved one-many: in fission, where my hemispheres go to two recipients both psychologically continuous with me, I remain identical to neither, yet each of them gets everything that matters. (3) I would enter Parfit's teletransporter without hesitation — the person who steps out on Mars has everything that matters, so my body's destruction on Earth costs me nothing I have reason to care about. (4) Death is bad for the one who dies by deprivation: killing harms the victim by denying her goods she would otherwise have received, and that harm, not the breach of autonomy alone, is what grounds the wrongness of killing. (5) Advance directives bind across the erosion of the mind: a patient's earlier, competent self has the authority to refuse life-sustaining treatment for the later, severely demented self, and that authority does not fade as connectedness between author and patient dwindles toward nothing. (6) I stand by the fission verdicts in (2), and in the seminar room I'm tempted to summarize them by saying that I will be both of the future people — though of course the two future people will not be each other.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture. Go through the set and tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension, which only look to be, and, for each genuine tension, what the cheapest repair is and what that repair costs me elsewhere in the set. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damning. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to stabilize the package — or, if the package is stable enough to present as is, tell me that and say where I should expect the hard questions in the Q&A. I know the Parfit literature as well as you do; don't re-explain it to me.
