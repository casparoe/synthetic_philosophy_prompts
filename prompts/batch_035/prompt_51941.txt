I hold a broadly Dennettian compatibilist view of free will: free will is not a power to escape causation but an evolved kind of flexibility — freedom from sphexishness, the term Dennett introduced in Elbow Room for the digger wasp that runs a rigid, pre-loaded routine and can be thrown into a pointless loop by a small nudge from the environment. A free creature, on this view, can notice its own routines, represent them as revisable, and reprogram them in light of anticipated consequences and social pressure; responsibility tracks this graded capacity for self-monitoring and self-revision, not some uncaused self. What would count against it? I am especially interested in three lines of attack: (1) the empirical footing — the classic wasp anecdote is reportedly exaggerated, with later observers finding the animals far less rigid than the folklore suggests; if sphexishness is partly a myth, does the contrast anchoring my view survive, or does it merely need re-description? (2) the vagueness of flexibility — modern AI systems are behaviorally flexible in ways wasps are not, yet many doubt they have anything worth calling free will; where does the view draw the line, and could an AI counterexample force an awkward concession? (3) the risk of insulation — if any flexible-seeming behavior counts as proto-freedom and any rigid-seeming behavior is dismissed as sphexishness we are only less obviously subject to, what observation could ever refute the view? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which a committed Dennettian would concede as genuinely counting against the position, and give an honest verdict on whether the view is well insulated — and, if it is, whether that is a defect for a thesis of this kind.
