My friend and I both belong to a club founded in the 1870s, and he has served for years on its general purposes committee, which votes on proposals to abolish the odder survivals in our rulebook. He describes himself as a Burkean, and he is fond of invoking the idea of Chesterton's fence — that a reformer should not clear away a practice until he understands why it was put there. His voting record, though, does not follow any single simple rule I can identify. When someone proposed allowing members to be proposed and seconded for membership by email rather than in person at a meeting — a requirement nobody could explain — he voted to keep it. Some months later a member found in the archive that the in-person requirement existed so that objections could be raised face to face before the ballot, and, the club having since instituted a written objection window that everyone agrees does the same job, he voted to abolish the requirement. Yet when the committee took up black tie at the annual dinner — a practice whose origin is well documented, the dinner having once been shared with a London society that the dress code marked out — he voted to keep it, even though that society is long defunct and every member can recite the history. When pressed, he said that the stated reason for a practice is rarely the only reason it exists.

Next month the committee votes on three further items, and I would like a careful prediction of how he will vote on each, not one made by vibes. First, the toast to the club's founding patron, drunk at every dinner: the patron died in 1912, the estate has had no connection with the club for a century, and the origin of the toast, deference to a living benefactor, is documented and plainly dead. Second, the rule that the captain must buy the first round after a lost race: the ledger shows this began as a self-imposed penalty introduced by a captain in the 1930s, whose point is obvious and, arguably, still operative in holding the crew together after a defeat. Third, the requirement that the minute book be read aloud in full at the annual general meeting, for which nobody has produced any explanation at all and which the members visibly resent. Please set out the rules that could fit his record so far — for instance, a rule turning on whether the original reason is known and now dead, one turning on whether the practice is administrative or expressive, one turning on whether its full working in the club's life is transparent — say what each rule predicts for each of the three items, give your best guess as to his actual votes with your reasons for preferring it, and then name the case, among these three or hypothetical, that would best discriminate between the candidate rules.

Two requests about the manner of the answer. First, please keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know Chesterton and Burke: I take it as settled that the fence is a rule about the reformer's knowledge rather than a claim that every inherited practice is good, but I gather it is contested how strong a presumption the fence creates, and whether Burke's appeal to inherited prejudice supports my friend's votes on expressive practices at all; please flag such points as contested rather than presenting one reading as the consensus. Second, if you think his record is better explained by plain status quo bias than by any principled Burkeanism, say so plainly — I would rather have that than a charitable reconstruction he would not recognize.
