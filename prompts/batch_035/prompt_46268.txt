I've been reading rationalist blogs and decision theory forums since the late 2000s, and for most of that time I've treated the Dutch book machinery as settled background — the sort of thing you cite in passing the way you'd cite arithmetic. Static incoherence means a bookie can construct a sure loss against you, so probability axioms are mandatory; the diachronic extension shows that an agent who updates by anything other than conditionalization can be pumped across time; and the money-pump analogues are supposed to do the same work for the expected utility axioms. That's the folk epistemology of my corner of the internet, and I absorbed it without much scrutiny.

Lately I've been trying to work out how much of that scaffolding actually survives careful examination, and specifically the diachronic half, which is the part everyone casually relies on when they say that rational agents must Bayes-update. Here is the proposition I want you to assess:

A diachronic Dutch book argument genuinely justifies conditionalization as a norm of epistemic rationality — that is, an agent who plans to update by some rule other than conditionalization is thereby irrational, not merely exploitable-by-a-sufficiently-informed-bookie.

By "resolution," I mean: on the best currently available philosophical analysis, does the argument succeed on its strongest reconstruction, not whether violators can literally be pumped for money (everyone agrees they can, under the right conditions).

My credence is about 0.55. My friend, who is also a longtime forum-goer but more of a pragmatist about epistemic norms, puts it at 0.85. I want your number, but more importantly the decomposition. At minimum, I'd want terms for these sub-questions, plus any others you think belong:

1. Whether the betting interpretation of credence is legitimate here — whether it's coherent to treat a credence as something that licenses accepting bets at matching odds in the first place, since agents with risk aversion, declining marginal utility, or simply a refusal to gamble can violate the axioms without any incoherence in their beliefs.
2. Whether diachronic sure-loss tracks epistemic irrationality or only practical exposure — the worry that the argument shows you can be made to lose money, which is a pragmatic fact, and that nothing follows about the rationality of your beliefs unless you add a bridge premise connecting vulnerability to incoherence.
3. Whether the bookie's informational position can be neutralized — the standard objection that the diachronic bookie has to know your updating rule and your future credences in order to set up the sequence of bets, and that this seems to let the bookie exploit agents who are perfectly coherent but merely predictable, which suggests the argument proves too much.
4. Whether the resolute-choice or precommitment defense fails — the thought that an agent who can bind herself to a policy, or who doesn't regard her future selves' credences as authoritative for her present betting behavior, escapes the pump, so the argument at most justifies adopting conditionalization as a policy rather than being required to conditionalize on each occasion.
5. Whether the depragmatized variants succeed — the attempts to reconstruct the argument so that the sure-loss is a formal symptom of inconsistency among credences rather than a route to losing money, with the bookie appearing, if at all, only as a device.

Give me a rough probability for each of these, one or two lines of justification apiece, and then the combination into your overall credence. Then tell me: which term is the final number most sensitive to, what specific argument or consideration would move that term most, and — given that I sit at 0.55 and my friend at 0.85 — which sub-question you think the two of us most likely disagree about. My own guess is that our disagreement lives in term 3, since he tends to think the bookie's knowledge can be stipulated away without damage, but I'd like to know if you read the disagreement differently.
