I could use some help with a puzzle that has stopped being academic for me, because my logic professor has actually done the thing in question. At the end of last week's seminar, she announced, verbatim as far as I can reconstruct it: "Next week there will be exactly one exam, on one of the five weekdays, and on the morning of that day you will not be able to know, on the basis of this announcement, that the exam is that day." She has apparently run this announcement every year for a decade, and by all accounts the exam has always caught the class off guard. My older brother, a graduate student in logic, spent dinner on Sunday walking me through the following argument, and I cannot find a flaw in it: (1) The exam cannot be on Friday, because by Thursday evening, having had no exam Monday through Thursday, we could deduce from the announcement that Friday is the only remaining day, and then we would know in advance, contradicting the announcement. (2) Given (1), the exam cannot be on Thursday either, because by Wednesday evening we could deduce Thursday as the last possible day. (3) Iterating this reasoning eliminates Wednesday, then Tuesday, then Monday, one at a time. (4) So no day is consistent with the announcement, and the announcement cannot be fulfilled. (5) Therefore my professor has told us something impossible, and there can be no exam next week.

Two things about my brother's use of the argument bother me, and this is where I would especially like your help. First, he goes beyond the case at hand: he claims the argument shows not merely that my professor happened to overreach, but that any announcement of a future surprise is logically self-defeating regardless of the announcer's reliability — so, for instance, the dean's promised "surprise audit sometime this semester" is impossible in principle, not just unlikely. Second, he sharpens the point with a one-day version: if the announcement had been "there will be an exam tomorrow, and you will not know in advance," that would be flatly self-contradictory in the way an assertion like "it will rain tomorrow, but you won't believe it" is, and he says the five-day case is just that pathology wearing a disguise. His favorite flourish is the epilogue: once you accept the proof, you stop expecting an exam at all, so the exam can now land on any day and genuinely surprise you — which makes the announcement true after all, and your proof the very thing that refuted your knowledge. He concludes that such announcements are neither true nor false but self-undermining in some way logic hasn't yet classified, which struck me as suspiciously dramatic.

My situation is practical as well as intellectual: exam week begins Monday, and I would rather not bet my grade on step (5). Please tell me where the chain breaks — which premise or inference I should give up — and what giving it up costs. I am strongly inclined to keep two things: the plain observation that my professor's announcement can be true (she has made good on it repeatedly), and the reading of the announcement my brother and I both accepted, under which the Friday elimination in step (1) looks airtight. If those cannot both be kept, I want to know which one survives and exactly what the standard responses each pay for their solution — in particular, whether any of them vindicates my brother's sweeping conclusion about announced surprises in general, or whether that conclusion is where his version first slides from what the argument actually establishes.
