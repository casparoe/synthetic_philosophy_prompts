My friend Nadia has very firm views about moral worth — the Kantian-flavored question of when an action done right still doesn't count morally because of why you did it. We keep testing her with hypotheticals at dinner, and I've started keeping a log because I think there's a system in there. Her verdicts so far: the shopkeeper who's scrupulously honest with customers purely because honesty keeps them coming back, and who would cheat the moment it became the better business move — no moral worth. The passerby who sees a child drowning and jumps in moved purely by the child's panic, never once thinking about duty or morality, and who we all agree would have jumped even at real risk to herself — moral worth. The guy who mails back a lost wallet stuffed with cash solely because he believes morality demands it — feels no sympathy for the owner, doesn't want a reward, just grimly does what he thinks he must — moral worth. And the woman who gives generously to charity but only through programs where her name goes on a plaque, and who stopped giving entirely when her employer switched to anonymous donations — no moral worth.

Next dinner I'm bringing three new cases and I want to walk in with predictions. Case one: Theo, who helps people constantly, happily does it anonymously and at real cost to himself, but when asked why, says honestly that helping is his greatest pleasure in life. He only ever helps people whose suffering he can vividly picture — a neighbor's limp moves him, but famine statistics leave him completely cold, and he gives nothing to distant causes. Case two: Maya, who pulls a stranger out of a river partly because a crowd is watching and cheering, and partly because the stranger's panic genuinely grips her — she admits she's not sure she'd have jumped if the riverbank had been empty. Case three: Jonas, who keeps every promise with religious strictness, but not because of anything owed to the person he promised — he keeps them because breaking one would make him "the kind of person who breaks promises," and he couldn't stand to be that person.

What will Nadia say about each? I'm less interested in the bare answers than in the rule she seems to be running — I can see at least two or three that fit her record equally well so far (something like "worth when you act from the features that actually make the act right," versus "worth as long as self-interest isn't in the driver's seat," versus something about what you'd do if the incentives flipped) — and in which of these three cases those rules actually come apart, since some of them might agree. Give me your best guesses with your reasons for preferring one rule over the others, and since I've been wrong about her before, also tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what consideration would push you to a different prediction — like, if she turns out to waver on one of the older cases, which one would most change the picture? And if the record really underdetermines some verdict, say so rather than forcing it. Please write it as plain flowing prose like you're emailing me back, no bullet points or headings or bold or anything like that.
