"Herbert Butterfield's 1931 pamphlet The Whig Interpretation of History diagnosed a recurring temptation in historical writing. And here's the student's answer, typed out as recieved:

"Butterfield coined 'the Whig interpretation of history' to name the habit of telling the past as the story of the present's arrival: the historian picks out the actors and movements that seem to be pointing toward us, treats them as the protagonists, and casts everyone else as obstacles on the road. The classic case is the Macaulay-style narrative in which the Protestant Reformation is retold as the long march toward constitutional liberty and religious toleration, so that everyone who resisted it becomes a villain in a story whose happy ending is the historian's own present. In other words, Whig history is essentially history written by the winners — the winners being whoever ended up on the side of the values we now hold — which is why Butterfield named it after the Whig historians, the liberal Protestants who wrote English history as the slow triumph of their own party's principles.

He says the temptation is hard to resist for two reasons. First, the present is where we necessarily stand, so our questions are already our questions, and arranging the past by its relevance to them makes a cleaner and more dramatic story than studying a period for its own sake, with all its dead ends and its people heading in directions that led nowhere. Second, it flatters the present: if history was working toward us all along, then our values aren't just ours but the point of the whole process.

His cure, though, is extreme: the historian must abstain from moral judgment altogether. Since we can't rank past actors from our vantage point without smuggling in the progress story, the only honest posture is description without evaluation. And if that's Butterfield's position, then it follows that every history written from a later standpoint is inescapably Whig — everyone stands somewhere in time — so the warning ends up applying to everything and therefore to nothing. At that point the pamphlet is really just a stylistic preference for technical research over narrative."
