Been trying to work out what I actually think about money pump arguments by going with my gut on cases. An agent whose preferences go in a cycle (A over B, B over C, C over A) and who will accept each trade for a small fee: irrational, that's the classic pump. Same cyclic agent, but she refuses any trade once she's been offered the same item twice: the pump stalls, but her prefernces still seem irrational to me. An agent with transitive preferences that violate the sure-thing principle: also seems irrational, and I gather you can still pump her somehow, fine. An agent who's exploitable only by a bookie who knows her exact indifference thresholds, so in practice nobody can pump her: feels rational, maybe? An agent who precommits to a plan and sticks to it: no pump, rational, fine. But an agent who just says "that's it, I don't do trades anymore" whenever things smell fishy feels like she dodged the argument rather than answered it. Is there a principle for when exploitability actually shows irrationality? My hunch is something like "the preferences are the problem, not the pump," but that can't be the whole story, or the threshold agent and the stalling agent come out the same, and they don't feel the same. Which of my verdicts would your principle overturn, and what would it say about an agent who's happy to pay a tiny fee just to keep her options open?
