An old coworker of mine, let's call her Dana, is applying for a team-lead position and listed me as a reference. Here's my problem: Dana is genuinely excellent at the technical side, but as a lead she was rough. Two people on her team quit within a year, and one of them told me flat out it was because of her. I promised Dana I'd be a reference, and I don't want to torpedo her, but I also don't feel right vouching for her as a manager.

My plan was a reference letter built out of things that are all true: she knows the product better then anyone I've ever worked with, she single-handedly rescued a project in 2021, etc. Every sentence checkable. My brother says this is fine, full stop. His take is that lying is wrong, but anything you say that's true can't be dishonest — that's just where the line is. And it's not like I'd be inventing praise; I mean every word of it.

The part that bugs me is that I'd be picking exactly those truths because I know how a reference letter reads. If the letter is two paragraphs of strengths and zero mention of how she manages people, any reader walks away thinking the absence means there's nothing to say. Nobody writes a letter that only lists the good stuff unless there is nothing else — that's just how the genre works. So I'd be building a false belief out of true parts, and my brother's rule says that's automatically fine because each part passed the test individually.

And then yesterday the recruiter called and asked me straight out: any concerns about her managing a team? I said I can only really speak to her technical work. Also true! But I could feel what it did — it sounded like a boundary about my knowledge, when actually I have a very specific concern I'm choosing not to share.

So I guess my question is whether the line my brother draws — say only true things and you're in the clear — is actually the moral line, or just a legal-ish line. A few things I keep going back and forth on. If every sentence I say is true, have I actually done anything wrong? If the reader's false belief comes from their own inference about what I left out, is it partly on them — nobody told them to assume the letter is exhaustive? Or is that a cop-out, since I know exactly what conclusion they'll draw and I'm engineering it? And is there a real difference between what I'm doing and just lying outright, given that both of us — me and a hypothetical liar — know the same false belief will result either way? The only difference seems to be that I've arranged for the reader to do the last step themselves, like I'm handing them the pieces of a conclusion and letting them assemble it. Does that change who's responsible, or does it just give me deniability with myself? Basically I want to know if my brother's rule is right, or if it's a loophole dressed up as a principle. And if it is a loophole, what do I actually do about the reference, given that I did promise?
