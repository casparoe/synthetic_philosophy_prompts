I work in the ethics/safeguards review unit of a regional development agency. We sign off on projects that can plausibly change who gets born a generation or two later — big resettlement schemes, dams, family planning programs, even cash transfers that shift when people marry and have kids. Someone on our team read about Parfit's nonidentity problem and pointed out that our whole harm-based framework quietly assumes the affected people would have existed anyway. She's right that once a policy changes who will be born, the complaint "this harms future people" gets slippery: the person born under the policy can't coherantly complain if their life is worth living and the alternative was that they'd never exist at all. Our review forms still have a checkbox that says "adverse effects on future generations," and nobody knows what to do with it in exactly the cases it was written for.

So: you're a professional philosopher specializing in population ethics, and I want you to think carefully about this — maybe start with a short brainstorm about which distinctions actually matter for a working tool and which we can drop, before writing the procedure itself. Then give us:

1. A screening question or two that tells a review officer when the nonidentity issue is even in play (most projects won't trigger it — help us not burn time on the ones that don't).

2. The ordered checklist: what to establish at each step, what counts as a sufficient answer, where it resolves, and where it stops and escalates to the panel because it's a question of values rather than facts. I'd expect at least one such handoff around the "is a life worth living" threshold — tell me honestly whether that's a judgment call dressed up as a step, and whether there's any defensible default we can just write down, or whether we should admit there isn't.

3. It should handle the distinction between choices affecting the same people and choices affecting the same number of different people, in plain language, because our officers will not read Parfit. If some distinction genuinely can't survive translation into plain language without becoming wrong, say that instead of pretending.

4. Worked runs on three cases: (a) a dam that floods a valley and resettles 40,000 people, changing marriage and livelihood patterns so that within a generation different people are being born downstream; (b) a voluntary family planning program in a region where most pregnancies are already wanted, so the program mostly changes timing; (c) a watershed restoration project whose effects all land within the lifetimes of people already alive.

5. One of the worked runs should come out somewhere the panel will hate — for instance that, on a strict nonidentity view, the resettlement case can't be criticized as harming future people at all, only via the currently living. Show what the procedure can and can't do there, and what we should write in the file instead of a fake answer.

Constraints, so you calibrate: roughly fifteen triggering projects a year, medium stakes (livelihoods, usually not life and death), each review has to be doable by two non-philosphers in about an hour, sign-off is a two-officer panel, and we have no budget for a per-project external philosopher. So no literature survey, no name-dropping for its own sake — the theory has to come out the other end as a tool. But don't dumb it down to the point where it's quietly wrong; if simplification loses something that matters, flag it.
