I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar this term and we just did a unit on what emotions actually are -- the old three-way fight between emotions as judgments (you can't fear the dog without believing it's dangerous), emotions as perceptions of value-laden situations, and emotions as just feelings of bodily churn, a la William James. For my presentation next week I want to go after an analogy that a friend of mine from an evolutionary psych course keeps using and that I suspect is doing more work than it admits: the "emotions are smoke detectors" picture. The evolutionary psych version is sometimes called the smoke detector principle: false alarms are cheap and misses are catastrophic, so any well-designed threat system will be ratcheted way toward over-firing -- it's better to bolt upright at 3am because of a branch against the window ten times than to sleep through the burglar once. My friend extends it from anxiety specifically to emotions generally: fear, disgust, anger etc. are all detectors built for fitness-relevant situations, they're calibrated by ancestral cost ratios rather than by accuracy, and -- here's the step I want to examine -- therefore an emotion is a *perception* of a danger (or contamination, or betrayal) in roughly the way an itch is a perception, and not a judgment, since detectors don't judge anything, they just fire. She also uses it to say that calling an emotion "irrational" is like calling a smoke detector stupid for going off when I burn toast.

I have to present on this and I don't want to just assert that analogies always break down somewhere; I want the actual audit. Give me the correspondence table first -- what plays the role of the smoke, the fire, the alarm, the threshold, the designer who set the cost tradeoff, the person who installs and relocates the thing -- and then the places the mapping fails. Some disanalogies I half-see (emotions can be recalibrated by learning in a way my hallway detector can't, and the "false positive" cost landscape has apparently changed massively since the savanna) but I don't know which failures actually matter for her conclusion versus which are just quibbles. Then tell me the real question: does the smoke detector picture support the *perception* view of emotions over the judgment and feeling views, or is that an extra step she's smuggling in -- is "built to detect X" enough to make a state a perception of X, and would the detector story also be compatible with a judgment theorist helping themselves to it? And separately, does "cheap false alarms" actually vindicate her move that irrationality talk is a category error, or does it just explain where the emotion came from without settling whether it can still be evaluated as fitting its object? If it helps, feel free to say what a better analogy would be for whatever claim survives. My presentation is supposed to be 20 minutes with discussion after, so I basically need your audit to be the spine of it.
