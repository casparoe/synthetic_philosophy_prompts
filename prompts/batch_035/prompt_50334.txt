My friend Dahlia and I sit in on a decision theory reading group, and over the years I've collected a pretty good ledger of her verdicts on the puzzle cases we argue about. When a near-perfect predictor offers her the standard two boxes — a transparent thousand in box A, and a million in box B if and only if it predicted she'd take only B — she takes only B, and she does the same in the variant where box B is glass and she can see the million sitting in it. Paired with an atom-for-atom copy of herself in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, she cooperates. In the case where a pre-existing mouth lesion causes both a craving for a certain chewing gum and, later, disease, while the gum itself slightly protects, she chews, even knowing the chewers' grim statistics. And in the counterfactual mugging — a fair coin was flipped yesterday, and now that it's come up tails she's offered the chance to pay $100 to a stranger who, had it come up heads, would have handed her $10,000 if and only if she was going to be the sort who pays here — she pays, without much agonizing. What she won't do is explain herself in general terms; she just has confident verdicts, one case at a time, and they've started to feel like a system I can't name.

Now our group has taken up the old Death in Damascus scenario, tweaked a bit, and there's a bet riding on what she'll say. In our version, Death — who in the story has been right about 95% of the people he's ever predicted — catches up with Dahlia on a street in Damascus and looks visibly startled to find her there; he tells her he'll be waiting for her tomorrow in Aleppo. She can stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo before dawn. Two questions I want your prediction on. First, does she stay or flee? I notice that his being startled in Damascus seems to tell her something about where he expects her tomorrow, but I can't tell whether that's the kind of thing her verdicts so far would have her use or exactly the kind she'd refuse to use. Second, there's a fair coin on the table, and Death's 95% record is a record of predicting people's choices: does she think flipping it improves her chances, leaves them the same, or makes things worse?

I'm less interested in the answers themselves than in the rule she seems to be running. It can't be plain news-value reasoning (the gum case rules that out) and it can't be plain causal reasoning (the boxes rule that out), so something else is doing the work — but I can see at least two readings of her track record that fit it equally well: one on which she chooses as if her current decision settles the output of every process that computes her, prediction or twin or branch, and one on which she treats those computed copies as teammates whose welfare counts alongside her own. If those two rules really do fit her record equally well, tell me whether they come apart in the Damascus case, which of them you'd bet she's running and why, and what further case — something constructible, spelled out — would settle which rule it is. If you think a third rule fits her five verdicts better than either, say so and hold it to the same tests.
