my roommate and I have been going back and forth all semester about whether "generations" are real things or just marketing categories with a sociology veneer, and he came back from break with an argument I genuinely can't find my way out of. It goes like this. (1) A real kind is just a grouping that supports reliable induction and explanation: if knowing that something is an F non-accidentally helps you predict other things about it, then F is a real kind. (2) Vague boundaries and a human-invented label are no disqualifier, since biological species are real kinds despite vague boundaries, ring species, and the fact that the whole taxonomic apparatus is in some sense something we built — if "vague and human-drawn" ruled something out, species would go too, and that's crazy. (3) Birth cohort does support induction and explanation: sociologists regress attitudes and outcomes on cohort all the time, and there are plausible mechanisms — the technologies you grew up with, what the housing market was doing when you turned 25 — that leave real marks on people. (4) So generations are real kinds, in the same sense species are. (5) And the fact that the official lines are a bit arbitrary (why does the boomer box end in 1964? why do millennials start in 1981?) matters no more than it matters that we had to pick some point where, say, one population split off from another.

So I tried to run the same template on a grouping I'm confident is NOT a real kind, and it seems to go through, which makes me think his argument proves too much. Take overlapping slices: instead of "born 1965–1980" use "born 1967–1979," or 1968–1980, or any of the dozens of near-miss windows. Sociologists could regress outcomes on those too and get predictive power, so by his step (1) each of them is a real kind, and now we've got dozens of almost-but-not-quite-generations all carving nature at their joints simultaneously, which can't be right. But then I'm stuck, because I can't point to which premise fails. (3) worries me the most — I've read that you can't cleanly separate cohort effects from age effects and period effects, since anyone born earlier is also older and everything happens at some moment, so maybe the "cohort effects" his step rides on are partly an illusion the statistics can't break apart. But I also can't see what's wrong with (2), since it really does seem like species and generations have the same vague-boundary structure, and I'd honestly LIKE the conclusion to stand — "ok boomer" is doing real explanatory work in my life. Which step should I reject, and what do I have to give up to reject it? Also tell me if the overlapping-slices objection is actually good, or if there's some way his template escapes it that I'm not seeing.
