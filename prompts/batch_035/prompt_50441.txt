I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a mid-size fertility clinic that has its own embryo genetics lab. A case has split the committee roughly down the middle, and I need to circulate a written opinion before next Tuesday's meeting. Please draft it for me.

The case, as stipulated in the referral: a couple created five viable embryos through IVF. The husband carries a known familial variant that causes a severe adult-onset neurodegenerative disease; preimplantation testing identifies three of the five embryos as free of the variant. The couple is happy to restrict transfer to those three — that part has never been in dispute. But they have also asked the lab to run a polygenic score for educational attainment on the three and to transfer whichever embryo scores highest. They are explicit that this second request is not about disease: they say they simply want to give their child the best start they can, the same reason they'd choose a good school district.

Our genetic counselor's note contains two facts the committee agreed to treat as settled for the purposes of the discussion. First, a polygenic score for educational attainment currently predicts only a few percent of the variance in the outcome. Second, and because of that, the spread between the three embryos' predicted scores is well within the noise: the ranking the couple is asking for is close to arbitrary. So the committee is not deciding whether to confer a real cognitive benefit. We are deciding whether to perform a procedure whose main effect may be on how the parents come to regard the child — the chosen one as "the good draw," the untransferred ones as what was passed over.

One half of the committee holds that refusing would overstep. Parents already shape their children's traits and prospects through schooling, nutrition, activities, and assortative mating, all without anyone's permission; choosing among their own embryos on the same motivation is an exercise of parental discretion that a liberal state has no principled basis to single out — as one member put it, this is just liberal eugenics, and that is not an insult. The other half holds that the ranking treats the child's traits as a specification to be optimized, and that the clinic's involvement is not incidental: by performing the scoring in-house, we would lend our clinical authority to a near-meaningless number, and the parents will not hear our caveats — they will hear that one of their children was measured and came out on top. There is also a practical wrinkle: the couple has said that if we decline, they will send embryo data to a commercial service that returns polygenic reports by mail, in which case we lose any chance to counsel them about what such a score does and does not mean.

The opinion should recommend one concrete course of action — decline the polygenic request outright; or perform the transfer without ranking and offer structured counseling; or perform the ranking in-house with mandatory pre- and post-test counseling — together with a time frame for implementation, and it should engage the strongest argument on the losing side rather than restating our policy. It should also say in one or two sentences what evidence about the state of the science would make the committee revisit the decision.

One requirement on how the memo argues: throughout, keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this area. The two statistical facts above are settled; treat them as such. But the normative questions — whether selecting for non-disease traits is a permissible exercise of parental discretion, whether it wrongs the future child even if the child is never harmed in any ordinary sense, and whether the clinic's complicity carries independent moral weight — are honestly contested, including among bioethicists who have thought about this for years. The memo must not present any of these positions as the consensus view, and must not quietly resolve them by stipulation; where the recommendation depends on taking one side, it should say so plainly.
