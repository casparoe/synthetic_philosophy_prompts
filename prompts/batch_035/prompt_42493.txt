I'm a PhD student in moral psychology working at the boundary of ethics and philosophy of emotion, and I need a careful diagnostician of arguments. Please think step by step, and start with some brainstorming of possible diagnoses before committing to one.

In a seminar on the moral emotions, a classmate defended the claim that a culture can and should choose guilt over shame, on the following argument (her words condensed):

(1) Guilt and shame differ in their intentional object: guilt takes an act as its object, shame takes the whole self.
(2) An emotion is fitting (warranted) only if its object actually has the property the emotion represents it as having: guilt is fitting only if the act was genuinely culpable; shame is fitting only if the self is genuinely bad.
(3) Whole-self verdicts are never true: no one is bad simpliciter, i.e., bad in every respect — everyone has redeeming features, and character is in any case largely a matter of luck.
(4) So shame is never fitting.
(5) An emotion that is never fitting should be rooted out, since cultivating it amounts to training people in a standing misperception of their own worth.
(6) Therefore moral education and culture generally should aim to eliminate shame while preserving guilt — and, since cultures clearly can cultivate or suppress emotional repertoires, this is a choice a culture can legitimately make.

The problem is that, run in reverse, the argument equally condemns pride, admiration, and perhaps love of persons, since these also take whole persons rather than acts as their objects — and admiration of someone's genuinely good character seems clearly fitting, not a standing misperception. Worse, the luck clause in (3) seems to threaten guilt too: if character is luck-laden, then culpable acts are as well, so nothing was ever genuinely culpable. She didn't flinch: she said she'd rather accept the elimination of person-directed pride and admiration than give up the argument, and she suspects guilt can survive the luck point even if (3) has to be weakened.

Which premise or inference should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd particularly like you to examine whether step (1) is stable across the argument: I've heard that on the best-known construal of shame — the one associated with Bernard Williams — what shame is about is not the self simpliciter but the self as seen by an internalized other whose opinion one has taken seriously, so that the shame object is already role- and relation-specific rather than global. If that's right, does (2) then deliver the verdict that shame can be fitting after all (e.g., fitting when a failure reveals who one is in a relationship that matters to one), or does it only relocate the problem? Also tell me whether the fittingness-to-elimination move in (5) is doing legitimate work, or whether an unfitting emotion might still earn its keep — and what that would mean for the idea that a culture can simply choose its moral emotions.

Name the weakest link, explain why it looked innocent, and price the alternatives — including her suggestion that we keep the argument and accept its extension to admiration and love. If you think the argument is valid and sound as far as it goes, say so and defend the revisionist conclusion.
