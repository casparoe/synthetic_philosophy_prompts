I'm preparing to commit to a position in the debate between epistemic and ontic structural realism, but I don't want to choose on the basis of whose slogan sounds better. Before I commit, I want the specification any adequate version of structural realism must meet as an account of what our scientific theories tell us about the world. Write out the desiderata, each with the consideration behind it. Among the things I'd expect on the list: it must capture the intuition behind the historical episodes its defenders cite — the retention of mathematical structure across radical theory change, as in the survival of the equations of wave optics through the shift from Fresnel's ether theory to Maxwell's, and the pattern Poincaré pointed to when he said it is the relations between things, and not the things themselves, that science grasps (state that point in your own words; don't quote); it must have a real answer to the Newman objection, which threatens to render any purely structural claim trivial; it must make clear what work structure does in the argument for realism, so that it neither collapses back into ordinary scientific realism nor into instrumentalism dressed in new vocabulary; it must either respect the distinction between knowing the structure of the things and knowing the things whose structure it is, or else argue explicitly for eliminating that distinction — and if the latter, say what is lost; and it must cohere with the actual physics its defenders lean on, since symmetry and permutation invariance in quantum mechanics are where structuralists take refuge, so the adequacy conditions should include not misrepresenting that physics. Mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are preferences of the epistemic or ontic camp masquerading as demands; and if some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, if escaping Newman pushes one toward the ontic view while the ontic view's eliminativism about objects cannot be squared with the role quantum mechanics seems to assign to individuals — say so, because that would be the most important result of the whole list. Don't score the existing contenders against the requirements; I'll do that myself afterwards. Do tell me which single requirement you expect to do the most work in excluding candidates. Finally, since I'll be relying on your judgment, tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail — which step in constructing the list is most vulnerable — and what argument or historical case, if someone produced it, would change your mind about which requirements belong on the list at all.
