After 1945, West German courts and legislators dealt with Nazi-era wrongs along two different tracks. On one track, courts sometimes declared that particularly vicious Nazi statutes had never truly been law at all, invoking the formula associated with Gustav Radbruch on which a statute of extreme injustice forfeits its legal character. On the other track, when no such move was available — for instance, where a denunciation or killing had been fully lawful under the Nazi order — the legislature resorted to openly retroactive statutes, and it repeatedly restarted or extended the running of limitation periods for Nazi-era murders, candidly acknowledging that it was imposing liability on past conduct that had not been punishable under the law in force at the time. What theory of law does this two-track practice presuppose? It looks as though the nullification track presupposes something like natural law — that legality depends on moral content — while the retroactivity track presupposes legal positivism, since it treats the Nazi rules as having been valid law that only new, acknowledged-positive law can now displace. Can any single jurisprudence license both tracks, as the participants in the Hart–Fuller debate might each reconstruct it, or do the tracks genuinely presuppose inconsistent views? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of the practice as a whole, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, identify where it is philosophically underdetermined such that several theories could accommodate it, and note what would have to change if the practice had to rest on one consistent theory.
