I teach a third-year metaphysics seminar, and next week's session turns to Humeanism about the laws of nature. I can present the basics without difficulty: the picture of the world as a vast mosaic of local matters of particular fact, with the laws picked out as the theorems of whatever systematisation best balances simplicity and strength against the whole distribution of events. And I can present the stock anti-Humean complaint — that on this picture the laws do not *govern* anything, they merely *summarise* — along with the Humean's reply that governing is a mystery we are well rid of. What I want from you is something subtler, and I want it pitched at the gut level rather than as a survey of positions.

Please explain the *inference problem* for the Best System account: why is it that when the Humean says the regularities obtain because they are consequences of the best systematisation of the mosaic, she seems to be explaining the wrong thing, or explaining it in a circle? I understand that the anti-Humean presses something like this: the systematisation is itself just a description of what happens across the mosaic, so it can explain why *we* expect the regularity to continue, but not why the world should keep cooperating. I want an intuitive reconstruction of this objection — one I could walk my students through without any formal apparatus — together with an assessment of whether the Humean's reply, that inference is always from within a system and the system is fixed by the totality of fact, is a genuine dissolution or merely a refusal to answer the question asked.

Then I would like your view on a suggestion floated by a colleague in our reading group. She proposes that the standards of simplicity and strength could be made fully objective by appealing to something like Kolmogorov complexity: the best system is simply the one whose axiomatisation most *compresses* the mosaic, and compression, she urges, is a matter of shortest description length rather than of what happens to strike us as elegant. On this reading the laws become those generalisations that figure in the most efficient encoding of the world, and nothing about that seems to depend on human psychology. My worry is that the proposal relocates the anthropocentrism rather than eliminating it: shortest program relative to *what* machine model, and is there any principled, non-question-begging way to privilege one such model? Assess whether the compression rescue succeeds, or whether the Humean must simply concede that lawhood is indexed to the cognitive constitution of the systematisers.

Finally, consider a thought experiment of my own that I suspect cuts across both debates. Imagine a world whose mosaic consists of a single perfectly homogeneous event-type repeated in every region of spacetime — call it the freeze-dried universe. The best systematisation of such a world is a one-line triviality, maximally simple and maximally strong. Yet intuitively it would be odd to say that anything in that world *obeys* a law, or that anything is explained by the one-liner; the compression is perfect precisely because there is nothing to be undone. Does this sort of case show that compression-based simplicity trades in cheap unification — trivial where the world is boring — and, if so, does the Best System account need some further ingredient, perhaps something at least gesturing in the direction of the anti-Humean's governing relation, in order to distinguish genuinely lawful worlds from frozen ones?

In your answer, please confine yourself to the conceptual argumentation and avoid bibliographic apparatus, as I shall be reading it away from my library. Do write in British English throughout, and aim for something I could adapt directly into seminar notes: intuitive where possible, but not at the cost of conceding the genuine force of the objection.
