a bit of background: three weeks ago I started as the welfare standards person at a small nonprofit that runs a certification label for aquaculture -- think dolphin-safe-tuna style, but for fish and shellfish welfare. farms pay us to audit them, get the little logo on their packaging, retailers like it. I have a marine bio degree, no philosophy background, and it turns out nobody here has ever written down how we actually decide what counts as acceptable welfare for these animals. its all vibes and whatever the auditor had for breakfast.

The hard part is the range of animals. Salmon, fine -- our customers mostly accept fish feel something. But we also certify shrimp farms, and I'm starting to get inquiries from lobster and crab operations now that decapod sentience is officially a thing in the UK (there was some big government-commissioned academic review that got them legally recognized, I still haven't fully read up on it). And some of the applications get genuinely weird. Like a shrimp broodstock operation that does eyestalk ablation on breeding females to ramp up egg production, and their defense is (a) they anesthetize first, and (b) shrimp in the wild die by the million as babies anyway, so who are we to act like farmed mortality is some special crime.

What I need is a decision procedure I can actually run on an application, per animal type. As in: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, where it stops with a "fail, can't certify," and where it stops with "this one goes to the review board" because what's left isn't a facts question but a values question -- e.g. whether a poor-region livelihood offsets a welfare hit, which I genuinely can't decide myself and don't think a procedure should pretend to decide for me.

Specific things it has to handle:

- The "natural suffering" defense above. Is there an actual philosophical answer to the "they'd die horribly in nature anyway" move, or is it a values call I have to route to the board? If there's an answer, fold it into the procedure; if not, say so.
- The evidence ladder for sentience -- nociception vs pain, what kind of behavioral evidence moves an animal up, and whether the procedure can give me a defensible cutoff between "certify but require improvements" and "won't certify, period." I keep seeing this framing where you only need a couple of knockdown-style criteria to clear the bar for sentience rather than a pile of everything, and I want to know if that can be operationalized or if it collapses back into judgment.
- A colleague keeps pushing this idea she calls "welfare floor plus gradient" -- her point being that avoiding bad lives isn't enough, the animals should have net-positive lives, so a farm shouldn't pass just because it's not torturing anything. I want the procedure to either incorporate that (and tell me what evidence could possibly establish net-positive lives for a shrimp) or tell me plainly it's unworkable.
- Slaughter rules: a small, locally beloved producer does "killed to order" with no pre-stunning because fresh-killed sells at a premium. I suspect the procedure should fail them, and I'd like it to do that clearly instead of me ad-hocing it and taking the heat personally.
- One where I won't like the answer: run it on our biggest client's salmon farm, which passed last year on water quality and stocking density and all the standard stuff. If a rigorous procedure doesn't clear them, I'd rather know now than after we've put their logo on another million packages.

Also be honest about where the procedure misfires -- the animals or farm types where it will give silly or needlessly harsh results, and the points where judgment can't be eliminated no matter how many boxes I tick. Assume about 30 applications a year, roughly two days of my time per application, and my manager (a vet, practical, allergic to sentimentality) signs off on final calls, with a quarterly review board above her. Don't give me a reading list, I've got no time and no library access -- I just need the procedure and the worked cases.
