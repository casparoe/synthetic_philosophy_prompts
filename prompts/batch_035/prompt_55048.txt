So I'm in a metaphysics seminar this semester and we just finished the whole endurantism vs perdurantism unit (worm theory stuff, Sider, Lewis, the usual), and our professor has this assignment where we have to write a short dialogue instead of a paper, and honestly I think dialogue is kind of a trap because it's so easy to make one side a total pushover. Anyway I have to draft mine this weekend and I want it to actually be good, so here's what I want: a dialogue between an endurantist and a perdurantist who are both smart and neither of whom caves. They should actually engage with each other's points instead of just trading slogans like "you're committed to temporal parts" and "you're committed to contradictions".

The setting my professor suggested (you can keep it or tweak it, whatever works): the two of them are watching a timelapse video of a topiary shrub that some gardener shaped into a dinosaur and then, over like fifteen years, gradually trimmed and retrained into the shape of a bear. A third gardener insists it's numerically the same shrub the whole time. So they get arguing about whether the shrub is wholly present at each moment or whether it's a four-dimensional worm of shrub-stages, and the dinosaur-shape stages and bear-shape stages are just different temporal segments of one plant. I also want them to hit the problem of temporary intrinsics at some point — like, the shrub is dinosaur-shaped at one time and bear-shaped at a later time, and each side has to explain how that's not a straight contradiction. And if you can work in the point about the worm theorist needing an account of which stages compose the shrub at all (why is it one plant and not two plants adjacent in time, sort of), that would be great, because that came up in seminar and I didn't fully get it.

One more thing: don't let the perdurantist just win by default. In a lot of the stuff I've read the endurantist gets like two lines and then gets steamrolled by the temporal parts machinery, and my GSI said the strongest endurantist replies (adverbialism, or indexing properties to times) deserve a fair hearing even if they have costs. Maybe end on them realizing there's some further thing they're talking past each other on, rather than a clean winner? Keep it to maybe 800-1200 words, and its fine if the tone is a bit informal since these are two gardeners arguing, not two journals. Also if its not too much to ask, have the actual timelapse/video setting do some argumentative work instead of just being flavor — like the fact that the gardeners only ever see the plant one moment at a time, never the whole four-dimesional object at once, if that makes sense.
