I teach a second-year course on strategic thought that mixes philosophy and international-relations students — 18 of them, one 75-minute session, a room with movable desks, and no budget, so no real stakes beyond points that count for a small participation grade. You are an experienced game designer and scholar of strategic studies who has run classroom simulations for two decades; think carefully before committing to a design, and sketch a couple of candidate structures first before settling on one.

The idea I want them to feel in their own incentives, not hear about in a lecture, is the central paradox of nuclear deterrence: that in a crisis, the capacity to lose control of events can be a bargaining asset — that a threat which carries a genuine chance of catastrophe no one would choose can be more credible and more useful than a threat one fully controls, and that a player who has burned their own options, or who is believed to be somewhat unable to back down, can outperform a fully rational player. Design the session so they run into this themselves. I'd like roughly this arc, though you should improve it where you can: first a short crisis-bargaining game in pairs where each side can veto the deal and destroy the prize for both, so they discover that the veto is worthless because using it is worse than conceding; then a modified version where each player must publicly set a tripwire — a declared line that, if crossed, hands the escalation decision to a die or another randomizing device neither side controls, so nobody ever chooses catastrophe yet catastrophe can occur anyway; then a final round of repeated play across several crises with reputations carrying over, where being known to have a hair trigger should start to pay. Build in genuine uncertainty — imperfect information about the other side's payoffs or resolve — so that nobody can win by calmly computing the equilibrium, and make the payoff scheme concrete enough to run with a stopwatch, index cards, and dice. No student should need any Cold War history; the game should work on its own.

For the debrief, map specific moments in the game onto the concepts without letting the debrief become the actual lesson — the point is that the tripwire round should already have shown them why a threat that leaves something to chance can dominate a perfectly controlled threat. Then give me three honest notes: what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it; which features of real nuclear brinkmanship the game structurally cannot reproduce (the sheer incomparability of the stakes, the fact that real decision-makers under stress and fatigue are not expected-utility calculators, and whatever else you judge essential); and the distortion I should worry about — my suspicion is that students will find the brinkmanship round exciting in a way that itself falsifies the emotional register of the real thing, and I want your view on whether and how to address that in the room rather than just acknowledging it afterwards.
