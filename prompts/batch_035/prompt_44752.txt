I serve as one of the citizen members of my state's sentencing guidelines commission. The legislature has referred a pilot proposal to us, informally called the "desert cap," and I need a decision memo to circulate before our next session. I'd like you to draft it.

The proposal, in brief: for a defined class of offenses, the retributive component of the guideline range would be capped at a fixed fraction — the draft uses one-third — of the current range. The remaining two-thirds could be imposed only on forward-looking grounds that the offender can subsequently "earn down": completion of treatment programs, restitution, demonstrated risk reduction. The sponsor presents this as a compromise between two positions our commission has been unable to reconcile. One faction holds that offenders deserve punishment in a basic, backward-looking sense tied to culpable choice, and that this desert is not a variable we may simply cap by statute; capping it, they say, is not a compromise but a tacit repudiation of the whole retributive rationale, since it treats deserved punishment as a bargaining chip rather than a claim of justice. The other faction holds that no one ever deserves punishment in the basic sense at all, because the conditions for such desert — genuine, ultimate authorship of one's character and choices — are never satisfied; on their view all the cap does is bring the law closer to what is already true, and the "forward-looking" remainder is the only part of any sentence that was ever justified. Between these sits a familiar compatibilist rejoinder: even if ultimate authorship is a myth, there is a weaker, ordinary sense of desert — tied to the capacity to respond to reasons — that survives, and the cap can be read as codifying exactly that weaker claim rather than abolishing desert.

Two features of the draft require specific attention in the memo. First, a "reopener" clause: if new information about the offender's circumstances at the time of the offense later comes to light — coerced participation, an undiagnosed neurological condition, credibly alleged abusive supervision by a co-defendant — the retributive third of the sentence may be recalculated, but the forward-looking portion may not be reduced on that basis; the sponsors claim this asymmetry simply follows from the nature of desert, since desert looks backward to the offense while risk and rehabilitation look forward, but I want the memo to examine whether the asymmetry actually works the way they assume, including the awkward case where the new information shows the "risk" assessment itself was contaminated. Second, the draft is silent on what happens when an offender completes all the earn-down conditions: whether the commission should treat the unreduced remainder as a ceiling, a floor, or a presumption, and what that choice implies about whether the retributive third was ever really treated as deserved.

The memo should: (1) state a clear recommendation — adopt, reject, or adopt with specified modifications — that the commission could act on, along with a suggested time frame for phasing in any pilot; (2) give the reasoning in terms the legislature could follow, engaging the philosophical disagreement about desert seriously rather than dismissing either faction, and honestly presenting the strongest consideration on whichever side loses; and (3) address the reopener asymmetry and the earn-down endpoint concretely, since these are the parts our deliberations have so far skipped. Please keep it to roughly 900–1200 words, formal in register, and structured as an actual commission memo rather than an essay. You should not rely on any sources you cannot verify; the memo should stand on its own reasoning.
