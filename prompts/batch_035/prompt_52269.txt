thinking of writing my term paper on thought experiments, but before I commit I want the ground rules first. everyone in philosophy leans on intuition pumps -- trolley cases, Mary's room, the chinese room, brains in vats -- and some people treat them as basically argument-by-imagination while others treat them as the core method of the whole discipline. what I want is the requirements list: what does a thought experiment have to do to count as giving me a reason to beleive something, rather than just planting a vivid picture in my head that I can't get out? like, does it need to be statable without the imagery, does it need to survive variations of the setup that shouldn't matter, does it need to not smuggle in empirical assumptions about how minds/brains/physics work, and so on -- you fill in the rest, with the case behind each requirement.

two half-formed ideas of mine I want you to actually engage with, cause I can't tell if they're real or just cute:

1. I keep thinking there's a difference between pumps that work because the case is rigged so your existing concept-application fires automatically (you look at the chinese room and your "that doesn't understand" detector just goes off) and pumps that work by making you notice you already had inconsistent commitments (you find yourself saying opposite things about two cases you agreed were alike in all relevant respects). the second kind seems like it's doing actual epistmic work and the first kind seems like it's just reporting a hunch with extra steps. is that a genuine split? if so it should probably show up as a requirement or as two different jobs that different pumps do, and the list should say which job matters.

2. my other hunch is a reversibility test: a good pump should fail when you run it backwards -- start from the opposite conclusion and see whether the same setup makes THAT seem obvious. if the case is equally persuasive in both directions it was never testing anything. is that a real desideratum or am I just reinventing a bias check?

and the part I most want: tell me which requirements can't be satisfied together. I have this feeling that vividness, which is where all the persuasive power comes from, is exactly what makes pumps dishonest, and if that tension is real then the list itself is a result -- it would mean the philosophy community has been choosing between honest-but-impotent and potent-but-sneaky without admitting it. flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of particular camps (armchair-friendly people vs people who want pumps to be continuous with scientific modeling, whatever).

dont bother with a bibliography or scoring every famous pump against the list -- one or two illustrations is plenty, I'll do the rest myself.
