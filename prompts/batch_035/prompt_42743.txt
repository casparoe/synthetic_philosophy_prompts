I serve as rapporteur for a working group that has spent two years mediating between two camps that have largely argued past each other: the record-first camp (archivists and documentary historians) holds that when a community's collective memory conflicts with the surviving documentary record, the record should give no ground, since collective memory confabulates, telescopes, and absorbs later political mythmaking; the memory-first camp (social memory scholars and community representatives) holds that collective memory carries an authority of its own, that archives are themselves selective artifacts of whoever had the power to write and preserve them, and that demanding memory always yield to the record mistakes one genre of truth for the only one. We have decided to stop adjudicating between them and instead run a proper adversarial collaboration. Please draft the joint statement the two camps should be able to sign after such a collaboration.

The document should contain, in this order: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims — substantive ones, not vacuous truisms, about what collective memory does, about the ways archives are selective and the ways memory is unreliable, and about what each is for — such that a neutral reader could see what real work the agreement does; (2) a disagreements table, with the precise points of disagreement stated in wording that each camp would accept as a fair statement of its own view; (3) the cruxes, i.e., the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp; and (4) for each crux, a concrete kind of case, argument, or piece of evidence both camps would agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance — including what each would count as evidence against its own side.

For calibration, assume the working group keeps encountering three recurring types of case, and require the joint statement to say something about each: (a) an oral tradition of a massacre that the surviving records deny or omit, where those records were produced by the very army said to have carried it out; (b) a community's founding narrative — a founding date or heroic founding event — that the documentary record dates differently or never mentions at all; (c) a memory of events within living memory that has demonstrably been reshaped by later political campaigns, yet remains the story around which the community organizes its identity. My own suspicion, which the document should test rather than assume, is that one crux is whether "which should give way?" is even the right question — whether part of the dispute dissolves once you distinguish revising the historical record from revising the community's story from deciding what a monument, a school curriculum, or a place name is for — but there may be deeper cruxes I have missed, such as whether accuracy is memory's only legitimate function or whether archival silence about an event is positive evidence against it. If you conclude that part of the dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why; if part of it is not verbal and cannot be dissolved, say what would actually move it.

Please write the statement in American English, in a register suitable for formal adoption by both camps.
