I cover science and ideas for a national newspaper, and a colleague's recent long-read on the ontological argument has generated a remarkable amount of mail from readers convinced it settles the God question once and for all. Before I write my follow-up column, I want to be sure about what his piece actually establishes. He summarizes the argument roughly as follows: Anselm defined God as the greatest conceivable being and argued that such a being must exist, since existing is greater than not existing. Kant then demolished the whole enterprise in the Critique of Pure Reason by showing that existence is not a real predicate — it adds nothing to a concept, so one cannot define something into existence. Gödel later revived the argument using the formal rigor he was famous for: he translated Anselm's idea into modal logic and proved, on the basis of axioms that are essentially just definitions, that if a God-like being is possible then one exists necessarily — and since the possibility is granted by definition, the theorem is as airtight as anything in mathematics. My colleague's takeaway is that the question has therefore been settled by logic itself: either the proof goes through and God's existence is as certain as any theorem, or the whole affair is exposed as word games.

I'd like a careful audit of this summary, claim by claim. Please separate what a philosopher of religion would accept as accurate, what counts as a defensible simplification for lay readers, and what is genuinely misleading — I'm particularly unsure about three points: whether Kant's objection, as stated, refutes every version of the argument or only the versions that trade on treating existence as a perfection; whether Gödel's proof is best described as a formalization of Anselm or of something closer to Leibniz's development of it, with its notion of positive properties; and whether the claim that the crucial axioms are "essentially just definitions" is where the argument quietly lives or dies, given that logicians who accept the proof's validity still argue about its assumptions. Please also address whether Gödel's theorem shows anything about the God of ordinary religion, or only about a maximal being in a technical sense. Conclude with a judgement on whether my colleague's either-or takeaway — theorem-certainty or mere word games — survives these corrections, and offer a fair one-paragraph gloss of the argument's real status that I could use in my column.
