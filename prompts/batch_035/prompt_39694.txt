I write about science and ideas for a newspaper, and for the past few years I keep ending up on the animal sentience beat. You probably remember the UK law from 2022 that finally put animal sentience into domestic statute after Brexit — the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act. The interesting bit for my purposes is that it doesn't just cover vertebrates: it was extended to decapod crustaceans and cephalopod molluscs after the government commissioned a big expert review, run out of the LSE, which sifted the evidence for sentience in crabs, lobsters, octopuses and so on. The review concluded there's strong evidence for some of these animals, and suggested welfare measures like not boiling lobsters alive without stunning them first. Bees and other insects, meanwhile, were left out, on the grounds that the evidence wasn't there yet.

So the whole machinery works by asking scientists a seemingly straightforward question — does this animal feel anything? — and weighing things like pain behaviour, painkillers being self-administered, trade-offs between harmful stimuli and rewards. And the legislation is written as if sentience is a plain fact about the crab that better experiments could settle.

Here's what's bugging me. Around the same time I've been interviewing consciousness researchers, and a decent chunk of them (the illusionist crowd, people like Keith Frankish) will happily tell you that phenomenal consciousness as normally conceived — the raw "what it's like", the feel of pain as something beyond its functional role — is a kind of user illusion generated by the brain. On their view there's no felt-quality-of-pain for science to go looking for in a crab or in me; there's a system that represents itself as having ineffable feels, and that's it. My editors have never noticed any tension between running "crabs can suffer, scientists say" pieces and "your inner feel is an illusion" pieces, but I'm starting to wonder whether there is one.

So: what view of consciousness does the sentience-law apparatus actually presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose that felt experience is a real property that animals either have or lack, or could an illusionist sign off on the whole evidence framework — in which case the practice is philosophically underdetermined? Does anything in how the review drew its line (crabs in, insects out) depend on a contested view rather than just caution? And what would a welfare law built on illusionist foundations actually have to look like instead?

One request: please keep clear what's genuinely settled versus what's actively fought over among people who know this literature — I don't want to hand my editor a piece that presents some faction's line as consensus.
