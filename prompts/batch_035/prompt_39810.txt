I am a retired physicist who spent most of my career on optimal control and estimation — Bellman's dynamic programming, Kalman filtering, partially observable processes, feedback laws versus open-loop policies — and I have lately been reading decision theory as a retirement hobby. I have run into a puzzle from 1990s economics called the absent-minded driver problem, due to Piccione and Rubinstein: a driver on a dark highway passes two identical intersections and cannot tell which one he is at. He must exit at the second intersection to get home (payoff 4); exiting at the first is worse (payoff 1); continuing past both is worst (payoff 0). Since he cannot distinguish the intersections, his only real choice is between a fixed probability of continuing at each intersection or exiting. What disturbs the economists is that reasoning about what to do at the intersection, once there, seems to come apart from reasoning about which overall strategy to adopt before starting out — as if the agent inside the loop is not quite solving the same problem as the designer outside it.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of decision theory who is also fluent in control engineering, and think carefully — you may want to brainstorm the mapping before committing to it. Restate the whole debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can: the driver as a partially observable process, the pre-commitment to a strategy as the design phase versus whatever the agent is doing at runtime, the strategy itself as a control law rather than a sequence of independent decisions, and so on. Do not smuggle in engineering assumptions and present them as translation — if my vocabulary quietly presupposes something the philosophers are disputing, say so explicitly. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the puzzle translate cleanly, which come through distorted, and whether anything in the philosophical debate — for instance, the suggestion that the driver standing at an intersection has no well-defined decision to make at all, or the dispute between evaluating an act by its causal consequences and evaluating it by the evidence it carries about how one acts elsewhere — has no counterpart in control engineering at all. Finally, tell me your view: is the residue a gap in my vocabulary, or a sign that the puzzle is less deep than the economists found it?
