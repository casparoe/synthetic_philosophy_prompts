I teach a philosophy of science survey course, and every year my seminar on natural kinds degenerates into students reciting "water is H₂O" at one another and assuming that settles the matter. This year I want to replace the opening debate with a decision tree that students walk through before they have read any of the labels — microstructural essentialism, homeostatic property cluster views, promiscuous realism, conventionalism — so that they arrive at whichever view they actually hold via the cases rather than the names. The tree should start from a commitment nearly everyone in the room will grant (that at least some classifications track how the world is, not merely how we talk), and then branch on concrete cases a non-philosopher can answer without knowing where the branches lead. I have been burned before by quizzes where every question could be answered "it depends", so each node should force a binary or ternary choice, with the case described concretely enough that evasive answers are visibly evasive.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of authors, but make sure it routes through at least these splits. First, jade: nephrite and jadeite are chemically distinct minerals that Chinese stoneworkers classified, and worked with successfully for centuries, as a single kind — was that classification tracking a joint or papering over two? Second, biological kinds: ring species and asexual lineages, where there is no shared intrinsic make-up that circumscribes the group, but there is common ancestry — can a kind be constituted by history and relation rather than by internal structure? Third, cases like the electron, where all the theoretically important properties are dispositional or relational, which pressure the thought that an essence must be an intrinsic hidden structure. Fourth, a functional question: must a natural kind earn its keep by supporting induction and explanation, or is that a downstream bonus rather than part of what makes it natural? And fifth, pluralism: when two sciences carve the same domain differently — chemical elements versus astrophysical stellar populations, say — must one carving be privileged as carving at the joint, or can both be right? The leaves must include at least: microstructural essentialism, the homeostatic property cluster view, a historical or lineage-based account, promiscuous or pluralist realism, conventionalism, and an eliminativist option for contested everyday kinds. Flag any region of the tree's answer-space that no one actually occupies, with a sentence on why you think that is (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored), and flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot genuinely separate.

Please deliver the whole thing as a single JSON object, followed by a short prose paragraph of commentary (outside the JSON) noting anything the tree distorts. The schema: a "title" field; a "root_id" field; a "nodes" array in which each element has "id" (string), "type" ("question" or "leaf"); question nodes additionally have "question" (the case and the forced choice, in plain language), and "branches" (an array of objects with "label" and "next", pointing at other node ids); leaf nodes additionally have "position_name" (use "unnamed" where no one defends the combination), "representatives" (an array of philosopher names, or an empty array), and "awkward_commitment" (one sentence on the view's most uncomfortable consequence). Add top-level arrays "unoccupied_combinations" (objects with "answers" and "diagnosis") and "cannot_separate" (objects with "leaves" and "why"). Keep it to between nine and thirteen question nodes. Please write everything, including branch labels and commentary, in British English.
