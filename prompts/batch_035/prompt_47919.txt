I've been teaching the material constitution debate in my metaphysics seminar for over a decade, and I've hit a wall I want help thinking through. The set-up everyone knows: there's a statue on the pedestal, there's a piece of clay occupying exactly the same region, and they seem to have different persistence and modal conditions -- the clay survives being squashed into a ball, the statue doesn't -- so by Leibniz's Law they can't be identical, and yet they share all their parts and there's no gap between them. The standard moves are the standard moves: the two-thingers say they're distinct but necessarily coincident; the one-thingers say they're identical and one of the seeming differences is an illusion; the nihilists say there is no statue, just clay arranged statue-wise; the relative identity people say there's one thing there which is the same clay as, but not the same statue as, itself... and so on. My students master all of this in about three weeks. And then nothing happens. Nobody converts. Nobody even seems to think conversion is on the table. The papers keep coming out and they all read like increasingly ornate versions of the same five moves.

My own hypothsis, offered as a hypothesis: this stopped being a disagreement about statues a long time ago, and is really a disagreement about what "there is exactly one object on the pedestal" commits you to -- maybe about quantification, maybe about whether counting objects is anything over and above counting stuff and counting sortal-satisfiers. If that's right the debate is verbal in one sense but not trivially verbal, because nobody can say what would settle the question underneath the question.

Here's a wrinkle I want you to take seriously as a test case. Last semester a student threw in something that broke the usual intuitions, and I still don't know what to do with it. Take a large language model and its trained weights. The model was produced by a training process, it doesn't seem identical to any particular copy of the weights file (there are thousands of copies sitting on thousands of machines, and it would be bizarre to say there are therefore thousands of models), and it doesn't occupy a region of space the way clay does. But people reach for the statue-clay toolkit anyway: the model is "constituted by" its weights, distinct from them, surviving re-quantization or a port to different hardware the way clay survives squashing. So is this the same debate wearing new clothes, in which case the diagnosis transfers wholesale? Or does it show the debate was never really about spatial coincidence and shared parts in the first place -- that persistence conditions, or sortal-dependence, or something about artifacts generally was doing the real work, and the clay was incidental?

Give me your actual diagnosis of why this debate never moves. Verbal, substantive-but-hard, or two questions under one heading -- pick one and defend it; don't lay the options out and leave me to choose. Use what the sides actually say, not textbook caricatures. And tell me whether the weights case supports your diagnosis or complicates it -- I honestly dont know which it does for mine. Then tell me what would count as progress, concretely: a distinction, a test case that splits the positions in a way statues never did, a change of question? If your answer is that nothing would, that this is just what metaphysics at its limit looks like, say that and say why.
