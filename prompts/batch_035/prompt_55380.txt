my philosophy club spent last thursday on Sen's liberal paradox and afterwards two of my friends got into a huge argument about what it actually proves. i was mostly just taking notes so i got to watch the whole thing, and now they both want me to referee, and honestly i can't. so here i am.

quick setup in case you don't remember it. there's one copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover and two people, Prude and Lewd. Prude's ranking: nobody reads it > Prude himself reads it > Lewd reads it (reading it in disgust is bad but at least it's not being enjoyed). Lewd's ranking: Prude reads it > Lewd himself reads it > nobody reads it (the best outcome for Lewd is apparently the spectacle of Prude being made to read it). If each person gets to decide whether they themselves read the book — the "liberal" bit — you end up with Lewd reads > nobody reads > Prude reads, since Lewd opts in and Prude opts out. But both people rank "Prude reads" above "Lewd reads", so by Pareto the social ranking has to put Prude reads above Lewd reads, and you get a cycle. Sen's point: unrestricted preferences + Pareto + everyone having some personal sphere they control can't all hold together.

ok so here's the fight, as best as i can reconstruct it:

(1) Dana said the lesson is that Pareto efficiency isn't the neutral, value-free criterion people pretend it is. if enough people are busybodies with opinions about other people's private business, you can generate a "Pareto improvement" by forcing the prude to read a book in his own bedroom, and a live-and-let-live society can count as Pareto-inferior to a nosy one. so "everyone prefers it" can't be a trump card.

(2) Marcus said no, Pareto is fine, and the thing that dies is liberalism-as-decision-rule. the fix is easy anyway: when you aggregate preferences you just don't count the meddlesome parts — only the parts of a person's preferences about their own sphere count toward the social ranking. launder the preferences, then apply Pareto, problem solved.

(3) Dana said the laundering is where ALL the work happens and there's no principled line to draw. is my preference that my roommate not smoke in our shared room "about my sphere" (my lungs, my stuff smelling) or "about hers" (her habit)? is worrying that my friend is drinking himself into a mess nosy or just being a friend? whoever decides what counts as nosy has basically already settled everything, and they settled it with something other than Pareto or liberalism.

(4) Marcus said fine, but then Dana's position collapses into "Pareto can be overridden whenever I find the preferences icky," which isn't a rule at all. and the actual alternative on the table — ditching the personal-spheres idea and letting everyone vote on everything, bedrooms included — is clearly worse. you have to pick a poison.

(5) Dana's last word was that maybe the paradox isn't telling us to patch either condition at all, but showing that the "spheres" themselves aren't given in advance. like, what counts as my own business isn't something we discover, it's something the norms and rules we already have partly created, so the paradox is really a chicken-and-egg problem, and both of Marcus's poisons are pretending the spheres came first.
