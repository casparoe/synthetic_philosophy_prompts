I had a long argument last night with a friend of mine — she's a decision-theory person, I've mostly read philosophy of science — about whether Aumann's agreement theorem actually tells us anything about real disagreements between real people. We know the theorem itself is airtight: two agents, common prior, common knowledge of each other's posteriors, and their posteriors about any proposition must be equal. I'm inclined to take it as a genuine constraint on rational disagreement, on the grounds that if my friend and I are both honest, informed, and updating correctly, then persistent disagreement between us is evidence that something in the theorem's conditions fails, and it's worth finding out what. She thinks this is all wrong: for her, the theorem is a fixed-point statement about an equilibrium, akin to a Nash equilibrium in which no one moves, and the whole machinery only looks dynamic because of the result showing that two agents who merely take turns announcing their posteriors will eventually converge — a convergence that in practice runs through far more back-and-forth than any human conversation could sustain. Common priors, she adds, are a fiction that does all the work: nearly all real disagreement is prior disagreement, so the theorem explains almost nothing about actual people.

I'd like a probability estimate with the reasoning decomposed. Specifically, give me your credence that a typical persistent disagreement between two smart, honest, well-informed humans is traceable to a violation of one of the theorem's conditions — epistemic irrationality in updating, failure of common knowledge, or disagreement about the evidence's reliability — rather than to something the theorem simply doesn't touch, like substantive prior disagreement. Then decompose that credence into sub-questions with a rough probability for each: whether the common prior assumption is a harmless idealization or a substantive begging of the question; whether real agents could ever cheaply establish the common knowledge the theorem needs, or whether the posteriors-are-common-knowledge condition presupposes something close to the agreement it yields; whether the turn-taking convergence result gives the theorem genuine dynamic content or merely shows that idealized chatter is not a model of actual inquiry; and whether the no-trade-style reading — that the theorem is really about what bettors already know — captures more or less of its significance than the agreement reading. Combine the terms however you think is honest, and tell me what the final number is most sensitive to.

Finally, my credence that she and I would eventually converge on this question itself if we kept arguing in good faith is about 0.7, while hers is, fittingly, about 0.3. Given your decomposition, tell me which of the sub-questions you think we most likely disagree about — she suspects priors, I suspect we disagree about whether convergence-by-conversation is cheap or expensive — and what evidence or argument would most shift your overall number in either direction.
