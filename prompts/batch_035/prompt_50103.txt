I've never studied philosophy, but I've been arguing with my friends about fairness and money since we read a news story about a billionaire's daughter, and I've ended up with a list of situations where I have strong gut reactions. The trouble is my reactions don't seem to fit together, so I want help figuring out whether there's a single principle behind them, and if not, which of my reactions I should give up. For each case I'll say what my gut tells me.

A singer starts out poor, gets famous, and millions of people each voluntarily pay a few dollars for her music and tickets. She ends up vastly richer than everyone else who started with her, though nobody was forced or tricked. My gut says this is fine — everyone chose to pay. But her children, who did nothing at all, inherit everything and never have to work, while the children of the people who bought the records struggle — and this bothers me, even though the will was written willingly and nobody was deceived. A man sells a painting at a flea market for twenty dollars, not knowing it's worth two hundred thousand; the buyer, who does know, pays up and says nothing. That was voluntary on both sides, yet I think the buyer did something wrong, I just can't say what was taken. A con man steals old people's savings, and before he's caught he uses some of it to start a business that now employs honest people and turns a legitimate profit — I feel the seed money was never really his, so something is owed to his victims even though the business itself is clean. A community's land was stolen two hundred years ago; the current owners bought it in good faith and have done nothing wrong, while the community's descendants remain poor — I feel something is owed there too, but somehow it feels like a different kind of owing than the con man case. And the case I keep going back and forth on: a landlord who, during a housing shortage, charges the very highest rent the market will bear. No force, no fraud, every lease signed willingly — and yet it strikes me as wrong, while the singer's millions don't, and I can't see what the difference is supposed to be.

Is there one principle that captures all or most of these verdicts? If so, state it as precisely as you can, tell me which case motivates each part of it, and tell me what it would say about two situations I haven't listed: whether someone may leave their entire fortune to a trust that pays their descendants forever, and whether a very poor person who freely sells a kidney should be allowed to. If no single principle fits, I'd rather you tell me that honestly than force one, and instead show me which of my verdicts would have to go, and why those rather than the others. One more request: where people who think about this for a living actually disagree — for example about whether anything is owed for historical wrongs, or about what makes a transfer voluntary enough — please say plainly that it's disputed and what the main positions are, rather than presenting one of them as if it were settled; and if something really is common ground among experts, tell me that too, so I know which of my reactions are fighting centuries of argument and which are just fighting me.
