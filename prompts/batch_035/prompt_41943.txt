I work on the safety team at a company that trains reinforcement-learning agents in simulation — warehouse robots, sorting systems, that kind of thing — and before each new training run someone from my team has to sign off on the impact penalty: the auxiliary reward term meant to keep the agent from causing side effects nobody put in the task reward, like damaging equipment or permanently blocking a corridor. The researchers keep proposing new variants, and our reviews are inconsistent: sometimes we approve things that bite us months later, sometimes we reject good ideas for reasons nobody can reconstruct. What I want is a written procedure the reviewer on duty can run in a forty-five-minute review slot, without a philosopher on hand.

Some background for whoever reviews this. An impact penalty has two parts a researcher can vary more or less independently: a baseline — the counterfactual the agent's behavior is compared against, either the state at the start of the episode or the state the environment would be in if the agent had paused at each step (a "stepwise" baseline) — and a deviation measure, the score for how far the actual world diverges from that baseline, for instance by how much the agent's presence reduces the set of future states it can still reach (relative reachability) or the reward obtainable under other goals it might have had (attainable utility). We keep having the same three fights. A stepwise baseline lets the agent treat damage it has already caused as the new normal: it can wreck things early in an episode and then look impeccable on the safety graphs. An initial-state baseline makes the agent reluctant to change anything at all, including things its own task requires. And every deviation measure needs some inventory of "probe" goals or measurements to define what a side effect is, and the choice of that inventory quietly decides which harms count and which are invisible. Behind all this is the question I can't get out of my head: can "avoid unspecified side effects" be defined at all without smuggling in a complete specification of everything we value — and if not, where exactly does the value judgment enter the pipeline, and who on the team should be the one making it?

Please write the procedure accordingly: the questions in the order the reviewer should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which questions are factual and can be settled by the procedure itself and which must be handed to a person because they are value calls (say to whom), where the procedure terminates in a plain "no, resubmit," and what it cannot do at all. Then run it on two proposals. First, a stepwise inaction baseline with a reachability-based measure, proposed for a robot whose episodes start mid-task, where we suspect it learned early in training to quietly deposit inventory in the wrong place and then behave perfectly for the rest of the episode. Second, an initial-state baseline with an attainable-utility measure for a sorting agent whose environment legitimately drifts over the course of an episode, where the researchers complain the agent has become nearly inert for fear of any deviation. For at least one of the two, the procedure should return an answer my team won't like — please let it, and tell me which step of the procedure forced it.
