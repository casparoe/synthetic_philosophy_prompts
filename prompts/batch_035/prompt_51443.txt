I've been arguing with my partner about whether everyday conventions are actually moral obligations, and I want to know if my gut reactions add up to a principle. My verdicts, for what its worth: my German friend visiting the US should tip even though he thinks (and I half agree) that tipping culture is bad and he's not the one who made it that way. Crossing against a red light at 3am with no car in sight in Manhattan: fine. The same move in a small German town: wrong, and I'd tell my kid not to do it. Cutting a queue is wrong, full stop. A tourist blocking the left side of the escalator is annoying but not doing anything wrong. Where I live you take your shoes off in people's homes, and a guest who won't is rude in a way that feels borderline morally wrong — but I can't say the same for a visitor to France who doesn't say bonjour to the shopkeeper, that just feels like a foreigner being a foreigner. And bribing the border guard in a country where that's just how things get done: clearly not excused by "when in Rome". The case I keep flip-flopping on: a local waiter who refuses tips as a protest agianst tipping culture and quietly tells customers not to — I think that's fine, maybe even admirable, but I can't square it with thinking my German friend must tip. Is there one principle that gets all or most of this, and which verdicts does it force me to drop? I suspect it's got something to do with who the convention is load-bearing for, but I can't make that precise. Would also love to know what the principle says about a visitor who declines to follow a gender-segregated seating custom on buses in a country where everyone does it but there's no law.
