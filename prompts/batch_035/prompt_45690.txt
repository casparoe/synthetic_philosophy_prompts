I'd like your credence — an actual number — on the following proposition, which I'll try to state as precisely as I can: that the asymmetry between checking and discovering (verifying a proof versus finding it; confirming a satisfying assignment versus producing one) marks a structural normative boundary for rational agents. By that I mean: there are real epistemic situations in which an agent's failure to arrive at a truth it could efficiently recognize as true, had it been handed the answer, is not a rationality failure at all — not merely a shortfall we charitably overlook, but something that changes what the correct standard of evaluation is for that agent. Put another way, the proposition is that ideal rationality is bounded not by computability-in-principle but by some notion of feasibility, and that the find/check asymmetry is where this bound bites.

My own credence is about 0.65. My colleague's is about 0.3 — she holds that idealization to logical omniscience is harmless in the way frictionless planes are harmless, and that conflating the norms governing an idealized Bayesian with the norms governing any reasoner we should care about is a category error. Neither of us has managed to shift the other in months of argument, so I want your number and, more importantly, the derivation.

Decompose the proposition into the sub-claims it rests on — at least these, plus any others you think belong: (a) that P ≠ NP, or more cautiously, that some robust and naturally occurring find/check asymmetry exists; (b) that resource feasibility, as opposed to mere decidability, is the right standard for normative epistemology — that the logical omniscience idealization distorts rather than simplifies; (c) that the find/check asymmetry specifically carries normative weight, rather than being one resource limit among many with no special status; and (d) that "rational evaluation" is coherent enough a notion, across contexts like peer disagreement about arguments, deductive reasoning tasks, and machine reasoning systems, for a single verdict to apply. Assign each a rough probability with one line of justification, show me how they combine into your final number, and tell me which term you're least confident in.

Two further questions. First, given my 0.65 and her 0.3, where does our disagreement most likely live — which single term, if settled, would most likely collapse the gap? Second, a hypothetical my colleague thinks favors her: suppose P = NP were proved tomorrow, with genuinely practical algorithms for the relevant problems. Would the normative boundary I'm describing simply reappear one level up — say, in the gap between checking and discovering at higher complexity classes — which would suggest the philosophical point never depended on the specific conjecture? Or would that result genuinely undermine the position? I have an answer to this, and I notice it makes me suspicious of my own 0.65, so I'd like yours.
