I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on historical injustice, where we recently discussed Karl Jaspers's postwar reflections on German guilt and the distinction he drew there between criminal guilt, political liability, moral guilt, and something he called metaphysical guilt. After the session, two students in the course stayed behind and got into a long argument about whether a nation, as such, can be guilty — especially across generations. I watched the whole thing and couldn't decide who was right, so I'd like a careful adjudication.

Their exchange went as follows. Lena opened by claiming that nations can literally be guilty, not just their individual members: a nation is a persisting collective agent, the way a corporation is, and it can act and be culpable over decades even as its individual members die and are replaced. She noted that when a state today pays reparations for crimes committed before any current official was born, we naturally say the country is making amends for what it did — and she took that way of talking at face value. Marcus replied that this is a category error: guilt requires a guilty mind, and a collective has no mind; corporate liability is a legal fiction adopted for regulatory convenience, and mistaking a useful fiction for real guilt is exactly the confusion the individualist tradition warned against. He added that the persistence claim fails anyway: since the membership turns over completely within a few decades, the 'nation' of today is related to the nation of the crime only as one ship of Theseus is to another, and nobody thinks the rebuilt ship ran aground just because the original did. Lena then invoked Jaspers: even if criminal guilt is strictly personal, he argued that political liability is shared by citizens simply in virtue of living under and benefiting from their state's institutions, and that there is a further, non-transferable guilt in failing minimal human solidarity with the victims. So, she said, there is room for a genuine, non-criminal form of collective guilt that does not reduce to anyone's individual blameworthiness. Marcus answered that this concession wins her nothing: if the inherited item is liability and obligation rather than blameworthiness, then the dispute is over a word — call it responsibility, stop calling it guilt, and the argument dissolves. Lena tried a different analogy: a club that acquired a stolen painting decades ago and whose current members, none of them thieves, enjoy displaying it and vote to keep it. She said we would quite naturally call the club's conduct shameful and hold the club, as a body, to account, demanding restitution and apology in its name — and that this shows collective blame is coherent even where no present member stole anything. Marcus closed with a pragmatic objection: even granting all that coherence, extending the concept of guilt across generations invites abuse — it licenses despising people for what their grandparents did, and history shows where talk of hereditary collective guilt leads; so even if the concept makes sense, we ought to refuse to use it, the way we retired other intelligible but corrupting concepts.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is right, mistaken, or right in part, with your reasons; then give an overall verdict on who is more right; and finally say whether there is anything of substance that both of them missed — in particular, whether the disagreement really does collapse into a verbal one, as Marcus claims, or whether something survives the relabeling.
