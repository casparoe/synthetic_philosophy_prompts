I am writing a novel set in the near present, in which a living relict population of small-brained hominins is discovered on an island in the Indonesian archipelago — a premise loosely modeled on Homo floresiensis, though my creatures are invented, and all the plot requires is that they are close kin to us, plausibly interfertile or nearly so. The story turns on a hearing at which the island's authorities must decide whether these hominins fall under statutes protecting "persons" or merely "wildlife," and my protagonist, a population geneticist testifying as an expert, is pressed from both sides. In my research I keep encountering the following analogy, drawn from popular treatments of the species problem: species boundaries are like the boundary between colors on a spectrum — red shades into orange with no non-arbitrary place to cut, just as, generation by generation, no child is ever born belonging to a different species from its parents. My antagonist, a lawyer for the developers who want the island, wields it to argue that because populations change only gradually, the line between species is drawn by taxonomists for their own convenience rather than found in nature — so the question "are they one of us?" is not something biology can answer, and the hearing must be decided by convention, politics, or sentiment instead.

Audit the analogy as it is being used here: set out what corresponds to what (is a species a stretch of the spectrum, an adjacent pair of swatches, the names of the colors, or the painter's cut?), where the mapping breaks down, and which features of the paint-mixing picture it quietly imports that biological populations lack — a continuum in a single dimension, an unchanging standard of comparison, the assumption that one boundary question is like every other. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for what the analogy is being made to do at the hearing from the ones that do not, and say whether the conclusion — that the boundary question is scientifically empty and the verdict must rest on something other than biology — actually follows, or whether it trades on conflating the existence of hard boundary cases with the general absence of structure. If there is a better analogy for what species boundaries turn out to be, I want to hear it. This is for verisimilitude: my geneticist needs to be right, and her antagonist needs to be wrong in an interesting way, not a stupid one.

Present your audit as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction, conclusion, or other apparatus.
