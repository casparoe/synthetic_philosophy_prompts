I teach intro ethics at a big state school, and every semester when we hit utilitarianism some student smirks and asks "if suffering is all that matters, why not just painlessly blow up the world?" and every semester I wave it off as a cheap shot. Except now I'm drafting a short position statement for a population ethics workshop and I've realized I honestly don't know how I get out of it. Here's where I've actually landed: (1) suffering counts way more than happiness — I'd trade any amount of bliss to prevent a single torture, and I don't think adding happy lives to an already fine world makes it much better, if at all; (2) I accept the procreation asymmetry — it's wrong to create a child you know will suffer terribly, but there's nothing wrong with declining to create a child who would've been happy; (3) I hold all this without the person-affecting crutch — suffering in some causally isolated galaxy is just bad, full stop, even though it touches nobody we know or care about; (4) I nonetheless think a happy, suffering-free life is genuinely good, worth living and worth having, not merely "not bad"; and (5) I still recoil at the painless-extinction button, and I even tell my students that reducing existential risk is among the most important things anyone could work on, which I mostly believe.

I can feel that (1) plus (3) puts pressure on (5): if the button removes all the suffering and the only thing destroyed is a pile of goods I've already said barely register, why isn't pressing it at least permissible? But I can't tell how serious that is or what exactly gives. I also suspect (2) and (3) don't sit together, since the asymmetry is usually propped up by exactly the person-affecting view I'm refusing to lean on — though maybe that's me misremembering why people lean on it. And I genuinely can't tell whether (4) flatly contradicts (1) or only contradicts a stronger version of (1) that I never should have wanted in the first place. If it's the latter, I'd like to know, because that changes which of my other commitments survive the fix.

What I want is a committed read, not a menu of options. Tell me which tensions are real and which only look real, order them by how bad they are, and then actually pick what has to give — if the package is salvagable with one small clarification, name it and say why it does the job, and if the whole thing is structurally unsound, say that and sketch the least costly rebuild. Don't hedge on my behalf; I'd rather recieve a definite verdict I end up disagreeing with than a balanced survey of the logical space.
