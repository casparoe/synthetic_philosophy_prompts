I've been in a consciousness reading group for two years now, and the argument over heterophenomenology has stalled in a way I'd like diagnosed rather than adjudicated.

To be clear about the dispute: by heterophenomenology I mean Dennett's proposed method for a science of consciousness, on which you treat each subject's reports about their experience roughly the way an ethnographer treats a culture's myths — as texts to be recorded, interpreted, and explained, but not automatically believed. The subject's word counts as data about how things seem to them, not as testimony about how things are; whatever actually goes on gets theorized in ordinary third-person terms, and the reports become part of what the theory must account for. His critics reply that this quietly assumes what's at issue: if consciousness is precisely the thing that is given from the first-person point of view, then a method that begins by suspending the subject's authority has decided the outcome in advance — it can explain why people say they have experiences, but it has ruled out the possibility that what they are reporting is the very phenomenon to be explained. The neurophenomenologists proposed to fix this by adding disciplined first-person methods — trained meditators reporting on their own states in ways meant to yield stable, checkable results — and that was supposed to be the live alternative. Thirty-odd years on, such studies now exist, and as far as I can tell neither side has moved an inch because of them.

My working hypothesis is that the two parties mean different things by data. The critics are not claiming that first-person reports carry some special evidential weight that third-person measurement happens to miss; they are claiming the reports are partly constitutive of the explanandum, so that a science which accounts for them without ever appealing to them has changed the subject. If that's right, the disagreement is not about methodology at all but about what a science of consciousness is supposed to be a science of, and no experimental result could settle it. But I hold this loosely, because a second reading — that it's a plain substantive dispute over whether trained introspection can be made intersubjectively reliable — predicts exactly the same stalemate, given that the meditation studies are contested on precisely that point.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Verbal, substantive, or two research programmes answering different questions under one name? Is a hidden empirical question — about the reliability of disciplined introspection, say — doing the real work, or do the sides differ about which cases count as data in the first place? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a first-person method that generates testable predictions, a conceptual distinction between evidence and explanandum, a reformulation of the question, or nothing at all — and if nothing, why? If you think my framing already concedes too much to one side — say, that suspending the subject's authority misdescribes what heterophenomenology actually does, or that the debate has in fact moved and I'm reading the wrong corners of the literature — say so plainly; I would rather be corrected than accommodated.
