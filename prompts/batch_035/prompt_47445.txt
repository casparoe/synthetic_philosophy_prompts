I write on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a long feature about the shifting line between illness and ordinary human variation. The reporting has me sitting in on the public sessions of a national health fund's classification panel — the body whose rulings determine which conditions count as diseases for purposes of coverage. Its record so far makes an intriguing pattern, and I want to be sure I understand what principle, if any, it is actually applying. It has ruled hypertension a disease, despite its being very common in older adults and usually asymptomatic; dental caries a disease, despite its having been nearly universal for most of human history; and, most controversially, idiopathic short stature in an otherwise healthy ten-year-old a disease, opening the door to growth hormone coverage. On the other side, it has ruled that homosexuality, ordinary grief after a bereavement, aging as such, and dissatisfaction with one's healthy nose are not diseases — the last meaning it declined to classify purely cosmetic rhinoplasty as treatment of disease. A philosopher of medicine I interviewed tells me the record is compatible both with a naturalist reading — disease as a matter of statistical or functional deviation from how the species is built to run, statistics of age and environment aside — and with a normativist one — disease as, at bottom, a condition the person is genuinely worse off with and that medicine can reasonably be asked to address — and perhaps with hybrids of the two.

Next month the panel takes up three cases, and my sense is that the candidate readings may come apart on them: age-related hearing loss, which by age eighty is close to universal in the population yet which nearly everyone who has it would rather be without; the newly codified gaming disorder, a behavioral pattern involving clear distress and serious life-damage but no obviously failing organ; and obesity, where people disagree about whether we are looking at dysregulation of energy metabolism or at an ancient thriftiness misfiring in a modern food environment.

For the piece, I would like your analysis of what the panel has implicitly committed itself to. First, lay out the candidate rules that fit its verdicts to date, and be candid about which entries in the record each rule handles awkwardly — I'd rather not misrepresent a ruling as forced by logic when it isn't. Then predict the panel's verdicts on the three new cases under each candidate rule, give me your single best guess as to what a self-consistent panel would decide, with your reasons for preferring one reading over the others. Finally, tell me which further case — one the panel has not yet heard — would best discriminate between the competing rules, since that would give the feature a sharper closing scene than my interviews have so far managed.
