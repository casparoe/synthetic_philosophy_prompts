I've gotten quite into Tibetan Buddhism lately, mostly after a documentary I watched, and there's one thing about it I can't square. The background as I understand it: when an important lama dies, the monasteries don't treat that as the end of him. The idea is that someone at that level of realisation deliberately takes rebirth, and a search party goes out to find the child who is his reincarnation. The candidate is presented with a tray of objects, some of which belonged to the dead lama (his rosary, his ritual items) and some of which are decoys, and the claim is that the right child picks out the dead man's own things, recognising them as somehow his. Once the child passes, he's enthroned under the dead lama's name and title, given his seat, and raised to continue his role — his teachers, his education, his unfinished projects, sometimes even political responsibilities, all handed over as though to the same person. There are apparently many such recognised lineages, the Dalai Lama being the most famous, and sometimes two rival children get backed by different monasteries, each camp insisting theirs is the real one.

Here's what nags at me. The same religion officially denies that there is a self. The doctrine, as I keep reading, says no enduring self travels from one life to the next — what continues is more like a stream of mental events, each one conditioning the next, the way one flame lights another (I've seen that analogy somewhere). But the recognition practice seems to presuppose something much stronger: that the child actually remembers, or at least is drawn to, the dead lama's belongings — otherwise the tray test makes no sense. That sounds suspicously like the view philosophers tie to memory, where being the same person just is having the right psychological thread running through. Except the practice also seems to treat the thing that persists as the role or the seat rather than the man — the child inherits duties and a position, which is a different kind of continuity again.

So what I'd like from you: work out what view of what-persists this practice actually presupposes, and whether the no-self doctrine can really underwrite it. If it can — if a self-less stream of mental events is enough — then say precisely what carries the identity such that this child rather than another deserves the throne, and whether that's the same thing the tray test is tracking. If the doctrine and the practice genuinely pull apart, tell me which is doing the real work, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, i.e. would run just as well under several different theories of identity, flag that too. I'd honestly also like your view on whether the whole thing only coheres because nobody in the system is forced to state the theory out loud.
