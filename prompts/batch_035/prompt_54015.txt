I run a small doctoral program in a department that is strong in formal logic and proof theory but has nobody working on truth directly. One of my students — sharp on the technical side, three years of funding, no particular attachment to any camp — wants to write her dissertation somewhere inside the deflationary debate about truth: roughly, the family of views on which truth is not a deep property but a thin, device-like notion whose whole job is disquotation, generalization, and expressive convenience. My worry is that the debate's canonical moves are by now so ritualized — the inflationist points at the explanatory work truth seems to do in compositional semantics, the deflationist says the explanation is really carried by the object-language predicates — that a student can spend three years polishing a move in a game whose stakes nobody has re-examined.

So I want a defended research agenda rather than a survey. Rank the genuinely open questions in this area by how much they deserve her three years, and defend the ordering explicitly. Some candidates I can see, though I don't trust my own list: whether a deflationist can really get by without truth playing an explanatory role in compositional semantics, or whether explaining meaning in terms of truth-conditions is an inflationary commitment smuggled in at the foundation; what deflationism should say about truth-value gaps and the paradoxes, given that disquotation for Liars seems to demand a gap while disquotation for ordinary sentences forbids them; whether the truth predicate has a constitutive normative role in the theory of belief, and whether that role is even compatible with the deflationist's picture of truth as expressing rather than reporting; and the creeping generalization problem — the temptation to extend deflationism to existence, to logical consequence, to reference, until it stops being a theory of one word and becomes a global quietism. You are free to add questions I've missed and to argue that something on my list is not really open at all.

For each item you keep, say what makes it worth the years: how much other work in the area silently depends on its answer, whether it's tractable for someone with her background — comfortable with schematic theories, sequent calculi, and infinite conjunctions as a technical matter, but with no taste for the history of the debate — and what would count as progress short of resolution. Then tell me what you'd put at the bottom and why: which of these, if answered, would change least downstream, so that her labor there would be impressive to the profession but philosophically inert. I'd also like to know what recent or foreseeable developments — a new treatment of the paradoxes, a shift in the semantic program, an argument that some dispute is merely verbal — should most change the ranking, so she can be watching for the right things.

Throughout, be candid about your confidence: distinguish the claims you'd stake something on from the ones you're gesturing at, and at the end single out the one point in your ordering you're least sure of, with an account of what would change your mind about it.
