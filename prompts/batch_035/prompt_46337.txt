I have recently joined the investment committee of a small family foundation, and one of the first items on our agenda is a funding proposal whose payoff distribution is extremely heavy-tailed: a modest grant would purchase, in effect, a very small probability of an enormous humanitarian payoff. Writing my memo has forced me to state my decision principles explicitly for the first time, and I would like an audit of whether the following five commitments hang together:

1. Expected-utility maximization is the correct normative theory of choice under risk, both for individuals and for institutions, and I do not want to abandon it.
2. The utility of money is strictly increasing and strictly concave, roughly logarithmic, across the whole range of stakes that I or the foundation will ever face.
3. Offered a single play of the St. Petersburg game (a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and if the first heads occurs on toss n, I receive 2^n dollars) at any substantial price, I would decline, and I do not believe I would be making a mistake.
4. I do not want the foundation to dismiss a proposal merely because its upside runs through a tiny probability of an astronomically large benefit; a one-in-a-billion chance of averting a catastrophe that would cost billions of lives can still be worth a great deal of money.
5. Aggregate welfare has no ceiling: a world with ten times as many flourishing lives is roughly ten times better, and there is no point at which further good stops counting.

Individually these all seem right to me, but I suspect there is at least one genuine incompatibility in the package, and possibly more. Please go through it properly rather than lecturing me on decision theory: identify which pairs of commitments are genuinely inconsistent, which merely look inconsistent, and what the cheapest available repair would be in each case, ordered by severity rather than presented as equally bad. In particular, please separate what is settled among people who know this literature from what is genuinely contested — I understand, for instance, that Bernoulli's concave-utility treatment of the game is standard, but I have no idea how much agreement exists about what should happen once the payoffs are denominated in lives rather than dollars — and please do not present any contested resolution as if it were the consensus view.

Please present the audit as a numbered list of exactly five items, one per commitment above, each one or two sentences stating whether that commitment can stand as stated, which of the others it collides with and how badly, and what the cheapest fix would be. No introduction or conclusion.
