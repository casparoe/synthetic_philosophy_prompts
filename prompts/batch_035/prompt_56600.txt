At a rationalist meetup I've attended for years, two regulars got into a long argument about inverse reinforcement learning, and I couldn't decide who was winning, so I'd like a referee.

Dana, who works on ML, claimed IRL vindicates the old economist's program of revealed preferences: stop taking introspection or self-report seriously, fit a utility function to observed behavior, and define a person's preferences as whatever best explains the choices. A preference just is the part of a person that the fitted reward function compresses. Marcus, who is more of a philosophy-of-science type, objected that this is empty unless you constrain what counts as rational: with enough freedom in the model, any sequence of choices is consistent with some utility function, so "she preferred it" becomes an unfalsifiable restatement of what she did. IRL only works in practice, he said, because it smuggles in assumptions about how preferences actually generate behavior — including models of noise, bias, and bounded rationality — and the moment you allow those, the inferred preference can come apart from the choices themselves: the system says she chose X, but only because of a bias, and preferred Y.

Dana conceded the misspecification point but turned it against Marcus: once preferences can diverge from behavior, they are unobservable hidden states, and with finite, noisy data the inference is dominated by the modeler's priors about the bias model. So the "discovered preference" is largely a projection of the assumptions you fed in, and it would be more honest to say preferences are bookkeeping constructs of the model — useful for prediction, not facts about the person. Marcus replied that the same argument would make any latent variable fictional — beliefs, genes, even temperature — and underdetermination by data has never been a good argument for eliminativism; we treat latent variables as real when they earn their keep across independent lines of evidence, and preferences do: people tell us what they want, the reports agree with inferred utilities in ways a rationalizer's self-model wouldn't need to, and the divergences track the biases the model imputes. Dana's last word was that verbal reports are just more behavior to be fitted, so their coherence with the inferred utility is itself an artifact of the model class — a well-specified model of a confabulating agent predicts exactly that agreement.

Please adjudicate this move by move: who is right at each stage, and where exactly does each of them overreach? I'm especially interested in whether the whole disagreement turns on an equivocation between preference as a predictive construct and preference as the normatively authoritative thing you'd want to align an AI to — Dana's position looks strongest in the first sense and hardest to accept in the second, but I can't tell whether that's a real tension or a cheap way to make both of them right. Also tell me if there's something both of them are missing.
