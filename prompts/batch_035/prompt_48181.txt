I've been rereading the opening books of Plato's Laws, where the Athenian Stranger argues, against the Cretan and the Spartan, that wine should be permitted at communal drinking parties — provided they're governed by a sober symposiarch — because drunkenness under supervision trains the young in self-control and reveals the character of the mature, and because wine was, on his telling, given to mankind as a medicine for the stiffness and timidity of old age. He also wants wine withheld from children entirely and rationed for young men, with freer access only for the older. This has made me try to work out my own view of when drinking makes sense, and I've been collecting verdicts on cases to see if they add up to anything:

A shy student has two drinks before a class presentation to borrow some confidence: not fine, somehow — it feels like counterfeit courage. A family deliberately gets the reserved, stiff grandfather tipsy at a wedding so he'll dance and talk: fine, even kind. A drinking club where members get quite drunk under a sober chair who keeps order and settles disputes: fine. Two glasses of wine alone, every evening, to unwind from work: not fine — I can't fully defend this one, but it bothers me more than the rowdy club. Drinking to blunt grief after a death: not fine. An executive taking a prospective business partner out and matching him drink for drink partly to see how he behaves when loosened: fine, though it feels a bit sneaky. Drinking only to seem collegial at a work dinner: fine. Teenagers drinking to feel older: not fine.

My tentative principle is that drinking is legitimate as a governed exercise — a controlled test of, or window onto, character — and illegitimate when it substitutes for some virtue or serves escape. But the lone-evening case and the collegial-work-drinks case don't look like exercises in anything, and the shy student and the grandfather both borrow ease, yet I judge them oppositely. Is there a principle that captures all or nearly all of my verdicts, and that would also vindicate or repair Plato's age grading? If you think no honest principle saves them, say which verdicts have to go and why, and please tell me plainly if you think my reading of the Laws is off or the case list is simply incoherent. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning; no preamble and no restatement of my question.
