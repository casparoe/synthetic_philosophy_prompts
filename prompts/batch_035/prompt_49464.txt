Every profile says "I love hiking and being spontaneous" and noone believes anyone, and my friend who did her PhD in animal behavior was like "that's just cheap signals, you need the handicap principle" and it clicked. Now I want my students to *feel* why cost is the thing that makes a signal beleivable, not just nod along to the peacock tail example.

Design an in-class game where that happens instead of me telling them. I'm imagining something like: some students are "employers" and some are "candidates" with hidden types, candidates send signals, employers accept or pass, and we run it twice — once where the signal is free to send and once where it costs something real but small (effort, points, candy, whatever works). The students shouldn't know in advance that the second round is the point; to them it should just be a hiring game with a rule change. Please give me full rules, materials, the scoring/payoff scheme (no real money, and no more than ~5 minutes of setup), and how to assign the hidden types so it's fair. Then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the machinery: why cheap signals get flooded with fakes, why the cost has to bite the low types harder than the high types for honesty to survive at all, and where they've seen this in their own lives — dating apps, job markets, apologies, whatever comes up.

Two asks on top of the design. First, tell me honestly what the game can't reproduce: it's basically one-shot, there's no evolution over generations of players, no frequency-dependence, and students might behave nicer than real signalers because I'm watching them. Say which parts of the idea the game genuinely demonstrates and which parts I have to supply in the debrief regardless. Second, tell me what will predictibly go wrong the first time I run it — students colluding, someone gaming the point system, the signal cost being so trivial that nothing changes between rounds — and what the fix is for each.

Last constraint: don't mention the handicap principle, peacocks, or anything biology-flavored until the debrief. I want them to think they're playing a job-market game and only find out at the end that they've been peacocks the whole time.
