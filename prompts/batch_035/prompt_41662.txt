My friend Devi and I got into it at like 1am last weekend about who deserves credit when a discovery gets made twice. She's been issuing verdicts on cases for months now, sort of as a running bit between us, and next week we're scheduled to do two more and I want to walk in prepared. Here's her track record so far.

Calculus: she says it was basically inevitable, the pieces were all sitting there, and Newton and Leibniz should split the credit straight down the middle, none of this Newton-was-first business. Natural selection: also inevitable in her view, since Malthus was lying around for anyone to read, but Darwin still deserves way more than Wallace because he'd worked it out decades earlier and put the evidence together while Wallace just fired off a letter. Penicillin: not inevitable, it needed the actual lucky accident, so Fleming gets the credit even though Florey and Chain were the ones who turned it into something that saved lives. Oxygen: inevitable given where chemistry was at the time, but the real discoverer is Lavoisier and not Priestley or Scheele, because those two were staring straight at it and didn't understand what they had. And the telephone: inevitable once the telegraph existed, and yet she's fine with Bell beating Gray on a same-day filing and keeping priority, which honestly bugs me, since it seems to cut against her whole sharing instinct, but she insists it's consistent.

The two cases on deck are Wegener and continental drift, where basically nobody was close to him, he had the right pattern and the wrong mechanism, and he died before plate tectonics vindicated him (plate tectonics itself, funnily enough, coming together in the early 60s out of several people's overlapping work). And the DNA double helix, where Pauling was circling with a wrong version, Rosalind Franklin had the data that basically settled it, and Watson and Crick did the assembly. So: predict her verdict on both, tell me the candidate rules that fit her record — I can see at least a leap-size rule, an understanding rule, and something like an effort-and-priority rule, but you tell me — say which one you think she's actually running and why, and give me the further case that would really tell those rules apart, the sort of thing I could spring on her at the next dinner. Also please tell me which of her existing verdicts is closest to breaking under whichever rule you pick, because I want to poke at that one specifically, the telephone thing is my current favorite target but maybe you'll find a worse one. Whatever you write, keep it as plain continuous prose like an email, no headings, no bullet points, no bolding, no tables, nothing like that. And go easy on me, we'd had wine.
