A student of mine fine-tunes language models, and a procedure she described has left me unable to say what I thought I knew about a classic piece of epistemology. Her team trains a model on its own outputs: the model drafts an answer, grades it against a rubric it also wrote, retains what passes, and retrains on that. I told her this could not teach the model anything about whether it grades well — you cannot lift yourself by your own bootstraps. She asked me to write down exactly which step of the ordinary reasoning I was forbidding, and every principle I produced condemned reasoning that seems unremarkable. Here is the chain, in its traditional form:

(1) This morning my car's fuel gauge read full, and by reading it I thereby came to know that the tank was full.
(2) On any occasion on which I come to know the tank is full by reading the gauge, I know that the gauge was accurate on that occasion.
(3) I can now tally: on occasions o1 through on, the gauge read full, and on each of those occasions it was accurate; so it has a perfect recent record.
(4) From that record I may infer, as from any record, that the gauge is reliable.
(5) Nothing in this procedure consults anything but the gauge itself — no dipstick, no mechanic, no independent check.
(6) So I can come to know the gauge is reliable without any independent check.
(7) But now take a source I have never checked and have no antecedent reason to trust: my late grandmother's barometer, a fortune-teller's crystal ball, a device that emits arbitrary symbols. I consult it on n occasions, conclude on each that things are as it says because it says so, tally the results, and — by the very same steps — compile a perfect record and come to know it is reliable.
(8) And the same holds for faculties: a brain in a vat, whose experiences are fed to it by the vat's computer, can run the identical procedure on its own seemings and come to know that things are by and large as they appear.

I cannot accept that the crystal ball gets certified by its own utterances. Yet I would much rather keep (1) through (6) — they look like ordinary learning from experience, and I am even tempted by the thought that a Moorean proof of the external world runs on the same fuel. Tell me which step to reject and why that step looked fine until now. In particular: is the trouble in (2), so that I never really knew the gauge was accurate on those occasions — and if so, does that also condemn the anti-sceptical use of the reasoning, or is there a principled difference between the gauge and the vat-dweller? Or is the trouble in the tally, so that a record compiled this way is not evidence of anything? I would like the alternatives priced: what each rejection costs in the wider debate — Chisholm's problem of the criterion, what Pryor calls dogmatism, the accusation of transmission failure — and I would like your honest view on whether the philosophical diagnosis, whatever it is, transfers to my student's self-grading model. If the bootstrapping reasoning is vicious, is her procedure vicious in the same way, or does its vice (if any) lie elsewhere?
