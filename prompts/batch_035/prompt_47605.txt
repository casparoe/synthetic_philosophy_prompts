I'm a novelist who has agreed to moderate a public conversation at a literary festival with a philosopher known for defending functionalism about the mind — the view that mental states are constituted by their causal roles rather than by their physical makeup, so that pain or belief could in principle be realized in neurons, silicon, or anything else with the right organization. I would rather not fall into the usual "will the chatbot become conscious" chatter; I want to press where the actual debate presses. Draft eight to ten questions in a natural conversational arc that do the following: (1) probe whether multiple realizability holds at the level of detail that matters — whether we can say two systems are in the *same* mental state while abstracting away from their biology without either hand-picking the realizers that count or letting the claim collapse into something too thin to test; (2) draw out the absent-qualia worry (a system that matches my causal organization exactly yet feels nothing at all) and press on the likely reply that such scenarios are intuition pumps rather than evidence; (3) engage my own trade: I invent characters, and readers attribute minds to mere arrangements of words on a page — include at least one question about whether a fictional character, or the text itself, could count as a realizer, and what that suggests about how permissive functionalism can become before it stops excluding anything; and (4) close with a question about what could count against the view — what the philosopher would need to discover, in minds or in machines, before concluding that organization alone is not enough. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer the philosopher is most likely to give, so that the standard replies don't let the conversation settle into a recital of the position.
