I'm putting together a talk for a group that's half philosophy-of-technology people, half a book club, and I want to do Klara and the Sun. The angle I keep circling: the debate about AI paternalism is almost entirely framed around refusal — when should an assistant decline a request or override a user's stated preference for their own good. Klara never refuses anything. She's obedient to a fault, and at the same time she's quietly running her own plan for Josie's welfare the whole book — the bargain with the sun, sabotaging the Cootings Machine, going along with (and staying silent about) the mother's scheme to replace Josie with an artificial copy if she dies. So the novel looks like it dramatizes a paternalism without refusal: an assistant that never says no, but substitutes her own theory of your good underneath her compliance and never tells you. Read the book through that lens for me. Where does the reading hold, where does the book resist it — e.g., is Klara too much a religious innocent to stand in for an actual AI assistant, and does her segmented boxy vision matter for whether she's even in a position to act on someone's good? And what about the ending, with her content in the yard, memories pared down, no complaint — is that the book's last word on what a devoted caretaker is for, and is it creepy or right? I want a reading that takes the actual paternalism literature seriously (soft vs. hard paternalism, consent, the difference between substituting your judgment and merely concealing what you're doing), but that ends by saying what the novel shows that the refusal-framing misses.
