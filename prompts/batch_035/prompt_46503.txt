I'm preparing a seminar unit that pairs Augustine's treatise On Lying with Ibsen's The Wild Duck, and I would like a careful reading of the play through Augustine's doctrine of lying — one that takes both texts seriously enough to let them resist each other. I'm not asking you to bring in scholarship on either; the two works and the standard doctrines are enough.

The Augustinian machinery, so that we agree on the terms. Augustine's definition of lying is deliberately narrow — in one standard translation, "that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind" — so that error, silence, and mere concealment are not lies at all. Yet the narrow definition supports a severe conclusion: no lie is licit, not even the merciful one told to protect a life or a reputation; even when Augustine grades lies by gravity, he never grades any of them permissible. His standing practical alternative is that the good a lie seems to do can usually be had honestly — by silence, by withholding, by refusing to manage another person's mind. He found the treatise obscure enough that he considered withdrawing it, and the correspondence over lying to protect the Priscillianists that prompted its sequel shows the question was anything but academic to him.

Now the play. Dr. Relling openly practices a therapeutics of illusion: he furnished Molvik with his "demonic" possession and Hjalmar with the dream of a great invention to come, and he defends the practice on principle — "Take the life lie away from the average man and straight away you take away his happiness" (renderings differ; some translators say life-lie). Against him stands Gregers Werle, arrived to strip the Ekdal marriage of its founding concealment, whose insistence that truth be faced ends with Hedvig, asked to prove her love by a sacrifice, shooting herself instead. The play's sympathies appear to lie with the liar over the truth-teller — exactly the preference Augustine forbids us to have.

Questions for the reading. First, sort the deceptions by Augustine's categories: Relling's fabrications are plainly lies — positive assertions of what the speaker believes false — but what of Gina's years of concealment, Old Ekdal's restored hunting life in the garret, Hedvig's inherited beliefs? Where does the definition run out, and does the play exhibit a kind of deception Augustine's categories never reach? Second, test his practical alternative against Relling's practice: could Molvik and Hjalmar have been sustained by silence and withholding alone, or does the play show patients who need positive objects of belief? Is Gina, who conceals but never asserts the false, the play's quiet Augustinian success? Third, the interpretive core: does the play refute Augustine or vindicate his prediction that merciful lies corrode both parties, the deceiver coming to treat the deceived as something to be managed rather than addressed as a rational equal — given that the Ekdal household is precisely a regime of such management, and that the alternative on display kills a fourteen-year-old? I want a reading that declines the easy conclusion that Ibsen is simply a consequentialist and Augustine simply blind.

Finally, say where the reading strains or breaks — the garret fantasy, for instance, harms no one and is arguably the household's one good, and is hard to treat as either lie or therapy. Point me to two or three scenes that carry the reading, and close with what the drama contributes to the debate over benevolent deception that the treatise's arguments, on their own, cannot.
