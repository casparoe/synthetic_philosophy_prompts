I am a philosophy of science professor, and for the last six years our departmental workshop has hosted what has become a curiously durable feud between two colleagues. One, a philosopher of biology squarely in the lineage of Larry Wright and Ruth Millikan, holds that a trait's function is fixed by its selection history: to say what the heart is for is to make a compressed historical claim about what ancestral hearts were selected for doing, and nothing else earns the word "function." The other, who works on explanations in cognitive science in the tradition of Cummins's causal-role analysis, holds that attributing a function is citing a component's disposition in an analysis of some capacity of the containing system -- and that this is what functional attributions in journals actually accomplish, selection history or no selection history. Neither has moved an inch, and the rest of us have learned to stop scheduling them as discussants of the same paper. Before one of them retires and the dispute dissolves into anecdote, I would like us to produce the document they should have written long ago: a proper joint statement, as from an adversarial collaboration.

Please draft it with the following structure. First, a numbered section of claims both could sign -- about the biological facts themselves, about what teleological language is doing in biology journals, about what each camp's account gets right as a description of some real explanatory practice. Second, a table of their remaining disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side would accept as accurate rather than as a caricature; I would particularly like to see how they would state the malfunction problem (my etiological colleague insists that a malformed heart still has the function of pumping, which he says selection history handles and pure causal-role analysis does not; my causal-role colleague says the etiological account makes function attributions hostage to paleontological luck). Third, the cruxes -- the subquestions such that settling one would move at least one of them. I have my suspicions about what these are, and I would like yours: whether there are genuine function attributions in biology that outrun selection history, such as a de novo gene that arose a few hundred generations ago and is useful from the first instance, or a co-opted trait where the explanatory target shifted (feathers and flight comes up every year); whether "function" talk in knockout experiments and neuroimaging studies is loose popular usage or a strictly proper usage that only one account vindicates; and whether malfunction talk is a test case or a distraction.

For each crux, please record a concrete case, experiment, or piece of historical evidence both parties would agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction in advance -- what the etiological colleague would expect the case to show and what the causal-role colleague would expect, so that the predictions could actually come apart. Finally, adjudicate the meta-question I have come to care about most: how much of this dispute is substantive and how much is verbal, in the specific sense that the two parties may be theorizing two different explanatory projects that happen to share the word "function." If you conclude that some portion is verbal, say which portion and why, and identify what would remain to fight about even after the verbal part is stipulated away.
