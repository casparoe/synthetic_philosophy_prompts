I am an early-career philosopher with a strong formal background — comfortable with axiomatic decision theory, basic measure theory, and enough economics to read a representation theorem — and I have roughly three years to write a dissertation within the debate over risk-weighted expected utility theory (REU). Rather than another paper defending or attacking the axioms directly, I want to choose an open question whose resolution would actually move the field. Please help me set an agenda.

Consider questions like the following, plus any you think I have misjudged or omitted:

1. The long-run objection. Critics argue that agents who depart from expected utility maximization, including REU agents with sufficiently risk-averse weightings, can be shown to fare worse in aggregate over sequences of choices, in a way that makes their preferences look irrational once the sequence is treated as a single composite choice. Defenders reply that the aggregation assumptions do the work and beg the question. Is there a principled way to settle whether long-run performance is evidence about the rationality of risk-weighted preferences, or is the dispute destined to come down to intuitions about composite individuation of choices?

2. REU under model uncertainty. Standard decision theory treats probabilities as given; the hardest practical cases combine risk (known chances) with ambiguity or deep uncertainty about the model itself. Can risk-weighted machinery be extended to second-order uncertainty in a principled way, and would such an extension vindicate or undermine the case for permitting risk-sensitive preferences in the first place?

3. The downstream stakes. Much applied work in ethics and policy — cost-benefit analysis with small probabilities of large losses, arguments about the value of averting existential risks, domino-style arguments in public health — assumes that expected value is the rational way to aggregate across outcomes. If REU-style permissions are genuine, some of this reasoning appears to be undermined, since a risk-averse agent may rationally refuse a positive-expected-value gamble. Which downstream implications are real, and which misapply decision theory in ways that no serious party to the debate would accept?

4. Whose rationality is being characterized. There is a persistent ambiguity in the debate about whether the permissibility of risk-weighted preferences is a claim about the ideals of rationality, about what advice a theory should give to boundedly ideal agents, or about the social function of permitting risk-diverse agents to trade with one another. Does separating these readings dissolve any of the standard objections, or merely relabel them?

For your ranking, please state explicitly the criteria you are using — importance to the broader literature, tractability for one person with my background and time budget, neglect, and how much else in decision theory turns on the answer — and defend the trade-offs among them. Identify which of these questions are upstream of which, so I can see dependency structure, and say which you would deprioritize because settling it would not change much downstream even if it were won. Finally, tell me what developments over the next few years — new theorems, new empirical work on risk preferences, changes in how applied ethics uses expected-value reasoning — would move a question up or down the list, so I can revisit the agenda as circumstances change.
