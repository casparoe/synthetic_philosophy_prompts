I'm trying to locate my disagreement with a colleague, and I'd like your credence on the underlying question: that death is typically bad *for the person who dies* — bad for them, that is, not merely bad for their survivors or a loss for the world. My own number is around 0.85; my colleague, who takes the Epicurean line seriously, is around 0.3. I don't want a survey of positions or an "it depends on your theory of well-being." I want your number, and the decomposition that produces it.

Please split your credence across at least the following sub-questions, assigning each a probability and one line of justification, then show how they combine:

1. That well-being is determined by what a life actually contains, such that a life cut short can be worse for its subject than a longer life containing strictly more good — as opposed to the view that well-being is exhausted by pleasant or favored states of the subject, which makes a deprivation account of death's badness unavailable in principle.
2. That an event can be a misfortune for a person at a time when that person does not exist — or, if you prefer to say the misfortune is not located at any time at all but is simply true of the person's life as a whole, that this relocation is a substantive claim rather than a semantic maneuver that concedes the Epicurean point while refusing to say so.
3. That the core Epicurean thought — when death is present, the subject is not, and when the subject is present, death is not — functions as an argument against death's badness rather than merely as a restatement of the assumption that harms must be experienced by their subject. If you think it's the latter, say so and say how that changes the weighting.

In addition, I want you to state where the Lucretian symmetry point stands in your reasoning. If you take the badness of death to be partly a matter of lost future goods, you owe an explanation of why the vast nonexistence before birth is not equally a deprivation, or an acknowledgment that the symmetry cuts against you; if you think the symmetry genuinely does undercut deprivationism, make that explicit and carry it into the combination. Please also fold in, if you think it belongs, the question of whether death can be bad for someone who never acquires the concepts of death or of the goods they lose — an infant, say — or whether that case reveals a limit of your account rather than an application of it. If you think any further term belongs in the decomposition, add it; if you think one of mine is a confusion dressed as a question, drop it and say why.

Finally, tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, which single argument or imagined case would move that number by 0.1 or more in either direction, and — given my 0.85 and my colleague's 0.3 — where you judge the disagreement between us most likely lives: sub-question 1, 2, or 3. Commit to an answer on that last point; locating the disagreement is the whole point of the exercise.
