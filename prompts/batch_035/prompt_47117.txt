My state legislature is currently debating a cannabis legalization bill whose organizing conceit, repeated by nearly every sponsor, is that we should regulate cannabis like alcohol: licensed retail, age gates, potency labels, excise taxes. At a town hall last week a speaker went considerably further. He argued that the drug war as a whole is simply national alcohol Prohibition re-run at planetary scale — a legally established market abolished by moralists, yielding the same black markets, the same enrichment of violent traffickers, the same adulterated product and corrupted enforcement — and that we already know how that story ended, with repeal, so the rational endpoint is legalizing and commercially regulating all of it, not just cannabis. My brother-in-law, who works in addiction treatment, thinks the analogy is rhetorical decoration and nothing more: alcohol was a single, deeply culturally entrenched substance that had been legal everywhere for centuries before being banned, whereas "drugs" is a heterogeneous grab bag of substances with different pharmacologies, different user populations, and no comparable pre-prohibition commercial baseline. I was assigned to summarize the arguments for our local policy newsletter and quickly realized I cannot adjudicate this without actually working through the comparison, which is what I'm asking you to do.

Please audit the Prohibition analogy properly rather than ruling on it by intuition. First, the correspondence table: what in the drug war plays the role of the bootlegger, the speakeasy, organized crime's rise, the adulterated product, the repeal coalition, and the post-repeal alcohol market? Second, the disanalogies — and here I'd like you to separate the ones that matter for the conclusion being drawn from the ones that don't: the absence of a pre-existing legal commercial market for most substances, the heterogeneity of substances lumped under one prohibition, the international treaty regime that has no Prohibition-era counterpart and constrains what any single country can do unilaterally, the medicalization of some of these substances and their entanglement with prescription systems, and the fact that alcohol's demand base was near-universal adult consumption while demand for, say, heroin is marginal. Third, tell me where Portugal actually fits, because everyone at the town hall cited Portugal as though it settled the matter, and I want to be precise about the fact that Portugal decriminalized possession and use without legalizing supply. Does the Prohibition analogy predict that halfway outcome at all, or is Portugal evidence that the speaker's inference from "prohibition fails" to "full commercial legalization" is stronger than the evidence supports?

I'd also like you to descend to the version that actually matters for the bill in front of us: does cannabis specifically sustain the "regulate like alcohol" mapping at retail strength — potency caps against ABV percentages, home cultivation against home brewing, the tax structure, drugged driving against drunk driving, the public-consumption question? And if there is a genuinely better analogy than alcohol for how to govern a widely used psychoactive substance, say so rather than grading Prohibition on a curve. A final request, made sincerely: if you think the analogy is doing no real argumentative work in either the speaker's mouth or my brother-in-law's, or that my framing of any of this — Portugal included — is confused or loaded, say that plainly. I have to publish a summary, and a tidy "both sides have a point" that the comparison doesn't actually support would be worse to me than an unmannerly verdict.
