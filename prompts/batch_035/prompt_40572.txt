A friend and I have been trading verdicts on group-based preferences, and I've noticed that hers fall into a pattern I can't quite pin down. Her rulings so far: she supports Norway's law requiring the boards of its public limited companies to be at least 40% of each gender; she supports American universities treating an applicant's race as one consideration among many in holistic admissions; she rejects schemes that set aside a fixed number of admission seats for a racial group and fill them regardless of where those applicants would otherwise have ranked; she supports India's constitutionally reserved seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in parliament and the state legislatures; she wants legacy preferences for the children of alumni abolished; and she applauds orchestras that hold auditions behind a screen.

Three new cases are coming up at our next argument, and I'd like to call her verdicts before she opens her mouth.

1. A state medical college automatically adds a fixed number of points to the entrance-exam scores of applicants from historically excluded communities, changing nothing else about how applicants are ranked or seats are filled.
2. A private fellowship for future public leaders reserves a fixed number of its places for members of those communities, but a panel interviews and ranks the candidates within the reserved pool — the way Indian parties still compete for votes within reserved constituencies.
3. A city allocates scarce subsidized apartments by lottery and gives applicants from those communities extra tickets in the draw.

I'm less interested in the three verdicts than in the rule she seems to be running. Her record looks consistent with at least two: one that turns on whether the preference operates through a human being's judgment — an electorate, a nominating committee, an admissions officer, an audition panel — or instead mechanically determines who gets what; and one that turns on the kind of good at stake, being readier to accept group preferences in the institutions that govern and in the gateways to them than in the distribution of other benefits. Each rule also owes an account of why legacy preferences are the one selector-influence she rejects. So: what does each rule predict for the three cases, which rule fits her record best and why, and what further case — not among the three — would most cleanly force the rules apart?
