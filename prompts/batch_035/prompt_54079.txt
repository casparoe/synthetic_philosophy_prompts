I run a small blog that's roughly half philosophy, half procrastination, and the next post is about the doomsday argument — the thing where you reason: "somewhere around a hundred billion humans have ever lived, so I'm roughly the hundred-billionth human; if I treat my birth rank as a random sample from all the humans there will ever be, it's surprising to find myself that early if the future holds trillions more, so doom probably comes sooner rather than later." Readers keep telling me it's "obviously wrong" and then can't say which premise they're rejecting, which is exactly what I want to catch. So I need a quiz, not another explainer.

What I want: a "which response to the doomsday argument do you actually hold?" quiz — 8 to 10 questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions. Every question must be answerable by someone who's never heard of self-sampling or self-indication assumptions; it should sort people by their intuitions about cases, not by which label they like. Please work in something like this set of cases (adapt as needed, but these are the ones that matter for my purposes):

1. The numbered-balls case: an urn contains either 1,000 or 10 numbered balls, and the numbers were stamped in order of creation; one ball is drawn at random and it reads 7. Does that shift you toward the small urn?
2. A God's-coin case: a fair coin decides whether to create one human or a trillion humans. You exist. Conditional on your existing at all, do you lean toward the trillion-world?
3. A non-random-sample case: you're in a prison and learn you're 57th in the queue for the phone; queues here happen to be ordered by remaining sentence length. Does being 57th tell you anything about how long the total queue is?
4. A reference-class case: does it change the argument if you count only humans versus all conscious observers — e.g. if the future were mostly digital minds rather than biological ones? Does your answer feel principled or like you're choosing the class to get the conclusion you want?
5. A many-origins case: suppose physicists discovered an infinite multiverse where every possible history occurs somewhere. Does your being early in this particular history still tell you anything about how long this history runs?

The scoring key should map onto these positions as leaves: (a) full acceptance — early birth rank is genuine Bayesian evidence that doom comes soon; (b) the "more people means more evidence I'd exist" reply — finding that you exist at all favours hypotheses with more observers, which cancels or reverses the doomsday update; (c) the sampling-fallacy objector — your birth rank was never drawn randomly from all humans, your existence is correlated with when you exist, so the whole likelihood computation is a mirage; (d) the reference-class skeptic — the argument's force depends entirely on which class you sample from, and there's no objective fact about that; (e) the "valid but proves nothing new" position — whatever update the argument delivers was already baked into your priors, so running it never actually moves you; and (f) the timespan version — the accepter who thinks you can run the same logic on how long the thing has existed rather than on birth ranks, and is happy with that framing. Feel free to give the leaves short names for the key as long as the descriptions carry the real content.

Two honesty requirements, since my readers will absolutely call me out otherwise: if two of those positions would recieve identical answer patterns from every quiz-taker, flag that instead of inventing a fake distinguishing question, and note any combination of answers that lands nowhere on the tree — with a sentence on whether nobody's there because the view is incoherent or just unexplored. Also please have the last question or two be about whether the person changes their answer when you scale the case up (ten trillion future people versus a hundred years of margarine), since I suspect that's where intuitions actually break.

Format stuff: start with the quiz itself, no preface or commentary — the scoring key, your notes on which leaves the tree can't seperate, and any reasoning can come after the quiz. British English throughout. Keep the questions concrete and a bit playful; definately no question of the form "do you believe your birth rank is randomly sampled?" — that's just asking people to pick a label.
