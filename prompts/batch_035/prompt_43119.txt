I serve on the committee at my university that administers a small fellowship whose only eligibility criterion is that applicants be "early-career," and this month I have to write a reasoned ruling on whether a candidate eight years past her PhD, with a five-year caregiving gap in the middle, counts. Drafting it has forced me to notice that my gut verdicts about vagueness don't obviously add up to one view, and since the ruling matters to a real person, I'd like to know what I actually believe. Here are the verdicts. From a mound of 10,000 grains, removing a single grain can never be what turns a heap into a non-heap — I accept every single step of that sequence — yet I also accept that zero grains is flatly not a heap, so I'm committed to a transition I can't locate. When a colleague floated the epistemicist line that there is some exact grain-count cutoff, fixed by our usage, that we simply could never know, I found it not just unknown but, if I'm honest, barely coherent. The endpoints do feel utterly settled, though: one year out of grad school is clearly early-career, twenty-five years out clearly isn't. For a borderline case, I'd resist both asserting "she is early-career" and asserting its negation, and if a colleague asserted the negation I don't think she'd have said something false — yet "she is either early-career or not early-career" strikes me as plainly true, and I can't say what makes it true if both halves are up in the air. I also think there's no sharp line separating the clear cases from the borderline cases themselves. But when another colleague concluded from all this that our ruling is unconstrained — that any decision would be equally correct — I balked: the word doesn't determine an answer, but it isn't toothless either; some rulings would be wrong.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of language and logic, and think carefully before committing — brainstorm the candidate accounts first rather than starting from a verdict. What single account of vagueness, if any, captures this whole pattern? Consider whether epistemicism or supervaluationism fits, and if neither does, whether some third option does better. Tell me explicitly which of my verdicts has to go if the best theory can't save them all, and which pair of verdicts is the deepest tension. Then tell me what the resulting view implies for the case I keep going back and forth on: the fellowship ruling itself. In particular, if I end up with a view on which the criterion neither determinately applies nor determinately fails to apply to her, what should the document I sign actually say — that there is no fact of the matter, that the committee's decision constitutes the fact, or something else entirely? Reason it through from the theories themselves; no reading lists or citations are needed, just the arguments.
