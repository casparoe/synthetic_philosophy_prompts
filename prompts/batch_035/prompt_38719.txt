I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and at work we score project proposals on a rubric of scale times feasibility times "nobody else is doing this" — basically a home-grown version of the importance, tractability, and neglectedness framework that effective-altruism groups use for prioritizing causes. I recently tried applying the borrowed version to my own charitable giving and produced an argument whose conclusion I can't accept, even though each step looked fine when I wrote it:

(1) A fixed budget should be directed wherever the product of importance, tractability, and neglectedness is highest.
(2) Importance is bounded above by the full scale of the problem, but tractability and neglectedness both fall as effort on a cause rises: the more people already work on something, the less each additional unit of effort accomplishes and the less neglected the cause becomes.
(3) So any cause that already receives serious attention gets pushed down the ranking, and the top of the ranking will always be occupied by a cause that almost nobody works on.
(4) Among causes nobody works on, the ranking is settled by importance times tractability, and since there is no shortage of untouched problems that are small but very cheap to fix, I can always find one whose product beats any crowded cause — it only needs to be somewhat less small than the last one I found.
(5) Therefore the framework directs my giving toward some obscure, minor, completely unattended problem, and away from any major problem, precisely because major problems attract serious funding.
(6) That can't be right: the framework was supposed to help me locate the most important causes, not to make me flee whatever anyone else cares about.

I can't tell which step to reject. I'd like to keep (2), since diminishing returns seem real, and I'd be relieved to learn that (4) is where the argument cheats, but I can't pin down exactly how it cheats. Where does this go wrong, and what does rejecting that step cost me?
