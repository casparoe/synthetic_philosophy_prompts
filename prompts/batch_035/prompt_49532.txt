So I write novels for a living (midlist literary fiction, nothing you'd have heard of), and I also run a small workshop -- ten people, a mix of serious hobbyists and one or two MFA dropouts, we meet monthly for a three-hour evening session at a community arts centre. Next month I want to do something different: instead of the usual workshop-their-pages thing, I want them to run headfirst into the moral saints problem, the one from Susan Wolf's essay -- the idea that a person who was perfectly, maximally morally good would be someone you wouldn't actually want to be or be around, because the sainthood crowds out everything else that makes a life (or a person) attractive.

But here's the wrinkle that makes this MY version of the problem rather than the standard one: for a novelist, it's ambiguous whether the discomfort with the moral saint is about the person or about the craft. A perfectly good character is a boring character, sure, but is that telling us something about goodness, or just something about what narrative needs (flaw, conflict, agency)? I want an activity where they can't dodge that ambiguity -- where they feel the pull of both readings and maybe can't settle it, which honestly is where I've landed myself after years of trying to write genuinely decent people who aren't doormats or dullards.

Design me the session. What I have in mind is something like: early on they each sketch a character and write a short scene, then we impose escalating rounds of moral upgrades -- constraint cards or rules of the form "your character now gives away anything they don't strictly need" or "your character can no longer prioritise their own projects when someone else could benefit more" -- and they rewrite or write new scenes under each round, so they watch the character get sainified step by step and hopefully notice what dies on the way. Then some kind of blind vote or forced choice at the end that separates the two questions -- would you want this person as a friend vs. would you keep reading a novel about them -- so the aesthetic reading and the philosophical one get pried apart at least a bit. No lecture from me up front, I don't want them knowing the Wolf essay exists until the debrief, and even the debrief should be mostly them talking, me mapping their gripes onto the idea. The debrief should also show them where each step of the upgrade could be resisted (e.g. maybe the constraint doesn't actually follow from morality, maybe it does and the character is just badly drawn, maybe the life is good and the novel is just unwriteable) and what it costs to resist it there.

Give me the full runnable thing: materials, timing for the three hours, the actual upgrade cards (maybe 8-12 of them, escalating, and tell me what order to deploy them in), the vote mechanics, and a facilitator's script skeleton for the debrief that stays light on jargon -- remember these are fiction writers, they know "flat character" but not "supererogation." Also tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guess: someone writes a saint who's secretly smug and everyone votes on the smugness, but you'll see other failure modes), and be upfront about which part of the actual philosophical problem no game with fictional people can reproduce -- I'd rather know my activity's blind spot than pretend it doesn't have one.

One more thing, since I'll be acting on whatever you give me: flag how confident you actually are in the various pieces -- which bits of the design you'd stake money on and which are guesses dressed up as advice -- and call out the single point you're least sure about. And please write it all in British English; I'm in the UK and "envision" makes my eye twitch.
