In a philosophy of mind seminar on Dennett, we were discussing a model of consciousness that posits a workspace architecture: a brain system that "broadcasts" information widely to otherwise independent modules, with the routing built up through learned connections. A visiting speaker dismissed the model as smuggling in a skyhook, on the grounds that the workspace does intelligent, top-down design work that its own explanation never accounts for; a student objected that a workspace is a data structure, not a miracle. Our professor waved the exchange off with "cranes, not skyhooks," and moved on — and I left unsure whether the slogan had settled anything. I understand the image comes from Dennett's Darwin's Dangerous Idea, where he writes that good reductionists suppose all Design can be explained without skyhooks, while greedy reductionists suppose it can all be explained without cranes. But I want to know what the slogan actually claims when someone uses it as a verdict on a specific proposal like the workspace model. Please lay out, as a list I can bring back to seminar, the distinct precise theses "cranes, not skyhooks" could be expressing: which are platitudes (e.g., "no supernatural causes in scientific explanation"), which are substantive and contentious (e.g., that all apparent design must ultimately trace to mindless, algorithmic processes like natural selection, with higher-level design never appearing ex nihilo), and which are readings that actually endorse greedy reductionism — skipping levels in a rush to the foundation — despite the slogan's intent. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, and whether it would license the speaker's claim that the workspace model is a skyhook, the student's claim that it is a crane, or neither — in particular, whether the slogan can rule on a mid-level mechanism whose components are mechanical but whose overall organization is being treated as doing real explanatory work.
