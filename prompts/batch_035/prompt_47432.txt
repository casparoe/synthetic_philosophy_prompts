I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I'm preparing a long-form piece on how historians assign responsibility for the First World War. The puzzle I keep colliding with is usually stated in the language of overdetermination, and I want to think it through with someone who knows the philosophy of causation rather than just the historiography.

Here is the tension. Popular writers and even academic historians routinely say the war was overdetermined: so many causes were supposedly in play — the alliance system, the Anglo-German naval race, the Austro-Hungarian ultimatum to Serbia, Russia's general mobilization, the German blank cheque to Vienna, the mobilization schedules that turned diplomacy into a countdown — that the assassination in Sarajevo should not be treated as the cause of the catastrophe. But when I set that claim next to how philosophers actually define overdetermination — the textbook case of two assassins firing simultaneously, each shot individually sufficient for the victim's death — the July crisis does not look like that at all. No single condition in July 1914 was sufficient by itself to produce a general European war; if anything, each seems necessary-but-insufficient, which is nearly the opposite causal structure. So my working suspicion is that the term is being misused, or that historians mean something quite different by it.

I would like you to analyze the outbreak of the war as a case in the philosophy of causation, and to be disciplined about the mapping. Specifically: first, sort the candidate causes of July 1914 into the taxonomy — which are best modeled as preemptors or potential preemptors, which as conjunctive conditions whose joint sufficiency would be picked out by something like a NESS test, and which are merely background enabling conditions that the standard views would decline to count as causes at all. Second, tell me what this causal structure implies, if anything, for the attribution question — the Fischer thesis that Germany consciously chose war in 1914 versus Christopher Clark's picture of the powers blundering into it together. Does the structure of the causation, as opposed to what the documents show, settle anything about responsibility, or is that a category mistake I should avoid in my piece? Third, assess honestly whether overdetermination is doing any real explanatory work in the historiography, or whether it functions mainly as a rhetorical device for declining to assign blame. And if it is the latter, say which concept from the philosophy of causation — joint causation, preemptor chains, whatever you judge apt — historians ought to be reaching for instead, and what would change in the public debate if they did.
