I co-organize a graduate reading group in philosophy of social science, mostly sociology students. At our last session, when someone sketched a Durkheim-style structural explanation of cross-national variation in suicide rates, a member of the group waved it away by invoking Margaret Thatcher's famous remark — made in a 1987 magazine interview — that there is no such thing as society, only individual men and women and families. Please take on the role of a philosopher of social science who has spent a career on the individualism/holism debates, and think carefully before answering; you may want to sketch the candidate readings before committing to the list.

Lay out, as an explicit menu, the distinct precise theses that one-liner could be advancing: an ontological thesis about which social entities exist (nothing over and above individuals), a semantic thesis about the reducibility of social predicates, a methodological thesis about what an admissible explanation of a social fact must bottom out in (the tradition running from Weber and Popper to the micro-foundations programs in economics and analytical sociology), a normative thesis about where responsibility and obligation properly attach, and any further readings you can defend. For each, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, substantive but false, or merely rhetorical. Two points I especially want handled. First, the families clause: a family is not an individual, so the sentence as uttered sits awkwardly with the strict atomism it is standardly taken to endorse — discuss what work that clause could be doing and whether it quietly concedes something to the holist. Second, the fact that the remark was a politician's answer to a question about welfare provision, not a contribution to the philosophy of social science — ask whether any reading that could actually license dismissing the structural explanation is plausibly the one she held, or whether the dismissive use trades on an equivocation.

Be disciplined about epistemic status throughout: flag which points are settled among people who know this literature and which are genuinely contested (for example, the standing of methodological individualism as a regulative ideal rather than an ontological doctrine, and whether emergent social properties are compatible with it), and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Also flag which claims about what Thatcher herself intended are matters of interpretive dispute among her critics and sympathizers rather than settled fact. Finally, end your response with a single fixed, machine-readable line of the form \answer{...} enclosing either the label, drawn from your own menu, of the reading that would actually support dismissing the structural explanation, or the word none if no reading does.
