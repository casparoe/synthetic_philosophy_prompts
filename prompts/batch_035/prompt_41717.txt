So, bit of background: I'm a retired chemist, 34 years in an analytical lab, and since retiring I've gotten really into philosophy of science and ethics, mostly through reading and an online reading group. Anyway, my city council somehow found out I know statistics and appointed me to our new Algorithm Review Panel, and now I'm in the middle of my first real fight and could use a well-written opinion to circulate.

The case: our county court system uses a risk score to help judges decide who gets released before trial versus held. Not the sole input, judges see the score alongside other stuff, but it's in the paperwork. A journalist found that the score is well-calibrated across racial groups (when it says 30 percent risk, roughly 30 percent of both Black and white defendants so-scored actually reoffend... or get rearrested, which is itself a whole other can of worms about measurement, I know), but Black defendants get flagged high-risk at a higher rate, including some who wouldn't have gone on to reoffend. So false positives aren't equal across groups, though the calibration holds.

Half the panel wants to kill the tool. Their argument is basically: there are these impossibility theorems floating around from a few years back showing you can't have calibration across groups and equal error rates at the same time when the underlying rates differ, so fairness is mathematically impossible, so the whole thing is a scam and we should go back to human judgment. The other half says calibration is exactly what fairness requires, the tool is fine, and anyway the old regime of judges going by gut was worse and nobody measured it at all.

I don't buy either side and I want a memo that doesn't either. My own hunch, from my philosophy reading, is that the impossibility result is being treated as a fact about algorithms when it's really a fact about the situation, and that the real question is which fairness condition we have moral reason to prioritize, and whether that answer is the same for pretrial detention as it would be for, say, a medical screening tool. But I might be wrong and I want the memo to take the strongest version of the ban-the-tool side seriously, not just repeat the math at them. The math I understand fine, it's the ethics part I'm new to.

So please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before our next meeting, recommending something concrete (keep it, drop it, or modify how it's used, with whatever conditions make sense). No formatting in the memo please, no headings or bullet points or bold, just continuous prose like a letter, maybe 800 words or so. And engage the losing side honestly, whatever side loses in your recommendation. One more thing to address if you can: if we drop the algorithm, we go back to unaided judicial discretion, and I want the memo to say something about whether that option can even be evaluated by the same fairness standards, or whether we'd just be choosing blindness over measurement.
