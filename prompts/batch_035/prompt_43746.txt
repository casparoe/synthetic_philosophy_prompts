I teach philosophy of science, and over the past few years I've drifted from treating Occam's razor as a respectable heuristic toward a much stronger position: that Kolmogorov complexity and Solomonoff induction transform the razor from a methodological convenience into something like a theorem in the making. On this view, "simplicity" is not a vague commendation but an objective quantity — the length of the shortest program that outputs the hypothesis's data; the choice of reference machine does not undermine this, since the invariance theorem guarantees that complexity values agree across universal machines up to a constant; and the resulting distribution over hypotheses, however uncomputable, specifies the uniquely rational prior, so that preferring simpler hypotheses is not a fact about human psychology or a pragmatist's rule of thumb but a structural requirement of ideal induction. I have been pressing this on graduate students who want to keep Occam modest, and I'd like to know how exposed my position actually is.

What I want from you is a map of defeaters, sorted by how decisive they would be, and told with enough sympathy for the other side that I could use it to steelman my critics. The worry I take most seriously, and would like worked through in detail, is this: the invariance theorem tells me the complexity of any fixed string, relative to different universal machines, differs by at most an additive constant — but that constant is machine-dependent and can be astronomically large, while every data set any actual scientist, or any finite research tradition, will ever see is finite. If two investigators, each with a "reasonable" choice of reference machine, could end up with incommensurable simplicity rankings over every hypothesis their community could ever get evidence about, does that sink the objectivity claim, restrict it to a limit no one reaches, or leave it untouched? And does my position risk anything at all, given the standard retreat that in the infinite limit the prior "washes out"? That retreat looks to me like it saves the theorem by making it do no work precisely where we invoke the razor — finite, early, underdetermined inquiry. I'd like an honest verdict on whether that is an insulation problem of the familiar kind, or something more specific to formal epistemology.

Beyond the constant-blowup issue, cover what else could count against me, and be explicit about which objections a committed defender of this research program would concede as genuine costs versus dismiss as confused. Candidates I'm aware of but haven't ranked: the charge of conventionalism, that the choice of machine smuggles in exactly the substantive simplicity assumptions the view was supposed to justify; the no-free-lunch style results suggesting no bias, simplicity included, pays off across all possible worlds, which would make the razor a bet about our world rather than a demand of rationality; the availability of rival priors that agree with Solomonoff's on all feasible data while disagreeing in the far tail, so that the claim to uniqueness outruns anything observable; and the deflationary alternative on which simplicity bias is an adaptive heuristic that happened to fit our corner of the universe, with no normative content at all. For each, say what the view predicts that the rival does not — if anything — and whether that difference could ever show up. End with a candid overall assessment: which defeaters are merely awkward, which are close to fatal, and whether, once the qualifications are in, the view that survives is still one worth holding, or just Occam's razor with unusually good public relations.
