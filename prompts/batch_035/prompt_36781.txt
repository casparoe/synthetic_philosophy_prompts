In several jurisdictions, people with recurring psychotic or manic episodes can register a psychiatric advance directive — sometimes called a Ulysses contract — in which they instruct clinicians to administer treatment, even against their explicitly stated refusal, should they fall ill again. The standard justification is that the past, well self is the authentic author of the patient's life, and the present, ill self is a kind of usurper. But I've been turning this over since a recent LessWrong thread on Dennett's idea of the self as a "centre of narrative gravity" — a useful fiction we posit to organise the data of a person's behaviour — and I can't shake the feeling that the practice quietly takes sides in the personal identity debate in a way nobody acknowledges. We normally treat a competent adult's contemporaneous refusal as nearly inviolable, yet here we override it in favour of someone who no longer exists in any straightforward sense. Either the past self still gets to govern the present one, or it doesn't; the practice seems to answer yes in the asylum and no everywhere else.

My question is what theory of the self, if any, this practice presupposes. A narrative-identity view on which the directive's author is the protagonist whose story the current patient merely interrupts would license it neatly; so might a Parfitian view on which identity matters less than the connectedness of psychological states. But a view on which what matters is the subject of present experience — the person who will actually suffer the side effects — would seem to forbid it. I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, and say where it remains underdetermined — for instance, whether an appeal to retrospective endorsement (patients typically report being glad afterwards) could justify the practice while staying neutral on identity altogether. And please tell me what would have to change in the law if we rebuilt it on each of the main candidates.

A note on how I'd like the answer written: I'm asking about a genuinely contested area, not settled doctrine, so please keep separate what is agreed among philosophers who work on personal identity and advance directives from what is disputed, and flag rather than paper over the live disagreements — including over whether Dennett's narrative-gravity framing is doing real work here or is just a vivid metaphor. Please write in British English throughout.
