My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about the problem of old evidence, and we ended not by resolving it but by realizing we disagree about what would even count as resolving it. To set the stage briefly, since it will matter for the spec I want: if I already know E with certainty, then E has probability 1 for me, and so does E given any hypothesis H I haven't ruled out — which means conditioning on E can't move my credence in H at all. Yet the canonical case feels like the opposite: astronomers knew about the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion for decades before general relativity came along, and when Einstein showed that the theory entailed precisely that discrepancy, everyone took the old anomaly to strongly confirm it. My friend's line is that the Bayesian diagnosis is simply correct and the intuition is retrospective psychology — the perihelion data confirmed nothing in 1915; people just had a warm feeling. I replied that by the time any evidence is published it is old to everyone except the person who produced it, so on her view no theory in the history of science was ever confirmed by any evidence, which seems like a reductio. She countered that confirmation is maybe a relation to an idealized reasoner who hasn't yet made the deduction, and there we stalled, trading cases: the scientist who knows the fact for years and then learns it was a consequence of H all along; the one who designs H specifically to fit data she already has, where the "confirmation" smells illegitimate; and the common situation where the evidence is held not with certainty but amid measurement error, so the formal problem arguably never arises.

Before I go back to her, I want the specification rather than the scorecard. Please write the desiderata that any adequate solution to the problem of old evidence must meet: the cases it has to get right (with, for each, the reason it is a genuine requirement rather than a parochial intuition), the distinctions it must respect — mere consistency with E versus positive support from E, learning that H entails E versus learning E itself, fitting the already-known data versus independently re-deriving it — and the constraints from the surrounding epistemology it cannot contradict, such as that confirmation by evidence is the normal case in science rather than an embarrassing exception, that a solution must not let E be counted twice, and that it must not make ad hoc rescue immune to correction in principle. For each desideratum, flag whether you consider it non-negotiable or a prejudice of one camp; the deny-there's-a-problem faction and the counterfactual-relevance faction will obviously draw the line differently, and I want to know where you draw yours. The part I most want to see: state whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. I suspect the demand that a solution stay within ordinary conditionalization and the demand that it handle the entailment-discovery case cannot both be honored, but I'd like your considered view rather than my hunch. Only at the very end, in a sentence or two each, note how the standard lines — denying there is a problem; evaluating E's relevance via a counterfactual credence in which E is not yet known; expanding the algebra so that learning a logical or mathematical fact becomes a genuine input; bootstrap confirmation that uses the theory itself to generate the test — fare against the list. Keep the whole thing under 650 words.
