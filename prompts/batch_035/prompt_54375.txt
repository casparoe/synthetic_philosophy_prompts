Reading Plato's Laws and I hit something odd: the Athenian Stranger's whole scheme for the symposium in Book II. He wants the city to actually run supervised drinking parties where wine is deliberately used to loosen people up — partly so you can see who someone really is when their guard is down, and partly as cheap, low-stakes training in resisting pleasure (the way courage training exposes you to fears, moderation training should expose you to temptation). And yet the same dialogue is basically temperance all the way down everywhere else — unsupervised drinking gets banned, slaves and children are kept away from wine entirely, and drunkenness in the young is treated as corrupting. So my question: what theory of virtue does the symposium scheme presuppose? That virtue is a skill you drill through graded exposure to temptation rather than something you protect by abstinence? And is the scheme consistent with the rest of the Laws' ethics, or does Plato just not notice the tension between using pleasure as a training tool and his usual line that the unruly part of the soul should be kept down? Basically, reconstruct the implicit account of self-control behind the drinking-party proposal, and tell me whether the practice really needs that account or whether several different theories of virtue would license the same institution.
