I am a PhD student in archaeology, working on the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, and my thesis has a conceptual chapter on what "collapse" is supposed to mean. My supervisor, who thinks the whole notion should be retired in favour of something like "reorganisation", walked me through the following argument, and I now cannot see where it goes wrong.

(1) A societal collapse is, on any workable definition, a substantial and relatively abrupt loss of sociopolitical complexity — administrative reach, specialised production, monumental building, population — by a society that previously possessed those things. (Something close to Joseph Tainter's criterion: a society involuntarily sheds a significant portion of its complexity.)

(2) Societies acquire and maintain complexity only as long as the marginal return on further investment in complexity exceeds its marginal cost.

(3) In any bounded system — one with finite land, energy, and organisational headroom — marginal returns on complexity eventually fall below marginal costs, and continue to fall.

(4) Therefore every society must eventually collapse, and its collapse is structurally guaranteed rather than contingent on drought, invasion, misgovernment, or bad luck.

(5) But a predicate that applies to every member of its domain does no distinguishing work. If every society necessarily collapses, then "collapse" is not something some societies do and others avoid, and talk of "resilient" versus "fragile" societies is empty.

(6) Worse still, if (4) is right, then the polities we treat as long survivors — Egypt and China are the usual examples — must in fact have collapsed repeatedly already, their collapses papered over by our definitional habits concerning what counts as "the same" society.

(7) Yet the destruction of the Mycenaean palatial system and the broader Late Bronze Age crisis around 1200 BCE were clearly real and distinctive events, and (5) and (6) strike me as absurd.

(8) So the argument proves too much — but I cannot locate the fault.

Tell me which step I should reject, why that step looked fine to begin with, and what giving it up costs. I would much rather keep (1) and (7) than anything else, and I am even willing to sacrifice (2) if need be; but note that my thesis chapter depends on "collapse" remaining a non-vacuous concept, so if the cheapest escape route drains the concept of content, I need to know what I am paying. In your assessment, be sure to consider whether the trouble lies in the slide from "every society eventually loses complexity" to "every society eventually collapses", and whether the move from "necessarily eventually" in (4) to "does no distinguishing work" in (5) is a legitimate inference or a confusion. Please write in British English, and do not provide citations, references, or a reading list — I want the reasoning itself.
