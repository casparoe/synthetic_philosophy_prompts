I'm a machine learning engineer, comfortable with computation graphs, forward passes, autodiff, and the standard grab bag of tricks we use to control what information flows where — stop-gradients, detached tensors, logging side-outputs that don't feed the loss, frozen encoders, distillation from a teacher whose internals the student never sees. I've gotten interested in epiphenomenalism, and the more I read the more it seems like the natural formalization of something I manipulate every day: a quantity that is computed as part of the forward pass but from which no gradients flow. Specifically, I want the classical position and the argument against it translated into my vocabulary. By the classical position I mean Huxley's idea that consciousness is a product of physical processes in the brain but exerts no causal influence back on them, and by the argument I mean what gets called the paradox of phenomenal judgment — roughly, that if experiences are causally inert, then when I sincerely say "this pain is awful," the pain itself plays no role in producing that utterance; the physical substrate produces both the pain and the report about it, and the report is in an important sense never about the experience it seems to be about.

So here is the translation task, in three parts. First, restate epiphenomenalism in my terms as precisely as you can: what would it mean, concretely, for phenomenal states to be like detached tensors — values present in the computation, readable by an external logger, but never influencing anything downstream that affects behavior? Use whatever analogue seems most faithful: stop-gradients, side-channel logging, observability dashboards, whatever fits. Second, translate the paradox itself. The nearest thing I can come up with is a training run where a metric is logged but never included in the loss, so the model's "beliefs" about that metric are shaped entirely by correlated signals that did flow gradients — but I suspect this analogy quietly changes the subject, and I'd like to know where. Third, the residue report: which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto this vocabulary, which parts have to be distorted to fit, and which have no counterpart at all? For instance, my vocabulary has no trouble describing systems whose reports about X are caused by correlates of X rather than X itself — is the philosophers' worry just that, or is there something about the first-person case that my framing can't express? And when you get to the responses philosophers have offered — interactionist denials, parallelism, the idea that the physical and phenomenal are two aspects of one underlying thing — translate those too, and say which of them can even be stated in my vocabulary.

I'd like you to be careful about a division I can't police myself. Please separate what is genuinely settled — what the epiphenomenalist position actually commits to, what the paradox is actually claiming, which moves are standard in the literature — from what is actively contested among philosophers who work on this. In particular, don't present one resolution of the paradox (including "the paradox refutes epiphenomenalism" or "the stop-gradient analogy dissolves it") as if it were the consensus view; where competent people disagree, tell me that they disagree and roughly along what lines. If some part of my framing smuggles in an assumption — e.g., that causation in brains is like gradient flow, or that "computed but inert" is a coherent category to apply to a natural system rather than an artifact of how we build software — flag that explicitly rather than quietly translating around it. The value of this exercise for me is as much in what the translation fails to carry as in what it carries, so please be unsparing about the failures.
