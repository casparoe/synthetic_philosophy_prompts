Hi, so, I'm a novelist and I've gotten roped into writing an essay for a small literary magazine about weakness of will -- my current book is about a man who honestly believes every winter that he'll leave his job in the spring, and every morning he re-enrols himself in staying. Below are my notes for the essay. Can you tell me whether the philosophical core actually holds up, and where I'm mangling the philosophy? That's what I care about most, not prose.

The idea: philosophers since the Greeks have treated akrasia as a puzzle -- how can you knowingly do what you judge worse? But novelists never found it puzzling, because from the inside the answer is obvious: the you that judges and the you that acts are barely the same person. I want to argue that hyperbolic discounting vindicates the novelist's view. We discount the near future so steeply that our preferences literally flip depending on how close the reward is, so akrasia isn't acting against your own judgment at all -- it's two successive selves with opposite rankings, and the weak self always gets the last word because their the one holding the pen at the moment of choice.

The catch I want to explore: this seems to dissolve the moral sting. If the morning self and the evening self are just two voters, we should stop blaming the drinker at 6pm for betraying his 8am resolution -- no more than we'd blame one citizen for outvoting another. And I want to say that's wrong. The whole point of making a resolution is that the morning self gets to legislate for the evening self, and hyperbolic discounting doesn't remove the right to legislate, it only explains why legislation keeps failing. So akrasia stays a genuine failure, just a predictable one.

So my questions: does the successive-selves framing really follow from hyperbolic discounting, or am I borrowing a curve from behavioural economics to license a metaphor? Is there a standard name for the view I'm resisting, the one where akrasia stops being culpable once you see the selves as distinct? And is my legislation picture coherent, or does it just push the problem back -- who votes for the legislator?
