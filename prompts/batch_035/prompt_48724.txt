I teach an upper-level seminar on formal epistemology and am writing the take-home final. Please draft one essay question on Dutch book arguments, suitable for an answer of roughly 1500 words, together with a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme. The question must not be answerable by reciting the textbook result that violating the probability axioms leaves one vulnerable to a sure-loss betting portfolio. Instead, it should force the student to confront what exactly that vulnerability establishes: whether it shows that incoherent credences are irrational, or only that an agent meeting certain auxiliary assumptions — linear utility in the stake currency, willingness to accept every bet offered, exposure to a bookie who knows the agent's credences — can be exploited. The question should also require the student to distinguish the synchronic case from the diachromatic argument for conditionalization, where the bookie's strategy may depend on knowing the agent's updating rule in advance, and to assess whether that difference matters to the argument's force. Finally, it should ask whether the argument can be repaired — for instance, by treating the guaranteed loss as a symptom of inconsistency among the agent's evaluations rather than as a harm worth avoiding in its own right — or whether, once the auxiliary assumptions are made explicit, the argument establishes something weaker than the normativity of the probability axioms. The model answer should not trade on attributing particular moves to particular authors, since I want to test reasoning rather than recall of the literature. Please write everything, including the question, model answer, and marking scheme, as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; the marking scheme should be the nearest thing expressible in prose, naming the specific moves an A answer must contain, the specific errors that should cost marks, and the point at which a merely competent B answer falls short.
