A friend in my bioethics reading group has an argument she built herself, and it has been nagging at me since, because every step sounds acceptable and I cannot accept where it lands. It is meant to show that the difference between killing and letting die is not intrinsically moral at all. Here it is, as she presents it:

(1) If the distinction between doing and allowing has intrinsic moral significance — if killing someone is worse than letting him die in and of itself, rather than because of intentions, side effects, or social conventions — then there must exist a pair of cases that differ in nothing except that one involves doing and the other allowing. Otherwise the extra badness of doing would have nothing to hang on.

(2) Here is a candidate pair. In case A, a patient will die within minutes unless his ventilator keeps running; the machine is running. A nurse who wants him dead walks over and switches it off, and he dies. In case B, everything is the same — the same patient, the same machine, the same nurse, with the same desire, the same beliefs, and the same knowledge — except that the machine's power fails on its own; the nurse, wanting him dead, simply does nothing, and he dies.

(3) The nurse's intention, desires, and beliefs, and the outcome, are identical across the cases. The only remaining difference is that in A the death flows through a movement of her body, while in B it does not.

(4) A difference in bodily movement — muscle contractions present in one case, absent in the other — cannot be what separates murder from mere failure to rescue. Morality is not keyed to physiology.

(5) Since the cases differ in nothing else, and one is a doing while the other is an allowing, the distinction itself carries no intrinsic moral weight.

(6) Whatever moral weight the distinction appears to have must therefore come from elsewhere: conventions about medical roles, expectations the patient and his family had formed, legal fictions about who "caused" what.

My problem is that the argument seems to prove too much, in a direction I did not expect. Consider a different contrast, one I am confident is real: whether I am the only person able to save a drowning swimmer, or one of fifty equally positioned people on the pier, makes a real difference to how much blame I deserve for staying put. But the only thing that differs between those situations is facts about other people — their existence, their positions — which are just as external to my body as the machine's power supply is external to the nurse. So facts outside someone's body can apparently ground differences in responsibility. If that is right, then something like (3) or (4) has quietly narrowed what counts as a real difference between the nurse's two cases: maybe what separates them is not the motion of her muscles but something else that is not in either her body or the list in (3) — though I cannot say what without smuggling in exactly what (1) demanded, a bare difference.

I can see two escape routes and dislike both. One is to deny that the pair in (2) really differs in nothing but doing and allowing, and to name the hidden difference; but the pair was engineered to be as bare as possible, so this needs to be more than a hand-wave. The other is to deny (1) and allow that a distinction can matter morally even though its significance is not inspectable in any single pair of cases; but that seems to hand a blanket exemption to anyone who claims a moral distinction without ever having to exhibit it. If it matters to the analysis, I would most like to keep (4) — I agree morality is not keyed to physiology — and the reality of the blame difference between one rescuer and fifty.

Please tell me which step, or which pair of steps, I should reject; what rejecting each candidate costs; and whether the rescue example shows that the argument fails outright or only that my friend has misdescribed what the cases have in common.
