I am a novelist finishing a book that keeps threatening to turn itself into a retelling of the binding of Isaac. The central character is a father who becomes convinced he has been called to do something that will cost his family everything; whether the call is real, and what it would mean for it to be real, is the question the novel refuses to answer outright. Around him I have drafted a cast of characters who, I have come to realize, each occupy a different position on the question of whether any relation to God, duty, or inwardness can suspend the ethical — the universal, shareable, public order of obligations we owe to one another. I want to be sure my cast actually populates the logical space rather than merely echoing Kierkegaard's dramatis personae back at me.

To that end, I would like you to build me a sorting instrument — a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz of eight to ten questions — that I can use both to place my own characters and to include, lightly disguised, in the readers' guide as a "which of my characters are you?" appendix. The requirements are as follows.

First, build the space before naming its occupants. The splitting questions, phrased so a reader can answer them without knowing any labels, should be things like: whether the ethical order has intrinsic authority at all; whether anything can in principle overrule it; if so, whether the overruling must arrive as a publicly verifiable command or can rest on a privately felt call; whether the agent expects the finite to be given back — the double movement of faith — or renounces it forever; and whether the exception is communicable to others or essentially incommunicable. Then locate the positions in the space you have built, and make sure the following appear as leaves: the universalist who denies any private relation can outrank the ethical; the tragic hero, who sacrifices the beloved for the higher universal and is thereby vindicated within the ethical; the knight of infinite resignation, who gives up the finite and expects no return; the knight of faith proper, who makes the second movement and expects the impossible back; the plain divine-command theorist; the aesthete who recognizes no binding order in the first place; and the suspicious reading, on which any felt call that overrides ethics is pathology rather than faith.

Second, the cases. Each question should be a concrete scenario drawn from recognizable modern domestic life — a felt vocation that costs a family its savings, a solemn promise broken on the strength of an inner conviction, a secret kept from a spouse on grounds that cannot be explained — with stakes escalating toward, but never quite reaching, the sacrificial. No question may simply ask whether the reader believes in divine commands, and no question may use any of the position labels.

Third, honesty about the instrument's limits. Note which combinations of answers no named position occupies, and diagnose why — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the quiz cannot genuinely separate; I suspect the knight of faith and the deluded fanatic may be inseparable by design, and I would rather be told so than flattered.

Please close with a final line of the form \positions{N}, where N is the total number of positions your scoring key can actually distinguish, including any unnamed ones.
