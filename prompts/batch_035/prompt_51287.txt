I run a decision theory reading group, about 12 people, 90 minute sessions, and next time the topic is the counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail. Problem is I know exactly how it'll go if I just open the floor: everyone recites the standard positions they've read a hundred times, one guy says "just be updateless," someone else says that's obviously exploitable, and nobody changes their mind because nothing is at stake. So I want a game instead.

Design an in-class activity with real but tiny stakes (think a few dollars per person max, facilitator holds a slush fund) where they get hit with BOTH problems in the same session and feel the difference rather than argue about it. They shouldn't get told the names of the scenarios beforehand either.

The part I care about most: at some point they should have to commit to a single binding policy before they know which situations they'll actually face. My idea is something like a policy auction — teams draft policies like "pay the mugger, refuse the blackmailer" or "refuse everything" or whatever they come up with, and one policy gets locked in and then applied mechanically, no discussion, when the scenarios hit. I want them to notice that the policy that looks best ex ante collides with what they'd choose ex post if you let them think it over. So maybe run it twice, once with the auction and once where they decide freely in the moment, and let them compare payoffs across the two runs.

Mechanics you'll need to figure out: the facilitator plays Omega, so there has to be a prediction mechanic that's actually credible to the room (sealed envelopes signed by a neutral student? predictions committed before the session? something). And for the XOR blackmail round, the letter's payoff structure has to be set up so that the sending of the letter is genuinely linked to the player's payment the way the scenario requires — if the correlations are fake or hand-waved, the whole lesson collapses, and I'd rather you cut the round than run a broken version.

Then the debrief: map what actually happened onto updateful vs updateless reasoning, and use the two runs to show why the same "ignore what you've learned about the past" logic wins in one scenario and gets you exploited in the other — where's the difference, is it about logical linkage between your action and the letter ever being sent. Also be honest about the thing the game fundamentally can't reproduce: there's no real logical correlation between a student's choice and what's in a sealed envelope, it's all theater staged by me, so what exactly does the lesson rest on? I want that question in the debrief, not swept under the rug.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — someone trying to renegotiate after the policy is locked in, stakes too small for anyone to care, the prediction mechanic not landing — and how to handle each. One facilitator, no assistants, materials I can print the night before.
