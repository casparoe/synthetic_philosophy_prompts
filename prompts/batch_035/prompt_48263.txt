Been having a running argument with my brother about whether, whenever you know something, you automatically know that you know it. Instead of arguing in the abstract I tried writing down my actual gut verdicts on cases, and now I want someone to tell me if there's a principle that captures them, or if I'm just being inconsistent.

- Ask me my own phone number: I know it, and obviously I know that I know it. Trivial case.
- A friend of mine is good at trivia but has been burned enough times confidently blurting wrong answers that he now hedges everything. Ask him the boiling point of lead and he'll say "I want to say 1750 something but don't quote me," and he'd only bet even money on things he actually gets right 99 times out of 100. My verdict: he knows the answer, but he doesn't know that he knows it.
- A student fills in 40 true/false questions with a coin flip and gets every one right. Obviously he doesn't know any of the answers, even though all 40 beliefs came out true.
- Those people who sort day-old chicks by sex at 98% accuracy and swear they can't explain how, they just look and it comes to them. I say they know, AND they know they know, even though they couldn't begin to justify it. Their confidence is calibrated by their track record, I guess, not by introspection.
- I used to think you always know whether you're in pain, it's like the one thing you can't be wrong about from the inside. But I've had migraines that fade in waves, and there are definately stretches where I honestly could not tell you whether the pain was fully gone or just very very faint. So now I'm not so sure.
- My grandmother with early dementia still knows tons of things — where everything in the kitchen is, who all of us are — but if you directly ask her "do you know who I am?" she gets anxious and says maybe not. Chat with her normally and the knowledge is all there. I want to say she knows who we are but cannot know that she knows.

So I clearly don't accept that knowing always comes with knowing that you know. Here's my problem though: my brother says fine, but notice all your anti-KK verdicts are about *other* people, or weird mitigated cases. When it's you, you can't name a single time you knew something and couldn't tell. His line is that whenever you know, you're at least in a position to settle whether you know, like running an inner check. And honestly that matches my own experience. Am I being partial to myself, or is there a real asymmetry there?

So: is there one principle that gets all my verdicts, including the asymmetry (or explains why the asymmetry is fake)? If the principle forces me to give one up, which one and why? And tell me what it predicts for the case I keep going back and forth on — someone is told (falsely) that a medication they just took causes mild confusion for an hour. They're actually completely fine. An hour later they answer every question correctly but keep second-guessing themselves. Do they know their own address, and can they know that they do?
