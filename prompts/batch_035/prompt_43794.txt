ok so, some context for this: I'm a journalist. I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and like a lot of us I also sit on our outlet's standards panel — a small committee that settles disputes about editorial formats, six people, meets whenever something comes up. I need help drafting the written opinion I'll circulate to the panel before our next meeting, and I'll admit upfront: I'm probaby the least qualified person in the room for this one.

The case. For roughly a decade we've run two science columnists who disagree about AI and research. One has spent ten years saying machine learning will be doing serious scientific work — real discoveries, not just data crunching — within a generation. The other says it'll stay a glorified lab assistant and a hype machine, and the real science stays human. They read each other, cite each other, quote the same papers back and forth. The disagreement never moves an inch. Our younger data editor thinks the stuckness is itself the story: he's proposed a joint feature where each columnist states a numerical probability for his own claim (like, actual numbers), they swap their complete evidence dossiers in advance, and then publish side by side with a final probability from each. His pitch: if two smart, honest people who share all their evidence still end up miles apart, readers get to see that the fight was never about the evidence — it's about what they walked in the door with. Their priors, or their temperaments, or whatever you want to call it.

I half-remember something from an econ elective I took forever ago — Aumann, I want to say 1976, something like that — the idea being that if two people start out with the same beliefs and each updates honestly on their own evidence, then once they know each other's final opinions (and know that each knows, and know that they know, and so on up the ladder), those final opinions have to match. Rational people can't agree to disagree. Under that picture, our columnists' stubborn split would mean either they never shared beliefs to begin with, or one of them isn't the honest updater we all assume.

The panel is split and I get both sides. One side says run it: it dramatizes something real about expertise, and the gap that survives the evidence exchange IS the news. The other side — honestly I think it might be the stronger side — says that theorem only applies under idealized conditions, that infinite ladder of knowing-about-knowing that no two human columnists ever satisfy, so a failure to converge proves exactly nothing, and we'd basically be staging a gotcha with math-flavored credibility. Plus there's a fairness question: are we setting up two colleagues to look like fools for the sake of a format?

Draft the panel's decision memo. Recommend one of: run as proposed, reject, or run with conditions — and if conditions, spell out what they'd actually be, including how we'd frame any non-convergence in the published piece (that's the part I most need nailed down). Give the reasoning, take the strongest point on the losing side seriously rather than waving it off, and don't just restate the theorem at me — I need to know what actually follows for this specific feature. About a page is right. Write it in American English, please. And have the memo end with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase giving the recommendation — that's the line our editor will read aloud at the meeting.
