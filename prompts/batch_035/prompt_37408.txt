Sixty years of epistemology have been organized around the Gettier problem, and by now the counterexample game has essentially stopped producing new positions: the justified-true-belief analysis is universally regarded as refuted, the fourth-condition repairs (no-false-lemmas, defeasibility clauses, causal and reliability conditions) were each killed by cases within a few years of being proposed, and Linda Zagzebski's near-formal recipe — for any analysis of knowledge as true belief plus some further condition X, one can construct a case where the believer gets to the truth through a justified X that is nonetheless lucky — seems to close the door in advance on any future repair. Meanwhile the field has scattered: knowledge-first theorists treat 'knows' as unanalyzable in terms of belief, contextualists and interest-relative theorists relativize attributions, and experimental philosophers report that ordinary people's intuitions about Gettier cases vary across cultures and may not even side with the philosophers. Yet everyone still teaches Gettier's 1963 paper as the central event, and no side takes the others' response as so much as a concession. My working theory is that the apparent disagreement about the fourth condition was never the real disagreement: the dispute is now about what a conceptual analysis was supposed to deliver in the first place, with one camp holding that there is a knowledge-like state whose nature can be unpacked into independently intelligiable components, another that 'knows' functions more like a term whose role is fixed by norms of assertion and action, and a third that the whole enterprise of testing analyses against intuitions about cases is methodologically bankrupt. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a genuine substantive question about the structure of knowledge still being fought over under a methodological veneer? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a new style of case that cuts across the camps, formal results about what analyses are possible, empirical findings about the concept, or an agreed reformulation of the question — or is this a dispute where no resolution should be expected, and if so, why has it nonetheless remained productive to teach?
