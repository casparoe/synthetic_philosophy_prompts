There's a weekly interpretability reading group I go to (mostly ML grad students, me tagging along as the philosophy person), and last session we were supposed to discuss the finding that a model trained only on sequences of legal Othello moves seems to develop an internal variable for the board state — people claim you can train a linear probe to read the board out of the activations, and even set an activation to flip one square and watch the model's move predictions change accordingly. Before we got ten minutes in, someone shut the whole thing down with "it's just next-token prediction, it doesn't have a representation of anything," everyone nodded, and we moved on to scheduling. It's been bugging me all week, because I'm fairly sure that one sentence is doing several distinct jobs and only some of them are any good.

Can you lay out what "it's just next-token prediction" could actually mean here, as a menu of seperate precise claims rather than one thing? Off the top of my head there seem to be at least these candidates: (a) the trivially true reading that the training objective was next-token prediction — fine, but it doesn't settle anything by itself, since evolution's only "objective" is reproduction and it still produced eyes; (b) a substantive empirical claim that inside the network there's just a pile of surface-level statistical associations, no compositional structure that would deserve the word "representation," which is at least the kind of thing probe and intervention results could threaten; (c) a deflationary point about the probes themselves — that a probe succeeding only shows you can train a classifier on top of the activations, and maybe the classifier is doing all the work, so this tells us about our own concepts being projectable onto the model rather than about the model; (d) a semantic claim that even if there's an internal variable tracking the board, "representation" requires some kind of aboutness or intentionality that a network can't have, making the whole dispute verbal; and (e) some normative gatekeeping about who's allowed to say "world model." I might be missing readings, or some of these might collapse into each other — you tell me.

For each distinct reading, I'd want: whether it's plausible, and — this is the part I actually care about — whether it supports the move my groupmate made, namely dismissing the probe evidence as showing nothing about the model. My hunch is that the dismissal only works via reading (b) or (c), i.e. via claims that are substantive and empirically exposed, and that people quietly slide between the safe reading (a) and the strong ones without noticing, so the slogan feels unassailable while doing no work. I'd like that checked rather than just vindicated, so push back if you think (d) really does make it all verbal or the probe worry is stronger than I'm giving it credit for.
