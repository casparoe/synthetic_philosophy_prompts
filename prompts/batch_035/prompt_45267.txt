I'm a PhD student in art history, and last week in our contemporary seminar someone dropped Du Bois's "all art is propaganda" — citing his 1926 Crisis essay "Criteria of Negro Art" — to shut down a painter's claim that her abstract canvases were just about color and light. The move was basically: since all art is propaganda anyway, apolitical painting doesn't exist, so calling your work non-political is either self-deception or bad faith. I know the essay comes out of the Harlem Renaissance fights, where Du Bois was pushing back against people who used propaganda as a dirty word and wanted art to just be beautiful, and he ends it refusing to apologize for his own writing being propaganda. But I can't figure out how much of the slogan actually survives being lifted out of that context and pointed at a living artist. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims the one-liner could be making — like, the descriptive/sociological one that all art functions to shape opinion whether the artist intends it or not, the normative one that artists (or Black artists in Du Bois's moment specifically) have a duty to use their work for the race, the semantic one where he's just reclaiming the word from its pejorative sense, maybe an institutional one about aesthetic standards being political through and through — and for each tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or just false, and crucially whether it gets my classmate his conclusion that the painter's self-description has to be confused. A bunch of these readings seem compatible with her work being non-propagandistic even if true. Lay it out as a menu I can bring to seminar next week.
