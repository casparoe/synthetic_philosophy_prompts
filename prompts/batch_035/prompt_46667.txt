I sit on our university library's policy committee, and because I hold the philosophy department's seat, the committee has asked me to write the opinion on a proposal we have now deferred twice. Our library, like many, runs an automated inventory system: the reshelving carts already log every volume that comes back from the tables and carrels before it returns to the stacks, and the turnstiles at the entrance already timestamp every entry and exit. The provost's office would like these existing streams joined and retained: by correlating what was found on the tables with when the building was occupied, the library could maintain a dashboard of what students and faculty actually read — not check out, read — for space planning and collection development, all without a name attached to a single record. The librarians on the committee are uneasy, citing the profession's long-standing commitment to the confidentiality of what readers seek; the dean's representative finds that sentimentality, since nothing done openly in a public reading room can be private, and nobody is harmed by a dashboard whose purpose is better seating and better weeding.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate before the March meeting. It should recommend a concrete outcome — approve, reject, or approve with enforceable conditions — but the reasoning must do more than pick a side. Our disagreement is really a disagreement about why reading privacy matters at all: whether privacy in a public space is even a coherent notion, whether the difference between what a passerby could have observed and what a durable, searchable, aggregable record preserves is a morally relevant difference, and whether a harm-based account of privacy can capture what the librarians are gesturing at, or whether something like intellectual freedom or a context-specific norm is doing the real work. State plainly which conception of privacy your recommendation rests on, treat the dean's position as the serious position it is rather than a strawman, and phrase any conditions concretely enough for the library systems staff to implement — retention limits, aggregation thresholds, who may query the data and under whose approval. Roughly 900 to 1,200 words, and it should read as a document the committee could actually vote on.
