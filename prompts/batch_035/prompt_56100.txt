so background on me: longtime lurker and occasional commenter in the rationalist sphere, been reading the old sequences stuff since back in the day, and one thing that got drilled into me was that beliefs have to pay rent AND stay consistent — you can't believe P and believe not-P, that's just having a corrupted epistemic state. i've also spend way too much time on calibration exercises and forecasting sites, so i tend to think in credences more than most people. which is probably why the preface paradox is my favorite thing the rationalist blogosphere basically never talks about: an author can be rational in believing every single claim in her book (each one checked, rechecked, sent to reviewers), and rational in believing that at least one of those claims is false (books that length always contain errors, and she knows it going in), and yet she cannot rationally believe the conjunction. it's basically Kyburg's lottery paradox wearing a tweed jacket, and the nice part is that nobody actually explodes into inconsistency the way classical logic says they must — the author just goes about her life.

what i want is a poem that enacts this rather than describing it. the body of the poem should be an itemized set of short declarative claims — numbered entries, or field-guide style, or a "things i know for sure" inventory — somewhere in the range of ten to fourteen of them. each one delivered in the flat voice of earned confidence: checked, source verified, no single one the speaker would retract under pressure, each the kind of thing that is, on its own, overwhelmingly likely to be true. then the final stanza, standing in for the preface, states in that same calm voice — with honest statistical grounds, the way an author of a technical book is actually entitled to it — that at least one of the claims above is false. it does not say which. and the list itself should be built so that a reader who goes back hunting finds two or three plausible candidates and no way whatsoever to settle it. that going-back-and-rereading is the poem doing its actual work, so treat it as a real constraint on which claims you pick, not an afterthought.

a few don'ts: don't have the speaker wink at the reader or subtly tip which line is the false one, and don't let the ending collapse into either "nothing can be known" or "eh, it's probably fine" — the paradox is supposed to survive the last line intact. if it helps, under the hood this is Makinson's preface paradox (the 1965 one) and it's what happens when the Lockean thesis — belief just is credence above some threshold — has a fistfight with closure under conjunction. but the poem shouldn't lecture or name any of that. the test is: someone who knows the paradox should recognize it purely from the shape of the thing, and someone who doesn't should just get a slightly unsettling inventory poem that keeps bugging them for the rest of the day.
