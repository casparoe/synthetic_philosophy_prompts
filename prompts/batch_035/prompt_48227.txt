I'm preparing material for a population-ethics reading group, and I've found our best discussions come from predicting a specific person's verdicts rather than debating in the abstract. The person is my friend from the seminar — call her Maya — who is consistent and reflective, not squeamish or confused, and whose track record on procreation cases is as follows. She is adamant that conceiving a child when genetic testing shows its life would be relentlessly miserable is not merely unwise but wrong. By contrast, she is equally firm that a couple who could have a child who would predictably be happy, but who choose a childfree life of moderate enjoyments instead, does nothing wrong — no one is harmed — and she bristles at any suggestion that they are being selfish. In one of our stranger hypotheticals, forced to choose between spending a sum of money on slightly relieving an existing friend's chronic pain and spending the same sum on a procedure that would bring into existence a new person with an extraordinarily blissful life, she said relieving the pain is better, obviously, and was unmoved when we pointed out that the new life would contain vastly more happiness than the pain-relief would add. And on a policy case: a government program that would slightly lower the wellbeing of future generations, all of whom would still have clearly happy lives, in exchange for modest gains to people alive now, she called it permissible, on the grounds that every future life remains well worth living, while insisting it would become wrong the moment any future life would fall even slightly below that threshold.

Please act as an expert in population ethics and think carefully about this before answering. Start with some brainstorming: lay out the candidate principles that fit Maya's track record. I can see two myself — a strict asymmetry, on which creating a happy life adds no value to the world while creating a miserable one adds disvalue, and a weaker asymmetry, on which creating a happy life is a genuine benefit, just one that cannot ground obligations and perhaps counts for less than benefits to existing people — but I suspect there are at least one or two others (I have a vague memory of threshold-style views in this literature), and I'd like you to say which of her four verdicts constrain each candidate and how. Then comes the case we plan to put to her at the next session. A fertility clinic is about to destroy a viable embryo; implanting it with a willing, well-resourced surrogate would cost no existing person anything, and the resulting person's life would be predictably wonderful. We will ask her whether the world in which the implantation happens is better than the world in which it doesn't.

Predict her verdict on that case, say which of the candidate principles you think she is actually running and why you prefer it over its rivals, and — since I'm less interested in the answer than in the diagnosis — tell me which further case would best separate the principles from one another. I have in mind something like a life that is happy but only barely, but design whatever case you think is decisive, and state what each principle predicts about it, so the group can see in advance where the explanations part ways.
