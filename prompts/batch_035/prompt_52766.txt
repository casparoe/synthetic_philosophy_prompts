So I'm a backend engineer (distributed systems mostly, zero formal philosophy training — I just went down a rabbit hole reading the Churchlands a while back and it stuck with me) and I've somehow let myself get talked into giving a 20-minute talk at our company's internal "big ideas" lunch series next month. The thesis I'm defending: belief/desire talk was a folk theory that worked because we were predicting systems whose internals we couldn't inspect, and it's headed the way phlogiston went for chemistry. Now that we can start cracking systems open — mechanistic interpretability for models today, hopefully decent brain-level explanations someday — the categories that survive will be whatever the internals actually implement, and there's no guarantee those come out belief-shaped. Concretely, for this audience: when my coworkers write "the model believes it's being tested" in an incident report, they're doing the same thing as an 18th century doctor blaming an imbalance of humours — it's predictive shorthand, and it won't survive contact with the actual mechanism.

The audience: about 40 people, mostly ML engineers. A big chunk of the interpretability team, including one guy who is fully convinced he's found a "user is in France" feature and will absolutely bring it up. Some evals folks whose whole job is writing statements like "the model believes X" and scoring models against them. Basically nobody with actual philosophy training. They're friendly but a bit smug — the general vibe is that philosophy is mostly vibes and their field already solved this with "it's just a world model, obviously."
