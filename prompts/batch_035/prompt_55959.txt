In my philosophy elective we do anonymous peer grading and I swear I got the hardest one to mark. Our teacher's question was: "A pile of 10,000 grains of sand is clearly a heap. And removing a single grain can never turn a heap into a non-heap. (a) Show how these two claims together generate a paradox. (b) Epistemicism says there is a sharp cutoff between heaps and non-heaps that we could never discover, while the second view we covered (supervaluationism) says that for borderline piles, 'this is a heap' is neither true nor false. Explain what each reply has to give up. (c) Say which of the two costs is smaller and defend your choice."

Here's the answer I have to grade:

"(a) If one grain can never make the difference, then by repeating the removal 9,999 times you get that a single grain is a heap, because each step preserves heapness. But one grain is obviously not a heap. So two claims that each seemed undeniable can't both be true — that's the paradox.

(b) Epistemicism, which is Crispin Wright's view, keeps everything classical: there is a last heap in the sequence, we just can't ever know which number it is. The cost is a fact about the cutoff that no possible evidence could reveal, which some people find spooky. The other view says that at some point the pile becomes borderline and for borderline piles the sentence 'this is a heap' has no truth value. This is basically supervaluationism: you consider all the ways of making the term precise, and since 'this is a heap' comes out false on some of them, the sentence is just false at that point. The cost is giving up bivalence, so you can't rely on excluded middle anymore — 'either this is a heap or it isn't' is no longer guaranteed true.

(c) The second cost is smaller. Since the meaning of 'heap' is fixed by how the community uses the word, and the community never settled where the line is, there is no fact of the matter about a sharp cutoff, which basically refutes epistemicism. So I'd pay the price in classical logic."
