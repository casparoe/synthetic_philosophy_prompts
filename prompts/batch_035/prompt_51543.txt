Hi! A bit about me first, so you know where I'm coming from: I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist, spent close to forty years measuring what electrons do in very cold crystals, and only in the last few years have I started reading philosophy of science seriously (my daughter says better late then never). I'd like you to take the role of a professional philosopher of science who knows the confirmation literature cold. Please think carefully before answering, and don't give me a bibliography or reading list -- I have more of those than I can read already.

Here's the thing that bugs me, and I want your help untangling it. Since the 1980s there's been a whole community of theoretical physicists working on string theory, and lately on the idea that we live in a multiverse -- a vast landscape of possible universes each with its own constants. Their field is thriving in every way except the one I was raised on: journals, conferences, generations of PhD students, permanent jobs whose holders are effectively "string theorists." And yet the theory has never been directly tested and, by most accounts, may never be. What's interesting is what they say when you press them. They don't just shrug and say it's beautiful math. They give what sound like epistemic reasons. They say there is no alternative -- that it's the only consistent way anyone has found to combine quantum mechanics and gravity. They say it keeps passing consistency checks that nobody imposed on it beforehand, and that this kind of unexpected coherence means something. And when the cosmological constant turned out to be absurdly tiny in 1998, they pointed back to a prediction made in the late eighties that had reasoned from the bare requirement that galaxies exist at all -- and they treat that as vindication of the whole program, multiverse included.

So here's my question. What theory of evidence does this whole practice presuppose? It looks like the community has quietly moved from confirming theories with experiments to raising confidence in a theory because of facts about the theory itself -- how hard it was to construct, how well it hangs together, how few rivals there are. Can that be made coherent, or is it rationalization dressed up as epistemology? Is it Bayesianism in disguise (maybe the lack of alternatives is telling us something about priors?), or would a strict falsificationist say the whole thing has left science entirely? And here's the part that really puzzles me, please spend extra time on it: the same community is scathing when somebody outside physics points to the fine-tuning of the constants as evidence of design. They say you can't do inference without independent checks on the explanation. But "it's the only game in town" plus "it fits what we observe" sounds suspiciously like the same move in different clothes. Is there a principled line between the physicist's version and the theologian's, or does the line really just run through who is making the argument and their track record?

Practical requests: begin with some brainstorming that lays out the candidate implicit epistemologies -- falsificationism, inference to the best explanation, Bayesian confirmation, maybe something sociological like trust in a community's past successes -- and then tell me which one the practice actually needs, which ones it's merely compatible with, and where it's underdetermined enough that several would license it equally well. Then say what would have to change if we insisted on strict experimental confirmation: would the journals empty out, or would the work just get redescribed as mathematics and carry on regardless? I care more about careful reasoning than confident tone, and its fine to end on "we don't know yet" where that's the honest answer.
