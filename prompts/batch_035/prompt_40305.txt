The exam question I wrote was:

"Consider Galileo's thought experiment in which a heavy cannonball and a light musket ball, tied together with a cord, are dropped. On the Aristotelian account, the heavy body should fall faster; but then the tied system, being heavier than the cannonball alone, should fall faster still, while at the same time the light ball should act like a brake and slow the cannonball down — an unstable situation. James Robert Brown holds that a special class of thought experiments like this one can deliver something no mere argument can: something like a perception of abstract laws of nature, analogous (on his view) to how we grasp mathematical truths. John Norton instead holds that every thought experiment is just an argument whose premises ultimately derive from experience, so nothing genuinely new is learned. Question: state one thing each account explains well about this particular case, one thing it struggles with, and say which you find more plausible here and why."

Here's the student's answer, copied as-is:

"The Brown view explains the phenomenological feel of the thought experiment: it seems obvious, once you run it in your head, that all bodies fall at the same rate, and on Brown's story that's because we're perceiving a law of nature directly, the same way we see a relation in geometry. What Norton handles better is the history: Galileo's conclusion was later confirmed by real experiments (astronauts dropping a hammer and a feather, etc.), and on Norton's picture this is no surprise, since the premises of the thought experiment were distilled from experience in the first place. A problem for Brown: if we can perceive laws of nature directly, it's odd that people who run the same thought experiment sometimes disagree or get it wrong, which shouldn't happen with genuine perception, and the appeal to misperception feels ad hoc. A problem for Norton: since the thought experiment is a valid argument, it can never fail — so Norton owes us an account of why some thought experiments mislead us, and his answer, that the misleading ones are bad arguments, seems circular. Overall I find Norton more plausible for this case, because his picture keeps everything within ordinary physics and doesn't require a mysterious extra faculty."
