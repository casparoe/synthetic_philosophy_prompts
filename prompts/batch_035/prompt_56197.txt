I'm a philosophy graduate student, and a version of the nonidentity argument from my population ethics seminar has been eating at me for weeks, because it keeps extending to conclusions nobody in the room will accept, and I can't find the load-bearing step. I'd like a careful diagnosis from someone who thinks about these issues professionally. Before you commit to an answer, please brainstorm the candidate failure points — valid but unsound, or invalid outright — and only then pick one.

Here is the argument as my study group has it, with the extension that worries me:

1. For an act to wrong someone, there must be a person who is worse off than she would otherwise have been. No victim, no wrong. (I'm committed to this: I don't want wrongs floating free of anyone's interests.)
2. A wealthy society adopts a reckless depletion policy — resource exhaustion, degraded climate — knowing that people three centuries hence will live shorter, harder, poorer lives, though lives still worth living.
3. The policy shifts economic patterns, which shifts who meets whom and when children are conceived, so the people who exist under depletion are numerically different people from those who would have existed under conservation.
4. Those people are not worse off than they would otherwise have been — the relevant comparison class never exists — and their lives are worth living. So the policy wrongs no one.
5. The same reasoning applies to a couple who conceives while knowingly taking a drug that predictably causes the child to be born with a serious but non-lethal condition: the child is worse off than she would have been with different parents or no parents at all? No — worse off than she would otherwise have been, and there is no such alternative for her. No one is wronged.
6. Here's the extension that made me stop sleeping. A large historical atrocity — conquest, enslavement, dispossession — scattered and recombined populations so thoroughly that the descendant communities who now live with its material legacy would simply not exist had it not occurred. Those descendants, like the depletion-era people, owe their existence to the wrong. By the reasoning in (4) and (5), the atrocity wronged no one among them, and their claims to repair are claims that no person is positioned to make: the people alive today are not worse off than they would otherwise have been, because there is no otherwise for them.
7. But the conclusion in (6) is not just false but obscene, and even (5) strikes most people as badly wrong, and (4) is at minimum uncomfortable.
8. Therefore some premise or inference among (1)–(6) fails, and I need to know which one, and what it costs to give it up.

Two constraints on the diagnosis. First, if you tell me to reject (1) — to allow victimless wrongs, or wrongs done to people who are not worse off, or impersonal reasons — I want to know exactly what I'm signing up for: does anything constrain the resulting view, or can it be used to argue that our policies wrong the merely possible inhabitants of better futures? Second, my honest suspicion is that something slips inside (6) rather than earlier — something about the move from the atrocity wronging no descendant to there being no standing claims at all, maybe an equivocation between being wronged and being entitled to complain — but I also half suspect that diagnosis is wishful, because if the inference is valid, rejecting only (6) means holding that depletion wrongs no one while reparations claims fail, and I'm not sure I can live with that package either.

- The price (roughly 300 words): what giving up that premise costs in the cases I didn't mention — does your fix leave the depletion case, the teratogen case, and the historical-injustice case sorted the way I'd want, or does it force a trade I should know about?
- The alternatives, priced (roughly 250 words): the one or two other respectable exits, and what each makes me swallow.
