I'm a second-year master's student in history, taking a historiography seminar that has become a running battle over one phrase. Our methods instructor opened the semester by writing "how it actually was" on the board — the English rendering of Ranke's "wie es eigentlich gewesen" from the 1824 preface to his Histories of the Latin and Germanic Peoples — and used it to stand for everything defective about nineteenth-century positivism: the fantasy of a historian who just transcribes the past, purges every trace of perspective and value, and mistakes archival diligence for objectivity. A week later, a visiting professor of German history countered that the instructor was attacking a mistranslation, since "eigentlich" can carry the sense of "essentially" or "characteristically," and that Ranke, a Lutheran who thought God was legible in history, was making a nearly opposite claim — that the historian's task is to grasp the inner nature of epochs, not to assemble a value-neutral mirror. Meanwhile a fellow student insists the whole fight is a straw man, because no serious historian ever believed the naive reading, so the phrase does no work either way. And at the back of my mind is Hayden White, whose scepticism seems aimed at something stronger than the naive reading: that historical writing is inescapably emplotted, a literary artefact, so that any choice among the facts is already a narrative decision.

What I would like, before I write my seminar paper, is a disambiguation rather than another round of the fight. Please lay out the distinct precise claims "how it actually was" could be expressing — from the banal (a historian should prefer primary sources to rumor, and shouldn't invent) through the methodological (source criticism can neutralize bias, and competing accounts can converge on what happened) up to the metaphysical and epistemic extremes (a complete, perspective-free reconstruction of the past is available in principle; the meaning of the past is exhausted by establishing what occurred; narrative form adds nothing to historical truth) — and, crucially, say for each reading whether it is defensible, hopeless, or something no one actually held. Then tell me which of these readings the various combatants need for their arguments to go through: does my instructor's critique of positivism require Ranke to have held the strong reading, or does the "essentially" translation rescue him from it? Does the naive-reading straw man worry land against the middle readings, which seem to be what working historians still implicitly rely on? And does White's narrativism genuinely refute all the readings, or only the ones nobody holds, leaving the modest evidential ones untouched? A structured list I can work through reading by reading would suit me best.
