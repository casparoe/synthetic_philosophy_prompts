My roommate has taken to ending every discusion about my life choices with "don't throw good money after bad." Latest instance: I'm three years into a PhD I'm lukewarm on, and she says finishing it just to justify the time already spent would be throwing good money after bad, so I should cut my losses and quit. Something about that move bugs me and I want to figure out what exactly the proverb is claiming when people wield it like this.

Can you lay out the distinct precise things "don't throw good money after bad" could actually mean? I can see at least these possibly coming apart: (1) the textbook decision-theory point that sunk costs shouldn't enter the forward-looking calculus at all, so I should decide about my remaining years as if deciding fresh whether to start a PhD-tail-end from scratch; (2) something psychological — a warning that people who've already invested tend to over-invest to avoid admitting failure, so the proverb works as a bias-corrector rather than a decision rule; (3) a policy or self-binding point — maybe a blanket quit-when-behind rule is rational for someone who knows they can't trust their own case-by-case judgments about their pet projects; (4) some reading on which the proverb isn't about sunk costs in the first place — notice it says good money after *bad* money, which sounds like a warning against escalating losses specifically, not a claim that the past is irrelevant to what you should do next.

For each reading, tell me whether it's plausible, whether it's trivial, and crucially whether it actually gets my roommate to her conclusion. Because here's the thing — on the decision-theory reading I'm not sure she wins. If I'm 80% done, the standing work arguably functions like an asset, and finishing could be the right call on purely forward-looking grounds (I'd rather be a person with a PhD than a person three years into one), no sunk cost reasoning required. And I've heard that sunk costs can still be legitimate *evidence* — my past self's willingness to invest years might carry information about the project's value that I shouldn't just discard, and quitting can itself be motivated by not wanting to sit with a mistake, which cuts the other way. So sort the readings into platitudes vs substantive-but-defensible vs just false, tell me which one she's actually invoking when she uses the proverb to end an argument, and whether that reading supports telling me to quit. If the honest answer is that the proverb can't carry the weight she's putting on it, say so — and tell me what a better version of her advice would have to establish to actually land.
