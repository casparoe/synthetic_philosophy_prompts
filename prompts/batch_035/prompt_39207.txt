I spent thirty-seven years as an experimental geochemist, where the working rule was always to gather one more datum before committing, and in retirement I have taken up decision theory. I have come to hold, on the strength of Good's theorem on the value of information, that a rational agent can never expect to be made worse off by free, costless information bearing on a decision she faces: since the expected value of refining one's probabilities is nonnegative, a Bayesian should always accept a free observation. Tell me what would count against this. I am not interested in the textbook caveat that information can be expensive or slow, unless you can show that cost and delay are doing all the work and nothing is left over. The cases I half-worry about are these: experimental subjects who pay to avoid learning a medical diagnosis; the deliberate use of blinding in randomized trials, which treats ignorance as a methodological good; my grandson's claim that someone Ellsberg-averse about an ambiguous urn will refuse a free look at its composition; and a wrinkle of my own — a hazard-monitoring decision in which the free information only arrives if I keep a costly evacuation option open for another week, so that accepting the information means paying for it in a currency the theorem does not count. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward; say which of them a committed defender of the theorem would concede and which she would dismiss as a change of subject; and take a definite stand on whether the theorem genuinely vindicates the scientist's reflex to measure first, or is merely a truth guaranteed by premises that already assume it.
