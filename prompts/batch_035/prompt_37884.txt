I am the recently appointed patient-experience director at a mid-sized regional hospital, and in two weeks I present to the board's Quality and Safety Committee a proposal to overhaul our patient complaints and grievance process. I took this job after my own father spent a week on a geriatric ward last year where his attempts to report confusion and over-medication were attributed to his age and then, once he grew frustrated, to dementia — and where our family found that the formal grievance channel, supposedly built for exactly this, had no vocabulary for what had happened to him other than "communication issue, resolved." I have since read enough philosophy to frame our complaint system as a machine for producing epistemic injustice, in Miranda Fricker's sense: it systematically under-weights testimony from patients whose credibility is already deflated by accent, disability, psychiatric labels, or age, and it demands that patients articulate grievances in categories that don't include the things patients actually experience — the accumulating dismissals that patients now call medical gaslighting, or care that was technically consented to but not meaningfully chosen.

My proposal has four parts: trained lay advocates empowered to lodge complaints on a patient's behalf, a plain-language complaint taxonomy co-designed with former patients, a quarterly "epistemic audit" sampling dismissed complaints to see who gets filtered out and why, and a rule that credibility judgments about complainants must be documented and justified. My thesis, which I plan to state openly, is that a complaint process that only hears the patients who already have the concepts, the confidence, and the standing to complain is not a neutral instrument with some bias at the margins but a structural mechanism of testimonial and hermeneutical injustice.

The committee is a specific bunch. Two hospital administrators who will hear any mention of injustice as prelude to a lawsuit or a press cycle and want to know what any of this does to liability exposure and patient-satisfaction scores. The chief of medical staff, a defensive believer in physician autonomy, who will resent the documentation rule as a mechanism for second-guessing clinical judgment after the fact. A nurse manager who is broadly sympathetic but will worry that every new documentation burden lands on her already stretched staff. A bioethicist on our ethics committee, trained in the principlist tradition, who knows Fricker's work and will probe whether my remedies — which are structural, procedural, institutional — abandon Fricker's own preferred remedy in the testimonial case, the cultivated virtue of the hearer, and whether an audit and a documentation rule can really do the work a virtue is supposed to do. And a health-services researcher who suspects that "hermeneutical injustice" is an unfalsifiable construct and will ask, in her vocabulary, about operationalization, inter-rater reliability, and what measurable outcome would tell us the program failed.

Prepare me for this room. Draft my objections sheet: roughly eight objections I will actually face from this specific committee — not the objections I would face from a philosophy department — ordered by how much damage each could do to the proposal's survival, each phrased the way that particular committee member would put it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Distinguish what I should concede up front from what I must hold at all costs, and flag the one reply that, if it fails, the whole proposal fails with it. Finally, include the question I am quietly hoping nobody asks — I have one in mind, and I would like to know whether you identify the same one.
