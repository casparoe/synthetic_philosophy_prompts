I do trust & safety analytics for a mid-sized marketplace that sells hobby stuff -- miniatures paints, 3D printing filament, board game accessories -- and about a year ago we made the call to show average star ratings prominently in search results and on the product page. Ever since I've been semi-obsessed with the possibility that our ratings are partly self-fulfilling: a product gets a couple of early good reviews, everyone who buys it after that sees the score before they've formed their own opinion, and the later reviews are then partly just... echoes. I've been reading about information cascades and the part that really gets me is that this can happen even if every single reviewer is being perfectly rational -- once the accumulated signal from previous reviewers is strong enough, each new reviewer is right to lean on it over their own noisy experience, and past some point the chain basically stops transmitting new information, so two early reviews can end up setting the consensus for thousands of later buyers. Our head of product thinks I'm overthinking it and that "the wisdom of crowds sorts it out eventually."

Here's what I want: an actual procedure I can run on a product's review history to decide how much of its rating is genuine quality signal versus cascade copying. I review maybe 25-40 flagged products a week, so it has to be something I can do in an hour or so per product, not a research project. I have decent data: timestamps, verified purchase flags, star rating, review text, whether the review came before or after our redesign that made scores more prominent, and roughly how each user got to the product (search, recommendation, external link). I can write SQL and do simple stats but I'm not going to fit a Bayesian reviewer model, and I can't run controlled experiments without six weeks of sign-off. Give me a set of tests in the order I should run them, what each one can and can't distinguish -- I keep getting tangled up on the difference between an informational cascade and a reputational one, and also between a genuine cascade and plain old social proof where reviewers just parrot what other reviewers said because it's easy -- where the procedure should just say "can't tell from this data", and where it hands the call off to a human because it's a values question, not a measurement. And be honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on two products I'm actively arguing about with the head of product. One is a miniatures primer that took off after a big YouTuber reviewed it, and whose average rating has drifted steadily upward with each new wave of reviews, most of which read like "lives up to the hype" rather than describing the product. The other is a coffee grinder that the same YouTuber trashed, whose rating has been sliding ever since, with later reviewers explicitly citing earlier reviewers' complaints. He wants to feature the primer on the homepage and bury the grinder, and my suspicion is that both ratings are partly cascade artifacts and that our search ranking is just amplifying whatever that one video did. If the procedure ends up saying he's right and I'm wrong on either product, I want to hear that too -- and tell me what the procedure flatly cannot resolve, like whether we should even be showing aggregate scores up front given all this, or whether hiding the average from first-time viewers is the only real fix.
