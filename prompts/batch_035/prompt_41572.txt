My closest friend from nursing school now sits on the governing board of a regional health trust, and for years she has been handing down verdicts on questions of just pay and just distribution. She has never studied philosophy, but at every budget meeting she repeats her one principle: a fair arrangement is one you would agree to before knowing which place in it you would occupy. Next month she rules on a pay dispute, and I want to predict her verdict before she gives it. Her track record so far:

1. Trauma surgeons paid several times what orderlies are paid: justified, because at equal pay the trust would lose its surgeons and the sickest, poorest patients would be far worse off.
2. One of two equally qualified technicians given a large raise purely because his supervisor favors him: unjustified, and the right repair is to match their pay.
3. Permanent, tax-funded support for people born with severe disabilities: required as a matter of justice, not charity.
4. Career advantages traceable to inherited money — private schooling, purchased credentials, family connections opening a first job: unjustified, even where the advantaged person works hard once inside.
5. Hazard pay for night staff during a chronic shortage, even though it creates a lasting gap with the day shift: justified, because it is what actually keeps the night shift staffed, and care for the trust's worst-off patients is better with it than without it.
6. Large performance bonuses for senior managers who would have stayed regardless, and whose results were mostly a matter of lucky timing: unjustified, even though the bonuses left everyone else exactly as well off as before.

The new case concerns a cohort of nurses who, during a decade-old hiring freeze, paid their own way through an expensive retraining program with no guarantee of anything, then spent years taking the least desirable postings in the region. The board can fund a substantial permanent pay increase for them alone. It is not needed to retain them — they would stay in any case — and on every index the trust uses, the least advantaged patients and the lowest-paid staff end up exactly where they are now: not a cent better, not a cent worse. Every gain stops with the nurses and, through their spending, with the reasonably comfortable. Their desert is real: costly, freely chosen, untainted by favoritism or luck, and it rescued services. Does she rule the raise justified?

What I want is not just the prediction. First, set out the rules that fit her entire track record equally well — I can see at least three that agree on all six of her past verdicts yet disagree here — and say what each one predicts about the nurses and why. Then give me your best guess at her verdict, with your reasons for preferring that reading of her; her refrain about agreeing to arrangements before knowing one's place is evidence of one kind, though I cannot tell how much weight it deserves against what her actual rulings suggest. Finally, give me the further case I should put to her afterward — the one that would best separate whichever of the candidate rules are still tied after the nurses' case.
