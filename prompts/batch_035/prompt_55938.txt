In three weeks I present to a university working group on health behavior design: roughly a dozen behavioral scientists who build and test digital choice architecture (defaults in appointment-booking apps, prompt timing, ordering effects in screening questionnaires), two policy staff who helped run a national organ-donation default campaign, and one Kantian ethicist on loan from the philosophy department who thinks valid consent requires full information up front. My thesis is that the popular criterion of manipulation — influence that bypasses the target's capacities for critical reflection — is hopeless, because nearly all effective influence, including clear argumentation, works partly through routes the target doesn't audit; I propose instead a criterion of transparency-conditional effectiveness: influence is manipulative when its causal route would predictably fail if the target knew exactly how it worked, and legitimate when it survives that disclosure. On this criterion I make three claims the room will dislike. First, opt-out organ-donation defaults are not manipulative, because they survive full disclosure: people who are told about the default still tend to go along with it, and going along with a disclosed default is acquiescence, not exploitation of a bias. Second, some fully transparent influence is still manipulative — transparent flattery directed at someone who knows she is vain, and knows you are flattering her, still operates on her vanity rather than on her reasons, which suggests that whether the influence addresses the target's reasons does independent work that transparency alone cannot capture, and I am not sure how to fold that back into my criterion without making transparency merely decorative. Third, some concealed influence is benign, because concealment is constitutive of the practice — surprise parties, poker bluffs, the hidden confederate in some experiments — so the criterion needs an exclusion clause I have not worked out. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections in the order this particular group will actually raise them, phrased in each subgroup's own vocabulary (the behavioral scientists will come at me with asymmetric paternalism and the claim that System 2 ratifies the nudge after the fact; the policy people with the thought that since everything is influence, my criterion just relocates the line rather than drawing a cleaner one; the Kantian with consent and treating people as mere means), ranked by how much damage each would do, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft; tell me what I should concede up front, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
