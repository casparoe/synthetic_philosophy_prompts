You are a professional philosopher of biology with a strong sideline in general philosophy of science, and I'd like you to think hard before answering rather than reaching for the standard survey. Feel free to brainstorm candidate diagnoses first and only then commit to one; what I want at the end is a single, argued diagnosis, not an overview of positions.

Here is my situation. I'm a biochemist who has followed the "what is life?" definitional debate for years — the one that orbits around viruses, prions, dormant spores, minimal synthetic genomes, and proposals that build definitions out of metabolism, self-replication, homeostasis, evolutionary capacity, or thermodynamic far-from-equilibrium self-organization. And as far as I can tell, the debate has not moved in decades. Everyone knows all the counterexamples; everyone knows the standard responses; nobody changes their mind. What I want from you is not another definition but a diagnosis of the standoff: what kind of disagreement is this, actually?

Three things I've noticed that make me suspect the disagreement is not really about any particular definition:

First, the two sides treat borderline cases asymmetrically. When someone proposes a definition, opponents immediately produce entities that the definition either includes embarrassingly (self-reproducing clay crystal systems, certain autocatalytic chemical networks) or excludes embarrassingly (sterile worker insects, dormant spores, mules if the definition leans on reproduction). For one camp, these cases are data — evidence about where the concept's boundaries really are, or should be. For the other camp, they are pseudo-problems — artifacts of expecting a folk-continuous term to have sharp edges at all. So the disagreement seems to be at least partly about the evidential status of the very examples that structure the debate, which is one level up from any disagreement about the examples themselves.

Second, some participants insist the whole definitional project is misconceived — that "life" is a family-resemblance term, or a leftovers category ("non-life plus a few interesting anomalies"), and that demanding necessary and sufficient conditions is a category error. But this quietist response never actually retires anyone, because operational definitions keep getting used anyway: the working definition used for life-detection purposes in planetary protection and astrobiology, which construes life roughly as a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution, is genuinely load-bearing in mission planning and sample-handling protocols. So it can't be purely verbal — the term does work in the world — and yet the quietists can't be simply wrong either, since the operational definitions visibly misclassify things in one direction or another and everyone shrugs.

Third, and this is my working hypothesis, presented as a hypothesis: I suspect the two camps are covertly answering different questions under one heading. One side is asking whether "life" tracks a natural kind — something whose boundaries we discover rather than stipulate, and whose unity might be historical (a last universal common ancestor making all terran life one clade-like kind) or multiply realizable. The other side is asking something more like an administrative question: given the purposes we have — deciding what to sterilize, what to protect, what to teach schoolchildren — where should we put the boundary, and is a boundary even the right thing to put? If that's right, the stall is explained: each side's strongest arguments are beside the point for the other, because they are conditional on a different premise about what kind of thing a definition of life is supposed to be.

So here is what I'm asking you to do. Diagnose the disagreement using what the sides actually say and do, and tell me explicitly which of these it is — or give me a better option: (a) a substantive disagreement with a hidden empirical core, e.g., resting on facts we don't have, such as whether life arose once or many times on Earth, or whether life elsewhere would share our chemistry; (b) a verbal or purely conceptual disagreement about the word "life," resolvable by stipulation and not worth the ink; (c) a disagreement about the function of definitions in science — whether they should carve nature at its joints or serve stated purposes — in which case the participants are doing philosophy under the impression they are doing biology; or (d) an ill-posed question that should be dissolved, with the residual, legitimate work distributed among successor questions. I'd also like you to address the case that embarrasses the dissolutionists: the operational, mission-planning use of definitions. If the debate is merely verbal, why does stipulating a definition have real-world consequences, and does that fact make the disagreement partly substantive after all?

Finally, be honest about what would count as progress and whether any should be expected. Would the discovery of genuinely alien life end it, or just add one more test case to the pile? Could a conceptual distinction — say, separating "life" as a theoretical predicate from "life" as an administrative or normative category — dissolve the standoff, and if so, why hasn't that distinction already done so, given that something like it has been in circulation for a while? If your diagnosis implies the debate is a stable feature of the terrain rather than a temporary impasse, say that plainly and explain what keeps it running.
