I'm a senior analyst at a foreign-policy think tank that publishes short memos for congressional staff, and every week we kill or revise drafts that lean on a historical analogy — the draft that says we must act now or repeat Munich, the draft that says any intervention is another Vietnam, the draft that warns the U.S. and China are caught in the Thucydides trap. My junior colleagues have a rough feel for why these analogies get misused, but nothing they can apply consistently under deadline, and different reviewers on our team flag the same analogy in opposite directions. Please write a decision procedure my analysts can run in twenty minutes on any draft that uses a historical comparison: the questions to ask in order (for example, about what work the analogy is actually doing in the argument, whether the similarity claims are doing any real inferential work or just re-describing the conclusion, whether the analogy was selected before or after the analyst formed a view, what disanalogy would change the recommendation), what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a firm no, and where it correctly hands the decision to a human because the question is one of judgment rather than structure. The procedure should be honest about its own failure modes — including that a checklist can launder a bad analogy into a respectable-looking one. Then run it on three drafts: a memo urging sanctions on a regional power by appeal to appeasement in the 1930s, a memo opposing any humanitarian intervention on the grounds that it will become another Vietnam, and a memo framing U.S.–China relations through the Thucydides trap. Include at least one case where the procedure permits an analogy I plainly distrust, and explain why that outcome is a feature of the procedure rather than a bug. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do — what an analyst loses by following it, and which misuses of historical analogy it lets through.
