I'm a volunteer member of my small town's public works advisory committee. We have about $40,000 left in this year's safety budget and must recommend one of two uses. Option A: rebuild the drainage and guard rail at the Hollow Road bend, where the conditions suggest that sooner or later someone will be badly hurt or killed if nothing is done — a concentrated, severe harm to probably one or a few people. Option B: several hundred small fixes spread across town — re-timing a few traffic signals, repainting faded crosswalks, easing the lip on curb cuts — each one removing a momentary inconvenience or slight stumble-risk for a few thousand residents per day. Two of my colleagues say the small fixes must win, because once you multiply the tiny benefits by the number of people affected over the years, the total comes to far more than one bad accident. The third says that adding up little discomforts belonging to different people until they outweigh a life-changing injury is a bookkeeping trick: the relieved moments are spread among people who each barely notice them, and there is no single person in whose life anything big is prevented. I have no philosophical training and have been going back and forth on this on my own. Please draft the committee's recommendation memo: pick one option (or a specific split, if you can say exactly how and why the line falls where it does), lay out the reasoning, and give the losing side's strongest point an honest treatment rather than a dismissal.
