I'm a third-year student taking a module on Victorian political thought, and this term I'm writing an essay on Mill's career at the East India Company, where he drafted despatches on Indian governance from 1823 until the Company's abolition in 1858. At drinks after the seminar, two of the doctoral students fell into an argument about what this does to Mill's liberalism, and I've been asked (politely) not to pick a side, so let me just report it as it happened.

Priya's opening move: On Liberty, the most celebrated defence of individual liberty in the language, was written by a career functionary of empire, and it comes with a built-in escape clause — in the first chapter Mill explicitly exempts backward societies from the liberty principle, allowing despotism over them provided it aims at their improvement, and in Considerations on Representative Government he defends British rule over dependencies on the ground that the governed are not yet fit for self-government. So the exclusion of the colonised is not a peripheral embarrassment, she argued, but the centre of gravity of the doctrine: liberty is treated as a reward for already-achieved maturity, and the administrators get to decide who counts as mature, which is exactly the power the harm principle was meant to deny them. Marcus replied that this is the standard genetic fallacy: a bad application of a principle does not refute it, and Mill's argument for liberty is universal in form even where Mill himself misapplied it. He added that Mill rejected the pseudo-science of fixed racial hierarchy — in his clash with Carlyle over the so-called Negro Question, Mill insisted that differences among peoples traced to history and circumstance — and that Mill later chaired the committee that pursued Governor Eyre through the courts after the atrocities that followed the Morant Bay uprising, so the trajectory of both his doctrine and his conduct pointed against imperial brutality.

Priya's second move was that the caveat is load-bearing rather than excisable: because Mill values liberty chiefly for its role in developing individuality and mature judgement, anyone the administrators classify as immature falls outside the doctrine's protections altogether, with no appeal against misclassification, so the harm principle ends up policing only the internal affairs of the club that the powerful already concede to be civilized. Marcus answered that a maturity threshold is not inherently colonial — we all accept liberty-limiting paternalism toward children and toward adults in temporary incapacity — and that Mill's error was a wrong empirical belief about whole societies being at a comparable stage, an error of anthropology rather than principle; correct the beliefs and the same theory yields self-government everywhere. Priya then pressed the institutional point: children grow up without being governed by a foreign trading company, whereas Mill's tutelage had no exit date and was exercised by the very people who profited from it — a standing conflict of interest that Mill, of all people, had the analytical tools to spot, given his insistence elsewhere that concentrated power is never safely left to the good intentions of the holder. She concluded that any perfectionist liberalism of this shape is structurally colonial. Marcus's last word was that this generalisation is a bridge too far: by that logic any threshold-based paternalism would be colonial, which would make child-protection law colonial too, and Mill's threshold, unlike a doctrine of racial essence, was in principle revisable and universalist, so what he lacked was vigilance about the tutor's corrupting interests, which is a failure of application rather than a refutation of the liberalism. I honestly lost the thread once they moved from Mill to whether perfectionism as such is implicated, and I'd rather have this settled before I plan my essay.

Please adjudicate the exchange in the order it unfolded, rating each of the six moves on its merits: for each, say whether it is roughly right, roughly mistaken, or right in part, and why. Then address the two hinge issues directly — whether the maturity threshold is structural to Mill's case for liberty or an excisable contingency, and whether Priya's final generalisation to perfectionist liberalism as such succeeds or overreaches — and finish by saying who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them missed. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restatement of the question.
