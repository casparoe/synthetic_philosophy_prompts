I've been circling the Lucas–Penrose argument for a few years now — started with Lucas's 1961 paper, then Penrose's The Emperor's New Mind and Shadows of the Mind, and I keep coming back to it — and what strikes me is that this debate has been running in place for six decades. The pattern never changes. The anti-mechanist says: hand me any machine whose output is all true arithmetic, I'll construct its Gödel sentence, and since I can see that sentence is true while the machine can't prove it, no machine captures what I do. The mechanist replies: you only got there by assuming the machine is consistent or sound, an assumption you can't establish for yourself any more than the machine can, so at most you've shown there's no machine whose soundness we can prove — which was never in doubt and is perfectly compatible with our being machines. Then Lucas or Penrose comes back with something about how if we were inconsistent, visible false theorems would eventually surface and we'd notice, and the other side says not necessarily, and around it goes. Very smart people have had essentially this same exchange since the sixties and nobody converts.

My hunch — offered as just a hunch — is that the whole impasse lives inside the phrase "we can see the Gödel sentence is true." On one reading that's a claim about provability in some stronger formal system we happen to use, and then the mechanist wins: the diagonal step is available to any system that takes on board the consistency of the one below it. On another reading it's a claim about a faculty of mathematical insight that outruns any formal system, and then the argument is just a restatement of its own conclusion. If that's right, the disagreement is closer to verbal than substantive — two camps reading one sentence two ways. But I'm not sure, because it could equally be a real dispute about whether mathematical understanding is the kind of thing with a determinate output that a machine could match in principle, and neither side seems able to get a grip on that question.

So: diagnose it for me. Is this verbal, or substantive? Is it secretly a disagreement about burden of proof — mechanist must exhibit a machine versus anti-mechanist must make "seeing" precise? Is it bedrock intuitions about what understanding is that neither side can argue for? Or is there a genuine formal question in here that nobody has managed to state correctly yet? Work from what each side actually says rather than giving me a survey. And be honest about whether anything could count as progress — a sharper account of mathematical intuition, some result in proof theory, developments in Penrose's non-computable physics (does anyone think that part still matters, or is it vestigial?) — or whether this is a debate that's ill-posed and should be dissolved rather than won. I keep feeling like somebody should have cracked this by now, and the fact that noone has makes me suspect the question itself is doing something weird.
