Both "causation by omission" and "acausal trade" are cases where the effect on the world happens through, roughly, not-doing rather than doing, or at least through a very ghostly kind of doing. In the one case, the gardener doesn't water the plants and the plants die, and we say the gardener caused the death even though there's no event of his that did anything (there's the whole problem of there being infinitely many omissions everywhere). In the other, two agents who can never causally interact (say, distant civilizations, or an agent and a copy of itself in a simulation) each implement a policy that benefits the other, and the "trade" is supposed to be real and rational even though nothing passes between them.

Compare these two. Specifically, I want to know whether the underlying move is the same move: are both of them just replacing ordinary causal dependence with counterfactual/structural dependence, and can a single theory (something Lewis-style counterfactual, or structural equations) do the work for both? Or do they come apart — e.g., maybe omission cases are still "causal" in a full-strength sense (there's a real counterfactual dependence plus a norm like "the gardener was supposed to water"), while acausal trade rests only on decision-theoretic dependence, which is arguably a weaker and stranger relation than causation? If you think they come apart, say exactly where the analogy breaks. Also, which of the two is the bigger revision of common sense: saying the silent gardener killed the flowers, or saying two civilizations can trade without a single signal exchanged? And is there a risk that whichever framework handles one of them best (say, structural equations for omissions, or functional decision theory for acausal trade) handles the other one badly? Would love a clear verdict at the end: same underlying phenomenon, or two unrelated oddities that just look alike because both involve not-doing?
