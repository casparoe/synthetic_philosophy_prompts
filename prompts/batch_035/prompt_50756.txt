My friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about whether it is rational to contribute to large-scale public goods, and she left me with a written argument I have not been able to shake off. The occasion was humdrum enough: I mentioned that I vote, that I bother with recycling, and that I give a small amount to disaster relief, and she said that, at the scale of a country, all three are strictly irrational. She then set out her case before leaving:

(1) It is irrational to incur a cost for the sake of some outcome when incurring that cost cannot bring the outcome appreciably closer — when the difference one makes is too small for anyone, ever, to notice.
(2) When a good is shared among millions, a single contribution makes exactly such a difference: one vote among fifty million, one household's emissions against billions of tonnes, one donation to a relief fund that moves sums in the hundreds of millions.
(3) So contributing to such goods is irrational, and free-riding is the rational policy, wherever the numbers get large.
(4) The same holds whatever the others do. Even if all of them contribute, my joining adds only one more imperceptible increment; and if I cannot justify my contribution by the increments the others make, I cannot justify it by reference to their contributions either.
(5) Therefore it is irrational for each participant to participate in a large cooperative scheme that succeeds only because all of them participate.

She conceded that (5) has an untoward ring, since it entails that in any successful mass scheme — every election turnout, every broadly observed vaccination programme, every payment schedule into a national pension system — the only rational participants were the free-riders. But she said the reasoning is sound and that common sense simply has to give way here: rationality and cooperation come apart at scale, and the tragedy of the commons is not a paradox but a plain tragedy.

I am less relaxed about it, because (5) seems to me to prove too much in the way she herself admitted. Yet I cannot locate the slip. I have two suspicions I have been unable to make precise. The first is that "imperceptible" does illicit work on the way from (1) to (3): a difference too small to notice is still a difference, and a small difference to an enormously valuable outcome can matter a great deal in expectation. The second is that (4) changes the subject, moving from what my contribution does to what it is worth in the light of what others do. Each time I pressed one of these, she had a reply ready, and I could not tell whether her replies were correct or merely quick.

Please tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. If more than one step is vulnerable, say which is least costly to give up and explain why the others looked secure. For my part, I would most like to keep (1), since abandoning it seems to license spending real resources on gestures that accomplish nothing; and I would like to keep the thought she gave up, that a million people doing the jointly best available thing cannot all be behaving irrationally. I accept that I may have to surrender one of these, or something else entirely. Please also state how confident you are in each of your principal claims, single out the point about which you are least sure, and write your answer in British English.
