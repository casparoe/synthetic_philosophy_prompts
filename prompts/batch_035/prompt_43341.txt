I teach a seminar on perception and the empiricist tradition, and Molyneux's question — whether someone blind from birth who comes to see could, upon first opening their eyes, visually tell a cube from a sphere before touching either — reliably consumes two weeks of discussion and ends exactly where it began. That puzzles me, because the question should be in better shape than ever. Locke made it famous; Cheselden's 1728 report of a boy whose cataracts were removed fueled the argument rather than settling it; the twentieth century added a scattering of comparable cases; and a recent, properly controlled study of children who gained sight after late removal of congenital cataracts found that they performed at chance when asked to match an object they had just felt to one they were shown, yet improved markedly within days of visual experience. The empirical core looks answered, and still the philosophical discussion has not moved. One camp says the negative result vindicates the empiricist thesis that visual space must be learned. A second camp says the experiment tests not spatial content but something like the acquisition of a skill — knowing how to use the eyes to answer a question put to you — so that nothing follows about what spatial representations the newly sighted already possess before training. A third says the question was ill-posed from the start, because "distinguish," "recognize," and "see as" come apart in the first hours of sight, so that any experiment answers only one disambiguated fragment of what Molyneux actually asked.

My working hypothesis — present it as a hypothesis, not a verdict — is that the residual disagreement is not about the data at all but about which cognitive achievement the question was supposed to target, and that the camps are answering different questions under a single name. Test that hypothesis against what the sides actually say. Is the standoff a substantive disagreement hiding behind shared vocabulary, a verbal one about "recognition," or a genuine empirical dispute deferred rather than resolved — a dispute, that is, about what the patients' brains are doing before any training? Distinguish the candidate diagnoses, argue for the one best supported, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a sharper decomposition of Molyneux's question into separately answerable parts, some further experimental manipulation, or nothing short of reconceiving what a cross-modal capacity is. If you think no progress should be expected, say so and explain why. Do not give me a historical survey of who defended which answer in which century; the object here is the structure of the current disagreement, not its lineage. Please write in American English.
