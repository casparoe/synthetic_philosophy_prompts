My flatmate and I had a three-hour argument about this last night and I'm still worked up, so I want to think it through properly instead of just staying annoyed. Background: she's Muslim, and at some point she mentioned that when she needs to know whether something is halal or haram -- her examples were a work contract with interest in it, and some fish thing -- she just asks her mufti and does what he says. I'd recently been reading about the moral testimony debate in philosophy and I told her that's exactly the kind of case the pessimists have in mind: you can't get moral knowledge from an authority's say-so the way you get medical facts from a doctor, because the moral judgment is supposed to be *yours*, done for yourself, not borrowed. She came back hard: I got a second opinion on my knee before surgery, and I'd get a third if two doctors disagreed. Which, fine. The argument went in circles because we were arguing about her as an individual, and it only hit me afterwards that there's a whole institution sitting here, and institutions have philosophical commitments baked into them whether or not anyone inside ever states those commitments out loud.

So here's the practice as she described it when I actually pressed her (she knows all this far better then me). Ordinary Muslims ask a mufti for a fatwa on concrete practical questions. The mufti is supposed to do ijtihad -- actually reason it out from the sources -- while the layperson does taqlid, meaning follows the qualified scholar's conclusion without being able to reconstruct the reasoning themselves. Taqlid is standardly considered fine for fiqh, practical religious law, but not for aqida, creed -- there you're supposed to hold the beliefs yourself, on the basis you personally find convincing. Fatwas are non-binding, and the reasoning is normally attached -- a fatwa cites the relevant sources and precedents rather than just announcing a verdict. And laypeople do sometimes ask a second mufti, or go to a scholar from a different school altogether, if they don't like the first answer or the scholars disagree.

What I want is an analysis of what this whole setup presupposes about moral or religious testimony. Specifically: does the practice presuppose that the fatwa functions as mere evidence for the layperson, or as a source of justification in its own right? Which of the standard positions in the moral testimony debate does it actually fit -- the reductionist view that testimony is just one more piece of evidence, the view that testimony on these matters is its own basic source, the view where you can borrow moral knowledge but only if you could in principle reconstruct the reasons, or the pessimists who say testimony never gets you moral knowledge at all -- and which does it rule out? Is the fiqh/aqida line tracking a belief-versus-action distinction about when deference is acceptable, and if so does that distinction help her side or mine? Does the fact that fatwas come with reasoning attached, and that mufti-shopping is tolerated, fit better with one theory than with the others? And where is the practice just underdetermined -- would several of these theories license the whole setup, so that no verdict can be read off the institution itself?

Be honest about the parts of the practice that pull in different directions, and at the end tell me who came out of the argument better, her or me -- but based on what the institution presupposes, not on who argued more fiercely.
