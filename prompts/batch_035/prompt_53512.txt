As part of my teaching duties for an introductory critical thinking course, I'm preparing a unit on the sunk cost fallacy, and I've discovered that my own convictions are less tidy than the textbook treatment I'm supposed to deliver. The textbook line is that rational choice attends only to future costs and benefits, since past expenditures are gone no matter what you do next; honoring them is supposed to be a paradigm of irrationality. When I go through cases and record my gut verdicts, though, I find I don't actually accept that line across the board, and my friends, whom I've been quizzing over dinner for two weeks, don't either. So before I stand in front of thirty undergraduates and tell them that only fools weigh sunk costs, I'd like to know whether there's a principle that captures the pattern in my verdicts — and I'd like your help finding it, or finding out that there isn't one.

Here are the verdicts. Continuing to read a dull novel two hundred pages in because I paid twenty dollars for it: irrational, and I'd say so to anyone. Staying through a film I'm loathing because the tickets were expensive: the same, though I confess to doing it. A government pressing on with a hugely over-budget rail project on the grounds that cancelling would mean the billions already spent went to waste: I take this to be the fallacy in its purest form, and I'd mark a student down for defending it. Staying in a ten-year relationship purely because ten years have been invested: fallacy again, and a corrosive one. But now the cases that resist. A friend in the sixth year of a PhD, three months from submission, staying on through misery largely because quitting would mean the six years were for nothing: I find myself unwilling to call this a fallacy, even though on the textbook line the six years are precisely as gone as the novel's purchase price. My grandmother refusing to sell the family farm at a very good price, because my late grandfather's decades of labor are, as she puts it, in that land: I think this is not merely excusable sentiment but in some sense the right way for her to value the farm, and I would not want a decision theorist to talk her out of it. And the one that disturbs me most: I find the argument that a war must be continued so that the dead shall not have died in vain rhetorically repugnant and plainly fallacious when I inspect it, yet when I read it in a eulogy I'm moved by it, and I can't fully dismiss the thought that something in it is being tracked by my emotions even if my reasoning rejects it.

I have a tentative principle of my own, and I'd like you to test it against these cases and repair or replace it as needed. It goes something like this: sunk costs are irrational to weigh insofar as they function only as evidence about the future — as when the billions spent on the rail project are cited as if they raised the expected value of finishing it — but they may legitimately be weighed when they are part of the content of the future, that is, when the value of what lies ahead genuinely depends on what lies behind, as with my grandmother's farm, where the past investment doesn't predict anything about the land; it constitutes what the land is worth to her. Does this principle, or any repair of it, deliver my verdicts on all the cases, including the PhD and the eulogy ones, where I most want it to succeed but least trust it? If some verdict has to go, tell me which, and why that one rather than another. And please tell me what the resulting principle predicts about two cases I haven't decided: a woman who has written seventy thousand words of a novel that a cold calculation says she will neither publish nor enjoy finishing, wondering whether to abandon it; and a parent deciding whether to make a twelve-year-old continue an expensive sport she has come to hate, on the ground that letting her quit would waste the years already put in. I'd like a precise statement of the principle, the case that motivates each clause, and an honest accounting of where it fails me — I have a lecture to write, and I would rather it be complicated and true than clean and something I don't believe.
