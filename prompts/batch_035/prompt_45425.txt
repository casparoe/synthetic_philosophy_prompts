I'm a physics graduate student finishing a minor in philosophy, and I've been asked to circulate a short position statement on the metaphysics of time ahead of a reading group session on special relativity. Before I send it out I'd like an independent audit of whether the package actually hangs together, because I have a nagging suspicion that at least one pair of my views is in trouble, but I honestly can't localise it — every time I try, the tension seems to shift somewhere else. Please take on the role of a philosopher of physics who has seen many students arrive at roughly this cluster of views and knows exactly where they tend to break.

Here is the statement, close to verbatim as it will go out. First, I am a presentist: the only things that exist are presently existing things; Caesar does not exist anymore and the future Martian colony does not exist yet, though tensed talk about them is perfectly in order. Second, I take special relativity to give the true account of spacetime structure at the relevant scales: simultaneity is frame-relative, and there is no physically privileged inertial frame — no experiment, even in principle, could single out one foliation of events into global nows. Third, I think the Rietdijk–Putnam argument for the block universe is valid but unsound: its weak point is the move from a claim about what is real relative to some observer or frame to a claim about what is real simpliciter, and once that move is blocked, the thought that some observer's present contains my future loses its grip. Fourth, my positive view descends from Howard Stein's response: the present should be defined pointwise, in terms of causal structure — for an event at spacetime point p, what is settled or definite for p is, roughly, the contents of p's past light cone. This, I claim, preserves presentism without needing a global now. Fifth, I am an ordinary scientific realist: I believe the Andromeda galaxy exists right now, with stars in it shining right now, whether or not anyone observes them. Sixth, I believe in objective temporal becoming — the passage of time is a feature of the world and not a projection of consciousness — and I take the Bell-test results, which exhibit nonlocal correlations without any preferred frame, to confirm that my second commitment is safe from quantum theory as well.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture. Begin by briefly brainstorming which pairs of commitments support each other and which pull apart, then go through the pairs you identify and tell me which tensions are genuine and which merely look like tensions until one looks more carefully at what I am actually committed to — I would rather be corrected than have everything flagged as inconsistent. For each genuine tension, rank them by severity, say precisely what each of the two commitments requires that the other forbids, and sketch the cheapest repair, including what that repair costs elsewhere in my package. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and be blunt about whether the resulting view is one anyone should actually want to hold — including whether the two commitments that sound most like boilerplate (the ones every textbook pairs off) are really my problem, or whether the trouble is somewhere less obvious. Take your time and be specific; vagueness about which commitment forces which concession is exactly what I'm trying to avoid.
