I'm a master's student in public policy, and I'm taking an elective in political philosophy that keeps colliding with my quantitative coursework in a way I can't resolve. In my cost-benefit analysis class, we learned how governments in the US, UK, and elsewhere monetize climate damages when they compute the "social cost of carbon": projected harms from warming — flooded cities, lost crops, mortality — are converted into dollar figures, and then those dollar figures are shrunk by a discount rate for every year they lie in the future, so that a hundred billion dollars of damage in 2200 registers as almost nothing in today's policy calculations. The rate itself is usually built from a formula credited to the Cambridge mathematician Frank Ramsey back in the 1920s, with two distinct components: a rate of pure time preference, which values a unit of welfare less simply because it arrives later, and a growth term, which values it less because future people are expected to be richer and thus to feel the loss of any given dollar less acutely. Famously, when economists plug in different defensible values — a prominent British government review in the mid-2000s used a rate near 1.5%, while the American modelers behind one of the leading integrated assessment models argued for something closer to 4–5% — the resulting estimates of the true cost of emitting a ton of carbon differ by a factor of ten or more, and with them the entire policy verdict about what emissions controls are worth doing.

What I want to understand is what this whole practice presupposes philosophically. My philosophy instructor says that the pure time preference term looks like it presupposes that the mere date at which a person exists affects how much their welfare counts — which is exactly what most moral philosophy of the last two centuries denies, and what the same governments seem to deny every time they say they owe a duty to their grandchildren. Is that a genuine contradiction, or is there some respectable way for a positive rate of pure time preference to be neutral about intergenerational equality? The growth term confuses me even more: it presents itself as a descriptive forecast rather than a moral premise, but the forecast is that the future will be rich, and the reason the discount rate matters at all is that climate damages might make the future poor — so the practice seems to bet on the very variable the policy is supposed to protect. There's also a wrinkle I don't know what to do with: several governments have recently adopted discount rates that decline over the horizon, on the ground that uncertainty about future growth argues for humility, and I can't tell whether that's a change of moral view or merely of modeling technique.

So here is my request: reconstruct the implicit theory of intergenerational justice that the discounting practice actually rests on. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes — some form of aggregative welfarism across persons and times? interpersonal comparability of utility? — from what it merely happens to fit well with. Say whether its components presuppose mutually consistent things, or whether the pure time preference term and the growth term quietly rest on rival views of why the future counts less. And tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could a sufficientarian view, or a rights-based view that forbids exposing future people to catastrophic risk regardless of aggregate welfare, license roughly the same regulatory numbers? Finally, sketch what would actually have to change in regulatory practice if a government accepted the philosophers' egalitarian objection and set pure time preference to zero, or abandoned the aggregative framework altogether — I'm curious whether anything concrete would move, or whether the philosophy, once you grind it through the models, ends up drowned out by the empirical parameters anyway. I'd rather you reason it through carefully than gesture at the literature; I don't need a reading list.
