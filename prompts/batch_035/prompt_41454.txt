I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or physics — everything I know about quantum foundations comes from popular books, the Stanford Encyclopedia, and too many arguments with colleagues after we started messing around with quantum computing frameworks at work. I've gradually settled into a set of views about Bell's theorem and locality, and before I write them up for an internal talk (which a few actual physicists will attend), I want to know whether the package is even coherent. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(a) I take the many-worlds reading of quantum mechanics seriously: the universal wave function is all there fundamentally is, and it evolves only unitarily — there is no collapse postulate.

(b) I hold that relativity's prohibition on faster-than-light influences is bedrock: whatever quantum mechanics turns out to mean, the fundamental physics contains no nonlocal influence. "Spooky action at a distance" is exactly what I think we should refuse to accept.

(c) I take the loophole-free Bell experiments from 2015 onward at face value: local hidden-variable theories are empirically ruled out, and this is a fact about how the world is, not an artifact of how the experiments were designed.

(d) I regard superdeterminism — the claim that the measurement settings were correlated all along with the particles' properties — as an unfalsifiable conspiracy theory, and I won't take it seriously.

(e) I likewise won't consider retrocausal stories in which the future settings influence the past particles. That seems like a loophole in name only.

(f) At the same time, I take it as obvious that each measurement has a single definite outcome: in each run of a Bell experiment, each detector flashes exactly once, and the data log contains one entry per run — not some superposition of entries.

(g) And despite the determinism that (a) seems to imply, I want to say the outcomes are genuinely chancy in a robust sense: when the detector flashes, it really could have flashed otherwise, with probabilities given by the Born rule.

Please audit this set for consistency. Which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, and how severe is each tension? Which pairs only look like they conflict, and why does the apparent conflict dissolve under closer inspection? And crucially, since I don't know where the problem is: what does (c), taken together with (d) and (e), actually force me to give up — and is there any coherent package in the neighborhood of my views, or does the whole thing have to come apart? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each one tell me what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. Be specific and unsparing; I'd rather find out now than from the physicists in the room.
