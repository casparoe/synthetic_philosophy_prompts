I've been arguing with my sister about assisted dying for months and I've finally written down my gut verdicts on a bunch of concrete cases, and now I want to know if they add up to a principle or if I'm just pattern-matching to whichever framing the last article I read used. Here's the list. ALS patient who's ventilator-dependent asks for the ventilator to be withdrawn, knowing she'll die within the hour: fine. Same patient instead asks her doctor to hand her a lethal prescription she swallows herself while she still can: fine, and honestly it doesn't feel very different from the withdrawal. Same patient asks the doctor to just inject her: also fine? I keep saying fine, which makes me suspicious I'm not actually tracking any distinction at all. A 45-year-old who's physically healthy but has decades of treatment-resistant depression asks for assisted death under the kind of psychiatric-suffering criteria places like the Netherlands and Belgium allow: not fine, something about it not being the endstage of anything. A woman with advanced dementia, living apparently contentedly in a care home, no longer recognizes anyone, but twenty years ago wrote an advance directive saying that if she ever reached that point she wanted to be helped to die: honoring it, I flip on weekly. A patient with untreatable cancer pain asks to be sedated into permanent unconsciousness with no food or fluids, expecting to die of dehydration within days (palliative sedation): fine. A terminally ill patient decides entirely on her own to stop eating and drinking and asks her caregivers just for comfort care while she dies over a week or two: fine, her body her choice. A doctor gives escalating doses of morphine for pain, foreseeing but not intending that a dose might suppress breathing enough to kill: obviously fine.

The killing/letting-die line clearly isn't doing any work for me, since I treat the injection, the prescription, and the withdrawal as basically the same. My tentative thought is that it's some combination of consent plus prognosis that's doing the real work. But my sister keeps hammering the palliative sedation and the voluntary stopping of eating and drinking, saying both are just the injection with extra steps and a slower clock, and I don't have a good comeback — I feel fine about them precisely because the dying comes from the patient's own body giving out rather than anything anyone administers, but I can't say why that matters if I've already said the injection is fine. And the advance directive case seems to break every candidate principle I try, because the person consenting and the person dying are two people in every way I care about. So give me your best shot at a principle (or two candidates if you need them), stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Then tell me straight which of my verdicts it can't save, and what it says about a case I haven't thought through: a man with a severe but stable high spinal cord injury, not sick, not dying, not in pain, who says life as a quadriplegic is not one he'll accept and asks for assisted death. Lead with the bottom line — the principle itself, in your first sentence — and then the reasoning; no preamble and no restating my question back at me.
