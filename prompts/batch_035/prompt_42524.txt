My reading group has been arguing about Kim's causal exclusion argument for weeks now and we keep just re-enacting the positions: one person goes full reductive physicalist, one digs in on non-reductive physicalism, and someone always ends up saying "so pain really doesn't do anything?" as a gotcha, and honestly I've lost track of what the actual commitments are. I'm writing a thesis chapter on this and before I read another single paper I want the specification straight. So: you're a professional philosopher of mind who has taught this debate for years and has no patience for camp loyalty. Write me the list of desiderata that any adequate account of mental causation has to meet. Off the top of my head I'd expect things like: consistency with the causal closure of the physical (and I want you to be careful about which version of closure is actually load-bearing — the completeness of physics, the no-gap-in-the-physical-story version, or something weaker); no systematic overdetermination, i.e., the piano can't be pushed into the lake by both the complete physical state and my decision, each individually sufficient; the mental has to make a genuine difference, counterfactual or otherwise, or we've just smuggled in epiphenomenalism with better PR; and enough mental/physical distinctness to keep "the mental caused it" from collapsing into a restatement of the physical cause. But there may be others I haven't thought of — maybe something about explanation, maybe about interventionist counterfactuals, maybe about what counts as a cause versus a background condition — include those if you think they genuinely belong.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, then mark which are non-negotiable and which are really just preferences of particular camps. I suspect "no overdetermination" does less work than everyone assumes, since the double-prevention and shared-cause cases everyone tolerates elsewhere already look pretty overdermination-y, but maybe I'm wrong and you should say so. The part I most want: if some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, show the argument explicitly. My working hunch is that the list, done honestly, just *is* the exclusion argument — with reductive physicalism, non-reductive physicalism, and epiphenomenalism falling out as three different choices about which desideratum to drop — and I want to know if that's right or whether there's slack in the list that the standard positions exploit. Think carefully and brainstorm the candidate criteria before you commit to the final list, since the framing matters a lot here. End with at most a short paragraph on which single requirement you'd drop first and why, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves, not on scoring the existing theories against them.
