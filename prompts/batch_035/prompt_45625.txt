I am writing a novel set at a state psychiatric hospital in 1962, narrated by a young staff psychiatrist who has just read Thomas Szasz's The Myth of Mental Illness and cannot decide whether the man is a liberator or a crank. To render him properly I need him to hold firm, articulate convictions about where illness ends and mere trouble in living begins, and since I can only write convictions I can actually locate, I have been interrogating my own verdicts on a sheaf of cases. They refuse to arrange themselves into anything tidy, and the novel rather depends on my narrator's position being defensible rather than merely habitual. My editor keeps asking whether the man is meant to be right, and I cannot tell her.

Here are my gut verdicts. Hypothyroidism that presents as apathy and slowed thinking: illness, plainly. Delirium tremens: illness. Epilepsy, even in the centuries when it was called demonic possession: illness retroactively, whatever the era's own verdict. A widower admitted after two years of inconsolable grief: suffering and deserving of help, but not ill — the hospital has made a category error. A woman with schizophrenia, hearing implacable voices, unable to feed herself: illness; I cannot make myself reclassify her as unlucky in her problems in living, though I notice Szasz's argument against the grieving widower applies to her with equal force. Homosexuality, listed as a disorder in the current diagnostic manual: not an illness, though I concede it is only my own era's convictions speaking through me. Drapetomania, the antebellum diagnosis invented for enslaved people who fled captivity: transparently not an illness, however medical its dress. Compulsive gambling: vice or weakness, not illness — unless it follows a demonstrable brain injury, whereupon, to my surprise, I find I do want to call it one. And anorexia in a teenage girl starving herself in front of a mirror: here I genuinely waver.

The obvious candidate principle is Szasz's own — that disease requires a lesion or demonstrable bodily malfunction, and that everything else psychiatry calls illness is metaphor or social control — but it appears to sacrifice my verdict on the schizophrenic woman and possibly on the injured gambler, while saving the drapetomania and widower verdicts I'm least worried about. So I am asking you to do the reflective work for me. State, precisely, the principle or small set of competing principles that best captures this pattern; tie each clause to the case that motivates it; tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which I ought to surrender; and apply it to the cases I have withheld — a returning soldier haunted and sleepless, a boy who cannot sit still in class, the girl before the mirror — so I can see whether it behaves. Finally, tell me plainly: on the account you land on, is my narrator's dawning sympathy for Szasz warranted or a mistake, and which of my cases would he and the old superintendent quarrel over at the ward meeting? I would rather the novel be philosophically load-bearing than merely period-correct, so do not soften the tension to spare me.
