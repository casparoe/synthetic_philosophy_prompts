I help run a small informal philosophy discussion group that meets in a pub once a fortnight — none of us are academics, I work in logistics, but we've been going through famous ethics papers together and having a great time. Last session's topic was John Taurek's 1977 paper "Should the Numbers Count?" about the rescue case where you can save either one stranger or five strangers, and whether you're allowed to just flip a coin instead of saving the five. I set the members a short written question afterwards, and one of them, Dave (ironically also a David, unlike Taurek's friend), sent me his answer. I told everyone I'd mark their answers and give feedback, but honestly I don't trust myself to spot everything — I only half-understand this stuff myself, and I don't want to be unfair to him by marking something wrong that's actually right, or vice versa.

Here is the question I set:

"In his paper 'Should the Numbers Count?', John Taurek considers a case where you can rescue either a single person or five people, but not both. Explain why Taurek thinks it can be permissible to flip a coin rather than save the five, and why he denies that the death of five would be a worse outcome than the death of one. Then explain what you take to be the strongest objection to his view, and how Taurek might respond."

And here is Dave's answer, copied and pasted as he sent it:

"Taurek's point is basically that each person's life matters to them just as much as anyone else's matters to them, so there's no impersonal standpoint from which the five matter more. He imagines that one of the people is his friend David, and says he'd save David straight away without flipping, which shows he doesn't think we're required to be impartial. But when it's six strangers, he flips a coin between saving the one and saving the five, and this is the fair thing to do because it maximises expected utility while giving everyone an equal chance — each of the six ends up with a 50% shot at being saved, so it's both fair and rational in the decision-theoretic sense. On the 'worse outcome' question, he argues that deaths are losses to individual people, and since one person can only die once, five deaths aren't any bigger a loss for anybody than one death would be, which proves one death and five deaths are morally equivalent. His general principle is that numbers never count, so even if it were 5 people versus 50, or 5 versus 5,000, you'd still be required to flip. The strongest objection is simply the utilitarian one: if you can save five lives or one, saving five obviously does more good, so Taurek is just wrong. Taurek would probably reply that the utilitarian is wrongly adding up losses that belong to different people."

Could you grade this out of 25 and give me point-by-point comments on what Dave got right and where he went wrong or oversimplified? I'd especially like to know whether his reading of the coin flip argument is accurate, because something about the "expected utility" bit felt off to me when I read it, but I can't quite put my finger on why. Also please flag anything he says that's right but under-explained. Please write your comments in British English, as we're a UK-based group.
