I've been thinking about something that happens with big, once-and-for-all life decisions, and I can't find a word for it anywhere. Consider deciding whether to have children. Before deciding, you cannot find out what it would be like for you: everyone who can tell you is someone who already decided, and by the time they report on it, the decision has had years to shape what they want and what they regard as normal. The testimony always comes from the far side of the choice, given by a person the choice itself helped produce. I keep seeing the same shape elsewhere: someone weighing whether to leave a career for a different one gets advice only from people who left and people who stayed, and each side's confidence looks like a byproduct of the life they ended up in rather than a measurement anyone could have taken beforehand. It isn't merely that the future is hard to imagine — everyone knows that part — it's that the evidence needed to judge the decision seems to arrive only after the decision, and to be generated by the decision, so nobody standing where the decider stands can obtain an uncompromised reading. Does philosophy or any other field have an established name for this situation? If not, I would like you to coin one, define it precisely, and mark its edges: in particular, does it persist when a choice does not change one's values at all but merely makes the alternative life unavailable for firsthand comparison? Please distinguish it carefully from the nearest existing ideas rather than giving me a bare label. Kindly write your answer in American English.
