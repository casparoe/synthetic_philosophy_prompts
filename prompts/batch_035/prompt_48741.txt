For most of a decade of reading rationalist blogs I've internalized the line that deference is fine when it's backed by a track record, and nowhere do I apply this more comfortably than aesthetics. My friend R.'s film recommendations have hit for me something like nine times out of ten over the years we've been trading them, and when she texts that a movie is masterful, I update hard and buy the ticket; if someone then asks me whether the film is good, I say yes without hedging and feel entitled to. A sommelier whose palate I trust tells me a wine shows blackcurrant and crushed stone — I believe her outright, even though I couldn't pick out either note. An art historian tells me a quiet little canvas is the keystone of the whole exhibition, and I'll repeat that at dinner. So far, so deferential. But there are cases where the same move feels broken, and I can't get the cases to add up. When a colleague with unimpeachable taste tells me a joke is hilarious, I believe him in some thin sense, yet it feels false to say I *know* the joke is funny until I've gotten it myself — the getting seems constitutive of the knowing. A blind friend, told by a trusted companion that the Rothko is overwhelming, seems to me not to acquire that piece of knowledge however good the companion; there's something faintly off about her reporting it as more than hearsay. And when the sommelier shifts from notes of blackcurrant to telling me the wine is *finer* than the one I actually prefer, I balk — that verdict feels like something only tasting could establish.

Here is the tentative principle I've been using, and I'd like it stress-tested: testimony transmits aesthetic beliefs as *predictions about my own experience*, and only those predictions can amount to knowledge; claims about the intrinsic aesthetic merit of the object can be received as evidence but not as knowledge proper. R.'s track record is a record of her predicting *my* reactions, so her "masterful" translates to "you will experience mastery," and my confidence is calibration, not credulity. The sommelier's blackcurrant claim I'm honestly unsure about — it feels knowable-by-testimony, which the principle should probably forbid since it's not about my experience, or maybe it's really a claim about the winemaking that she's reading off instruments of a sort. The joke case fits nicely: until I've gotten it, there's no experience to predict, so no knowledge. But the Rothko and the "finer" wine fit too well, and here's the pair I can't reconcile: my dinner-table repetition of the art historian's "keystone of the exhibition" claim, which I'd swear I know, even though it's about the object and not about me at all — and my refusal to let the blind friend know the Rothko is overwhelming on equally good testimony. Either the me/about-the-object line is doing work the track-record story can't do, or one of those verdicts is sentiment rather than principle. And there's a lurking worry that the whole distinction is a confusion: "the film is masterful" and "I will find the film masterful" may be one proposition wearing two hats, in which case my friend can know it flat-out and so, by her mouth, can I.

So, please: state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, as precisely as you can; say which clauses each of my cases motivates; and be honest about which of my verdicts your favored principle has to overturn rather than save — I'd rather lose the dinner-table one than gain a two-tier epistemology of taste with an undefended boundary. Then tell me what the principle predicts for cases I haven't considered, especially these three: a colorblind person told by a designer that two swatches clash; a friend who has studied my sense of humor assuring me a joke I didn't get is funny, in a language where I'd get it if I heard it again after she explains the missing context; and me, a week after finally seeing the film R. praised and loving it, looking back — did I know it was good all along, or only that I would?
