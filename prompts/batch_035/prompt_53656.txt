Hi! I'm a PhD student in biostatistics, and for the last few years I've been the "stats person" for a mid-sized regional water utility (long story — started as a side gig during my masters and now I can't get rid of them). The single most common question I get is some version of: "this number has been stable for X years, can we assume it stays that way?" — for sizing spare part inventories, deciding whether to keep a monitoring program, setting maintenance budgets. And I've realized I don't have a principled way to answer it; I basically vibe-check based on how long the stable period was and whether anyone in the room looks nervous.

I've read enough philosophy of induction to know the rough shape of the problem — Hume obviously, and Goodman's grue thing, where whether a regularity projects depends on which predicate you describe it with, not just on how many confirming instances you have. Goodman's own answer seems to be something about how "entrenched" the predicate is, but entrenchment is defined by the predicate's history of successful past projections, and from where I sit that's circular-ish: we have exactly one past, one history, no control group. So what I want is to turn all this (plus whatever else is actually usable — reference class stuff, whether you have a mechanism or just a pattern, extrapolation vs interpolation) into a procedure that two utility engineers and I can run in an hour-long meeting, per forecast. Concretely:

- an ordered list of questions, each answerable by a domain engineer with no stats training,
- what counts as a sufficient answer at each step,
- the conditions under which the procedure outputs "do not extrapolate, budget for the uncertainty instead" versus "extrapolate, but state a horizon",
- and the points where it has to admit it can't settle the question and hands the decision back to a human — flag those honestly, don't bury them.

The grue-flavored problem I keep actually hitting: a lot of our historical regularities are indexed to categories that were created at a particular date — meter types, pipe materials introduced by code changes, "accounts opened before the 2009 billing software migration." So the regularity is only ever observed on one side of a time boundary, and the category itself was drawn at that boundary. I want the procedure to include a test for this that an engineer can apply themselves (some red-flag questions about how the categories were constructed and whether the cut date has anything to do with the outcome), not just a warning to "watch out for grue-like predicates."

Then run it on three cases: (1) pump failures — 15 years of stable rates, known failure mechanism (seal wear); (2) household demand by meter generation, where the newest generation was only rolled out 2 years ago, so we have almost no data on how the observed pattern behaves as those meters age; (3) the one where I actually disagree with the engineers — they want to keep extrapolating a 20-year seasonal demand pattern even though the region's housing stock has been turning over fast, and I suspect the procedure should tell us to stop, but run it honestly even if it sides with them.

Also tell me what the procedure can't do — I assume it says nothing about genuinely unprecedented events, but if there are other failure modes I haven't listed, I'd rather find out now than in the next budget meeting. And if some step is basically a judgment call dressed up as a question with a checklist feel, say so; the director has to sign off on whatever we produce and I don't want to hand her false precision.
