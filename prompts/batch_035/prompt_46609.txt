I chair the examinations committee of a professional body that certifies software engineers. Our certificate functions as a licence to practice: employers and, in some jurisdictions, regulators treat it as evidence that the holder can be trusted with production systems. Until recently our rules were simple — a closed-book written exam on fundamentals, plus a practical exam on a sandbox machine with a standard editor and the language documentation installed. Now we receive a steady stream of requests, several per quarter, to allow or disallow particular resources on the practical exam: full web access, autocomplete and refactoring tools, AI code assistants of varying power, a candidate's personal snippet library, a printed cheat sheet, a designated colleague on chat to clarify requirements. Each request needs a ruling within a few weeks, with a written rationale, because candidates can and do appeal, and inconsistent rulings keep getting overturned. Our committee members are working engineers, not philosophers, and they currently decide by intuition.

I would like our rulings grounded in the extended mind thesis. My understanding of it, roughly: Clark and Chalmers argued that when an external resource is constantly available, automatically trusted and endorsed in the course of thought, and smoothly integrated into a person's cognition, it functions as part of that person's mind — thinking does not stop at the skull, and Otto's notebook does for him what Inga's biological memory does for her. The committee's competing instincts map onto this neatly: if a tool genuinely functions as part of the candidate's mind, then examining the candidate without it examines a stripped-down person who will never exist in practice; but if we admit every tool, we may end up certifying the tool rather than the engineer, and the certificate's meaning quietly changes.

Please write a decision procedure the committee can run on a request. It should list the questions to ask in order, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, mark the points where the procedure returns a ruling outright and the points where it instead hands the case back to the committee because the remaining question is one of professional values rather than anything the extended mind framework can settle. It must be usable within an hour by a non-philosopher, and it should be candid about which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three requests: (a) a code-completion assistant trained on general code; (b) the candidate's own curated snippet library, built up over their career; (c) full web access, including documentation and Q&A sites. At least one ruling should be one the committee will dislike, and you should say which one it is and why the procedure nonetheless returns it. Finally, tell me where the procedure is most likely to misfire as code assistants improve over the next few years, and identify what in the extended mind framework itself — not merely in our implementation of it — limits how far it can carry us here.
