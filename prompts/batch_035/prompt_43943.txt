I'm a phd student in decision theory who's been pulled into an alignment-adjacent reading group, and something keeps bugging me: everyone argues about whether CEV-style ideas are good or bad before anyone says what a theory of idealized preferences has to actually deliver. You know the genre — take people's preferences, imagine them under better conditions (more knowledge, more coherence, more reflection), and go with what comes out the other end. Before I read another take, I want the spec. Write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of idealized preferences has to get right, with the case behind each one. Some things I'd expect to see on the list, so you know the flavor I'm after — it can't just launder the designer's values into "what you would have wanted" (the old line about the ghost in the extrapolation machine), it has to say something about people whose preferences actively resist idealization, and it has to handle the fact that reflection doesn't obviously terminate — but don't just do my list, build the real one. For each desideratum say whether it's non-negotiable or a camp preference, and crucially: tell me if you think some subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable. My hunch is that there's a triangle — fidelity to what people actually want, improvement over what they actually want, and determinacy of the output — where you can pick two, and that hunch is honestly the thing I most want tested. Then a quick scorecard at the end: how does CEV as originally sketched, plain economic rationality as the idealizer, and old-school reflective equilibrium do against the list. Keep it short tho, the criteria are the point, the scoring is just a sanity check.
