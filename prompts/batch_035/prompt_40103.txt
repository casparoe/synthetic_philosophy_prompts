I've been going through the peer disagreement stuff for a few months now — Elga's equal weight view, Kelly's right reasons stuff, Christensen on higher-order evidence, van Inwagen's steadfast instinct — and the debate seems completely stuck in a way that almost nobody admits. Everyone agrees the case descriptions (two people, same evidence, same track record, disagree about the proposition), everyone agrees peerhood is possible in principle, and then conciliationists say split the difference and steadfast folks say hold your ground, and every paper since like 2010 is a reply to a reply to a reply. My working theory is that the fight is mostly verbal/artifactual: "epistemic peer" gets defined with tokens like equal evidence and equal epistemic ability, but those tokens do all the work, so each side just constructs cases where the stipulation already contains the conclusion they want — conciliationists describe cases where your first-order reasons feel thin, steadfast folks describe cases where you actually can see the flaw in the other guy's reasoning — and neither case is a case the other side accepts. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there something substantive hiding underneath, e.g. a real disagreement about whether higher-order evidence is evidence about the proposition or evidence about yourself as a reasoner? And what would even count as progress here — a better taxonomy of cases, a formal model of self-trust, giving up on the peer idealization altogether — or is this just a clash of bedrock intuitions about self-trust that no argument can settle?
