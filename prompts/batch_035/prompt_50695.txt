Retired physicist here — condensed matter, forty years of measuring susceptibilities and phase transitions at a national lab — and since retiring I've gotten deep into philosophy of mind, mostly to prove to myself that all this consciousness talk was soft. It didn't work. The thing that actually keeps me up at night now is that I can't find a clean argument for why experience should switch on only above some threshold of organizational complexity, and once you can't rule out that some experiential aspect goes all the way down, you're at least in the neighborhood of panpsychism.

Here's what I want you to analyze. Look at the practices we actually have for guarding against suffering: lab animal regulations that draw lines at vertebrates (with some jurisdictions recently moving octopuses and crabs across it), welfare laws that ignore anything without a nervous system, AI ethics boards that only worry about systems that behave like they might feel something, and so on. Every one of these practices uses the same implicit test — is there complex organized stuff of roughly the right kind, and does it react like it cares? What view of mind and of suffering does that whole pattern presuppose? My guess is some kind of emergence story where consciousness is something organized systems do and simpler systems just have no inner life at all, but I want to know whether that's strictly presupposed or whether a panpsychist could keep every one of those practices intact and just say the nervous-system criterion is a decent proxy for where experience gets ethically interesting — enough intensity, enough unity, whatever the relevant variables turn out to be.

And then the harder direction: if you took panpsychism seriously, what would actually have to change, and what wouldn't? I've seen people claim the stakes become literally cosmological — every rock and every lump of hot plasma in a furnace is a locus of experience, so the moral universe balloons beyond comprehension. Other people say it changes nothing, because proto-experience without structure can't suffer: suffering needs valence, valence needs contrast, and undifferentiated experiential stuff can't prefer anything. For a former experimentalist this is the part I actually care about — which is it, and where exactly does the line fall between "our practices are philosophically underdetermined, several theories of mind would license them" and "our practices quietly commit us to emergence whether we like it or not"?

Not looking for a reading list or citations — just the analysis, and if you think my framing has a hole in it (e.g. that panpsychism and emergence aren't the only live options here), say so.
