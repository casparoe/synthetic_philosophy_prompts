For the final paper in my political philosophy course, we've been asked to write up our considered commitments on distributive justice — not a survey of the literature, but a personal profile that classmates will then attack in seminar. Before I submit mine, I'd like an outside audit of whether the package actually hangs together. Here is what I've written down:

(1) I'm a luck egalitarian: the point of distributive justice is to neutralize the effects of brute luck — the circumstances people land in through no choice of their own — while leaving people to bear the consequences of their genuine choices.

(2) I accept what seem to be the harsh implications of the view. If two people start with equal resources and one prudently insures while the other gambles everything away, the gambler has no claim to further aid, and taxing the prudent to rescue him would be unjust. I hold this even though it sounds terrible, because I think respect for people as the authors of their own lives is precisely what the view is for.

(3) I believe the expensive-tastes objection is fatal to welfare egalitarianism and that the luck egalitarian escapes it: someone who cultivates a taste for vintage wine has no claim to extra resources to indulge it.

(4) I hold that a choice is only genuinely one's own — only responsibility-generating — if its springs are under one's control: the preferences, values, and willpower from which it flows. And I take seriously the thought, which I find hard to resist, that those springs are overwhelmingly the products of genetic endowment and upbringing. My own industriousness, or lack of it, is not something I ever chose.

(5) I believe, as a matter of justice rather than charity, that no one may be permitted to fall below a decent social minimum — including the gambler from (2), and including people whose dire situation is in some sense self-inflicted.

(6) Alongside all this, I favor compulsory insurance schemes: everyone must contribute to unemployment and disability funds rather than being free to opt out and absorb their own bad luck as it comes.

What I'd like from you is a consistency check. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which apparent conflicts dissolve on inspection, and what is the least costly revision overall? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each one tell me concretely what would have to give and what giving it up costs the rest of my position. I've stared at this for a week and can't tell whether the trouble is real or whether I've just made it sound that way.
