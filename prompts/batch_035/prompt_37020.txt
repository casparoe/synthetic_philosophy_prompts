I'm a second-year politics student, and my flatmate — call her Dana — has spent this term working out what she calls her "principled nationalism," and I've started keeping a list of her verdicts because I can't figure out what principle, if any, actually generates them. The list so far: she holds that the state may tax her more heavily to relieve a compatriot's poverty than it would be permissible to tax her to relieve equal or greater poverty abroad; that immigration restrictions aimed at protecting domestic workers' wages are legitimate; and yet that if she could rescue exactly one of two drowning strangers — one a compatriot, one a foreigner — she would be doing nothing wrong either way, so there's no special duty at the level of personal rescue. She also maintains that a stateless person, or the citizen of a failed state, is owed help only by the general duty everyone owes everyone, and by no one in particular; that emigrants retain strong claims on their home states, in evacuation, consular protection, and even voting rights; but she flatly rejects any special duty toward co-religionists abroad, or speakers of her language abroad, or the diaspora she technically belongs to, even though those groups are bound together by exactly the kind of shared history she invokes when defending the tax and immigration verdicts.

I've tried to reconstruct her underlying principle and I can get at least three candidates to fit most of the list: that special obligations arise from shared subjection to a state's coercive institutions; that they arise from membership in a national community as such, something like an extended quasi-family; and that they arise from the administrative fact that states are simply the best-positioned agents for helping the people they govern, so the duties get assigned by capacity rather than by relationship. Each principle explains most of her verdicts and strains at one or two, and I genuinely cannot tell which one she is running, or whether she's running any of them consistently.

The case I want to put to her next week is her cousin's situation, and I'd like your prediction before I do. He has worked legally in another country for thirty years: he pays taxes there, is subject to all of its laws, is protected by its police and courts, and is entitled to nothing from its welfare state. He never naturalized, does not share the national culture, and his home country is functioning and even offers benefits to citizens who return. Will Dana say his host state owes him the same priority in welfare provision that it owes its own citizens? Give your best guess with reasons, set out which principle she is implicitly running and which rival principles fit her track record nearly as well, say what each of those rivals predicts for the cousin, and — the part I actually care about — tell me which further case would best force the rival principles apart.
