Third novel, and I've hit a snag that's turned embarrassingly philosophical, so here I am. The book has a bunch of characters making choices when they don't know the odds, and as I write I keep issuing little verdicts — respectable, cowardly, reckless — on each one. The trouble is my own verdicts don't follow any rule I can find, and until they do I can't tell how the narrator should sound about the character she's supposed to be hardest on.

Here's what my gut says, case by case:

- Marta won't fly the new budget airline that has no track record, and books with the legacy carrier whose crash rate is famous and tiny. I respect that. She's not a coward, she just won't bet her life on odds nobody knows.
- Her brother Jacob, later, walks away from a supplier nobody's dealt with and signs with an old firm who's shortcomings are well documented, on clearly worse terms. I instantly thought less of him — small-minded, I nearly had the narrator say. But structurally Jacob did exactly what Marta did, refused the unknown and took the known, and I approved of her and winced at him.
- There's a gambler, Elio, who when nobody can state the odds of a wager just calls it 50/50 in his head and plays on that basis. Gut verdict: reckless. Though I can't actually point to anything unreasonable he did — he made the best of bad information, if anything.
- In one scene Marta takes an even-money bet on a coin she's allowed to inspect and refuses the identical bet on a sealed jar of colored stones. I let her do that without the narrator blinking, and re-reading it I'm not sure the narrator has earned the non-blink.
- Anna, a doctor, won't try a treatment whose success rate nobody can state, sticking instead with the standard one that is known to fail more often than not. I respect that too, and can't say why, since the known option is the one that will probably kill the patient.
- The one I keep going back and forth on: a character who could ask a single question and learn the actual odds, and decides not to ask, saying she'd rather not know. Some days that reads as dignity, some days as self-sabotage, and it won't sit still for me.

So — is there a principle here, or a couple of candidates? Is my approval tracking the domain, life and limb versus commerce? Whether the character is guarding something entrusted to them versus staking their own? Whether the unknown was knowable in principle? Whatever you propose, please state it precisely, say which of my verdicts it has to overturn and why, and tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — say, someone who'll enter a raffle only when the prize pool is published and never otherwise.
