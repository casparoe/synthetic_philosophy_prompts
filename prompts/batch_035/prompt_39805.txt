My roommate has been on a big antinatalism kick and thinks he's found a way to weaponize Benatar's asymmetry against my grandma, of all people. I can't find the flaw and it's bugging me. Here's the setup: I'm willing to grant him all four of the asymmetry's claims, since he insists each one is just common sense — (a) present pain is bad, (b) present pleasure is good, (c) absent pain is good even when there's nobody around to enjoy the absence, and (d) absent pleasure isn't bad unless there's somebody who's deprived of it. The never-born can't be deprived of anything, so their missing pleasures don't count against never existing, but their avoided pains count in favor. Fine, that's the asymmetry. Now the grandma part:

1. Grandma's future contains some pain — arthritis, grief, whatever — even though on balance her remaining life is happy.
2. If she dies tonight, painlessly in her sleep, then all of that future pain is absent, and by (c) absent pain is good even with nobody around to enjoy the absence.
3. Her future pleasures would also be absent, but by (d) that's not bad, because after she's dead there's nobody who's deprived — dead people, like the never-born, don't miss anything.
4. So dying tonight is better for her than her happy future is, and the same goes for anyone whose future holds any pain at all, which is everyone.
5. Therefore a painless death benefits every person alive, including grandma.

So he says the asymmetry that's supposed to show coming into existence is a harm also shows staying in existence is a harm, and if you accept the package you're commited to thinking we'd be doing grandma a favor. That can't be right, and he knows I think that, which is exactly why he keeps bringing it up at dinner. Something about how "deprived" is being used feels slippery to me, but I can't pin down where, and I don't want to just stomp my foot and insist death is bad for her, since that's the very thing he's arguing about. For what it's worth, I'd rather give up one of the four asymmetry claims than accept the grandma conclusion — but he claims all four are undeniable.

Where exactly does the reasoning slip, and what does fixing it cost? In particular: whatever you say to block the grandma argument, does it also block the original argument about the never-born? Because if blocking one automatically blocks both, that looks like trouble for my roommate, and I'd like to know whether to point it out or whether he has some principled way to treat the two cases differently.
