I've been recording my gut verdicts, case by case, on when inference to the best explanation actually warrants belief, and I'd like to know whether they add up to a coherent principle or whether some of them have to go.

The detective case: four independent traces — mud of a distinctive composition in the hallway, a missing spare key, the alarm silenced from inside, a neighbor's sighting at the right hour — and exactly one suspect whose presence that evening would explain all four jointly. Inferring he was in the house: clearly warranted.

Neptune: irregularities in the orbit of Uranus, and one hypothesis — an unseen planet of a certain mass at a calculable position — explains them and predicts where to point a telescope; the telescope is pointed, and the planet is there. Warranted, and I'd say more strongly than the detective's.

The quack: a diagnostic framework under which fatigue, insomnia, weight loss, and weight gain all confirm the same underlying imbalance, so that whatever the patient reports was to be expected. Not warranted — and not despite its fitting everything, but because of it.

The conspiracy: a hypothesis that ties together dozens of otherwise unrelated events, is simple to state, fits every datum including the absence of evidence, and yet strikes me as not merely unwarranted but absurd. Not warranted.

Ptolemy: epicycles accommodated the astronomical observations superbly and were the best explanation available for centuries, and they were false. Here I can't settle my own verdict: reasoning as they did then seems to have been warranted, and yet I'm pulled toward saying that what they inferred — the truth of the system — was never warranted at all. Whether my verdict should be indexed to the inquirer's evidence or to how things actually turned out, I genuinely don't know.

Underdetermination: two hypotheses about which machine on the line produced the defective part, each explaining the evidence about equally well. I suspend judgment rather than pick, even though either explanation is decent.

Now the pairs I can't reconcile. The detective and the conspiracy are structurally alike — each is the one simple hypothesis that explains everything — yet my verdicts are opposite, and simplicity can't be the separator, since the conspiracy is the simpler story. And the quack verdict suggests that explaining everything well can itself be a defect, while the Neptune verdict suggests that explaining everything well is the paradigm of success.

So: is there one principle — perhaps something about how far the best explanation outclasses its rivals, or about which hypotheses would have explained the data whichever way it came out — that captures all of these? State it precisely, say which clause earns which verdict, apply it to a case I haven't listed (dark matter strikes me as structurally like Neptune, and yet I feel much less sure, so tell me whether that hesitation is principled or mere prejudice), and be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot save. And I'd like to know whether the principle itself can be defended by inference to the best explanation without going in a circle.
