I TA a grad seminar mixing ML and philosophy of mind, and every discussion of whether RL agents can suffer ends the same way: half the room says reward is literally valence -- if the agent's policy works to avoid a signal, that signal is its suffering -- and half says it's just a number in a tensor, and nobody notices they're answering different questions. I want a decision-tree handout to sort people properly. The tree should start from something nearly everyone in the room accepts (a trained agent consumes a scalar signal, and its policy is shaped to get more of some values of it and less of others) and branch on questions a student can answer by thinking about concrete cases, not by knowing the position names. Branches I care about, roughly: whether mere approach/avoidance is enough for valence, or whether you also need the agent to represent that things are going badly for it; what to do with negative reward that mostly matters during training, since a well-trained policy then avoids those states and never takes the hit again; whether nociception-style machinery or an evolutionary history of bodily defense is required; and whether the pain-vs-suffering distinction (raw unpleasantness vs something like distress) does any work here. Leaves should include at least the strong functionalist reward-is-valence view, a weaker functionalism where valence is about internal organization but reward specifically isn't it, a higher-order view requiring self-monitoring of one's own states, a biological/embodied view that suffering needs bodily-defense machinery, and the deflationary no-fact-of-the-matter option. Around 5 or 6 branch nodes and 6-9 leaves. Please build it from the logical space first (which combinations of branch answers even hang together), then locate the actual positions in it, and note any coherent-but-unoccupied combos -- saying for each whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Also flag any two leaves the tree can't really seperate; I'd rather know that than have the handout pretend otherwise. No citations or references needed, since the stduents won't be able to look anything up.
