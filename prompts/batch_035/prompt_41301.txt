I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background beyond a few pop-philosophy podcasts, and I recently came across a blog post (on a general-audience science and technology site) that introduced Quine's indeterminacy of translation. It went something like this: Quine imagined a field linguist trying to translate the language of a community with no shared history or bilingual interpreters. A native speaker sees a rabbit run past and says a word we might write as "gavagai", and the linguist naturally writes down "rabbit". But the post argued that this is a guess rather than a discovery, because the word could equally mean undetached rabbit part, rabbit stage, or any number of other things, and every observation the linguist could ever make would be consistent with all of these hypotheses. The post said the linguist could just point at a rabbit to settle it, but claimed Quine showed that even this fails, since pointing is itself just another piece of behaviour that needs interpreting. It concluded that there is literally no fact of the matter about what "gavagai" means -- that meaning is not something words have independently of a particular translation manual, so any translation we produce is ultimately an arbitrary convention rather than something that can be correct or incorrect. As a framing device, the post compared this to how two large language models trained on the same corpus can end up with different internal representations that both predict the same sentences, suggesting that underneath the observable use of language there may be nothing determinate to be right about at all.

Something about the article felt slightly too tidy, though I can't put my finger on what. Is this a fair summary of Quine? In particular, I'd like to know: (a) is the gavagai example being used the way Quine used it, and does the pointing response really fail for the reason given; (b) is the claim that there is "no fact of the matter" the actual thesis, or has the article upgraded something more modest (perhaps something about the limits of what evidence can establish); and (c) does the conclusion that translation is "ultimately arbitrary" follow from what Quine argued, or is that an overstatement that Quine himself would have resisted -- for instance, is he saying all translation manuals are equally good, or something more careful? Please separate what a Quinean would accept, what is a common simplification or conflation (I've read there may be several distinct theses bundled together here -- translation versus reference, and something about underdetermination of physical theory), and what is simply a misreading. I'd also appreciate your view on whether the machine-learning analogy at the end illuminates Quine's point or distorts it. Please write your answer in British English, and assume I have no background in formal philosophy -- explain any technical vocabulary you use.
