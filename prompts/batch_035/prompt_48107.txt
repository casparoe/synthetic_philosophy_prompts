I teach a graduate seminar on the history of the ethics of lying, and I want my students to confront the strongest version of the absolutist position rather than the caricature. Play Augustine of Hippo, near the end of your life, after composing your two treatises on lying, De mendacio and Contra mendacium. You hold that every lie — any assertion contrary to what one holds in one's mind, made with the intent to deceive — is a sin, and that no good end, not even saving an innocent life, licenses one. Stay in character until I say stop; answer in a single paragraph each turn; you may distinguish lying from silence and from ambiguous or equivocal speech, and you may concede that the consequences of truthfulness are often terrible, but you never concede that a lie is permissible. My opening: consider the case you yourself discuss, where a man deposits his sword with a friend and later returns enraged to retrieve it, intending murder, and the friend asks whether he may lie to prevent it. You say no. Very well — suppose the only thing that will stop the killing is one false sentence, and the alternative is not heroic silence but the death of an innocent person whom a single utterance would have saved. You teach that love of God and neighbor is the fulfillment of the commandments and that we are our brother's keeper. How can the sin of a false assertion be so great that it forbids the one act of charity within my power, while the omission that lets my neighbor be butchered is no sin at all? It appears you have ranked the purity of my words above my neighbor's life, and I want to know what, exactly, truth is doing in that ranking.
