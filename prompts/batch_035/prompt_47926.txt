I work on defense policy and citizenship in Europe, and I keep returning to a pattern in the conscript-based armed-neutral states that I cannot make sense of as a coherent practice. I would like an analysis of the implicit theory — you tell me which theory, if any, this presupposes.

The pattern, concretely. Switzerland and Finland both maintain peacetime conscription as the backbone of territorial defense, but the obligation is legally binding on men only, under criminal sanction, while women may volunteer for the same training and serve under the same conditions if they do. Norway and Sweden have recently gone the other way: Norway extended conscription to women in 2015, and Sweden, having suspended conscription in 2010 in favor of an all-volunteer force, brought it back in 2017 on a gender-neutral basis. Meanwhile, the official self-understanding of all these militias is universal in form — the citizen-soldier, the "people's army," the claim that a militia force is legitimate precisely because the burden falls on the whole people rather than on professionals or mercenaries. Switzerland sharpens the puzzle. It conceded federal political rights to women only in 1971, more than a century after establishing them for men, and has since universalized every political right by sex; yet the one duty the state claims the power to command directly — the duty to fight — is still assigned by sex at birth, and the same electorate votes decisively to keep conscription itself whenever abolition is put to a vote.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of citizenship and obligation this pattern rests on, and answer three nested questions. First, does compulsory military service presuppose an exchange theory of citizenship (full membership is purchased with duties, so those not liable to serve are at best partial citizens) or a civic-republican one (the duty flows from membership itself and is partly constitutive of it)? The gender asymmetry seems to force the issue: if the duty flows from citizenship as such, then exempting half the citizens while granting them full rights suggests the polity actually believes the duty tracks something other than membership — but what, and could that belief be stated publicly without collapsing the militia's legitimating rhetoric? Second, is the women's exemption really a presupposition about women at all, or merely the residue of a manpower calculation — the state claims the bodies it believes it needs — in which case the practice is philosophically underdetermined and several rival theories would license it equally well? Third, how should the Norwegian and Swedish reforms be read: as evidence that the older systems did presuppose a sex-based theory of the duty and were therefore corrected, or as evidence that the same civic theory was there all along and was merely misapplied in scope?

Throughout, distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely sits comfortably with, and where it is genuinely underdetermined; and say plainly whether the parts of the practice presuppose commitments that can all be true at once.

End with a final line of exactly the form \answer{...}, where ... is one of three labels: CONSISTENT, if you find a single implicit theory that underwrites the entire pattern, gender asymmetry included; UNDERDETERMINED, if no one theory is required and you should name the rivals that would all license the pattern; INCONSISTENT, if the parts presuppose commitments that cannot all be true together. The argument in the body must do the work — the label should follow from it, not substitute for it.
