I edit a mid-tier philosophy journal — think generalist, maybe 400 submissions a year, no paid staff, just me, an associate editor, and a book review editor. Our reviewer pool is collapsing. Acceptance rates on review requests have fallen off a cliff over the last few years, and the same twenty or so saints are carrying the load, which means they're burning out too. Our editorial board meets next month and I have to circulate a written recommendation beforehand. The two live proposals: (a) a hard quota — everyone who publishes in the journal owes, say, one review per paper of theirs we publish, tracked in the system, with submission privileges suspended if you shirk; or (b) something softer, a credit system where reviewing earns you goodwill like fast-track treatment or waived page charges, with no sanctions at all. One board member also keeps insisting we just pay reviewers a small honorarium out of library agreements, which honestly sounds like a nightmare but might be the only thing that actually works.

The reason I'm asking you rather than just polling the board is that this feels like it instantiates the tragedy of the commons, but weirdly, and I want the memo to get the weirdness right. The resource — reviewer labor — isn't depleted by use, it's depleted by non-contribution, which makes it more like what some people call a collaboration good: the stock only exists because people chip in, and consuming it (getting your paper reviewed) doesn't take anything from anyone directly. So I'm not sure the Hardin framing even applies, and the quota proposal smells like the classic mistake of applying a cattle-pasture fix to a gift-economy problem. But the soft option has its own issue that I half-remember from Ostrom-adjacent literature and want handled carefully: whatever sanctions or norms we adopt, monitoring and enforcing them is itself a public good, so we risk a second-order free rider problem where nobody wants to be the enforcer. And there's a reputational currency running through all this — people review partly to be seen reviewing, and if we formalize it too hard we might crowd out the informal goodwill that's currently doing most of the work.

So: draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the board meeting. Recommend a concrete course of action — quota, credits, honoraria, some combination, or something you think is better — with reasoning the board members who back the other options could actually engage with rather than dismiss. Give the strongest version of the losing side, especially whichever of Hardin-style coercion vs. Ostrom-style graduated sanctions you end up rejecting. And please write it as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or anything — the board is mostly old-fashioned and I want it to read like a letter, maybe 700-900 words, the sort of thing someone could read over coffee and come to the meeting with an opinion about.
