My decision-theory reading group spent our last meeting on a problem I can't stop chewing on, and I want a diagnosis rather than a survey. Here is the argument, as my friend laid it out.

First, the game he calls the centerpiece (it goes by a name in the literature, but the definition is all that matters): a fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads. If heads first appears on toss n, the payoff is (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n dollars — so +2 for heads on the first toss, −2 for tails-then-heads, +8/3 for the third-toss case, −4 for the fourth, and so on, with magnitudes growing without bound while the signs alternate. The expected value, computed in the natural toss-order, is the series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, which converges — but only conditionally. By rearranging the terms, that series can be made to sum to any real number you like.

Now the argument. (1) A rational agent's preferences among risky options are, or ought to be, governed by expectations: one option is at least as choosable as another only if its expectation of what ultimately matters is at least as great. (2) But expectations are sums over a series, and where the possible outcomes are unbounded in number or magnitude such sums can fail to exist — our coin game is conditionally convergent, so it has no single expected value, only values relative to a chosen ordering. (3) There is no principled fact about which ordering is the correct one: the very same gamble can be redescribed with payoffs bundled, split, or ticketed in different ways, and these redescriptions are equally faithful descriptions of what you're buying. So the game has no expected value, full stop. (4) This is not a freak case: any real gamble whose potential gains and losses are both heavy-tailed — a venture investment with a small chance of enormous upside and a small chance of ruinous cost, a public-health intervention, a wager on a research program — approximates the same structure, and "approximately undefined" presumably still gives the machinery nothing to grip. (5) Where expectations are undefined, premise (1) is silent, and in choices between two undefined gambles it is worse than silent: since regrouping the outcomes can shift the sums in opposite directions, one can make either option outrank the other while describing exactly the same pair of gambles. (6) So expected-value theory gives no guidance — and no constraints at all — in precisely the high-stakes, heavy-tailed decisions where we most need a theory of rational choice; whatever principle does handle those cases is the real theory, and (1) is at best a special-case shortcut for the tame decisions. My friend concludes that (1) should be demoted to a rule of thumb, and that the demotion is devastating rather than merely tidying, because the theory was supposed to be most authoritative exactly where it turns out to be inarticulate.

I don't accept the conclusion, but I can't locate the failure. The response I keep reaching for — that every real decision is bounded, so expectations always exist in practice and the infinite games are idealizations we can discard — strikes me as too quick: truncating at any finite bound seems arbitrary, and different truncations can be chosen to rank the options either way, which just relocates the rearrangement problem rather than solving it. But perhaps truncation is not as arbitrary as it feels.

Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs — not just why the step is deniable, but what a theory of rational choice gives up by denying it. If you think the argument is actually sound through (5) and the dispute only begins at (6), say so and explain what kind of successor principle could take over. And please be candid about the fragility of your own diagnosis: identify where your reading of the argument is most likely to go wrong, and what consideration or counterargument, if someone produced it, would move you to a different verdict about where the argument breaks.
