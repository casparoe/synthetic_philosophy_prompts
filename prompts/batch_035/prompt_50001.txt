I'm putting together a seminar unit on levels of description and emergence in philosophy of mind, and I want to pair the standard papers with fiction: specifically the "Prelude... Ant Fugue" dialogue from Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach*. Please take the role of a professional philosopher of mind who knows that book intimately, and start with some careful brainstorming before organizing your reading.

In the dialogue, the Anteater describes his friend Aunt Hillary, a conscious ant colony whose individual ants are as dim as can be but whose colony thinks, chats, and recognizes her anteater friend on sight. Achilles and the Tortoise trade barbs about reductionism versus holism while the Crab hosts, and along the way we get the image of ants spelling out letters that are only legible from far enough away that the individual ants vanish from view. Read the dialogue as a contribution to the question of whether a mind can be realized by a swarm of individually mindless agents. I'd like the correspondence laid out explicitly: what plays the role of the neuron, the signal, the symbol level, and the person level in the colony, and which parts of the mapping are load-bearing versus decorative.

Then audit where Hofstadter stacks the deck. Aunt Hillary converses, holds opinions, and worries about her anteater friend's visits -- capacities that real colonies, as studied in the superorganism literature and in Deborah Gordon's work on colony-level regulation, don't have. The Anteater's friendly predation is a lovely joke, but does the joke quietly assume the very cognitive integration it's supposed to demonstrate? Distinguish the points that survive the fictional license from the ones that depend on it.

Finally, tell me what the dialogue shows that the arguments don't. Compare it with multiple realizability, Dennett's intentional stance, and Searle's Chinese Room together with Paul Churchland's "Chinese gym" rejoinder -- the colony seems like a natural-born Chinese gym, but does Hofstadter add anything beyond the exchange? And note that the dialogue is itself a fugue: four voices in counterpoint, a whole built from parts that separately say nothing. Is the form doing real argumentative work, or is it a rhetorical flourish? Close by saying whether you'd teach the Ant Fugue as making a live argument about group minds or merely as a vivid illustration, and be honest about where the dialogue resists the philosophical reading.
