In my school's philosophy club we have been reading about Strawson's idea that the reactive attitudes, like resentment and gratitude, are what make sense of holding people responsible rather than the other way around. During the discussion, another student argued that we should stop blaming psychopaths, on the grounds that they are morally colorblind: just as someone who cannot see red is not blamed for mismatching their clothes, a psychopath who cannot perceive moral reasons should not be blamed for violating them. She said the analogy is exact — the reasons are the color, remorse and guilt are the faculty that picks them up, and blame presupposes a working faculty that the psychopath simply lacks. I found this persuasive in the moment, but now I suspect the comparison is doing more work than it can carry. Could you set out the full mapping — what corresponds to the stimulus, the perceptual faculty, and the resulting judgment, and what would even count as the corrective lens in the moral case? Then explain where the analogy breaks down: in particular, whether moral reasons are the kind of thing that is passively perceived or whether, on something like the Strawsonian picture, our reactive attitudes partly constitute what it is for someone to be a moral agent at all. And after the disanalogies are on the table, tell me whether her conclusion — that blaming psychopaths is unfair in the way blaming the colorblind would be — still has independent support, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. If there is a better comparison for the psychopath's condition, I would like to hear it.
