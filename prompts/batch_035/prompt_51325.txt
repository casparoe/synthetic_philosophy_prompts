I want a thought experiment built to spec, about Simpson's paradox, but running in the direction nobody talks about.

You know the standard presentations: the kidney stone one (small vs. large stones, treatment A vs. B) and the Berkeley admissions thing. In both of those, the disaggregated numbers are the ones you should trust and the aggregate is the trap. I want the opposite: build me a case where a Simpson's reversal is sitting right there in the data — treatment B looks better within every subgroup — but the subgroup analysis is the trap, and the aggregate is the number you should act on.

My hunch is the way to get this is to make the grouping variable something the treatment itself influences, so that splitting on it means conditioning on a consequence of the thing you're evaluating. So that's a hard constraint: the subgroups must be defined by something that happens after, and partly because of, the treatment — not a pre-existing trait like stone size or patient sex. Realistic setting please: a hospital, a workplace, a sports team, whatever — no abstract urns. And the scenario itself should be one paragraph, with actual small counts in it, not percentages, that I can check by hand: within each group B beats A, but the pooled table favors A. Both reversals should fall out of the numbers, not be asserted in the prose.

After the scenario: (1) show the check, both tables, so I can verify the arithmetic; (2) explain in plain terms why conditioning on a post-treatment variable misleads here; (3) tell me which feature of the case is doing the work; (4) say what happens if I rewrite the case so the grouping variable is fixed before treatment — does it collapse back into the standard kidney-stones kind of case, where dissaggregating is the right call?

I'm planning to use this to teach the point that "disaggregate your data" isn't a universal fix, so please don't stipulate the conclusion inside the scenario — the aggregate has to be right because of how the causal story is set up, not because the narrator says so. And finish with how someone who thinks you should always split your data would push back on the case, and whether their reply is just "control for more stuff" — and if it is, whether that actually dodges the point or concedes it.
