A friend in my aesthetics reading group has built an argument whose conclusion he happily accepts and I cannot, though I have so far failed to locate the failure. The argument runs:

(1) Testimonial justification is subject-matter blind: you are justified in believing p on someone's say-so whenever the speaker is likely to believe p only if p is true, regardless of what p is about. (2) A good critic is likely to believe a work is beautiful only if it is beautiful, and the same holds for the critic's beliefs about brushwork, structure, pacing, emotional arc, and every other aesthetic property of the work. (3) So you can be justified, on a critic's say-so, in believing any aesthetic claim about a work. (4) There is no principled limit on how much testimony of this kind you could absorb: the descriptions can be as detailed and as reliable as you like, covering every aesthetic property the work has. (5) So for any level of justification that viewing the work yourself could give you for your aesthetic beliefs, there is a body of testimony that would give you the same level of justification for the same beliefs. (6) Then someone who had absorbed such a body of testimony about a painting they had never laid eyes on could know everything there is to know about that painting's aesthetic character without ever seeing it. (7) That is absurd: a person who has never seen the painting does not know everything about its aesthetic character, however much they have read. (8) So something above has to give.

I would very much like to keep (1) — the thought that testimonial justification does not change its nature with the topic, which seems right for weather reports, for mathematical testimony, for moral testimony — and I cannot accept (6). My friend, for his part, accepts (6) without distress, and notes that the same argument would let someone who has never tasted wine know everything about how a particular vintage tastes, which he also accepts, which makes me suspect the whole thing proves too much. My own suspicion is that the trouble lies between (5) and (6), somewhere in the neighborhood of the phrase "everything there is to know," but I cannot articulate what is going wrong there, if anything is.

Please answer in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion: first, the premise or step you reject; second, why that premise or step looked fine and what exactly goes wrong in it; third, what rejecting it costs, including any parallel commitments elsewhere that rejecting it forces me to give up; fourth, the cheapest alternative rejection if I insist on keeping (1), and the price of that one; fifth, what, if anything, my friend's argument gets right, or whether the felt absurdity of (7) is mere aesthetic snobbery.
