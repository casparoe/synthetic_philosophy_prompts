I'm taking a philosophy of economics course this semester and we just spent two weeks on Keynes's Treatise on Probability, and it kind of broke my brain in a good way. Mostly the idea that probabilities can be non-numerical — where you can honestly say one argument is more probable than another but there's no number for either of them and no way to say by how much — plus the weight of evidence thing, where two arguments can leave you at the same probability while one is backed by way more relevant evidence than the other. And then later Keynes (in the stuff on uncertainty vs risk) basically saying that for a lot of real decisions there's no scientific basis for any calculable probability at all, we just don't know.

Here's why I care. I sit on our student union's grants committee. Every week we get like 15-20 funding applications from clubs and event organizers — travel money, equipment, a conference, a one-off event, bailouts for things that went over budget — and we have to decide how much each gets. Our current process is basically vibes plus a spreadsheet. The treasurer keeps pushing us toward expected-value scoring: estimate expected attendance, estimate some impact score, multiply, rank, fund from the top until the money runs out. After the Keynes readings something about that feels wrong to me, but I can't articulate it well enough to actually argue for anything better.

So: can you turn Keynes's distinctions into a procedure our committee could actually run? Constraints, to be realistic: we have about ten minutes per application, most of the other members haven't read any of this and won't, the treasurer has final sign-off on anything over 1,000 (units whatever, euros-ish), and the committee votes when we disagree. I'm imagining something like: questions to ask in order, what counts as an answer good enough to move to the next step, where the process should stop and hand things to a straight vote because it's genuinely a judgment call, and — this is the part I care most about — where it should force us to admit that the EV spreadsheet is pretending to a precision we don't have, and say what we should do instead of a number in those cases (rank-orderings? confidence labels? just "this one is unrankable against that one"?).

Then run it on three applications like the ones we actually get: (a) a club requesting bus rental for a trip that may or may not fill, with no real track record either way; (b) a first-time event here that the same format has run at three other universities, where it flopped at two and did well at one; (c) an established club with a genuinely good track record asking for something twice as big as anything they've run before.

Two things I need you to be honest about. First, where does Keynes just not give a committee like ours anything usable — which of his distinctions collapse or become decoration once you only have ten minutes and nobody's read the book. Second, include at least one case where your procedure gives a different answer than the treasurer's spreadsheet, including one where — if I'm being honest — I'd want the spreadsheet's answer, and tell me which answer should actually win and why.
