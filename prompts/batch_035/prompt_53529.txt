I'm a PhD student writing a term paper on Moore's proof of an external world, and I've hit a wall trying to articulate, in a way I actually find convincing, why the proof is supposed to fail. As you likely know, Moore's strategy was to stand up in front of an audience, hold up his hands, and infer along roughly these lines: here is one hand, and here is another; hands are external things existing independently of my mind; therefore, there is an external world. Most epistemologists concede that the argument is valid and that each premise is more certain, for Moore, than any premise of the skeptic's argument — and yet almost nobody feels that the proof really answers skepticism. I want to understand the contemporary diagnosis of this.

My supervisor keeps telling me the key is the distinction between the closure of warrant and the transmission of warrant, but her explanations in passing have been too compressed for me to get a proper grip on. From what I gather, the thought is roughly this: closure says that if you have warrant for p and you competently deduce q from p, you thereby have warrant for q; transmission is stronger, requiring that the warrant you had for p can be used to give you new warrant for q, one you did not already need. The alleged defect in Moore's proof is then not validity but transmission failure: warrant for the premise that I am perceiving a hand is only available to me if I already have some warrant for the conclusion that there are external things, so the proof cannot extend warrant to that conclusion — it presupposes the very thing it is meant to establish, not logically but epistemically.

Please explain, at an intuitive level rather than a fully formal one, why that diagnosis is supposed to work. In particular: why does the failure of transmission not simply follow from the failure of closure, or vice versa — what is a case in which warrant is closed under competent deduction but fails to transmit? And what exactly is doing the work in the diagnosis: is it that the skeptic can demand warrant for the second premise's link to perception, or something about the dialectical role of the proof as a response to doubt? If you can give me a clean illustrative example of transmission failure from outside the external-world debate entirely — a case in some other domain where an argument is perfectly sound but cannot extend warrant to its conclusion because warrant for a premise presupposes warrant for that conclusion — that would help me enormously, since I suspect my paper's reviewers will want the notion applied more widely than Moore's hands.
