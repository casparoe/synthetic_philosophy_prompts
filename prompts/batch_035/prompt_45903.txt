I am a high school student working on a final project for an elective called "Can History Be a Science?", and one of the options is to write a satirical document instead of an essay. I would like a piece satirizing cliodynamics, and I would like to request it as follows.

Please write a satirical press release, roughly 600 words, issued by a fictional government body called the National Bureau of Cliodynamic Standards, announcing a new subscription service called the Secular Outlook. The voice should be flat, procedural, and deadpan — the register of a central-bank communiqué or a statistical agency's product bulletin — and the satire should target the ambition of cliodynamics, associated with Peter Turchin, to turn history into a quantitative, predictive science. The humor must come from the actual content of structural-demographic theory and the actual criticisms of it, so that someone who knows the field finds the jokes fair rather than generic "history is hard" gags. Concretely, please build jokes out of the following real features and objections, choosing at least four of them: elite overproduction (the theory's claim that societies generate more credentialed aspirants than there are elite positions to absorb); popular immiseration and declining real wages relative to elite incomes; state fiscal distress as a third converging pressure; the idea that the relevant cycles run on roughly generational timescales, so the Bureau must offer forecast horizons measured in decades while its competitors issue quarterly guidance; the criticism that the theory can retrodict anything after the fact, so its model performs superbly on data it has already seen; and the criticism that its forecasts of crisis say roughly when pressures peak but not what will happen, letting the Bureau claim vindication for nearly any alarming news. Include at least one passage a critic of cliodynamics would consider a fair hit — for example, a footnote in which the Bureau thanks an unnamed dynasty for its cooperation with retrospective data collection, or a disclaimer that confidence intervals are widest exactly when readers need them most. All officials, subscribers, and commentators in the piece should be invented; do not quote or attribute anything to real living people. Please stay in the deadpan register throughout, without winking at the reader.

After the press release, please add a short postscript of about 150 words, separated from the satire and written straight. In it, state plainly where the piece takes liberties with what structural-dememonic theory actually claims — for instance, whether Turchin's program issues point forecasts at all, or rather conditional scenarios — and identify which joke rests on the most contested reading of the theory. Finally, say what would change your mind about the piece's implicit verdict that cliodynamics is overconfident: name the kind of evidence, such as a prediction registered in advance and later borne out in its specified terms, that would make the satirical framing unfair enough that you would want to revise it, and note where your reasoning in the postscript is most likely to go wrong. If you are unsure whether a given feature of the theory is real or contested, flag that uncertainty in the postscript rather than papering over it.
