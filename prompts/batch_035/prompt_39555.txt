I'm a retired chemist who's gotten really into philosophy over the last few years, mostly through arguing with my neighbor Dieter, a retired electrical engineer, and my niece Sam, who's doing a masters in philosophy. Last week they got into it over coffee about dispositions and I honestly couldnt tell who was winning.

It started with an example I'd read somewhere: a live wire hooked up to a device that instantly cuts the current the moment anyone touches the wire, so it would never actually shock you. Sam said the wire is still live -- being live is about its internal state, its voltage relative to its surroundings, and the gadget doesn't change any of that, it just blocks the manifestation. Dieter said that's mystical: a property you can never detect, not even in principle, isn't a property the wire has; if nothing would ever happen when you touch it, "live" is just the wrong word. Sam countered that Dieter's test makes dispositions depend on the surroundings in a crazy way -- the very same wire, moved from the lab bench to a spot next to the guardian device, would flip from live to not-live and back, and real properties shouldn't flip like that depending on where you put a thing. Dieter answered that properties flip with environment all the time, a chunk of iron is unmagnetized in one field and magnetized in another, so that's no objection; and anyway her view makes dispositional talk unfalsifiable, since every time the glass doesn't break you get to shrug and say the conditions weren't right. Sam said that's just what "other things equal" means, and it's perfectly testable -- the glass packed in foam is still fragile, you find that out by unpacking it. Dieter said if you have to unpack it first, then the packing matters to whether it's fragile, which was his whole point. Sam then tried a distinction: a device that catches the glass before it hits the floor is different from a device that somehow hardens the glass the instant it's dropped -- one shields the fragility and the other removes it, so only the second changes what the glass is. Dieter said you can't tell the two devices apart from the outside -- both glasses never break -- so the difference can't be doing any real work.

After an hour of this they both turned to me, since I'd foolishly admitted to knowing some philosophy now. Can you go through their exchange round by round and say who's right at each step -- not just who wins overall -- and also what, if anything, they're both missing?
