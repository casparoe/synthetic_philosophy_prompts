Write a dialogue between a compatibilist in the mold of Daniel Dennett and a free-will skeptic in the mold of Gregg Caruso, who have been asked to co-author a short statement for a sentencing-reform commission on the role of just deserts in punishment. They agree that retributivism as actually practiced is a disaster and that punishment should be substantially forward-looking, but they disagree about whether the idea of basic desert — offenders genuinely deserving suffering for past wrongdoing — can be salvaged, and whether reform needs it. The skeptic wants to replace desert with a public-health model on the analogy of quarantine: justified strictly by protection and rehabilitation, never by giving offenders what is owed to them. The compatibilist suspects the quarantine analogy breaks down for serious crimes, where refusing to say the offender deserves anything starts to sound like a euphemism, and worries that denying desert outright erodes the self-authorship and accountability that rehabilitation itself presupposes. Have them press each other on the real fault lines: whether compatibilist desert is desert worth wanting or a change of subject, whether the skeptic can hold people responsible in any robust sense while denying they ever deserve blame, and whether their shared policy conclusions can really be neutral between their justifications or only appear to be. Neither should be a strawman, neither should concede easily, and the dialogue should end with the joint statement stalled over one specific sentence they cannot agree to sign.
