I've been reading up on the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem (the one saying that any voting rule with 3+ candidates is either dictatorial, fails to elect some candidate in some situation, or is manipulable — meaning at least one voter can sometimes benefit by lying about their preferences). I get the statement fine but not really the premise structure, and I learn best from concrete cases, so heres what I want you to build.

Construct a realistic scenario — roommates picking a movie, a small hiring committee, whatever feels natural, no abstract voters A/B/C — that runs the whole theorem through. Exactly three alternatives, and a specific named rule (plurality, Borda, your pick). Then do three things:

1. Pick one voter and one preference profile where that voter gains by misrepresenting their preferences under your rule. Show the before and after: what the rule picks when everyone is honest, what it picks once this person lies, and confirm the lie strictly helps them. No ties, no coin flips — make it clean.

2. Now the interesting bit. The theorem has escape hatches: drop to two alternatives, make the rule a dictatorship, make it not "onto" (some candidate can never win no matter what), or restrict the domain of allowed preferences. Pick one of these — I'd suggest the domain restriction, but its your call — and make the smallest tweak to your scenario that activates it. E.g. if you go with domain restriction, give the roommates' tastes a structure (everyone orders the options along a single axis) such that, among only those preference profiles, some rule becomes strategy-proof, and show concretely why a lie no longer pays for anyone whose preferences fit the structure.

3. Finally, be honest with me about the engineering: which premise of the theorem was doing the work in your original manipulable case, what exactly changed when you tweaked it, and whether the two-alternative escape would have been easier or harder to demo in the same scenario. Also, is there something sneaky about the domain-restriction escape — like, in a real friend group, is "their preferences just happen to have the right structure" something you could ever actually rely on, or does the strategy-proofness evaporate the moment one person's tastes are weird?

Constraints: no math symbols, everything in terms of the actual people and options in the story. The manipulation in part 1 has to be something a normal person would recognize as strategic voting, not a weird corner case (don't need voters with identical preferences, nothing riding on a 51-49 knife edge). And one thing that would count as cheating: don't make the liar better off only via a tiebreak that the lie tips — the gain has to be the lie itself changing the winner. Same for part 2, the strategy-proofness should come from the structure of the case, not from you just stipulating nobody wants to lie.
