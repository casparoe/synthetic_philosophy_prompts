My decision theory reading group spent last Thursday on Kavka's toxin puzzle and it spilled into the group chat over the weekend. The setup, briefly: a billionaire offers to put a million dollars in your bank account tonight if, at midnight, you intend to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon. The money is transferred at midnight based purely on the intention (say the billionaire can read intentions with perfect accuracy); whether you actually drink tomorrow is up to you. The toxin will make you very sick for a week but won't kill you or leave lasting damage. Once midnight passes and the money lands, drinking the toxin is pure downside — it's painful and gets you nothing. Kavka's point, as I understand it, is that you can't get the money by *deciding* to intend to drink, because you know now that tomorrow you'll have every reason not to, and intending to do something you know you'll have decisive reason not to do seems to require fooling yourself.

One of the guys in the group, let's call him Daniel, thinks he can turn this into a general argument about commitment, and it goes like this. (1) An intention to do A is rational only if, at the moment you'd be doing A, doing A would be rational. (2) Tomorrow afternoon, drinking the toxin is irrational — cost, no benefit — and no amount of tonight's money changes that. (3) So a fully rational agent cannot rationally intend to drink, and therefore cannot collect the million. (4) But there's nothing special about the toxin: the structure — a payoff now for intending now to do something later that will be irrational later — shows up everywhere. When you promise your landlord you'll pay rent on the 1st, when you sign the gym contract in January knowing that in February you'd rather stay in bed, when Ulysses has himself bound to the mast, when you leave your credit card at home before a night out you know you'll regret. (5) So by parity, fully rational agents can never bind themselves to anything, and every institution that runs on credible commitment — contracts, vows, treaties, the whole idea of a resolution — depends on people being irrational in just the way Kavka's agent is. (6) That's absurd. (7) Therefore (1) is false, and rational intention does not require action-time rationality. Daniel is pretty smug about this because he likes the conclusion: he thinks rationality should be about whole plans, not acts one at a time.

I have two worries and I can't resolve them myself. One is that (5) quietly equivocates — in the toxin case the payout is for the intention itself and is already in your account before you act, whereas in the rent and gym cases the payoff presumably depends on what you actually do later, or the sanctions for not doing it are what make the action rational at the time. But then Ulysses bothers me, because when he's tied to the mast he screams to be released and has no reason at the time to stay bound — the binding works precisely by removing the option. So maybe the toxin case isn't as isolated as my first worry suggests, or maybe removing an option and rationally intending against your future inclinations are doing different work in the argument. My other worry is about giving up (1), which seemed obvious to me five minutes ago: if intentions don't need to survive till a time when the intended act is rational, what stops rational agents from intending whatever is convenient right now and shrugging tomorrow? So: which step in the argument would you reject, why did that step look fine to begin with, and what does rejecting it actually cost? Also, and I mean this — if you think the argument is resting on a confused premise or that Daniel (or I) have misdescribed something, or that "rational intention" is being used in two different senses across the steps, just tell me that plainly instead of picking a step to keep the peace. I'd rather know the argument is broken than get a tidy diagnosis of where it's strongest.
