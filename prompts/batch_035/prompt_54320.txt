I've been persuaded by Browning's account of Reserve Police Battalion 101 — the Hamburg reservists who shot some fifteen hundred Jews at Józefów in 1942 — that the men were largely 'ordinary men' moved by situational pressures: conformity, deference to authority, the pull of the peer group, gradual habituation to killing, rather than the zealous, eliminationist antisemitism Goldhagen diagnosed in them. Treat that situational explanation as my position and tell me what would count against it. I want the possible counter-evidence ordered by how damaging it actually is: the minority who took the commander's offer to step out without obvious penalty, and whether their existence helps or hurts the situational reading; what the men said in postwar interrogations and how much weight self-interested testimony can carry; comparisons with other perpetrator groups — SS units, Lithuanian and Ukrainian auxiliaries, non-German collaborators — and whether the situational story's predictions about them hold up; and whatever else you think genuinely bears on the question. Please distinguish the difficulties a committed situationalist would concede are real from those only a Goldhagen-style critic would press. Finally, assess candidly whether the situational explanation has been qualified into unfalsifiability: is there anything in the historical record it could not absorb? Answer in American English.
