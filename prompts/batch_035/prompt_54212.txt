Background first so you know what I'm dealing with: I'm a retired analytical chemist -- 35 years of running a mass spectrometry lab, mostly characterizing whatever the synthetic group cooked up that week -- and since retiring I've gotten properly hooked on political philosophy. I've been working my way through the old "equality of what?" business, the argument between people who say justice is about equalizing welfare, people who say resources, people who say capabilities. Mostly I understand the positions. What I don't have a feel for is how they behave as *decision procedures*, and I've stumbled on what I think is a natural experiment for that, though I need help running it.

My neighbour Diane and I sit on the hardship fund committee for our village co-op. The co-op pays out a small fund every year to members who come and ask. Diane has never read a page of philosophy, but she hands down verdicts like a judge, and after a decade on this committee I've got a shelf of them, and they're remarkably consistent. Here's her track record:

- Frank was born with a metabolic condition and needs a special imported medical diet that costs him £150 a month. Verdict: compensate.
- Ellen, in her sixties, developed a passion for a rare tea that runs about £200 an ounce, and refuses all substitutes on the grounds that they're not the same. Verdict: don't compensate, don't even discuss it.
- Grace has been blind from birth and, honestly, is cheerfully one of the happiest people in the village -- higher spirits than anyone else I can think of. Verdict: compensate.
- Harold was injured scrambling on a cliff he knew was loose, and now has chronic pain and can't work his allotment. Verdict: no.
- Ines took the same kind of injury in a rockslide nobody could have predicted, same lasting pain, same lost plot. Verdict: compensate.

So. What I want is basically what we'd do in the lab: fit candidate models to the data, then design the discriminating experiment.

The new application is Jonas. He's got the same health as anyone, same garden plot, same share of co-op payouts as every member. But he has a severe stutter and an old burn scar on half his face, and the awkward truth is that a chunk of the village still treats him differently -- they don't sit with him at meetings, they quietly skip him in the work rota, the informal trading circle that actually makes the allotments viable just doesn't include him. His holdings are identical to everyone else's, but his real options are maybe half of what a typical member's are. Speech therapy would help somewhat, at real cost. There is no amount of money that makes the neighbours decent people.

So my questions. First, predict Diane's verdict on Jonas -- I have my own guess but I want yours. Second, lay out the candidate principles that fit her whole track record (there should be at least two; I suspect three -- something about equalizing welfare, something Dworkin-ish about equal resources with a line between bad luck and bad choices, something Sen-ish about capabilities, plus whatever hybrids you think fit). Say what each of those actually predicts on Jonas and why. Third, give me your best guess at her verdict with your reasons -- and be honest about whether the record underdetermines it. Fourth, and this is the part I actually care about most: what next case should I put in front of the committee (realistically, something a village co-op could plausibly face) that would do the best job of splitting whichever candidate rules are still standing after Jonas?

And then one last thing. In the lab, if two models kept needing epicycles to fit the new data, we'd start suspecting the whole framework. Does Jonas's case -- where the deficit seems to live partly in other people's heads -- suggest that picking ONE currency of equality is a mistake at the design stage, the way a wrong assay does? Or is that me getting out of my depth? Be blunt, you can tell I'm a tourist here, but I do know what a badly specified experiment looks like.
