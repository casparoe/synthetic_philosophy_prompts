I sit on a commission drafting the franchise rules for a newly chartered city-state, and I have been testing my gut verdicts on cases in the hope that they trace back to a single principle. Lifetime residents who never naturalized: they should vote. Citizens living abroad for two decades who retain the passport: they should not. Sixteen-year-olds who hold jobs and pay taxes: no. People who have completed prison sentences: yes. Tourists present on election day: obviously not, even though they are subject to our traffic laws the whole time they are here. Persons under guardianship for severe cognitive impairment: no. Yet I also want the assembly's deliberations to weigh the interests of future generations and of the wider region affected by our emissions, without either group casting a ballot. And I find my verdict flips on contract workers admitted for renewable five-year permits depending on whether I picture the city as a cooperative scheme among ongoing participants or as the seat of authority over everyone subject to its rules. Is there one principle — affected interests, stake in the cooperative scheme, subjection to coercion, or something else — that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? If it takes two principles, say exactly what work each does and which cases lie at their boundary, rather than stitching them together vaguely. Tell me what your preferred principle predicts for a case I have not listed, and be explicit about which of my verdicts you would overturn rather than elaborate the principle to save it. Please answer in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
