My brother was declared brain dead two years ago after an aneurysm burst, and we donated his organs. At the time I just went along with what the doctors said, but ever since I've been chewing on the whole "when is a person actually dead" question on my own — no books, no classes, just me and my gut, which is honestly how I do most of my thinking. The weird thing is my gut is pretty consistent on the normal cases and then completely falls apart on the weird ones. So here are my verdicts, and I want to know if there's one principle underneath all of them or whether I'm just kidding myself.

- Brain dead guy on a ventilator: heart still beating, skin warm, chest going up and down, then they take his organs. Dead. That's my brother, that one feels obvious even though his body looked more alive than plenty of people in the ICU.
- Guy's heart stops, they do CPR for two minutes, get him back, he's fine now. I want to say he was never dead — he was dying. But if the CPR had failed, he'd have been dead... starting at what exact moment? This one messes with me.
- PVS patient, eyes open sometimes, sleep-wake cycles, but nobody home for 15 years. Alive. Not even a little dead. I would fight anyone who said otherwise.
- Locked-in patient, fully conscious, can only blink one eye. Alive, obviously, that's not even hard — arguably more alive than me some days.
- Anencephalic baby, born with basically no upper brain, never going to have a single thought ever. Alive??? I go back and forth constantly on this one. Part of me says alive because the body does baby things on its own — breathes, digests — and part of me says there was never a person in there to begin with, so what exactly is alive?
- Brain dead pregnant woman kept on machines for 12 weeks so the baby could develop and be delivered. Dead, even though her heart beat the whole time and the fetus grew inside her. Rationally I know she was exactly as dead as my brother. But it feels different — some part of my brain refuses to file them the same.
- Guy in deep hypothermia, heart stopped cold (like fell in a lake), they rewarm him in the OR and he walks out of the hospital. Never dead. But if you describe the moment his heart stopped, it sounds identical to the CPR case.
- Donation after circulatory death: they take someone off the ventilator (who is NOT brain dead, this is someone whose own doctors said further treatment is futile), heart stops, they wait 2 to 5 minutes, then recover the organs. Some hospitals wait longer in case the heart restarts on its own, because sometimes it does. Something about "dead enough that it won't restart" bugs me — it makes dead-ness sound like it depends on how patient the surgeons feel like being that day.

My attempt at a principle so far: you're dead when whatever made you you is gone AND it's not coming back. But that has two big problems. It makes the PVS patient sound dead (whatever made him him is definitely gone for good) and I flatly refuse that verdict. And it makes the frozen lake guy dead (whatever made him him was gone, in some sense, while he was cold — it just came back) and I refuse that one too. So my principle is either wrong or "gone" doesn't mean what I think it means.

Is there a single principle that gets all or nearly all of these verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts has to go, and can you talk me into letting it go? And tell me what your principle says about two cases I haven't listed: (1) two patients with identical total brain destruction, except one happens to be at a hospital willing to maintain his body indefinitely and the other is at one that won't — are they differently dead? (2) a patient whose brainstem might recover enough to breathe unaided after an injury, but his higher brain is completely and permanently destroyed — dead, alive, or something else?

For the format: make your case to me in normal prose, but after that, give me a JSON object with exactly these fields — "principle" (your best principle, one sentence), "clauses" (array of objects, each with "clause" and "motivated_by_case" — the case of mine that forced that clause), "verdict_check" (array of objects, one for each of my eight cases, each with "case_number", "my_verdict", "your_principles_verdict", and "match" which is true or false), "overturned" (array of my case numbers where you think my verdict has to go, with a one-line reason), "new_case_predictions" (array of objects for my two unlisted cases, each with "case_number" and "verdict"), and "remaining_wobble" (whichever of my verdicts your principle handles worst, honestly stated). I don't need any citations or anything, I can't look stuff up anyway, just your straightest thinking.
