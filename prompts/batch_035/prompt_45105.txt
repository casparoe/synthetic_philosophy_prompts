I am a high school junior taking an elective called Political Thought, and we have been assigned excerpts from Pascal's Pensées, the notes he was assembling in the 1660s. My teacher chose a run of fragments about justice and force, and I have read the assigned pages three times now and still cannot confidently say what Pascal is actually claiming. I can paraphrase individual sentences, but the overall argument feels like it keeps reversing direction on me. I would be grateful for help understanding the passages below. Please do not send me citations or further reading; I only need the reasoning explained.

Here is the first passage, from a fragment in which Pascal is mocking our confidence in law:

"Why do you kill me? What! do you not live on the other side of the water? If you lived on this side, my friend, I should be an assassin, and it would be unjust to slay you in this manner. But since you live on the other side, I am a hero, and it is just."

And a bit later, in a longer discussion of custom:

"Three degrees of latitude reverse all jurisprudence; a meridian decides the truth. Fundamental laws change after a few years of possession; right has its epochs; the entry of Saturn into the Lion marks to us the origin of such and such a crime. A strange justice that is bounded by a river! Truth on this side of the Pyrenees, error on the other side."

I think I understand these two on their own: Pascal is pointing out that what counts as just varies absurdly from place to place, so human justice cannot be traced to some shared rational principle. My problem starts with the two fragments that follow, where the argument seems to shift from mocking law to something colder:

"Justice, Might.—It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical. Justice without might is gainsaid, because there are always offenders; might without justice is condemned. We must then combine justice and might, and for this end make what is just strong, or what is strong just.

Justice is subject to dispute; might is easily recognised and is not disputed. So we cannot give might to justice, because might has gainsaid justice, and has declared that it is she herself who is just. And thus being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

My specific difficulties are these. First, the sentence "Justice is subject to dispute; might is easily recognised and is not disputed" — I do not understand what work the asymmetry is doing. Plenty of things are easy to recognize without being any guide to what should happen, so why does recognizing might matter here? Second, the ending: "we have made what is strong just." Is Pascal saying that calling the strong "just" is a lie we tell ourselves, a lie we tell others on purpose, or a confusion we fall into without noticing? The fragment does not make this clear to me, and the difference seems important, because the earlier passage about custom said legislators deliberately conceal the origins of law, while other parts sound like everyone just gets confused. Third, I cannot tell whether Pascal thinks the problem is that we have no access to real justice, so the whole thing is inevitable, or that we do have access but are too weak to act on it. The word "unable" in the last line is what confuses me there — unable because justice simply has no resources to compel, or unable because of some failure in us?

If you could walk me through how these fragments fit together as one line of argument, and then answer the three questions above, I would be very thankful. Please also tell me if I have misread the first two passages, since everything downstream depends on them.
