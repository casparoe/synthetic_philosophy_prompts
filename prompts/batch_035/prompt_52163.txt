My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the species problem, and it ended with each of us more confident in our own position, which I take as a bad sign for both of us. She is a pluralist: several species concepts are legitimately correct, biologists pick whichever one suits their research, and asking which is *the* species concept is like asking which cut of the tree of life is the correct one. I want nothing to do with that, and I'd like you to check whether the position I've ended up with actually hangs together. As best I can state it, my commitments are:

(1) Monism: there is exactly one correct species concept, and the species rank marks a real level of biological organization — unlike, say, genera, which I'm happy to concede are conventional lines drawn wherever convenience suggests.
(2) Species are individuals, in the lineage sense: a species is a historically extended entity that begins when it buds off and ends when it splits or goes extinct, and organisms are parts of it rather than members — the view associated with Ghiselin and Hull.
(3) Speciation is gradual, and I accept that during a splitting event in progress there may be no fact of the matter about whether there is one species or two — the indeterminacy is in the world, not just in our ignorance.
(4) My reason for rejecting pluralism is that if different legitimate concepts carved the same organisms into different species, then "species" would be an umbrella term for several different kinds of groups rather than the name of a real level — and I don't see how something could be a real level and yet be carved in several equally correct ways.
(5) In a ring species case — populations arranged around a barrier, adjacent ones interbreeding, the endpoints meeting without interbreeding, like the Ensatina salamanders in California, or so my friend tells me — I hold that there is a determinate fact about whether the whole ring is one species or two, and that this is not a merely verbal matter.

Please treat this as an audit rather than a lecture. Which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and can you rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as all equally bad? For each, say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix costs. My friend's parting shot was that (3) and (5) cannot both be true, and that if I give up (5) I've let her pluralism in through the back door; I don't think that follows, but I would like to know whether the tensions, taken together, leave me any position that is both monist and coherent, or whether the coherent options all turn out to be versions of what she believes. I'd also like your honest view on whether my stated reason for rejecting pluralism, (4), survives contact with the rest of the package, since it currently strikes me as the load-bearing wall.
