At a recent workshop on meditation-based interventions, a clinical psychologist kept invoking the line "you are not the voice in your head — you are the one who hears it" as if it settled everything. She used it to defuse patients' anxious self-talk: the thought isn't you, so you needn't act on it or identify with it. I came away uneasy. As a PhD student working on consciousness, the slogan sounds to me like it's doing metaphysics while pretending to do therapy, and I suspect that on some readings it smuggles in precisely the picture it claims to dissolve.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be making, as a menu with verdicts? At least these, though add others if you see them: (1) a trivial grammatical or indexical point, distinguishing two roles — the thinking and the witnessing — that everyone can grant; (2) a substantive ontological claim that there is an inner listener, a spectator of one's own stream of thought, whose existence somehow secures distance from the thought; (3) a deflationary claim on which there is no listener at all, just further thoughts about thoughts, such that "the one who hears" is itself another draft; and (4) a merely practical or therapeutic claim that stepping back from self-talk is useful, with no commitments about who or what does the stepping.

For each reading, say whether it is plausible, whether it is philosophically contentious, and — most important to me — whether it would actually support the defusing use my colleague put it to. In particular: does reading (2), which is presumably what makes the slogan feel profound to patients, amount to the Cartesian theater — an inner audience witnessing the show of experience — and if so, does the multiple drafts model give us a way to keep the therapeutic effect without the theater? I would like the answer to be usable in a comment I'm preparing for the workshop discussion, so please be explicit about which reading she was most likely trading on.
