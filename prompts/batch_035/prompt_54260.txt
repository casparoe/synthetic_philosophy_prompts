okay so, i'm a software engineer, no philosophy background at all, so excuse the framing. every time cancel culture comes up at dinner with my friends, one side says it's basically the criminal justice system minus every safeguard we spent centuries adding: the accusation is the charge, the pile-on is the trial, losing followers/gigs/relationships is the sentence, and there's no appeals court, no sentencing guidelines, no double jeopardy protection — so the whole thing is unjust by design, and the fix is to bolt the safeguards back on (burden of proof, a statute of limitations, some kind of appeals process). the other side always fires back that it's not a justice system at all, it's just individuals deciding who to hang out with — more like a market, people revoking their attention one at a time, and nobody owes anybody a platform or a friendship, so demanding "due process" for that is a category error.

i can feel both sides are leaning hard on analogies and i want someone to actually audit them rather than just pick a team. can you lay out for each analogy what maps to what — charges, verdicts, sentencing, appeals, the state's monopoly on force for the court one; exit costs, network effects, information asymmetry for the market one — then where each mapping breaks, and what each quietly smuggles in (like, does the court analogy assume the sanction is imposed by a centralized authority that doesn't exist? does the market analogy assume exit is cheap and reversible when a google search of someone's name maybe isn't?). then tell me which conclusions, if any, each analogy can actually carry: does the disanalogy work kill the "bolt the safeguards back on" proposal, or does some weaker version survive (informal norms about proportionality, forgiveness over time)? and is there a better third analogy, maybe something from how i'd think about it at work — like, is a pile-on closer to a retry storm, where individually tiny actions aggregate into something none of the participants intend or see? also does deplatforming specifically change the analysis, since that's a platform making a centralized decision rather than a million individuals quietly unfollowing? sorry for the sprawl, i've just never seen anyone actually seperate the good parts of the comparison from the rhetorical parts.
