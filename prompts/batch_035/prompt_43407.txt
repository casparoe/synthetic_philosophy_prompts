I'm taking a political philosophy course this semester, and we've just finished Mill's On Liberty. I came into it more or less a libertarian about paternalism, and I came out confused, because I find that my actual judgments don't track the harm principle the way I thought they did. Mill, as I understand him, holds that the only legitimate ground for exercising coercive power over an adult against their will is to prevent harm to others, and he even grants an exception for the person who would voluntarily sell himself into slavery, on the reasoning that a choice that permanently destroys the chooser's capacity for future free choice isn't the kind of thing liberty is meant to protect. My brother-in-law, who is very much a live-and-let-live type, keeps pressing me on where I actually stand, and I've discovered that my answers form a pattern I can't articulate. So I'd like your help doing reflective equilibrium on my verdicts and seeing whether there's a principle behind them.

Here are the verdicts. I think the state may ban the sale of heroin outright, but I don't think it may ban cigarettes, even though cigarettes kill far more people. I think motorcycle helmet laws are legitimate, but I think a city banning the sale of large sugary drinks is an outrage. I think a ban on dueling, where both parties are competent adults who have consented in writing, is legitimate, but I don't think a ban on professional MMA is, even though both involve mutual consenting violence. I think contracts of voluntary lifetime servitude should be legally void, and I think that's clearly right. I think gambling should be legal even though it devastates families, and I think selling one of my kidneys should be legal even though my brother-in-law's wife thinks it's the most exploitative thing imaginable. And the one I keep going back and forth on: I think laws against hate speech that advocates no specific act of violence are illegitimate in principle, but I feel the pull of the argument that group defamation creates a climate in which the targeted group's members are harmed in ways that aren't captured by pointing to any particular assault.

Here is my tentative repair, which I'd like you to test and fix or discard: the state may restrict liberty (a) to prevent harm to others, and (b) to prevent a person from making a single, irreversible, or effectively irreversible choice that destroys their own future capacity for free choice — but never merely to reduce risk to the person's own health or welfare. If you can, state a principle, or a small set of candidates, precisely enough that each clause is motivated by one of my cases; tell me what it predicts on cases I haven't listed, such as physician-assisted death for nonterminal depression, mandatory childhood vaccination laws that also constrain adults, prohibitions on anabolic steroids in adult recreational bodybuilding, and bans on extremely high-leverage cryptocurrency trading; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts no defensible principle can save, so I know which ones to surrender to my brother-in-law. Please answer in American English.
