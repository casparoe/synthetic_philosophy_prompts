I recently became the subject, rather than the student, of a well-known problem in decision theory, and a dinner-party argument about what I should do has left me unable to settle it myself. I would like you to adjudicate the argument my two friends had, move by move, and I will describe the situation in full so that no outside knowledge is needed.

Three weeks ago, an acquaintance in an online community I belong to proposed the following arrangement, to be executed by written agreement and attested by a neutral third party who flipped a fair coin and recorded the result. If the coin came up heads, my acquaintance would send me $1,000 and I would owe nothing. If it came up tails, I would send him $100 and he would owe nothing. No further contact would occur after the flip in either case, the arrangement was strictly one-shot, and it was set up so that neither party's decision after the flip could influence anything the other party had already done. The coin came up tails. I have a day left to either send the $100 or renege, and I have not yet decided.

When I described this at dinner, my friends Daniel and Marta fell into the following argument, which I have reconstructed from my notes as faithfully as I can.

(1) Daniel said paying is straightforwardly irrational: the $1,000 was never going to be mine on this branch; from where I now stand, the only live choice is between having $100 less and having $100 more, and sending the money is simply a loss. He added that my earlier written agreement is a sunk cost and gives me no reason now.

(2) Marta replied that the agreement is not the point — the point is the decision procedure. When I accepted the arrangement, its expected value was strongly positive, and the procedure that accepted it is one that, in the heads branch of the very same arrangement, collects $1,000. If my decision now is the output of that same procedure, then refusing to pay means the procedure I ran was one that takes the good branch and refuses the bad one, and a procedure like that should never have been endorsed in the first place, since a counterpart offering such deals would then only ever be handing me the bad branches.

(3) Daniel answered that whatever procedure ran three weeks ago has already run; nothing I do now reaches back and changes which procedure it was, or what happened on the heads branch. I cannot affect the past, and a reason that requires affecting the past is no reason at all. He accused Marta of dressing up a sunk cost in the language of policies.

(4) Marta said she is not claiming backward causation. The claim is about which algorithm I should want to be, evaluated across the counterfactual branches as they were arranged ex ante; "one-shot" changes nothing, because the evaluation of an algorithm does not depend on how many times it is run, only on what it does in each situation it confronts — and in this situation, the algorithm that pays is the one that walked away with $1,000 in expectation when the deal was struck.

(5) Daniel then granted, for the sake of argument, that agents who pay in such cases do better across a life of similar encounters, but said this only works if the paying is somehow observed or repeated, so that a reputation for following through attracts the favorable offers. Our arrangement was explicitly anonymous and one-shot: no one will ever know, no further deals are conditioned on this one, and so the long-run argument has had all its machinery removed. Stripped of repetition and observation, he said, Marta's position reduces to a feeling that one ought to be the sort of person who pays, which is a sentiment, not a reason.

(6) Marta's final reply was that if that is all it takes to refute her, then it refutes Daniel too: his own rule — pay when and only when payment is causally efficacious — is also a policy, adopted once and applied across situations, and nothing about it is privileged just because each application looks locally like an isolated choice. She said the real disagreement is about whether rationality attaches to particular token decisions or to the procedure that produces them, and that Daniel has simply assumed the former without argument.

I could not referee this, and I have to decide by tomorrow. Please do the following. Adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each numbered move, say whether it is right, mistaken, or right only under an assumption the two of them never managed to state, and justify the rating. Then give an overall verdict on the question I actually face. In addition, tell me explicitly what consideration would change your verdict, and identify the step in your own reasoning most likely to fail. I would prefer your answer to open with its bottom line in the first sentence and proceed straight to the reasoning, without preamble and without restating my situation back to me.
