I've been invited to give a seminar paper to an interdisciplinary audience on what I'm calling the "ratchet thesis" about the origins of agriculture, and I want a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis, roughly, is this: the standard progressivist story gets the direction wrong, but so does the simple version of the "worst mistake" story. The early welfare costs of cultivation were real but patchy — some early farming populations seem to have done fine, and the health evidence from skeletal markers is noisy and geographically uneven. What made agriculture a trap was not that the first farmers were uniformly miserable, but that cultivation and sedentism set off a ratchet: food surpluses allowed higher fertility, higher populations demanded more intensification, and population pressure made going back to foraging demographically impossible long before anyone could have voted on it. So the "mistake" was made by a dynamic, not by anyone, and the interesting question is what, if anything, that implies about how we should evaluate a transition that no one chose.

The audience is the problem. It's a joint session of an archaeology department and an anthropology program, and I know exactly who will be in the room. There are two paleopathologists who spent their careers on enamel hypoplasias, stature decline, and cribra orbitalia — they largely accept the case that early agriculturalists show more stress markers than foragers, but they are fierce about how biased skeletal assemblages are and how badly the "forager baseline" is sampled. There's an economic anthropologist who thinks the whole hunter-gatherer affluence literature, from the original essay in the sixties through the Kalahari work-hour studies to the revisionist dustup about whether the !Kung were marginalized herder-serfs rather than pristine foragers, poisoned the well with a romantic political agenda, and who will treat any talk of forager "leisure" as naive. There's a behavioral ecologist who models foragers as optimal foragers and will want to know how a fitness-reducing strategy could persist for millennia without being abandoned — which I suspect cuts against the trap framing in a way I haven't fully worked out, since fitness and welfare come apart exactly where my thesis needs them to. And there are a couple of graduate students working on population-pressure accounts of agricultural origins who will ask whether the ratchet is just their supervisor's model with gloomier adjectives.

Please draft the prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it (the paleopathologists' vocabulary is not the behavioral ecologist's, and I want to recognize the objection in flight), with a two-minute reply to each and a frank note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front — I'd rather preempt the skeletal-sample complaint than fight it — and identify the one question I am hoping nobody asks, with whatever honest answer I can give if it comes anyway. I'm not looking for a defense of the thesis; I already believe it, or I wouldn't be presenting it. I want to know where this specific audience will hit it hardest.
