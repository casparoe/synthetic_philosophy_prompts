Been having a running argument with my roommate about hypocrisy and when people have the right to blame, and I've noticed my own gut verdicts are kind of a mess. Here they are. My aunt quit smoking fifteen years ago and now tears into my uncle for smoking in the house with the grandkids around — I think she's definately entitled to blame him. A guy at my office who is himself the worst gossip got all self-righteous when someone else spread a rumor — no standing, obviously. My friend has never driven drunk, but honestly that's mostly luck — somebody always ends up driving him, and if he'd ever been stuck I'm sure he'd have done it. He blasts people for drunk driving and I think he does have standing, though something about it feels off. Then there's a guy I know whose hands are completely clean, but when he blames a coworker for an error it's really a scapegoating move, meant to draw attention away from his own team's mess — I say no standing there, even though he did nothing wrong himself. And a woman at my church preaches about ethical consumption while her closet is full of fast fashion — no standing when she goes after other people for buying sweatshop stuff. Finally, the case I keep flip-flopping on: if my aunt had quit smoking two weeks ago instead of fifteen years ago, does she still have standing? Some days I think obviously yes, she's turned it around; other days it feels like she's just been lucky so far and would've kept going if quitting hadn't stuck.

So is there a principle (or maybe a couple of principles) that captures all this? The natural candidate seems to be something like: you lose standing when you're still committed to the flaw you're blaming, and you get it back by genuinely changing. That handles the ex-smoker and the gossip. But it doesn't obviously cover the scapegoater (clean hands, still no standing) or my friend who's only clean by accident, so maybe it needs extra clauses, or maybe I should just give up one of those verdicts. Tell me what fits best, which of my verdicts you'd overturn and why I should let it go, and what your principle predicts for a case I didn't mention — say, someone blaming a friend for telling a racist joke when they laughed at a different one an hour earlier without telling it themselves.

Also, please be upfront about where your answer is most likely to fail — which of my verdicts a good critic would say you bent the principle to keep, and what argument or consideration would actually move you to a different overall account.
