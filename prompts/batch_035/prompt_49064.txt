quick context: I'm on the collections committee at a mid-size regional art museum and we've got a situation brewing that's way more philosophical than our agendas usually get. Since the 60s we've displayed a Dutch Golden Age seascape attributed to a fairly minor 17th century marine painter -- call it the Storm off the Coast. It's honestly one of the most loved objects in the collection, our education deparment uses it constantly for school groups, and it was gifted by a prominent local family whose name is on one of our wings.

Last month a conservator ran pigment analysis ahead of a loan request and, long story short, the thing is a forgery. Late 19th century, done by someone who genuinely knew what they were doing -- good brushwork, good composition, apparently the only tell was a pigment that didn't exist yet in the 1600s. So the physical object visitors have stood in front of for sixty years is exactly what it always was; only the story attached to it collapsed.

The committee is split roughly three ways. One faction says a forgery has no business on our walls, pull it, maybe deaccession. Another (mostly education folks) says rehang it with an honest new label telling the whole story -- the tale of how it fooled people for a century might be more interesting to visitors than the painting ever was as a "genuine" work. And the director is mostly worried about the donor family, who are still around and will take this personally.

What I need is the written opinion I'll circulate ahead of next month's meeting. I want a concrete recommendation the committee can actually vote on -- pull it, rehang it with new interpretation, quietly storage it, whatever you think is right -- with real reasoning behind it. But here's the part I keep getting stuck on, and I want the memo to engage it head on rather than dodge it, because half the committee believes it and I don't think it's stupid: the canvas didn't change, the paint didn't change, the photons hitting the visitor's retina are identical. If you spent twenty minutes loving that painting last year, you were loving *this* object. So what exactly are we now saying is worth less? Either nobody was ever actually looking at the painting all those years, or our sudden embarrassment is pure pedigree-snobbery with nothing aesthetic about it. There's something deeply weird about the idea that a lab result changes what the thing looks like, and yet, when I stand in front of it now, it does look different to me and I can't fully explain why.

So: take whatever position you think survives that argument, but show your work on it. And treat the donor situation as an actual practical constraint the memo has to handle, not a footnote. If your recommendation costs us the family's goodwill, say so and say why it's still worth it.
