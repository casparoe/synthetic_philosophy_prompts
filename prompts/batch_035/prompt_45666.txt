I'm building a case-study unit for a course on democratic theory, and I want one ancient case treated with the same rigor people usually reserve for modern ones. The episode is the Mytilenean debate of 427 BCE, as Thucydides recounts it. After Mytilene's revolt from the Athenian empire was crushed, the Athenian assembly voted, at Cleon's urging, to execute the entire adult male population and sell the women and children into slavery, and a trireme was immediately dispatched to carry the order out. Within a day, enough citizens regretted the decision that the question was forced back onto the agenda; a second debate was held, in which Diodotus, arguing against the massacre, pointedly refused to invoke justice at all, insisting that only expediency was a proper basis for the vote, and the assembly reversed itself. The reversal then came down to a race between two triremes: the first crew, with no reason for urgency, rowed at leisure, while the second, carrying the reprieve, rowed in shifts around meals and with the promise of a reward, and overtook the first only barely in time. Even the reprieve was partial: Athens still executed roughly a thousand men it held responsible and dismantled the city's walls, and Thucydides reports that the second vote itself carried only narrowly — the show of hands was nearly a tie.

The lens I want applied is the epistemic defense of democracy: the family of arguments, running from Condorcet's jury theorem through modern deliberative-democracy theory, according to which large bodies deliberating openly tend to correct their own errors — the many outperform the few, and public deliberation is the mechanism that does it. Treat the episode as a test case that runs in both directions. First, map it carefully: who counted as the deliberators, what the actual options and arguments were on each side of each vote, what the two votes within roughly forty-eight hours say about the stability of assembly judgment, and what to make of the strangest feature of the second debate — that the winning case disclaimed the moral question entirely, so it is genuinely unclear whether the assembly converged on a better decision or merely on a cleverer framing of the same one. Then be honest about what the epistemic theory explains here and what it cannot: the same body issued a massacre order and then nearly split on reversing it; the reopening of the question was engineered by citizens who regretted the first vote, amid assembly irritation at being asked twice, which raises the question of agenda control that the theory rarely addresses; the final policy was itself a compromise that preserved a mass execution; and the whole correction nearly failed for logistical reasons — the leisurely pace of the first crew — that no deliberative theory has anything to say about.

So the deliverable is a disciplined case analysis, not a retelling and not a survey: theory illuminating episode, episode testing theory. Say explicitly where the epistemic defense is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and then give a considered judgment about whether the episode supports it, weakens it, or shows that its proponents need to add a clause — about agenda control, about the gap between decision and implementation, or about whether deliberation tracks truth or merely effective rhetoric — that they rarely state. End with a machine-readable bottom line: the very last line of your answer should be of the form \answer{label}, where the label is exactly one of vindicated, vindicated-if-qualified, undermined, or undetermined, followed inside the braces by at most fifteen words of justification.
