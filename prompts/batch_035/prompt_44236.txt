So heres my situation. I edit a mid-size science news site — we're not Nature, we're the place people read on their phones. Our health and psychology desk gets a steady stream of drafts that lean on heritability numbers, and the same mistakes keep coming back: "X is 60% genetic, therefore it's basically fixed at birth," or "twin studies show Y is heritable, so differences between countries/races/classes must be genetic too," or a writer reporting a heritability estimate from one population as if it were a constant of nature. I know enough to flinch at these, but I can't articulate the flinch, and my writers definitely can't — most of them have no stats background at all, they're good reporters who studied English.

What I want is a procedure my desk can actually run on a draft, in about fifteen minutes, before the morning pitch meeting. Not a tutorial on quantitative genetics — a checklist. Something like: question 1, question 2, question 3... in order, where each question has a pass/fail-ish answer, and the output is one of four verdicts: publish as-is, publish with a specific qualifier, kill the heritability framing (rewrite the story around something else), or escalate to me because it's a genuine judgment call. The thing it has to police is the leap from what heritability actually is — a statement about variance in genetic differences versus variance in everything else, within one population, in the environments that population actually experiences — to whatever the writer's sentence claims. I'd want it to catch at least these failure modes, and any others you think matter:

- "heritable" quietly becoming "genetic and therefore unchangeable" (the PKU / glasses-at-the-front-of-the-lecture-hall point, though don't lean on any one example too hard)
- within-group heritability getting used to say something about differences between groups that weren't in the study at all
- an estimate from a population with a narrow range of environments (one country, one school system, one decade) presented as if it transfers everywhere
- twin/sibling designs vs. GWAS-based estimates getting mixed up or treated as interchangeable, including when a writer cites a GWAS "explained variance" percentage as if it were the same animal as a twin-study heritability
- "the environment" treated as one bucket, when what matters for these numbers is whether the environmental variation that's actually present in this population is big or small

The procedure should say, for each question, what counts as a good enough answer from a writer who has twenty minutes and the paper's abstract, and where the checklist has to stop and hand it to a human because it's a values or style call, not a fact call — I'm thinking one of those is whether we ever put the bare word "genetic" in a headline, which I don't think any checklist can settle for me.

Then run it on three drafts so I can see it work:

1. A GWAS press release saying identified variants account for roughly 15% of the variance in educational attainment; the writer's draft says these genes "determine a sixth of how far you'll go in school."
2. A twin study done in a country with a very uniform school system, finding reading ability is highly heritable there; the writer wants to run "reading ability is in your genes" with a line about how tutoring is overrated.
3. One where the procedure gives a verdict I won't like. Honestly: I'm personally sympathetic to the writer in this one — the draft argues that since twin studies put heritability of BMI around 50-70%, public-health campaigns about diet are mostly theater, and part of me thinks that's a spicy, satisfying take. Make case 3 whatever kind of claim would put the procedure's verdict against my own prior, and tell me plainly whether the checklist held up or whether this is a spot where I just have to eat the disappointment and decide myself.

Finally, be straight with me about what the procedure can't do. It obviously can't audit the study itself — sample size, statistical power, whether the twins were actually raised apart, none of that. Is there anything else it will systematically miss? And is there a class of heritability claim where you'd tell me the checklist is honest but the whole genre is so misleading that we should have a blanket rule instead? I'd rather know now than after we publish something dumb.
