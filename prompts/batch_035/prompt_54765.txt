For over a year my metaphysics reading group has been stuck on counterpart theory versus transworld identity, and I have begun to suspect that our problem is not the arguments themselves but the shape of the disagreement. Here is where we are, and what I want is a diagnosis.

Everyone in the room knows the moves. On Lewis's side: nothing exists in more than one possible world, so a de re modal claim about Humphrey is translated as a claim about some world containing someone sufficiently resembling Humphrey who wins the election. The standard complaint — Kripke's, that this turns claims about Humphrey into claims about somebody else — was met with the reply that the translation preserves truth-conditions, so nothing we ever wanted to say is lost. Lewis himself at one point suggested that, at least for a language without an actuality operator, the difference between the two frameworks was a difference in notation rather than in doctrine. On the transworld side: Kripkeans and Plantingans hold that Humphrey himself, and not a proxy, exists in other worlds and has his properties there; Kaplan showed how to regiment genuine transworld individuals using haecceitistic proxies, which suggested that each camp can formally have whatever it wants. Then Fara and Williamson complicated the scorecard in the opposite direction: they argued that once modal discourse's talk about what is actually the case is taken seriously, no coherent version of counterpart theory represents it plausibly — so the frameworks are not mere notational variants, and the formal question is not settled but contested.

And yet the seminar still fights, and the fighting does not track the formal results. The counterpart faction insists the framework reflects the real structure of modality: the counterpart relation is a similarity relation, similarity is the sort of thing that can be more or less objective and even measured, and de re modality just is comparative similarity of worldmates across worlds. The transworld faction insists this conflates metaphysics with pragmatics — whether Humphrey might have won should not be hostage to which similarity metric is contextually salient. Two pressure points never resolve. First, the counterpart relation is not transitive: someone can be my counterpart in one world and be a counterpart of someone in a second world who is not my counterpart at all, which the transworld side takes to show that counterpart theory cannot be stating what things might have been, only how various things resemble each other. Second, cross-world counting and quantification: ask how many possible people there are, and one framework counts individuals while the other counts world-relative things standing in counterpart relations, and the answers look different. Meanwhile one downstream application seems to favor the counterpart side: fission. If I split into two future people, identity being one-one means I do not survive as two, but I can stand in the counterpart-like I-relation to both fission products, each of which has the pre-fission person as a temporal counterpart — a treatment the transworld camp allegedly cannot copy without saying either that I did not survive at all or that two people were sitting in my chair all along.

My working hypotheses, offered only as hypotheses. First, the residual disagreement is not about what modal sentences mean but about what makes them true — whether cross-world sameness is a primitive fact about individuals or a projection of an overall similarity ordering, so that the translation results and the metaphysical dispute simply do not engage. Second, if that is right, the debate is substantive but at a level of abstraction where the formal equivalence machinery is irrelevant, since translation preserves entailments, not truthmakers. Third, the actuality problem, the counting questions, and the fission application are where, if anywhere, the frameworks genuinely come apart in practice rather than in bookkeeping.

I want a diagnosis, and I want you to commit to one: verbal disagreement, substantive metaphysical disagreement, two research programs answering different questions under a single heading, or a standoff over bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for. To test whatever diagnosis you give, run it against three cases. First, the modal archive: suppose an archivist must catalogue all of possibility and must choose between filing by individual — one entry per person, listing the worlds that person is in — and filing by world, one entry per world-relative person, with cross-references recording degrees of resemblance; decide whether anything, in the world or in our modal practice, settles the filing scheme, or whether the choice is as arbitrary as a library's shelving convention, and say what that implies about the debate. Second, the non-transitivity of the counterpart relation: decide whether it is a deep defect, a harmless artifact of context-sensitivity, or evidence that the counterpart relation does pragmatic rather than metaphysical work. Third, the fission application: decide whether it really gives counterpart theory an advantage that no translation result can absorb, or whether the transworld theorist can copy the treatment by distinguishing identity from whatever relation the fission products bear to me. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress on this debate — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected, and explain why the literature has gone quiet rather than converged. Take a definite position at every step; I can assemble the menu of options myself, and what I need is someone to actually choose among them and defend the choice.
