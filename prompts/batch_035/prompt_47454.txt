I am a retired ecologist — thirty-six years spent on mutualisms and host–parasite population dynamics — and since retiring I have been working through evolutionary game theory and the philosophy of biology. I have finally written down my own position on where cooperation comes from and what, if anything, it obligates us to, and before I circulate the essay to my reading group I want a consistency check from someone who will not be polite about it. The position has five parts. (a) In a genuine one-shot prisoner's dilemma between agents with no reputation at stake and no prospect of future interaction, defection is the uniquely rational play; cooperating there is a mistake, however admirable. (b) The cooperation we observe in anonymous one-shot laboratory games is an artifact: our psychology was assembled in and for repeated, reputation-bearing interactions, and the machinery simply does not fully distinguish the one-shot case from the iterated one. Axelrod's tournaments long ago persuaded me that cooperation among self-interested agents lives on the shadow of the future, and I have never seen a reason to revise that. (c) At the same time, the large-scale cooperation of modern societies — taxpaying, queuing, giving to strangers we will never meet — cannot be explained by direct reciprocity or kinship alone; sustaining it seems to require costly third-party punishment of defectors, something on the order of what the behavioral economics literature calls strong reciprocity. (d) I am, however, unmoved by group selection stories: selection among groups strikes me as at best a bookkeeping device, and I do not accept that it built genuinely self-sacrificial dispositions into our species. (e) Finally, I hold that morality just is our species' machinery for sustaining cooperation: there is no further fact about what one ought to do over and above what the cooperation-sustaining norms of one's community require.

For what it is worth, I still tip in restaurants I am certain never to revisit, and I do not regard myself as behaving irrationally in doing so — but I may be wrong about that, and I do not want you to spare any of the five claims on my account. Please go through them pair by pair: tell me which pairs stand in genuine tension and how severe each tension is, which pairs only appear to be in tension and why the appearance dissolves on inspection, and, for each genuine tension, what would have to give and what the revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as an undifferentiated list, and do not assume I am protecting a favorite — I genuinely do not know where the trouble lies, which is why I am asking. Please write in American English.
