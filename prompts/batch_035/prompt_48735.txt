Next term I'm co-teaching a graduate seminar on archives and power — how records constitute rather than merely reflect social reality, whose lives get documented, and how silences get produced. We pair archival theory with works that dramatize it, and for the literary unit I want to use Toni Morrison's Beloved (1987). Please give me a reading of the novel through the archival lens, in enough detail that I can build two seminar sessions around it.

A few threads I want the reading to take up seriously:

First, the novel is saturated with records, and every one of them is an instrument of domination rather than neutral memory. Schoolteacher keeps a notebook in which he sorts Sethe's characteristics into human and animal columns, and Sethe's chance reading of it is what breaks her world at Sweet Home — she discovers that she exists, for the master's archive, as an entry to be annotated. Stamp Paid shows Paul D a newspaper clipping about a fugitive woman who killed her child, and Paul D's first instinct is that the picture can't be Sethe — the record's authority collides with his knowledge of the person. Sethe buys her dead daughter's headstone with her body, and the word cut into it is a record too. If you can, read these as a system: what kind of person does the archive of slavery produce, and how does the novel show its subjects discovering themselves as records?

Second, the question of silences. The historical kernel is the Margaret Garner case of 1856, which Morrison found in a newspaper article titled "A Visit to the Slave Mother who Killed Her Child" while working as an editor on The Black Book — a compilation of clippings of Black history. The public debate at the time turned on whether Garner should be charged with murder or with destruction of property, which is itself an archival fact of breathtaking violence. But the novel also invents what no archive holds: Sixo, tied to a tree to be burned, laughing and shouting that his Thirty-Mile Woman is escaping with his unborn child, so that his line continues even as his story ends with his body; Baby Suggs preaching in the Clearing, an archive that is oral, bodily, and leaves no ledger. The seminar will have read Trouillot's argument that silences enter history at four moments — the making of sources, the making of archives, the making of narratives, the making of history — and I want to know where Beloved locates each moment, and whether it identifies any moment the silence cannot fully colonize.

Third, and most delicate: the novel's own status as counter-archive. Its dedication is to Sixty Million and more — a dedication to people who are precisely uncounted, and who become countable only as a fictional gesture. The book restores interiority to a woman who exists in the record only as a case, but it also ends on a doubled refrain, played twice, about whether this is a story to pass on — ambiguous between "not to be transmitted" and "impossible not to hand down." Does the novel license fiction as a repair of the archive, or does it dramatize the presumption of inventing where the record is silent — filling silence with the wrong kind of content? Beloved herself, the returned dead who feeds on the fragments of others' stories, might be read either as the archive's repressed returning or as a warning about counterfeit recovery.

Please be faithful in both directions: don't reduce the novel to an illustration of archival theory, and note where it resists the reading — for instance, the novel is hardly naive about oral memory either, since the community's failed witness and Sethe's isolated "rememory" are part of what destroys the family. Tell me which scenes I should put at the center of each session, where the archival metaphor breaks down, and what you think the novel gives the debate that archival theory by itself does not. I'm not looking for a bibliography or citations — just the reading, argued from the text.
