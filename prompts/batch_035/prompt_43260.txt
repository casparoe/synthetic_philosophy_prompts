My roommate (philosophy grad student, hates Dennett) and I have been fighting for months about the "user illusion" picture of consciousness -- the idea that the phenomenal qualities of experience, the intrinsic redness or awfulness we think we find in our own minds, are like the folders and trash can on a desktop: cartoon features the brain's interface displays that no actual process has. I actually find the picture attractive, she thinks it's a fraud, and last week she came home with an argument that has been bugging me ever since, because it feels way too easy and I can't find the broken step. It goes:

(1) An illusion is a case of misrepresentation: something appears to have a property that nothing in fact has. The Müller-Lyer lines look unequal and aren't; the desert oasis looks wet and isn't.
(2) In every genuine illusion there's a gap between the appearance and the thing it's an appearance of -- that gap is what makes the illusion possible. The lines can look unequal while being equal because how the lines look and how the lines are is not the same fact.
(3) According to the user-illusion view, when I seem to be having a pain with an intrinsic felt awfulness, nothing anywhere has an intrinsic felt awfulness; my brain is just representing itself as having one.
(4) But the phenomenal properties are supposed to be properties of the experience itself, not of some external object the experience is about. So for the illusion to work, there would have to be a gap between how the experience seems and how the experience is.
(5) Nothing can differ from itself like that. A thing's seeming F can only come apart from its being F when the seeming is one thing and the thing is another -- so the appearance/reality gap can never open up *inside* a mental state, only between a mental state and the world.
(6) So either experiences really do have the felt qualities they seem to have, which the user-illusion theorist denies, or the "illusion" has no subject matter at all -- it's not an illusion, just a false description with nothing it's false about.
(7) Therefore the user-illusion view is incoherent: an illusion of qualia would itself have to be a quale.

Here's why I don't just concede. The argument seems to prove way too much. Run steps (4) and (5) on beliefs instead of experiences: a belief's apparent content is a property of the belief itself, and yet people are demonstrably wrong about what they believe all the time (confabulation, self-serving bias about their own motives, whatever). And the desktop metaphor itself seems to survive: there are no little folders inside my laptop, yet calling the icon an "illusion" seems fine and even illuminating -- so apparently an illusion doesn't need a real instance of the property hidden somewhere. So I'm confident something in her chain is broken, I just can't say what. My instinct is that the trouble is somewhere around (4) and (5) -- maybe a quiet shift in what "the experience" is supposed to refer to between the two steps -- but I honestly can't tell whether that's one complaint or two, or whether the real problem is earlier, in her general account of what illusions require in (2).

So: which step or slide should I reject, why did it look so plausible, and what does rejecting it cost -- including whether the cost lands on her side or on mine? I'd rather keep the door open for the user-illusion view than not, but don't do me any favors: if her argument is actually sound and the belief case and the desktop case are both red herrings for some principled reason, say so plainly. And if you think I've misdescribed the user-illusion view itself somewhere -- if the view doesn't actually commit to what I say in (3) -- call that out too instead of grading her argument against my possibly-wrong version of it.
