I serve as rapporteur for a reading group of non-specialists that has spent this term on the classical debate about space and motion, and our most recent discussion — on Newton's bucket — ended in a standoff I was asked to write up. Since I genuinely could not tell, at any point, who had the better of the argument, I would be grateful for a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange.

The disagreement unfolded as follows. (1) One member argued that the bucket experiment settles the matter against relationism: in the early phase, when the relative motion between the water and the bucket walls is greatest, the water's surface is flat, whereas later, when bucket and water rotate together and there is no relative motion between them at all, the surface is concave. Since the surface's shape depends on something other than the water's motion relative to the bucket — or to any nearby body — rotation must consist in motion relative to space itself, and space must exist as a thing in its own right. (2) A second member replied that this shows only that the surface doesn't track motion relative to the bucket; a relationist is free to say the water rotates relative to the distant masses, and can even offer a prediction: in a universe containing nothing but the bucket, the water would never climb at all. (3) The first member answered that this prediction could never be checked, so it is a stipulation dressed up as physics; moreover, in that one-object universe it still seems there would be a fact about whether the rope had been twisted — a fact the relationist's position cannot accommodate. (4) The second member countered that absolute space is equally beyond observation, and worse off: a universe in which everything is shifted or rotated en bloc differs in no respect whatever, so absolute positions and directions are meaningless posits, and the relationist explains everything actually observed with strictly less ontology. (5) The first member closed by claiming that the relationist explains nothing about the concavity but merely redescribes it in terms of ever more distant bodies, and that modern physics, in which spacetime carries its own inertial structure, reads as a vindication of the substantivalist. The second member's parting shot was that the same physics is standardly read the opposite way, as confirming that inertial structure depends on the distribution of matter.

Please adjudicate each of the five moves in turn: state what each participant is right about, where each goes wrong if he does, and whether any move deserves only a partial verdict. Then identify any position both overlooked — in particular, whether anything in the exchange forces the choice between absolute space and pure relationism as they frame it, or whether the live options are wider than either of them allowed. If any of the disagreement turns on an ambiguity — for instance, about what rotation is being claimed to be relative to, or about whether the dispute concerns ontology or merely the facts about motion — please make that explicit.
