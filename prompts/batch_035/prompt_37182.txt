I work on causal inference methods in epidemiology and read philosophy of causation on the side. For roughly thirty years the debate between Lewis-style counterfactual theories and Woodward–Pearl interventionism seems to me to have run in place: the Lewisians patch their similarity metrics to handle preemption and trumping, the interventionists enlarge the notion of an intervention to cover background contexts and idealizations that could never literally be carried out, and each side ends up endorsing the other's verdicts on nearly every concrete case. In applied work the situation is even stranger: my colleagues and I all use DAGs and the do-operator regardless of metaphysical allegiance, and I cannot find a real-world example where the two frameworks recommend different conclusions. The only cases that divide them involve variables that cannot, even in principle, be intervened on — the mass of the electron, the fundamental constants — and there the disagreement seems to be about what a counterfactual is, not about what causes what.

My working hypothesis is that this is a dispute about the semantics of counterfactuals — closest possible worlds versus structural equations under mutilation of the graph — wearing the clothes of a dispute about causation, with the genuinely causal content shared between the camps. But I can see an alternative diagnosis: the two sides are answering different questions under one word, with the Lewisians doing conceptual analysis of the folk concept while the interventionists engineer a notion suited to inference and manipulation, so that neither framework can be wrong by the other's lights. It also seems possible that the dispute is substantive after all and that I have simply not located the dividing cases.

Diagnose the disagreement for me, using what each side actually says — for instance, how each framework handles a variable that cannot be intervened on in principle, or whether a causal claim is made true by a pattern of counterfactual dependence or by the structural relations that support interventions — and tell me whether my hypothesis survives. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a class of cases that cleanly separates the frameworks, a formal result connecting them, or an argument that one of the two questions is the right one to ask. If you think the impasse is permanent, say why. Please answer in American English, in no more than 600 words.
