I'm a PhD student in epistemology drafting a paper, and I want comments on the argument before I write it up in full. The idea: the dogmatism paradox and the bootstrapping problem are usually run together as two symptoms of the same Moorean disease — reasoning one's way to substantive conclusions from an evidence base that was never supposed to support them. I want to argue they come apart. The dogmatist about perceptual justification needs only that experience gives immediate justification for 'here is a hand', from which one can, in principle, deductively obtain 'I am not a handless brain in a vat'. The bootstrapper needs more: a reliable gas gauge can seemingly be used to establish its own reliability, one reading at a time, and that looks bad in a way the Moorean inference does not, because it involves treating a source's own outputs as evidence of the source's reliability — a failure of independence between the track-record evidence and the very hypothesis it is meant to confirm. My claim is that this independence failure has no analogue in the paradox case: perceptual evidence for 'here is a hand' is not, in the same way, the product of the hypothesis that perception is reliable, so a view can accept dogmatism while still diagnosing bootstrapping as vicious. If that is right, Pryor-style dogmatism survives the bootstrapping objection that is often thought to sink it. Please give me exactly five numbered comments, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, written in British English. I would like the five comments to cover: whether the independence framing genuinely distinguishes the two cases; whether the bootstrapper's inference can be redescribed as ordinary deductive Moorean reasoning after all; the strongest objection I have not anticipated; whether the paper is interesting enough to be worth pursuing; and one concrete suggestion for sharpening the central claim.
