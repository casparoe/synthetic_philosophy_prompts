I do evals at a small AI lab — mostly on models we didn't train ourselves, so we have weights but no training data access, and our compute budget is a joke compared to the big players. My job lately is standing up an anomaly-detection monitoring setup: we run a pile of queries through the model, look at what the activations do, and flag when something unusual is going on. The part I keep getting stuck on, and what I want your help with, is the conceptual question hiding inside the practical one: when I say two flagged things involve "the same mechanism," what am I even claiming?

Concretely: suppose we find the model's activations look weird on a family of jailbreak prompts, and we label that cluster "the jailbreak thing" or whatever. A few weeks later a new jailbreak family shows up, its activations land in a somewhat different region that partly overlaps the old one, and I have to decide: same mechanism re-used, or a different one that happens to do the same job? This matters because we report to the safety team, and "the detector caught a new instance of a known mechanism" reads very differently from "a genuinely novel mechanism appeared" — one is a paper cut, the other is potentially a headline. And I honestly dont know if there's a principled answer here or whether "mechanism" is doing the kind of work a schmategorization does elsewhere in science, like species or diseases, where the boundary is drawn partly by nature and partly for our convenience. A friend in our org theory reading group said philosophers have fought about natural kinds vs nominal kinds and that this might actually apply, but I dont know that literature at all.

So heres what I'm asking for: a procedure my team can actually follow. The questions to ask in order about a newly flagged cluster, what counts as a sufficient answer to each (with actual tests we could run — ablating the old direction and seeing if the new behavior dies, steering/perturbing activations, checking what fraction of the behavior survives, whatever else is standard-ish in interpretability land), where the procedure stops and says "mechanism-matched" vs "novel," and where it hands off to a human because the question is partly a judgment call about how finely we're willing to carve up the model's innards. Then run it on three cases: (1) a new jailbreak family where ablating the old direction blunts the new behavior but only partly, maybe 60%; (2) a refusal-bypass case where the activations sit right on top of the old cluster but the surface behavior is completely different (the model sycophantically agrees with the user instead of refusing); and (3) a cluster in a region our tooling barely reaches, where "novel mechanism" and "we can't see whats happening" are hopelessly confounded — for that one I want the procedure to be honest that it can't tell those apart. Also please say what the procedure can't do: I'd much rather tell the safety team "we can't tell" than hand them a checklist that outputs false confidence. And if you think the whole framing is wrong and "same mechanism" isn't a fact of the matter at all, say that too, but then still give me the best procedure you can under that assumption.
