I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and a chapter of my dissertation concerns choice under conditions where the options resist comparison — a career in medicine versus a life in music, or spending the next decade caring for a parent versus pursuing one's own research. My advisor's standing criticism of my drafts is that I argue against everyone else's theories of hard choices without ever saying what I would accept as adequate; meanwhile a co-grad student of mine insists the whole problem dissolves once you allow that our value judgments are vague or indeterminate, and I don't think I can answer him without a list of what the theory is actually for. So before I write another negative paragraph, I want the specification: the desiderata any adequate account of value comparison between options must meet, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and a verdict on which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of one camp.

A few things I believe belong on the list, though I'm open to being told they're prejudices. The theory has to handle the small-improvement cases: two options where neither is better and they are not equal, yet improving one slightly still does not make it better; the theory owes us an account of whether that indicates a genuine fourth relation, a failure of exact comparison, or something else. It has to distinguish incomparability as a fact about the options from mere unsettledness in us, since my co-grad student's vagueness story conflates them. It has to be usable by the agent at the moment of decision, not just by a spectator ranking completed lives from outside; and it must respect that in a hard choice the agent seems to do something — commit, make a stand — rather than merely discover which option was ahead all along. It should also coexist with transitivity wherever transitivity is worth keeping, or else say exactly where and why it gives way. Finally, there is the coin-toss datum: flipping a coin feels perfectly sensible for a tie but somehow wrong for one of these hard cases, and a theory on which they come out the same has missed something.

Please write the full requirements list — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, what it must deliver to the deliberating agent — with the motivating case behind each, mark which requirements you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable in any subset (if some set of them cannot be met together, say so; that would be a result in itself), and identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude the main contenders, whether that is a strict trichotomy-plus-vagueness view, Raz-style incommensurability, or parity. Keep the weight on the criteria rather than on scoring the contenders against them — the scoring I'll do myself in the chapter. And no reading lists or bibliographies, please: I want the standard, not a syllabus.
