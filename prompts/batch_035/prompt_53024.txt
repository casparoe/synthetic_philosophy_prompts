So I'm the (relatively new) program manager at a mid-sized regional transit authority, and one of my jobs is chairing the monthly "continuance review" -- the stage-gate meeting where we decide whether projects that are over budget or behind schedule keep getting funded or get killed/paused. We run maybe 15-20 active projects at a time, everything from bridge refurb contracts to an automated fare system to little pilot programs, and at any given moment 3 or 4 of them are in trouble. The meetings are two hours, the people in the room are engineers, a finance person, and a council liaison who gets twitchy about anything that looks like waste right before an election, and whatever we recommend goes to the board, which can and does overrule us for political reasons.

I got handed this job after our previous fare-system integration went something like four years and way too much money past its original estimate before someone finally pulled the plug, and the postmortem basically said "escalation of commitment" and left it at that. Since then I've been reading up, and the dollar auction framing really clicked for me: once you're in a dollar auction, bidding again can be locally rational because quitting means eating your whole loss for sure, and yet the whole thing only works as a trap because everyone reasons that way. Which is a much sharper diagnosis than "people irrationally honor sunk costs" -- in the auction the sunk cost reasoning isn't even exactly fallacious, it's the tournament structure that's broken. Or at least I think that's right, tell me if I'm mangling it.

What I want from you is a procedure. Something the review committee can actually run in the meeting, on one page, question by question: what to ask about each project, in what order, what a sufficient answer to each looks like, and where the procedure should output "this is now a values call, escalate to the board" rather than pretending to have derived the answer. Some things I specifically want it to handle, which I think the textbook version doesn't:

1. Quitting isn't free in the auction and it isn't free for us. When we kill a project we face write-offs, possible litigation with the contractor, and -- this is the part nobody writes down -- staff whose careers are invested in the thing, plus a public "the authority wasted X million" story where X includes everything we already spent. A procedure that treats stopping as a clean reset will get ignored. So the procedure has to honestly price the exit, not just the continuation.

2. The classic advice is "don't enter the auction," but we're already in basically all of these. I want something for mid-auction decision-making: is there a principled difference between "keep bidding" and "bid once more, with a credible pre-commitment to a stopping rule we set now, under which future-us is bound"?

3. We tried a rule that the project's own champion can't be the one to present the continuance case -- fresh eyes, no sunk costs of their own, since a colleague with no history on the project is basically a new bidder entering the auction at zero. It half worked and half turned into theater where the fresh presenter just gets steamrolled by the project team. Is there a version of that idea that actually works, or should the procedure formalize something else about who speaks and who decides?

Then run the procedure on three of our real-ish cases so I can see if it survives contact with them:

- The fare system replacement (the successor to the disaster): two years late, vendor keeps saying 90% done, we've already paid most of the contract, and the old system it replaces is physically dying.
- A contractor walked off a bridge refurb about a third in, we'd have to rebid at current prices to finish, and the bridge is currently closed either way so the sunk-cost pressure partly comes from the public seeing a closed bridge.
- A small pilot -- on-demand shuttle service in a low-ridership area -- that shows basically no ridership gains after a year, but a couple of council members love it and it's cheap. I suspect the procedure will say kill this one, which will make my life hell, and I want you to be honest about whether that's what it says and what it says about the procedure if the politically easy answer and the procedurally correct answer diverge this neatly.

Also be upfront about where the whole framing stops applying -- we're not literally in a winner-take-all auction, the "prize" here is a functioning asset not a fixed dollar bill, and I'd like to know if the disanalogy is cosmetic or if it breaks the procedure. If escalation-of-commitment theory says our meeting structure is itself part of the trap (e.g. monthly review = we keep paying to stay at the table), say that too, even though fixing the meeting is above my pay grade.
