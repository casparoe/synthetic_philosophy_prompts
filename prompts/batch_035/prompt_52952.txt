I spent the better part of four decades doing observational epidemiology, where nearly every interesting claim is a causal claim wrapped in confounders, and where one learns a certain humility about what a correlation can carry. Since retiring I have been reading my way into the philosophy and social theory my scientific training crowded out, and nothing I have encountered puzzles me more than the century-long argument over Weber's Protestant ethic thesis. On its face I recognize it immediately: an exposure (ascetic Protestantism), an outcome (the spirit of modern capitalism), and an obvious set of confounders — the commercial regions of early modern Europe were precisely the ones that went Protestant, so which is moving which? But the debate does not behave the way an unresolved identification problem behaves. The materialist critics keep pressing the same point, that the values themselves demand explanation and that appealing to them as causes is either circular or quietly hands the real work to background economic conditions; the Weberians keep replying that ideas can be autonomous — that given this constellation of anxieties about salvation and disciplined labor, the psychology of accumulation followed — and neither side gives an inch.

My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that this is not a disagreement about historical fact at all but about what a completed explanation looks like. The materialist side seems to require a closed causal order: values may figure as causes only if they are themselves the effects of something non-cultural, so that the explanatory buck terminates somewhere else. The Weberian side seems content with a conditional, open-ended model: given values V, behavior B follows, and the genealogy of V is somebody else's paper. In epidemiology we never confused ourselves about this — a model that takes diet as given and traces its downstream effects is not refuted by a model of how diet is determined — but perhaps the analogy is unfair. There may be a substantive issue buried here: whether values have the temporal stability and autonomy to behave like exposures at all, or whether culture is more like a thermostat, continually reset by the very outcomes it is said to cause, in which case the Weberians' conditional model is describing a feedback loop and mistaking one arc of it for a straight line.

Please diagnose the disagreement. Is it verbal? Are the two sides answering different questions under one name, or applying different standards of explanatory completeness to the same question? Or is there a genuine empirical or conceptual question hiding underneath, one that better historical evidence could in principle settle? Support whatever diagnosis you give with what each side actually says, not with a survey of the literature. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a sharpened distinction, a research design, a reformulation of the question — or whether the impasse should simply be expected to persist, and why.
