I teach a graduate seminar on virtue ethics and its situationist critics — the cluster around Harman, Doris, and Merritt, with the Milgram experiments, the Good Samaritan study, and the mood-manipulation helping studies as our recurring exhibits. For the final unit I want to break from the journal literature and assign a novel instead: Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. My thought is that Stevens is perhaps the most carefully constructed case study in twentieth-century fiction of a man whose behavior looks explicable without reference to character: a butler whose every action is a function of his role, his employer's standing orders, the class code of professional service, and seemingly trivial features of circumstance. So I would like a reading of the novel through the situationist critique, and I want it to be faithful in both directions — to the actual psychology experiments and arguments, and to what the novel is doing, rather than turning Stevens into a rehearsed illustration of Doris.

Here is the material I want the reading to work through. The episode in which Stevens carries out Lord Darlington's decision to dismiss the two Jewish maids, despite privately believing the decision wrong, and despite having a plausible exit (he could resign, he could object, he could delay) — is that the situationist point in miniature: a situational pressure (the employer's will, the timing, the servant's role) overwhelming whatever compassion or courage Stevens might be credited with? Or is it rather evidence of a stable trait — deep deference, or an overdeveloped professionalism — doing exactly what a character-based explanation predicts? The evening of the 1936 conference, when Stevens's father dies upstairs while Stevens stays at his post below, is the same question sharpened. And then there is Miss Kenton, working inside the same house and the same situations, who objects, threatens resignation, and eventually exercises exactly the kind of agency Stevens lacks — which the situationist, I take it, must treat as a difference in situations (her role, her marriage prospects, her position in the household) rather than a difference in character. Does the novel let that reading stand, or does she quietly refute it? Finally, the frame of the whole book: the elderly Stevens on his motoring trip, narrating himself into an unreliable, self-exculpatory story, then slowly losing the story — his admissions about Lord Darlington, about his father, about Miss Kenton — until the final scene on the pier, where he resolves to master the art of bantering for his new American employer. The situationist critique is often accused of leaving no room for self-knowledge or reform; the novel's whole structure is an act of retrospective self-interpretation ending in the fragile resolution to change at the very end of a life. I want to know whether the novel comes down on either side at all, or whether it exposes something the philosophical literature misses — perhaps that character, on either theory, is partly constituted by the stories agents tell about which situations they are in.

Please organize your response under these headings, in this order: The situationist reading of Stevens (roughly 600 words — make the case the novel supports the critique as strongly as it can be made, using the specific episodes above); Where the novel resists (roughly 450 words — the honest counter-reading, including whatever Miss Kenton and the narration itself do to undermine the first section); What the novel adds to the debate (roughly 350 words — what a critic can see that the argumentative literature, on both sides, cannot say, and what I should therefore tell the seminar the novel is for). Please paraphrase rather than quote the novel, and treat the empirical studies as the philosophers of the debate present them rather than re-litigating their replication status.
