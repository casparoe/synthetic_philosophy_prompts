Some context: for the past decade I have started each morning with the same dark-roast coffee, but lately it tastes bitter and flat to me, and I honestly cannot tell whether the coffee itself tastes different to me than it used to, or whether it tastes the same and I have simply stopped liking that taste. This puzzle has recently become personally urgent, and a philosopher friend told me it maps onto two related but distinct lines of thought: Dennett's attempt in his work on quining qualia to show that qualia, as classically conceived, are an incoherent posit, and Chase and Sanborn's argument that introspection can systematically fail to distinguish a change in one's experience from a change in one's standards or preferences. I would like to understand how these two projects relate, and I would appreciate it if you would take on the role of a professional philosopher of mind and reason carefully rather than reciting textbook summaries.

Please compare Dennett's quining of qualia with Chase and Sanborn's treatment of introspective error, addressing in particular the following questions. First, Dennett argues that the traditional marks of qualia — that they are ineffable, intrinsic, private, and immediately grasped in experience — cannot all be satisfied at once, so qualia as standardly conceived are an incoherent posit that should be discarded rather than explained. Chase and Sanborn, by contrast, seem willing to grant that there are determinate experiences; their point is that our introspective reports about them can be wrong, for instance when someone can no longer say whether coffee now tastes different to them or whether they now respond differently to an unchanged taste. Does their argument presuppose exactly the sort of qualia Dennett wants to quine — determinate experiential facts that introspection may misreport — or does it instead support Dennett by revealing that the supposedly immediate datum of taste is actually a fallible judgment? Second, which of the two approaches better accommodates the first-person phenomenon I am actually living through: the fact that the coffee's taste seems simply given to me, so that I feel there must be a fact of the matter I am somehow failing to access? Third, if Chase and Sanborn are right that I cannot introspectively settle my coffee question, what follows about the authority of first-person reports in philosophy of mind more generally — is their point local to cases of changed taste, or does it generalize into an argument against introspective authority anywhere?

Before committing to a verdict, please brainstorm the main positions one could take on how the two projects relate — for example that they are compatible allies in a common deflationary program, that they are rivals because one eliminates the objects of experience while the other retains them, or that they operate on different levels and cannot directly conflict. Then argue for the position you find most defensible, and close with a brief statement of what you take the single deepest point of disagreement between the two approaches to be.
