I'm a novelist and I'm about two thirds of the way through a book about a woman who stays at a workplace that gets worse by such tiny increments she never quite gets a scene where she can point at the door and leave. My editor keeps saying the book needs a stronger spine, and rereading Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day for research I realized it's basically the same machine I'm trying to build, except Stevens the butler lives a whole life inside it. But I also studied philosophy way back, and I've started to suspect Ishiguro accidentally (or not so accidentally) built the plot around a genuinely contested problem in value theory, so I want a proper critical reading that takes both sides seriously, and I'd appreciate American English since that's what I write in.

The problem I mean is the one about whether "better than" is transitive, and the spectrum arguments people use to attack it. My old philosophy friend explained Warren Quinn's puzzle of the self-torturer to me recently: a man hooked up to a device where each turn of the dial increases pain by an amount too small to notice, and each turn comes with a payment, so every single choice looks fine, but a thousand turns later he's in agony and would pay everything to go back. That's Stevens to me. Serving Lord Darlington in 1936 versus 1935, overlooking one more thing, keeping silent through one more dinner, dismissing the two Jewish maids when it was just one more order obeyed from a man he'd always obeyed — none of the adjacent comparisons ever registered as a real sacrifice, and yet the 1956 Stevens sitting on that bench in Weymouth is plainly devastated when he finally compares where he ended up to where he could have been. The philosophers fight about what follows: some say betterness must be transitive and the trick is that there are unnoticed differences between adjacent options all along (my friend thinks this is the Stuart Rachels / fine-grained values move, and Temkin is the big name on the intransitivity side), others bite the bullet and say the comparisons just don't chain.

So here's what I want: read the novel through this debate. Which scenes would you point me to as the actual turns of the dial, and does Ishiguro stack them so the adjacent ones really are indistinguishable-in-cost, or does he cheat somewhere? Where does the reading strain — I can hear the objection that Stevens isn't honestly indifferent at the time, he's a self-deceiver whose profession hands him ready-made language (all that dignity talk) for discounting what he feels, which is not what the self-torturer case assumes at all? Does the novel actually favor one side: is there a scene where a single step is framed as decisively worse (the maid dismissal? the night Miss Kenton announces her engagement and Stevens stays upstairs?), which would support the hidden-fine-grained-differences camp — or does Stevens's inability to rank his lived life against the counterfactual one at the end read more like Temkin's intransitivity? And finally, tell me what the novel adds that the papers can't — I keep thinking the answer is that fiction lets you have a narrator whose indistinguishability judgments are motivated rather than sincere, which is a case the philosophers never run, but maybe I'm flattering my own project. I'd love to hear if that's actually what the novel does or if I'm mining it for my own book.
