I am a novelist at work on a chapter in which my protagonist — a woman who has lived in three countries and is visiting an American city — refuses to tip at a restaurant on principle: she believes tipping entrenches an unjust wage structure, and she would rather be thought rude than be complicit in it. Her friend accuses her of treating a local convention as though it were optional etiquette, when in her host country it functions as part of the wage itself. I want the argument in this chapter — put in the friend's mouth, though I do not want him to read as a mouthpiece — to establish a genuine moral obligation to follow the everyday conventions of the place one is visiting, not merely a prudential or aesthetic reason to do so. Please construct that argument.

A few constraints, so that the chapter does not collapse into a lecture:

1. The argument must work at the level of conventions generally — tipping, but also queueing, greeting customs, whether one crosses against the light — and must explain why the obligation transfers across cases whose content differs so much, without reducing all of them to one uniform kind of duty.

2. It must take my protagonist's strongest objection seriously: some conventions are locally contested or even locally regretted, and a visitor's principled refusal might be a way of joining the local argument rather than violating the local practice. If the argument cannot accommodate this, it should concede the point and mark exactly where its border lies.

3. I would prefer the argument to run through the idea of a convention as a mutually expected, self-sustaining practice — the way two rowers can fall into matching strokes without anyone promising anything — rather than through law, promise, or mere politeness; but if you think that route is weaker than some other, say so and take the stronger one.

4. The conclusion should concern visitors specifically: whether those who did not grow into the practice, will not stay to bear its costs, and did not choose where it binds them, are obligated in the same way and to the same degree as locals. If the argument yields different weights of obligation for the two, that asymmetry should be stated openly, not papered over.

Finally, since the chapter will be read by people who already disagree with its conclusion, tell me plainly where the reasoning is most likely to fail, and what case or consideration — pressed by a careful opponent — would lead you to withdraw the claim that visitors are obligated to conform.
