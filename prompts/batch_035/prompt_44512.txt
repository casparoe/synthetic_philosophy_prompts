At our last reading group I got cornered by someone who builds dialogue systems for a living, and I have been stewing for a week about the argument he made on the Turing test: each premise sounded fine as he said it, and the conclusion is one I will not accept. It runs as follows. (1) Turing proposed the imitation game as a replacement for the question whether machines think, on the ground that thought in another system is never inspected directly; whatever we know about another mind we know from conduct, and that is how we settle the question for each other. (2) The original form of the game drives this home, he says: the interrogator's task was first to distinguish a man from a woman through typed conversation alone, with the machine later substituted into the man's chair, and nobody treats the man's success at impersonating the woman as evidence that he thinks; so the test's whole evidential force must come from conversational indistinguishability itself. (3) Conversational indistinguishability, in turn, is a fact about outputs, a stream of typed replies to typed questions. (4) If a machine's outputs are indistinguishable from a thoughtful human's for long enough and across enough contexts, our evidence that the machine thinks is exactly as good as our evidence that any human interlocutor thinks, and if that is not enough for the machine it is not enough for the human either. (5) The same standard cuts the other way: a system whose outputs are distinguishable from a human's, whether slower, simpler, more repetitive, or simply absent, gives us no grounds for attributing thought to it, since conduct is all there is to go on. (6) He then applies (5) where I balk: the patient with global aphasia who cannot sustain conversation at all; the pre-linguistic infant; the locked-in patient who types one character every few seconds with her eye, so that her replies are distinguishable by latency, brevity, and fatigue long before they are distinguishable by content; even, he says, anyone speaking to us through an interpreter or over a bad line. (7) We are exactly as entitled to attribute thought to those people as to anyone we chat with, and if pressed he would extend it to the family dog. (8) Therefore (5) is false, and with it the behaviorist reading of the test in (1) and (2); the test cannot be what Turing wanted it to be, a criterion that settles the machine question by settling what the word means. He calls this the test devouring itself: if the pass condition were really the condition for thought, we would owe most of humanity an apology, so the condition was never tracking thought, and the operationalization fails on its own terms. My suspicion is that something moves between (3) and (5) that never appeared in the premises, but every time I try to name it he has a reply ready, and I would much rather keep (1) and (7) than accept (8). Tell me which step to give up, why it looked fine to me when I accepted it, and what the alternatives cost, including what it costs to give up (5) if that is where the pressure really goes. Please write in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a letter, and in American English.
