I'm a third-year PhD student in comparative political economy, writing a dissertation chapter on tax compliance, and I keep running into a conceptual question I don't have the vocabulary for. Maybe you can help me think it through, because my advisor's favorite line -- "institutions are congealed beliefs" -- is doing a lot of hand-waving in my drafts.

Here's the contrast that's bugging me. In Denmark and Sweden, the tax authority pre-fills your return: your employer, bank, and pension fund have already reported your income to the state, the agency calculates what you owe, and most people just comfirm it, in Denmark from your phone in a couple of taps. In the US, you assemble your own numbers (there's third-party reporting too, sure, but a fair amount rides on what you choose to tell them -- cash tips, side-gig income, how much of that home-office deduction is really an office), you sign under penalty of perjury, and the IRS audits only a small fraction of returns, something like 0.4 percent overall. The standard story in my field is that the first arrangement embodies the Scandinavian high-trust citizen-state relationship and the second embodies American suspicion of government. But when I look at what the practices actualy require of citizens, the story seems almost inverted. The Nordic system leaves nearly nothing to the taxpayer's honesty at the moment of filing -- the state has already seen your financial life, so trust in the citizen is doing no work there. The American system asks the citizen to volunteer information the government does not independently know, and then mostly takes your word for it. So which practice genuinely presupposes trust, and in which sense: trust as a presumption of honesty, trust as a legitimacy that makes comprehensive third-party reporting acceptable in the first place, or something else entirely? Is the standard story just wrong, or is it tracking trust at a different level -- upstream, where trust is what licenses the reporting infrastructure rather than being needed at the filing event? And a related worry: I've been told the US system is also compatible with a pure principal-agent story (assume opportunism, verify a sample, punish visibly), which would mean the practice doesn't presuppose trust at all and my whole chapter is confused.

What I'd like, in sections in this order: first "Reconstruction" (about 250 words) -- lay out what view of the citizen-state relationship each practice strictly needs in order to make sense; second, "Presupposed vs. compatible" (about 200 words) -- distinguish what each system genuinely presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, since I suspect the same practice could be licensed by several theories (deterrence, principal-agent, civic-republican); third, "The asymmetry" (about 150 words) -- say whether the Nordic system presupposes more trust or less, given that it verifies nearly everything, and at which level trust is operating; fourth, "What would change" (about 150 words) -- what tax administration would look like if you swapped in a pure deterrence view or a pure honor-system view, and whether either arrangement is stable without the other; and finally a short "Bottom line" (under 100 words) on whether "institutions are congealed beliefs" does real work here or is just vibes.
