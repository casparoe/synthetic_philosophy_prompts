My closest friend is finishing a physics degree, and after a long argument over dinner I walked home with a question I can't put down. She was describing how her curriculum handles superseded theories, and the pattern is stranger than I'd assumed. Newtonian mechanics gets drilled as the standard tool for orbits and pendulums even though the course on relativity declares absolute space and time to be mistakes; the wave theory of light was originally worked out for waves rippling through an ether, and when the ether was abandoned the equations were kept essentially intact; even the mathematics of heat conduction was first derived in a theory that treated heat as a fluid pervading bodies, and that fluid is now said never to have existed, yet the equations are in every textbook. So the same institution, in the same degree, teaches that these theories were wrong about what there is and right enough about the numbers to keep using forever. At one point she said something like "so we keep the structure and throw away the furniture," and dropped the phrase "structural realism," which is how I ended up reading philosophy of science at 2 a.m. instead of sleeping.

What I want to know is what this teaching practice actually presupposes, because my friend and I couldn't agree even on that much. She thought it obviously presupposes that a theory's equations can be approximately true while everything the theory says exists is flatly false. I pushed back: calling an equation "approximately true" already assumes you know what it is that's true — if it's just a pattern in the numbers that keeps surviving theory change, maybe the practice doesn't presuppose anything so strong, and a thoroughgoing pragmatist or instrumentalist could run the same curriculum without embarrassment. And there's a second wrinkle, which is that the same textbooks teach electrons, quarks, and fields in a confident realist register, as things that exist, while treating the discarded ontologies of the past as never having existed. Is the practice then committed to some asymmetry — current ontology believed, past ontology discounted, mathematics trusted across the board — and is that combination actually coherent, or is it just historical accident dressed up as philosophy?

So: reconstruct the view of scientific knowledge and of truth that this pedagogical practice needs in order to make sense, and tell me where it is genuinely underdetermined — which rival philosophies could license the same curriculum without strain. Then say where the practice presupposes things that don't fit together, if anywhere: the confidence about current unobservables, the agnosticism about superseded ones, and the loyalty to the surviving mathematics seem to pull in different directions. Finally, suppose we replaced whatever implicit view it rests on with a straightforward instrumentalism — what, concretely, would have to change in how the curriculum is taught, and what would be immune to change? I want the answer to distinguish sharply between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely sits comfortably with, because that distinction is exactly where my friend and I got stuck.
