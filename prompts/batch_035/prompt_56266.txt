I'm writing a term paper for a graduate seminar on Davidson and have ended up with an argument from radical interpretation that I can't bring myself to accept, even though each step still looks fine to me when I read it on its own.

Here is the argument. (1) Radical interpretation must start from scratch: the interpreter initially knows neither what a speaker's sentences mean nor what the speaker believes, and must fix both at once from the pattern of what the speaker holds true in observable circumstances. (2) Given this circularity, the only way to break into the meaning-belief circle is the principle of charity: the interpreter must take most of what the speaker holds true to actually be true, since without a background of agreement there is nothing against which to distinguish the speaker's mistakes from the interpreter's. (3) So a community is interpretable at all only if most of its beliefs are true. (4) Interpretation does not sort communities into the mostly right and the mostly wrong: we understand phlogiston chemists and pre-Copernican astronomers using exactly the techniques we use on our neighbors, and if anything they are easier to interpret, their false beliefs being local and theoretical rather than pervasive. (5) So the phlogiston chemists, the pre-Copernicans, a village of deceived brains-in-vats — any community we can understand at all — must have mostly true beliefs. (6) But that appears to make it a deliverance of a priori philosophy that no scientific revolution ever involved large-scale communal error about its own subject matter, which seems to settle the history of science from the armchair, and I don't believe philosophy can do that.

My tentative escape is to distinguish charity as a maxim of method from charity as a truth about the interpreted: perhaps the interpreter is only instructed to project agreement until a workable theory of meaning emerges, and the resulting theory assigns truth-conditions relative to whatever is actually causing the utterances — so the brain-in-vat's tree-talk comes out mostly true of the computer states behind it, and communal falsity is preserved by relativizing what the community's words are about. But I worry this saves truth only by changing the subject, since the dupes take themselves to believe things about trees, and I can't see how the move avoids implying that even my own beliefs are about whatever happens to cause them rather than about trees. Tell me which premise of the argument to reject and what rejecting it costs; say whether you think the equivocation, if there is one, sits between (2) and (3), between (3) and (5), or somewhere I haven't looked; and evaluate my proposed escape — in particular whether it is a recognisable position in the literature or a confusion. As you do this, please keep separate what is more or less settled among people who work on Davidson from what is genuinely contested: I understand, for example, that it is disputed whether charity requires truth rather than mere consistency and agreement, and whether the anti-skeptical argument of the coherence-theory paper actually succeeds, and I would rather not have one of those readings quietly presented to me as the consensus.
