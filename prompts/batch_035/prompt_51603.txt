Question:
"Buddhism teaches anatta, 'no-self'. (a) Explain what the no-self doctrine claims. (b) Explain how, on the traditional Buddhist view, rebirth is possible without a persisting self. (c) Some philosophers have suggested that the Buddhist position amounts to a Parfit-style reductionism about personal identity. Assess that suggestion."

Student's answer:
"The doctrine of anatta holds that there is no self. The Buddha analysed the person into five aggregates — form, feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness — and none of these is the self, nor is there a sixth item over and above them. So the self is simply a bundle of the aggregates, which is essentially the conclusion Hume reached when he looked within and found only perceptions, never a perceiver. Because the aggregates are impermanent, the person is a stream of events rather than a persisting thing, and this is what allows rebirth without a soul. My craving and volitions now condition a new life; since the causal chain links them, the person who is reborn is, strictly, the same person as me — the tradition says the relation is 'neither the same nor different', but what Buddhism denies is only that there is an extra soul in addition to the stream. The Milindapañha makes the same point with the chariot: 'chariot' is a convenient designation for the parts arranged in a certain way, and likewise 'person' is a designation for the aggregates. The designation is useful, which is why the Buddha spoke freely of persons at the conventional level. Here Parfit agrees: for him personal identity is not what matters, what matters is psychological connectedness, and Buddhist rebirth is a case of that — I persist into the next life to the extent that connectedness carries across, which is why past-life memories are the main evidence for rebirth. Both views, then, locate the deep facts in the stream and treat 'same person' as a convention; the difference is ethical, since only the Buddha draws the soteriological conclusion that seeing through the convention is part of liberation."
