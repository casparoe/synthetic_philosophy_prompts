I'm a novelist working on a book set in and around a youth justice conferencing scheme — the sort, now used in a number of countries, in which a teenager who has committed a burglary or an assault is referred before sentencing to a conference: the young person, the victim, and family members on both sides sit in a circle with a facilitator, each speaks in turn about what happened and what it did to them, and the outcome — an apology, restitution, undertakings about school or work — is agreed in the room rather than imposed by a judge. The scheme's facilitators invoke the southern African idea of ubuntu, which they render, roughly, as the claim that a person is a person through other persons, so that a wrong is a rupture in a web of relations and the business of justice is repair rather than the infliction of proportionate suffering. As a novelist I need to know what picture of the person my scenes can honestly depict. What does the conferencing practice actually presuppose? Does it strictly require that relational conception of personhood — on which personhood is achieved and maintained within relationships, and wrongdoing damages the wrongdoer's own standing as much as the victim's — or could a straightforwardly individualist view, on which persons are self-standing bearers of rights, license the very same procedure for different reasons, say as victim empowerment or efficient settlement? And is the practice internally consistent, given that the same courts that refer these cases still sentence the ones that remain in court on principles of individual desert? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, mark where the practice is philosophically underdetermined so that rival theories would license it equally, and tell me what would visibly change in the conference room itself if the relational view were replaced by the individualist one.
