A couple weekends ago I had two friends over, Maya and Josh, for a blind tasting thing I put together (mostly cheap bottles, a few nice ones, brown bags, we definately overbought), and they got into it about whether Maya's training has literally changed what wine tastes like to her. Maya's worked in a tasting room for three vintages and is studying for her sommelier cert; Josh is a psych grad student. The whole evening kind of went of the rails into an argument that went roughly like this:

1. Maya said that after three vintages she tastes things the rest of us simply can't — she picked out cork taint in one bottle and brett in another within seconds, and she said it's not that she's faster at flagging problems, the flawed wine actually tastes different to her: the mustiness is right there in her mouth, she couldn't ignore it if she tried.

2. Josh said that's a confusion between perception and post-perceptual judgment: her tongue takes in the same molecules as anyone else's, and what training gave her is a set of concepts she applies to the signal very quickly — like a mechanic who "hears" the engine problem, which he insisted is fast classification, not different hearing.

3. Maya answered that it's not just speed, it's that she can't un-taste it. She compared it to the moment you finally hear the misheard lyric — once you know the words, the song literally sounds different, and you can't go back to hearing the old nonsense. Same, she said, with a wine she used to love: now it tastes obviously sweet and flabby, the experience itself changed, and no amount of "I'm just classifying faster" captures that.

4. Josh granted the lyric case but said flavor is a terrible test case — taste is so smothered in cognition and affect already that finding penetration there wouldn't tell us anything about the senses the debate is actually about, like vision, where the question is whether what you know changes what you see (color, motion, that stuff). He also said what Maya's describing is perceptual learning — a slow rewiring of the perceiver over years — which he wants to keep seperate from cognitive penetration proper, where your current beliefs and expectations are supposed to flow into the experience right now.

5. Maya's comeback was that the line he's drawing is scholastic: for every purpose that actually matters to her — trusting her own palate, who gets hired, whether critics' tasting notes mean anything — the wine tastes different to her either way, and whether that happened over three years or three seconds changes nothing downstream.

6. Josh said it changes everything, actually: if your expectation is what's producing the flavor, then your tasting can't be used to check the expectation — that's circular, the tasting note justifies itself. That, he said, is the whole reason anyone cares about the distinction in the first place.

Then they both looked at me and I honestly had nothing. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right at each step? In particular I'd want to know (a) whether Josh's learning/penetration distinction in step 4 is a real distinction or a dodge, (b) whether he's right that flavor is a degenerate test case that can't tell us anything about vision, and (c) whether his circularity point in step 6 survives Maya's practical point in step 5, since those two seemed to be aiming past each other.
