My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether the brain is a computer, and I came away unable to tell which parts of what he said are standard doctrine and which were rhetorical fast balls. He is a programmer, and near the end of the argument he laid out his position in a compact statement, which I reconstruct here as faithfully as I can:

"Computationalism just is the claim that the brain is a computer and the mind is the software running on it. The Church-Turing thesis proved that any physical process whatever can be simulated by a Turing machine, so in principle a sufficiently detailed simulation of a brain must itself be conscious — that's what substrate independence means: the same computation yields the same mental states whether it is implemented in neurons, silicon, or hydraulic pipes. Searle's Chinese room showed that syntax alone never yields semantics, but that objection only bites against symbol-shuffling programs, and the brain is not a program, it is the hardware, so Searle's argument does not touch the view I actually hold. And the weather analogy — that a simulation of a storm makes nothing wet — misses the point, because unlike weather, the brain is digital at the level that matters."

I would like a point-by-point audit of this statement. For each of its claims, please tell me which of the following it is: something a careful computationalist would accept as stated; a defensible core claim wrapped in an overstatement (if so, identify the overstatement); a conflation of two things a careful defender keeps apart; or something defenders of the view themselves deny. In particular, I want to know three things.

First, his appeal to the Church-Turing thesis. Is it right that the thesis is about what can be simulated in principle, and is it right to say it was proved — and does anything in it, or in any result like it, license the step from "a brain can be simulated" to "a simulation of a brain would be conscious," or does that step require an additional, independent commitment that he never states?

Second, his reply to Searle. It struck me during the argument that this reply sits badly with his earlier claims: he had just insisted that the mind is substrate-independent software that could run on pipes, and then answered Searle by saying the brain is hardware rather than a program. Please assess whether these two halves of his statement are jointly coherent, whether either is how computationalists typically respond to the Chinese room, and whether his reply, if taken seriously, would cost him the substrate-independence claim he wants.

Third, his dismissal of the weather analogy. Does the analogy target exactly the step from simulation to duplication that he relies on, such that answering it by asserting that the brain is digital at the relevant level assumes what was at issue — or is there a genuine disanalogy between brains and weather that a defender could invoke here without begging the question?

Finally, please conclude with an overall verdict: stripped of the overstatements and conflations you identify, how much of his statement would a working philosopher of mind sign, and does his headline conclusion — that a sufficiently detailed simulation of a brain would itself be conscious — follow from the parts that survive the audit? For context: I am inclined to think he is broadly right but argued sloppily, so please correct me where that inclination is wrong.
