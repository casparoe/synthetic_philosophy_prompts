A popular book on probability I picked up secondhand has a section on Bertrand's paradox, and I want to know how much of it I can trust before I repeat any of it to my study group. Here's the gist as close as I can remember:

If you draw a chord on a circle "at random," what's the probablity it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? The book says Bertrand showed the principle of indifference -- the rule that when you have no reason to favor one outcome over another, assign them equal probabilities -- gives three different answers to this one question depending on how you set it up: 1/3 if you pick two random points on the circumference and join them, 1/4 if you pick a random radius and a random point along it, and 1/2 if you pick a random point inside the circle and draw the chord having that point as its midpoint. Since the principle yields contradictory answers to a single question, it's inconsistent, and it was basically abandoned after that. The book then says this was widely taken to kill the idea of an uninformative prior, so that all priors are now understood as subjective -- you just bring your own assumptions and there's nothing to say about which are better before the data arrive. Then there's a paragraph saying the whole thing is really an artifact of sloppy wording: "at random" doesn't specify an experiment, and once you specify the physical procedure (throwing straws at the circle from far away, say) you get a perfectly definite answer, so the paradox is dissolved rather than solved. And a throwaway line that Jaynes cleaned up whatever was left anyway with some requirement about invariance. It ends with the moral that prior probability assignments are always arbitrary, so Bayesian statistics resting on flat priors is on shakier ground than its fans admit.

Some of this rings true, but a couple things felt off even to me -- like the claim that the principle of indifference is flat-out inconsistent. My impression was always that the real lesson is that "a random chord" is an underspecified description, which is different from saying the principle contradicts itself. And I half remember some other example from a philosophy course, something about a factory producing cubes and whether you should be indifferent over side length or over volume, which makes me suspect the problem doesn't vanish just by demanding a physical setup. Can you go through the book's account piece by piece: which parts a philosopher of probability would accept as stated, which are subtle misstatements, which are oversimplifications of something real, and whether the final moral about priors being arbitrary actually follows from the parts that survive? Please be careful to seperate what's settled from what people who actually work on this genuinely disagree about -- my sense is that both the "just specify the experiment" line and whatever Jaynes was up to are themselves contested, and I'd rather know where the live debates are than have one side handed to me as the consensus.
