I've spent years on rationalist blogs and forums, and on welfare policy I keep hitting the same wall: the debate is always empirical — does workfare raise employment, does conditionality save money, are sanctions effective — while nobody asks what the institutions themselves presuppose. So let me lay out the practice side by side, as I understand it. Unemployment benefits in contributory systems were originally sold as insurance: you pay premiums while working, and when you lose your job you draw on what you paid in — and an insurer doesn't demand that you keep shopping around for the risk you insured against. Yet the same systems now attach mandatory job-search, documented weekly, on pain of sanctions, to exactly those benefits — while attaching no work requirements to someone living passively on investment income, and while withdrawing benefits steeply as claimants begin to earn, so that the poor face effective marginal tax rates that would cause open revolt if applied to the middle class. What does this pattern presuppose: that a benefit is payment for work rather than compensation for loss, that desert rather than contract grounds the entitlement, or simply an empirical premise that the unemployed specifically — unlike rentiers or pensioners — present a moral-hazard problem requiring surveillance? Reconstruct the implicit theory, tell me whether the parts are consistent — can the same payment be an earned entitlement when the state justifies taxing for it and discretionary charity when it justifies sanctioning? — and say where the design is underdetermined, so that several rival theories of the undeserving poor would each license it. Then tell me what would have to change in the design if the presupposed view were replaced by a purely insurance-based one.
