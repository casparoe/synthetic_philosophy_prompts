Ok here's my situation. I'm a mid-level analyst at a state environmental agency, and last month at a public hearing on our proposed restriction on a class of insecticides (the pollinator debate, think neonicotinoids-adjacent) a policy analyst from a free-market think tank stood up and said he *agrees* with the precautionary principle — agrees with it harder than we do — and then ran this argument at me:

1. Precaution, properly stated, reverses the burden of proof: when an activity could plausibly cause serious or irreversible harm, the proponent of the activity has to show it's safe before it goes forward, and scientific uncertainty about the harm counts in favor of holding off. (He said he was paraphrasing the 1998 Wingspread statement, the activist formulation, and as far as I can tell that's roughly what it says — burden on the proponent, uncertainty weighs against proceeding.)
2. Now generalize: for literally any activity whatsoever, there is some mechanism by which it could plausibly cause serious or irreversible harm — the whole lesson precaution is built on is that novel activities have unforeseen consequences that outrun our evidence.
3. So every activity, everywhere, needs a prior safety case from its proponent before it can proceed.
4. But not acting is also an activity. A status quo is maintained by continuous ongoing choices, and blocking an activity has its own tail risks — he'd done his homework, noting that banning our target insecticides means more crop loss, more land brought under cultivation to make up the yield, possibly more total harm to insects than the status quo.
5. So in any binary choice, both options are activities that each need a prior safety case.
6. A burden-of-proof rule that lands on both sides of every choice gives no guidance at all — it forbids everything, so it forbids nothing.
7. Therefore the precautionary principle, stated as a burden-shifting rule, is literally empty, and our rule can't claim a precautionary justification. We'd have to justify it on ordinary risk-benefit grounds, which he was quick to add he thinks we lose on.

The room liked it way more than I did, and I get why — every step sounds like something my own agency says. My boss wants a written response and I keep circling without landing on the step I actually want to reject. For what it's worth, I'd rather keep the burden-shifting core in (1) if I possibly can, and I'm attached to the idea that uncertainty counts against proceeding. But I also don't want to save the principle by gutting it into "just weigh the risks, all of them" — at that point the word precaution is doing no work and the think tank guy wins anyway, just slower.

So: where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Walk me through the steps and tell me which one to reject and what rejecting it costs me, and pay attention to any step that's fine as stated but hides a shift between two readings of some word (plausible, activity, uncertainty, whatever it turns out to be) — I want to know which readings are doing the work and which reading the argument quietly swaps in where. Also tell me whether my response survives when he inevitably runs the same move against whatever weaker principle I retreat to, and what the strongest version of my position looks like that can't be run in reverse against itself. And please write the answer in American English.
