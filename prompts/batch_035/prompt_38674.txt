Exam question: "Explain Susan Wolf's argument that moral sainthood is an undesirable personal ideal, discussing the two models of the moral saint she distinguishes and the role nonmoral virtues play in her argument. Does her conclusion follow?"

Student's answer: Wolf argues that moral sainthood, meaning perfect or near-perfect moral goodness, is not a desirable ideal for a person to strive toward. She distinguishes two models of the moral saint: the loving moral saint, whose benevolence and warmth are organized around caring for others' welfare, and the Kantian moral saint, who is committed to duty and does the right thing from principle. Both kinds of saint are, morally speaking, as good as a person can be — that's what makes them saints — but Wolf says neither is attractive as a personal ideal.

Her main argument is about the nonmoral virtues. A moral saint's character and life must be devoted entirely to moral goodness, so there's no room left for nonmoral excellences like a good sense of humor, athletic or cooking skill, or appreciation of art and music. If every trait and every moment is put in the service of improving the welfare of others or doing one's duty, traits like wit get crowded out. And crucially, the moral saint wouldn't even be morally better for having those nonmoral virtues — playing great tennis or being funny doesn't make you a morally better person — so from the moral point of view there's no reason to want them, which is exactly why the moral saint lacks them.

Ultimately Wolf's deeper point is that no actual human being could sustain that level of selflessness anyway, so the ideal of moral sainthood is incoherent — no one could really be the loving saint, since constantly putting others first is psychologically impossible for a finite being. And since the ideal is incoherent, her conclusion is that whenever nonmoral values like humor, friendship, or art conflict with moral demands, the nonmoral values win out. Morality is only one value among many, so we're entitled to let it be outweighed whenever our personal ideals pull the other way.
