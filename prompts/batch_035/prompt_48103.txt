At last week's colloquium a colleague from linguistics pressed an argument on me that, if sound, would demolish Quine's indeterminacy of translation, and I confess I cannot locate its weakest joint. It runs as follows.

(1) Quine's thesis is that all possible behavioral evidence available to a field linguist underdetermines translation: the native's word "gavagai" may be rendered as "rabbit," "undetached rabbit part," or "rabbit stage," and no fact of the matter decides among these manuals.
(2) The very same kind of evidence — dispositions to assent, patterns of discriminative application, every observable difference in behavior the speaker could ever manifest — is all that is available to the speaker herself concerning her own word "rabbit."
(3) If such evidence underdetermines the reference of the native's word, it equally underdetermines the reference of the speaker's own word, since it is evidence of the same kind.
(4) So there is no fact of the matter about what any speaker's terms refer to, including the terms of mathematics.
(5) It then follows that it is indeterminate whether a number theorist asserting what she calls Fermat's Last Theorem has asserted something about addition or about a gerrymandered function that agrees with addition on every case she or anyone has ever checked but diverges thereafter — and hence indeterminate whether the theorem has been proved at all.
(6) But it is not indeterminate whether Fermat's Last Theorem has been proved; that was settled in the 1990s.
(7) Therefore the indeterminacy thesis is false.

Each step looks defensible to me: (2) seems to follow from the refusal to recognize private facts of meaning beyond what speakers could display, (5) appears to be nothing more than Kripke's quaddition puzzle transposed from rule-following to reference, and (6) is among the firmest claims we have. I would very much like to keep both (1) and (6), though presumably I cannot have both. Tell me which step or inference to reject, why it nevertheless looked irresistible, and what rejecting it costs. Structure your reply as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
