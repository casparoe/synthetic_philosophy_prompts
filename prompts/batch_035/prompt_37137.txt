I've been arguing with a colleague about the 'man of his time' defence as applied to Jefferson's slaveholding, and I'd like your credence that it substantially succeeds, with the reasoning fully decomposed. By 'substantially succeeds' I mean something specific: not that his upbringing causally explains his conduct, but that it does real excusing work — enough, say, to cut his blameworthiness by at least half. An explanation is cheap; an excuse is a normative achievement.

Please decompose along at least these lines, giving each component a rough probability and one line of justification:

1. The general excusing question: the probability that being raised in a society where a practice is near-universal and morally unchallenged excuses an agent's participation, rather than merely explaining it. Here I'd want you to confront the symmetry problem — the objection that everyone is a person of their time, so the defence proves too much unless it can discriminate between cases — and the question of whether it matters that the agent never encountered a live alternative versus encountered one and found it socially costly.

2. The application question: the probability that Jefferson in particular satisfied whatever excusing conditions you identified in (1), given that abolitionist arguments were in circulation among his contemporaries, that he himself wrote critically of slavery, that he continued to own people, profit from their labor, and free only a small fraction even upon his death, and that some contemporaries — Quaker abolitionists, some of the founders in the North — did take the other side at genuine cost to themselves.

3. The framing question: the probability that blameworthiness is the wrong target for historical judgment altogether — that we should be assessing character or drawing forward-looking lessons rather than assigning blame — in which case the defence may be answering a question we shouldn't be asking, and the credence in (1) and (2) becomes partly beside the point.

Combine the components into an overall number, tell me which one drives it most, and identify what evidence or argument would move that component the furthest. My colleague's number is around 0.6; mine is closer to 0.15, and we suspect we're not really disagreeing about the facts but about which component matters — if you can tell us where two reasonable people at 0.6 and 0.15 most likely diverge, that would be worth more than the number itself. And if you think the decomposition is misframed — for instance, that excusing conditions and the availability of alternatives can't be cleanly separated — say so, but still commit to a number for the composite question.
