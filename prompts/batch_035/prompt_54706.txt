I've recently taken over teaching duties for the future-generations unit of an upper-level bioethics seminar — fourteen students, a single ninety-minute slot — and I'd like your help designing the session's centrepiece activity. My own interest in this material has just become rather personal: a close friend is deciding whether to attempt conception whilst taking a medication that raises the risk of birth defects, and working through the ethics of that with her has made me realise how differently the nonidentity problem lands when you meet it as a decision rather than as a puzzle. My students, by contrast, treat it as a debating-game: they can recite the move — the depletion policy changes who exists, the people who exist under it have lives worth living, so on a counterfactual account nobody is harmed — without ever feeling its bite. The session follows two weeks on Parfit, so they know the territory; what I want is for the problem to happen to them before anyone names it.

Concretely, I'd like a role-play in which the class forms a deliberative committee for a fictional island republic choosing between a conservation policy and a rapid-depletion policy (the depletion path yields a short resource boom, after which everyone is permanently poorer, but — crucially — the two policies lead to different people existing a few generations on, all of whose lives remain worth living). I want each student to receive a role card for a named future person, with the following distribution built in: most cards describe people who exist only under one policy or the other, with the depletion-born characters leading recognisably worse but still decent lives; a few cards describe people who exist under both policies and are harmed by depletion in the ordinary identity-preserving way; and — this is the wrinkle I most want you to keep — at least two characters who exist under both policies and are materially better off under depletion because their families captured the boom. The committee must reach a verdict, and I'd like a second round in which the same committee confronts a policy that harms only identity-affecting parties, so the students feel the contrast between the two kinds of case by bumping into it rather than by being told.

Please give me the full runnable package: materials and preparation, a timeline for the ninety minutes, rules for deliberation (including what information is common knowledge and what stays on the cards), and a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the philosophy — the counterfactual account of harm, the person-affecting intuition, the way the "better-off under depletion" characters complicate any simple appeal to harm, and how the students' own objections, pressed hard, will have trouble identifying a victim. I'd also like your honest assessment of what the activity cannot reproduce (I suspect the temporality is the problem: nobody's existence is actually made contingent by the vote, and I suspect some students will see through that) and practical advice on the two failure modes I anticipate: students abandoning their characters to philosophise at the table, and the vote simply descending into a utilitarian headcount of the role cards. If you can suggest a payoff or stake structure that keeps the deliberation honest without real money — even something as simple as the facilitator's later report card determining whose character's descendants "get written into" the epilogue — I'd welcome that too.
