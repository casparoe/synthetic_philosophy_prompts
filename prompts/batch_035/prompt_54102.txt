I got assigned this one and I keep going back and forth on it, so I'd like a second opinion before section on Thursday. The practice question was:

State the zombie (conceivability) argument against physicalism, making each premise explicit. Then present what you take to be the strongest objection to the argument, and explain how a defender of the argument could best reply to that objection.

And here's the answer I have to grade:

A philosophical zombie is a being physically identical to me down to the last particle but with no phenomenal consciousness, no inner experience at all. The argument runs: (1) If physicalism is true, the complete physical truth fixes every other truth, so a physical duplicate of our world with no consciousness is impossible. (2) But zombies are clearly conceivable — I can imagine a world physically just like ours where nobody has any inner life, and there is no contradiction in that idea. (3) Whatever is conceivable is possible. (4) So zombies are possible, and therefore physicalism is false.

The strongest objection is that we cannot actually concieve of zombies. When I try to imagine my own zombie twin, I end up picturing someone who winces and yells when stabbed and happily tells you all about his rich inner life — and that person seems conscious. So the imagined zombie always ends up seeming conscious, which means the supposed conceivability is an illusion: we are really just imagining a conscious person and mislabeling him. Conceivability turns out to be just a report of our psychological ability to picture things, and that ability is a bad guide to possibility.

The defender's best reply is that the argument works anyway because of Mary. In Nagel's famous black-and-white room, Mary knows all the physical facts about color but still learns something new when she sees red for the first time, which shows the physical facts don't fix everything. The zombie argument is really just the same argument in modal form, so any objection to one is an objection to the other.

Finally, Dennett showed that the whole argument begs the question by assuming that physical truths can't entail phenomenal truths, and this is now generally accepted, so what's really left is a puzzle about why the argument feels so convincing.

What grade would you give this out of 20, and what specific comments justify it? I want to go point by point through the premises, the objection, and the reply rather than just get a general vibe.
