So here's my situation: I'm a staff biologist at a state wildlife agency, and I'm the one who has to prepare recommendations for our listing committee about whether populations count as "species" under our state endangered species act. The statute just says "species" and doesn't define it, which means every decision gets fought over. We had a hearing last spring where a consultant for a developer argued a bat population was "just a subspecies, and subspecies aren't real anyway" while a conservation group's expert said it was a diagnosable evolutionary lineage and cited a bunch of population genetics papers nobody on the committee could evaluate. The committee is five people, two of them political appointees with no genetics background, we meet quarterly, and the stakes are high because listing triggers permitting requirements that people litigate. I need something better than "go with whatever the most recent published paper says."

What I'd love is a decision procedure the committee can actually run: a set of questions to ask about a candidate population, in order, with some guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and -- this is the part I care about most -- where it should stop and say "this is a judgment call, not a biology question," because I think half our fights are actually about values (how much do we care about local adaptation vs. how many total animals exist) dressed up as taxonomy. It should draw on the actual mess that is the species problem: that reproductive isolation comes in degrees, that gene flow doesn't switch off cleanly at some threshold, that phylogenetic and morphological and biological species concepts can give different answers for the same organisms, and that some lineages (all-female hybrid salamander complexes, for instance) just don't fit the reproductive-isolation picture at all.

Then please run it on three real-ish cases for us. First: an all-female unisexual salamander lineage that persists by stealing sperm from a related sexual species but contributes no genes back. Second: an isolated island population that looks distinct but hybridizes freely with the mainland form at one contact zone -- the "is it a species or a variant with a range gap" problem. And third, a case I'm dreading: two populations currently listed separately that new genomic work suggests are one gene flow continuum, such that if we follow the procedure it says lump them, which would mean one of them comes OFF the endangered list. That last one is the answer I won't like, so I want to see whether the procedure really gives it or whether there's a defensible stopping point before it. Also flag honestly where the procedure will misfire -- e.g. when a species-splitting recommendation would just be ignored because nobody has budget for two recovery plans. Keep it usable by a smart non-specialist: no steps that require the committee to run its own genomic analyses, since we'd just get whatever data already exists in the literature. Thanks!!
