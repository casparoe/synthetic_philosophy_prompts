I edit a decision-theory quiz column for a magazine aimed at thoughtful non-specialists: readers comfortable with the phrase 'expected value', but with no formal training. The next instalment concerns how people actually respond to tiny probabilities of enormous stakes, and I want a quiz rather than an essay, so that readers are sorted by what they do with cases rather than by which camp they already identify with. Please write it to the following specification.

Eight to ten questions. Each question is a concrete case with two or three answer options describing what the reader would do or which claim they would endorse — never a question about which position they like the name of. The cases should include, roughly: a mugging in which the promised payoff is scaled up until the arithmetic favours handing over the wallet; a donation case in which one option certainly prevents a moderate amount of suffering and another offers a minuscule chance of eliminating a vastly greater amount; a St Petersburg-style lottery and what, if anything, the reader would pay to play it; a case in which individually negligible risks accumulate over many repetitions; and at least one case that forces the reader to say whether there is any probability so small that they would refuse to act on it even as the stakes are made arbitrarily large — and, if so, whether the refusal is about the probability itself, the size of the payoff, the quality of the evidence, or the character of the person making the offer. You may design further cases of your own, but every question must be answerable without technical vocabulary, and no two questions may probe the same distinction.

Then a scoring key mapping patterns of answers onto positions, which must include at minimum: the straightforward fanatical expected-value maximiser, who holds that one is rationally required to take the small-chance option whenever its expectation is higher, however small the chance and however ordinary the alternatives; the bounded-utility view, on which sufficiently large payoffs stop mattering in proportion to their size; threshold views, on which probabilities below some floor simply do not get acted upon; the debunker, who holds that one of the two competing intuitions in these cases is an evolved bias, and who must say which; and the evidential response, on which the right move is neither to cap utilities nor to cap probabilities, but to revise one's credence in the offer as the numbers grow more outlandish. The key must do two further things: identify which questions are genuinely diagnostic, in the sense that the positions come apart on them, and flag any pair of positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate — in particular, say whether risk-aversion proper is orthogonal to fanaticism, and, if so, how this quiz tends to conflate them. Where some coherent combination of answers corresponds to a position nobody actually defends, note it and speculate briefly about why the combination is unoccupied.

One final constraint: the quiz must not smuggle in a verdict. Every question should be one that a fanatical maximiser, a bounded-utility theorist and an ordinary careful person could each answer sincerely, and the scoring key should report what the reader holds, not scold them for holding it.
