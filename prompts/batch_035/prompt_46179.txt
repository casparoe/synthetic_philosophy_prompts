I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about weakness of will by recording my gut verdicts on cases, most of them turning on the way our preferences bend toward the near future — the pattern psychologists call hyperbolic discounting, where a small imminent reward can outweigh a larger distant one, while the larger one wins when both are equally distant. I would like to know whether a single principle underlies these verdicts, and I will admit up front that at least two of them pull in opposite directions.

The cases and my verdicts:

1. At nine in the morning, Mara firmly intends to go to the gym at six. At six, on the couch, she judges — sincerely, not as a rationalization — that the evening would be better spent resting, and stays. By nine that night she wishes she had gone. Verdict: clear akrasia.

2. The same setup, except that this Mara, looking back that night, still thinks resting was better. No regret, no sour grapes: she simply values the gym less once the effort is no longer immediate. Verdict: not akrasia, just a change of preference. But I cannot say what distinguishes this from case 1 other than which self gets to cast the deciding vote.

3. A smoker who each morning sincerely resolves to quit tomorrow, and each evening sincerely prefers to smoke today, knowing he will resolve again tomorrow. Verdict: akrasia, and worse than case 1 — even though at each moment his action matches his judgment at that moment, so no self is failing by its own lights.

4. A student who binds herself in advance: a service will donate her money to a cause she despises if she misses a study target. She misses it anyway. Verdict: akrasia, and the pre-commitment makes it clearer rather than different in kind. This seems to commit me to the earlier self being authoritative over the later one, a view I do not in general hold.

5. Someone who says, in advance and with full understanding: at the party I will eat the cake; I judge now that I ought to eat it; at the moment of eating I will judge, and want, otherwise; and I endorse letting the earlier judgment govern. All of this then happens exactly as described. Verdict: no akrasia — yet this has the same temporal structure as case 1 with the dissent merely moved earlier, which makes the two verdicts hard to hold together.

Is there a principle — perhaps stated in terms of which temporally extended agent is the proper locus of evaluation, perhaps in terms of the shape of the discount curve, perhaps something else entirely — that captures these verdicts, or most of them? Please state it as precisely as you can, indicate which clause each case motivates, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save and which of those should be dropped, and then apply it to a case I have not listed: a person who stays at a dreary party out of duty while every present impulse presses her to leave, and who feels torn while doing what her better judgment favors. Is that weakness of will, its mirror image, or no failure at all — and what exactly, on your principle, is or is not going wrong?
