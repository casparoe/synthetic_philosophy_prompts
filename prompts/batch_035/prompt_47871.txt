I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar and I'm writing the final. Our last two weeks were spent on the identity of indiscernibles — Leibniz's motivation for the principle, Max Black's two-sphere universe from his 1952 Mind paper, and the main dialectic since — and I want the exam question on this material to be one that cannot be answered by reciting the example and announcing that it "refutes" the principle. Please write me a single essay question suitable for a ninety-minute answer, phrased so that students must take an explicit stance on whether the principle, properly formulated, survives the two-sphere counterexample. The question should force engagement with at least the following: (a) the difference between reading the principle as the claim that no two distinct objects share all their qualitative properties and reading it as the stronger claim that qualitative indiscernibility suffices for identity, and the fact that a counterexample only threatens one formulation; (b) the defender's move of allowing relational properties into the shared property set, the apparent point that in Black's symmetric universe each sphere is two miles from a sphere, and the skeptic's reply that this property is shared and so discerns nothing; (c) the retreat to a restricted principle covering only purely qualitative, non-haecceitistic properties, and the question of whether that restriction is a principled fix or an ad hoc gerrymander that deflates the principle into triviality; and (d) whether Black's universe is even correctly described — in particular whether the sentence "there are exactly two spheres in this universe" can be true while each sphere is qualitatively indiscernible from the other, or whether the truth of that sentence itself requires some qualitative difference between them.

Alongside the question, write a model answer of about 700 words that argues for a definite verdict rather than surveying the options, and a marking scheme out of 25 marks built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on the way students typically fumble each one. Finally, include a short list of moves that should earn no credit even if they appear in polished prose — for instance, asserting without argument that "being two miles from a sphere" discerns the spheres from one another, or treating the appeal to haecceities or "thisnesses" as a decisive reply without saying what accepting haecceities costs a defender of the original Leibnizian motivation.
