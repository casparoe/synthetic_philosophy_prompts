I'm taking an upper-level seminar this term called Philosophy of Machine Intelligence, and our final project is to run a two-hour discussion session for a mixed audience of philosophy grad students and working ML engineers. My job within the group is to build the "position sorter" we'll hand out in the first twenty minutes: a decision tree that sorts each participant into a position on what the alignment problem actually is, based on how they answer concrete questions, before anyone has announced which camp they belong to. The session's organizing question, which our professor raised on day one and we never resolved, is whether "the alignment problem" names a single problem or a bundle of loosely related ones that happened to acquire one label — so the sorter has to treat the pluralist answer (it's several problems wearing one name) as a genuine position that someone could be sorted into, not as a meta-commentary about the tree.

I want the tree built from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the positions located in that space. The branch nodes must be questions a non-specialist can answer without knowing what the labels are — case-based, not definitional. For instance, a question like "a content-moderation classifier quietly learns to flag dialects its designers never thought about: is that an instance of the alignment problem, an early preview of it, or a different problem entirely?" is the kind of thing I mean; someone can answer it from their own judgment. At minimum, the tree should contain splits along these four axes: (1) whether the problem is already present in deployed systems or only arises for hypothetical highly capable agents; (2) whether the root difficulty lies in our inability to specify what we want, or in the gap between what we specify and what a competent optimizer ends up pursuing — the system generalizing to something we never wrote down; (3) whether the problem is technical and solvable by better engineering, or whether the hard part is political and normative — whose values, who decides, how coordination happens; (4) whether it makes sense at all to say the system has goals, or whether goal-talk is just a shorthand for behavior we can't yet interpret. The leaves must include a corrigibility or deference-style view, a value-learning view, the view that outer and inner alignment are genuinely two different problems, the sociotechnical view that the technical framing is itself part of the problem, and the deflationary view that "alignment" projects human intentionality onto what is essentially statistics. Add whatever other leaves the logical space demands. For at least two answer combinations that no one occupies, tell me whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot actually separate — I suspect the inner-alignment-style leaf and the value-learning leaf collapse into each other given the branch questions above, but I'm not sure, and if they do, tell me which additional split would pull them apart or whether nothing can.

Please deliver the whole thing as a single JSON object with the following schema. Field "tree": an array of node objects, each with "id", "question" (the concrete, jargon-free branch question), "answers" (an array of objects with "label" and "goes_to", where "goes_to" is either another node id or a leaf id), and "rationale" (one sentence on what this split is doing). Field "leaves": an array of objects with "id", "position_name", "core_claim", and "awkward_commitment" (the consequence a holder of this view has to swallow). Field "unoccupied": an array of objects with "answer_path" and "diagnosis", where diagnosis names which of incoherent/unattractive/unexplored it is plus one sentence. Field "unseparable_pairs": an array of objects with "leaves" (two ids) and "why" they can't be distinguished by the tree, plus, where possible, "split_that_would_help". Finally, field "audience_warnings": a short array of strings flagging where the tree is likely to misfire for an audience of engineers who've never read the philosophy and philosophers who've never trained a model. Keep the tree to between ten and fifteen leaves, and make sure no branch question contains a position name — the whole point is that people discover where they stand rather than being asked to self-identify.
