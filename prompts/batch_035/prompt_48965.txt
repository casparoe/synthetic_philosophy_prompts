I am a novelist, and I have spent two months plotting a detective story whose ending has now become the object of a philosophical quarrel I am not competent to referee. The ending, as I have planned it, has my detective assemble the clues — the torn ticket, the switched medication, the alibi that holds only if the clock was moved — and declare, before the assembled household, that exactly one explanation accounts for all of them. I described this ending at a dinner party, and two guests, E. and R., have been corresponding about it ever since. E. is a graduate student in philosophy of science; R. spent twenty-five years as a homicide investigator before retiring. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move, because I need to know whose position the last chapter can honestly depict as winning — or whether the scene must be written some third way.

The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it from their letters:

(1) E. opened by saying my ending is a lie to the reader. However carefully the clues are planted, he wrote, they will be compatible not only with my intended solution but with indefinitely many rivals: the valet did it and framed the nephew, the nephew did it and framed the valet, the death was an accident that a frightened household conspired to disguise. This is the old underdetermination problem: bodies of evidence do not single out explanations on their own. A fair ending, he said, would show the detective choosing among equally evidence-compatible stories on grounds that are not evidence at all — plausibility, elegance, convenience — and would own up to it.

(2) R. replied that this is a philosopher's conceit. Real investigations do not die of underdetermination, because rival explanations are never on equal footing. The frame-up story requires a second person's intentions to line up, an unexplained absence of fingerprints, and a motive nobody can supply; the intended solution requires nothing but what the record already shows. Choosing among explanations by economy, coherence with background knowledge, and explanatory power is not a leap past the evidence — it is the ordinary procedure of both science and police work, and it has been vindicated often enough to be trusted.

(3) E. conceded that the rivals differ in what he called their virtues, but pressed the point: the virtues are ours, not the world's. The universe is under no obligation to be economical. Preferring the simple explanation is a fact about human psychology and a policy of our institutions, not a fact about who administered the medication. So the detective's confidence, he argued, should be modest, and the declaration that exactly one explanation fits should be cut from my ending.

(4) R. answered that the rivals are not fixed points standing forever beyond the evidence's reach — they are testable, and they fall. The frame-up story predicts the valet bought gloves on the fourteenth; that can be checked, and in the checking the rival either survives or dies. Abstract underdetermination does not infect practice, because practice keeps pulling new evidence in, and at every stage there is a best explanation while the survivors are peeled away one by one. Since my book ends when it ends, the detective's declaration simply marks the rational stopping point of that process.

(5) E. seized on exactly that. Precisely because the book ends, he wrote, the detective's certainty outruns his evidence: within the world of the story, the checking has stopped, and nothing further will come in. What the detective says at that moment — that one explanation is the only one consistent with the clues — is stronger than anything he is entitled to. Modest confidence would be honest; the pose of deduction is a genre convention masquerading as epistemology.

(6) R.'s final letter argued that juries convict on evidence of exactly this kind, and they do it under a standard — beyond reasonable doubt — that already acknowledges every possibility E. describes and dismisses the surviving rivals as unreasonable rather than impossible. Demanding more, he said, is demanding a certainty nothing in life offers, including verdicts that send people to prison for decades. And if my novel ended with the detective shrugging that, for all he knows, the valet's twin did it, no reader would experience that as intellectual honesty; they would experience it as evasion.

I confess I cannot tell who is winning, and I do not want the outcome to be settled by authorial fiat. Please adjudicate the six moves: say who is right about which specific point, what each gets wrong, and what, if anything, both are missing — for instance, whether the disagreement turns on a distinction neither of them draws, between underdetermination as a permanent feature of evidence and as a merely temporary condition of an unfinished inquiry, or on whether explanatory virtues track anything beyond our habits. And then tell me plainly whose victory, if either's, my ending can honestly stage, or whether the scene needs to be written some other way entirely.
