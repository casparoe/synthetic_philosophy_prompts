I'm putting together a short position statement for a graduate seminar on the ethics of suffering, and before I circulate it to the group I'd like somebody to check it for coherence. My sense is that I'm a fairly ordinary suffering-focused consequentialist, but I've been told my views contain a "textbook inconsistency," and I genuinely can't see it. Here is the set of commitments as I'll present them:

1. Aggregation. Welfare is the kind of thing that can be measured on a single cardinal scale, compared across persons, and summed. The betterness of an outcome is determined by the total sum of everyone's welfare.
2. Lexical priority for severe suffering. Some states — torture, agony, profound grief — are so bad that no amount of happiness, no matter how large or how distributed, can counterbalance them. A world with one instance of torture plus endless bliss is worse than a world with neither.
3. The procreative asymmetry. It is wrong to create a person whose life you can foresee will contain more suffering than happiness, but there is nothing wrong, and no missed duty, in refraining from creating a person whose life would have contained more happiness than suffering.
4. The deprivation account of the badness of death. Death is bad for the one who dies, and it is bad in virtue of the happy experiences it prevents them from having — not in virtue of any suffering it inflicts.
5. A verdict on a case: a world containing a billion genuinely wonderful lives, each of which includes exactly one episode of severe agony, is clearly better than an empty world, and it would be absurd to deny this.
6. Intra-personal tradeoffs. My own mild discomforts can be outweighed by my own later enjoyments — a miserable night is worth it for the trip — and this is unproblematic.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to, with the tensions ranked by severity rather than listed as equally bad. For each real tension, say exactly what would have to give and what it would cost me to give it up, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Lead with your overall verdict in your first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble and no restating of my list back at me.
