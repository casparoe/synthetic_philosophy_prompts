I am a novelist, some way into a book whose protagonist is a cardiologist given to speaking of the body as an instrument. His creed, in his own voice in the draft, runs roughly as follows: a heart is made to pump, as a clock is made to keep time; a heart that will not pump is a failed heart, not a heart with unfashionable habits, and that is a fact about it rather than an opinion of his, just as a clock stopped at noon is simply a failed clock. I want him to be more than rhetorically impressive; I want his self-understanding to be defensible, or at least to fail in instructive places, and I cannot tell on my own where it fails. So I am asking for an audit of the analogy that does the work throughout the draft: the organ as designed artifact.

Give me, first, the correspondence table — what plays the role of the designer, the blueprint, the intended use, the type against which any given token is judged, the malfunction. Then the places the mapping breaks, and, since I need to know which failures are dramatic and which merely pedantic, a verdict on which of the break points matter for the conclusions my character wants to draw. What he wants to draw, specifically, is this: that illness is objective; that "this heart is diseased" is like "this clock is broken," a fact rather than a valuation, so that his standing quarrel with the hospital ethics committee — who decides what a body is for — can have a right answer rather than a negotiated one.

Four cases from the draft should be run through the table, because I suspect they come apart differently:

1. A patient whose red blood cells are sickle-shaped. The shape maims him, yet it runs in his family because, in the malarial country his people came from, the same shape kept his ancestors alive. The cardiologist calls it a defect; the evolutionary biologist in the book calls it a bargain. Which of them, if either, is talking about the cells' function?
2. A newborn with a first-in-the-family mutation whose heart never pumped properly from its first hour. Can that heart be malfunctioning on the artifact picture, when nothing like it ever did its work anywhere in the line — is there a spec for it to have failed against?
3. The middle ear, which was jawbones before anything asked it to hear. Repurposed — but by whom, and under what reading of "for"?
4. The cardiologist's own craft: he reads hearts by their sound. Nobody designed the heart to be audible, yet the murmur tells him more about the organ than most of its other doings. Function or side effect, and does the answer differ between the two standard accounts?

Then tell me whether the objectivity conclusion survives. Once the disanalogies are on the table, does "this heart is diseased" remain a fact in the way "this clock is broken" is, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? And if the watch is the wrong picture, say what the right one would be — the biologist should be able to offer it to him in a late chapter, so give me an image rather than an argument.

Finally, a request about how you report all this. Please separate what is settled among philosophers of biology from what is genuinely contested, and flag the contested points as contested rather than presenting them as consensus. I have in mind two in particular: whether one of the two standard accounts of biological function — the one grounding function in what a trait was selected for, versus the one grounding it in the trait's present contribution to some capacity of the organism — is the standard, or whether pluralism about function is now the respectable view; and whether "dysfunction" can do any work in medicine without a value judgment smuggled in alongside it. If the cardiologist's creed depends on a contested call, I need to know, because it decides whether I write him as a man with a philosophy or a man with a temperament.
