You are a professional philosopher specialising in personal identity and philosophy of mind, with a side interest in philosophy of film, and you have been asked to contribute a reading guide for a university film-and-philosophy screening series aimed at second- and third-year undergraduates. Before writing anything, take a moment to brainstorm and organise your thoughts, and be careful to get the details of both the film and the thought experiment right, since the students will know them well.

The text pairing for the session is Duncan Jones's Moon (2009) read against Daniel Dennett's 1978 short story Where Am I?, from Brainstorms. I want a reading of the film through Dennett's puzzle, not merely a summary of the two side by side. Please write the guide in British English.

Recall the shape of both works. Dennett's narrator has his brain (Yorick) removed and kept in a vat in Houston, whence it runs his body (Hamlet) by radio link; when Hamlet is destroyed retrieving the STUD device, his brain later drives a new body (Fortinbras); and once Hubert, a bit-for-bit real-time computer copy of Yorick, exists, the narrator alternates between them, ending in confusion about which substrate he is running on and where he is. The story's hook is first-personal: the narrator insists on locating himself where his point of view is, and finds he cannot think himself into the vat. Moon gives us Sam Bell, the lone operator of the Sarang lunar base harvesting helium-3 for fusion power on Earth, who discovers after an accident that he is one of a series of clones with the original Sam's implanted memories, kept on a rolling three-year contract, with the live feed home conveniently jammed; two of these Sams coexist for most of the film, the older one physically deteriorating, before the younger escapes to Earth and the older dies watching it rise.

The reading should do the following, though not necessarily in this order:

1. Map the film onto the puzzle and say where the mapping holds: Dennett's fission-adjacent wrinkle with Hubert versus the film's actual, embodied branching of two Sams with a common memory origin; the implanted memories as a kind of counterfeit causal stream; the jammed live feed and the recorded messages from home as a deliberately severed input channel, which is the inverse of Dennett's severed radio link.

2. Say where the mapping strains. In particular: Dennett's story is told in the first person and its puzzles are generated by the narrator's stubborn sense of where he is, whereas the film cannot occupy Sam's point of view in that way once there are two of him — the camera has to be as neutral between the Sams as it would be between Yorick and Hubert. Does the film thereby dramatise a limitation of the first-personal method, or does it quietly rely on the audience's identification with one Sam (and if so, which, given that both are equally the clone)? Note also that Dennett's story keeps only one of the pair active at a time, while Moon forces genuine coexistence, so the film is in some ways a harder test than the thought experiment for any view that ties identity to a single stream of consciousness.

3. Bring in the dimension Dennett's story has almost none of: the ethics. The Sams are exploited disposable labour with implanted personal histories, and the film plainly cares about this. Say something about whether the moral questions (what the corporation owes the Sams, what their moral status is) come apart from the identity questions, or whether the film is arguing that they come apart — that is, whether Moon's real claim is that personal identity debates are beside the point when the wrong done is one any person-numbering convention would condemn.

4. Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration of Dennett, and do not bend the thought experiment to fit the film. Where the reading genuinely doesn't work — for instance, if the film's emotional logic depends on something like narrative identity that Dennett's functionalist framework is ill-placed to capture — say so plainly rather than papering over it.

5. Conclude with a short section (roughly 200 words) addressed to the students: two or three specific scenes we should rewatch and discuss after the screening, each with one focused question for discussion, and one paragraph on what the film adds to the personal identity debate that the thought experiment alone cannot — bearing in mind that the thought experiment is free to stipulate whatever tidy conditions it likes, and the film is not.

The guide should run to roughly 1,200–1,500 words. Do not use verbatim quotation from either the film's dialogue or Dennett's text; paraphrase or describe instead. Aim for prose a bright undergraduate can follow, but do not simplify the philosophy to the point of distortion.
