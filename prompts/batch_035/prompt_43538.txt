At a philosophy-of-mind reading group, a colleague — call her Dana — laid out the following argument, which I can neither accept nor diagnose. I would be grateful for a careful dissection.

(1) Pain asymbolics — patients with certain cingulate lesions — retain everything in pain that can be reported: they localize the stimulus, grade its intensity, describe its quality, and report that it does not bother them; they watch the needle, note the pain, and do not withdraw. (2) Since every reportable feature of the sensation is preserved and only the minding is gone, what asymbolia removes is not the sensation but an aversive attitude toward it. (3) If the badness of pain were intrinsic to the sensation — invariant under changes in everything else about the subject — it would survive the removal of any attitude toward the sensation, since attitudes are something over and above the sensation. (4) It does not survive: for the asymbolic, an intense pain is no worse than a neutral warmth of equal vividness; nothing in the episode counts against it. (5) So the badness of pain is carried by the aversive attitude and not by the felt quality; the feel of pain, considered in itself, is axiologically inert. (6) And what holds of pain holds throughout: pleasure is feeling-plus-liking, fear feeling-plus-dreading, and in each case the value lives in the attitude and never in the feel. No experience is intrinsically good or bad; all value in experience is attitude-dependent.

Dana draws the corollary herself: a procedure inducing universal asymbolia — flattening every caring while leaving every sensation intact — would yield a world containing nothing bad, however much torture occurred in it. She accepts this serenely, and when I object, she points out that my objection is itself just an attitude, which is precisely her thesis. She has also rehearsed the reply I thought was my escape: that the bearer of badness is the composite episode, the hurting-and-hating taken as a whole. But then, she notes, the torture world with no attitudes in it contains no such composites, so I still owe her an account of what is bad there — and by my own lights I cannot point to attitudes to supply it.

I trust (1) absolutely; the clinical picture is the fixed point of the discussion. And I cannot accept the corollary about the torture world. So something in (2) through (6) fails, but each step looks unremarkable to me. Name the step that fails, explain why it looked fine, and price the alternatives. Dana predicts I will end up paying more than she does; tell me whether she is right.
