I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind (mostly cog sci majors, 14 students, one 90-minute session), and I want a live physical activity — a game with rules and roles, not a discussion — that gets them to feel a puzzle about levels of description.

Here's the thought experiment the activity should be built around, since they won't have read it. Somewhere in Gödel, Escher, Bach (I think it's Hofstadter, it's been years since I read it so don't quote me on chapter and verse) there's an image of a special billiard table called a careenium: simmballs — simulated billiard balls — roll around on it among fixed bumpers, and the bumpers are arranged so the geometry *is* arithmetic. A simmball enters a certain region and comes out as two; feed in two and you get four out. An observer watching the table can either describe it as balls caroming off bumpers, or as the table working out 2×2=4 — and here's the question I love: do the symbols push the simmballs around? The "answer" of 4 shows up, the computation "happens," but nothing down at ball-and-bumper level knows anything about numbers. Is "the arithmetic made the balls go that way" a harmless shorthand, a mistake, or somehow literally true?

So what I want is for the class to *be* the careenium. The picture in my head: most students are simmballs moving around a floor-grid "table" with human bumpers, obeying dumb deterministic rules (enter zone X, split, merge, whatever) that I want you to actually design — rules simple enough to run without mistakes but rich enough that something identifiable gets computed, and the class should NOT be told in advance what's being computed. A couple students should be observer/physicist roles who only track positions and write down what they see. Then a second phase I really want you to design carefully: some way for the group to try to intervene from the symbol level — like, the "output" of the computation gets read off and fed back in as new input, or the group is asked to change what the table computes using only symbol-level language — so they run headfirst into the question of whether talking about the symbols gives them any causal handle at all, versus having to go physically move a bumper.

Then the debrief: map specific moments onto the distinction between the substrate-level and symbol-level description, and onto whether downward causation is real or just bookkeeping. Also: (1) the symbols shouldn't be named until the end, no lecture up front; (2) tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing students will start playing agentic and "helping" the computation along — how do I design the rules so that drifts shut down without me having to police every move?); and (3) be honest about what the activity fundamentally can't reproduce and I have to supply myself. My worry there is that human simmballs following rules still each have a little intentionality inside them — nobody in a real careenium is deciding to split — so the whole thing smuggles in a homunculus at the substrate level, which kind of riggs the game. Maybe you can find a patch, but if not, say so and tell me how to make the point verbally without ruining the reveal.

No real money, materials from a normal classroom, and the whole thing has to fit 90 minutes including the debrief. Prefer rules I can fit on one handout.
