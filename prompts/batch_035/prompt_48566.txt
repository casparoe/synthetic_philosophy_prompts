My roommate is doing philosophy of science and she has an argument that, on the Bayesian analysis, nothing ever confirms anything, and I can't find the hole in it. It goes:

(1) Confirmation is credence-raising. Officially: E confirms H for you iff P(H|E) > P(H) — the thing that would go up if you learned E.
(2) You can only learn what you don't already give probablity 1.
(3) Conditioning on a certainty is a no-op: if P(E)=1 then P(H|E)=P(H).
(4) Take the classic case. Astronomers had known since the 1850s that Mercury's perihelion precesses about 43 arcseconds per century more than the Newtonian story allows — Le Verrier flagged the anomaly in 1859 and nobody could explain it. Then in November 1915 Einstein shows general relativity spits out exactly that number. By then every physicist who cared had the perihelion data at probability 1; it had been sitting in the literature for over fifty years.
(5) So by (1)-(3) the perihelion evidence confirmed general relativity for nobody alive in 1915. Nothing got conditioned on.
(6) And this isn't special to Mercury. There's always a lag between an observation being made and a theory being proposed to account for it, so whenever a theory meets its evidence, that evidence is already old for everyone who's heard of it — which, once the data is published, is everyone in a position to do the confirming. So no theory is ever confirmed by any evidence after the data goes public, and all the evidence for every mature theory is public.
(7) But the perihelion calc is the textbook example of a theory getting confirmed by its evidence, and if (6) holds then mature science is a pile of unconfirmed theories, which is crazy.
(8) So (1) is false, and her stated moral is that the Bayesian definition of confirmation eats itself with its own updating rule.

Here's where I keep getting stuck. When I push on (5), she concedes that what got learned in 1915 wasn't the data, it was the fact that GR entails the data — but then she says what got confirmed was some conjunction, and calling that "the perihelion confirmed GR" is a bookkeeping trick about which proposition you condition on, which is exactly her point. When I push on (6), I honestly can't tell whether I'm denying a real generalization or just refusing to look at it. And I keep feeling a slide between the "would raise" in (1) and the "did raise" in (5) — for old evidence the antecedent of (1)'s counterfactual is impossible, you can't learn what you already know, so I don't even know how (1) is supposed to get applied in (5). But if (1) can't be applied there, is her argument invalid, or just unsound, or actually fine? Sort me out: which step is the weak one, is the flaw hidden in the reasoning or is it a genuine dilemma about which plausible premise to give up, and what does each way out cost? If I have to keep one thing it's (1), since its the only definition of confirmation I actually understand, but tell me straight if thats the one that has to go.
