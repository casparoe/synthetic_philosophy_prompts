I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends the Quinean dissolution of the surprise exam paradox: the students' backward induction never gets started, because they never actually knew the exam would occur during the week at all — the teacher's announcement, however sincere, is not something the students know to be true, so they have nothing to eliminate days from. My audience already knows the basic paradox, so I don't need a rehash of the setup. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the actual mechanics of the view. At least one should target the epistemology of the announcement itself: I announce surprise quizzes in my own teaching and it seems my students can know, from my track record, that a quiz is coming — so what exactly is the difference when the teacher announces a weeklong surprise exam? At least one should focus on Friday morning: if no exam has occurred by then and only one day remains, the student appears able to know it's today, and I want him to say what, precisely, is missing there. At least one should ask about the case where the announcement comes from a teacher who has been perfectly reliable for years, and at least one should ask what evidence could count against his dissolution, as opposed to merely favoring rival solutions that locate the trouble in self-reference or in the iterated "you know that I know" structure. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give. Keep the register conversational but sharp — questions he can't answer with a one-sentence slogan.
