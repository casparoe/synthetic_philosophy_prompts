A classmate and I have been arguing about Nietzsche's On the Genealogy of Morality, and he ended with an argument that seems to prove far more than he wants. (1) A valuation is debunked when we learn that we would hold it whether or not it is warranted, because the causes that produced it would have produced it either way. (2) The genealogy shows that our valuations of compassion, humility, and equality arose from the slave revolt in morality: the weak, unable to avenge themselves on the strong, recast their impotence as merit and strength as guilt, and valuations of this kind would have taken hold whatever they were worth. (3) So our belief in the value of these virtues is debunked. (4) But the genealogist's own valuations — his esteem for strength, his demand for honesty about one's motives, his contempt for comforting illusions — have causes of the same kind: they express a particular temperament and its needs, and he would hold them whether or not they are warranted. (5) By parity, the genealogist's valuations are debunked as well, including the valuation of truthfulness that motivates the inquiry itself. (6) So the genealogy, taken seriously, undermines its own standpoint, and (7) its critique of slave morality carries no critical weight. I don't want to accept that genealogy is mere history with no debunking force, but I also can't accept that it refutes itself, and I can't locate the faulty step. Which premise or inference should be rejected, why did that step look acceptable, and what does rejecting it cost?
