I serve on the grants committee of a community hardship fund in my town — twelve volunteers, no staff, meeting once a month. We distribute emergency grants of roughly $300 to $3,000 out of an annual pot of about $45,000, mostly for car repairs, overdue rent, medical bills, that sort of thing. We have no written criteria beyond "help the worst off," and I have slowly come to believe that our perpetual arguments are not about the applicants at all but about a question nobody has said out loud: worst off in what? One faction wants to rank applicants by the size of the gap between the expense and what they can pay — arithmetic, receipts, done. A second insists that how bad things actually feel for the person should count: they point to a woman with long-standing chronic pain who always says she's used to it and seems content, versus a man in a panic over a burst pipe, and argue about which of them is really worse off. And I have drifted toward a third position I can't quite articulate: that the same money buys different lives for different people — a $1,400 car repair is a lifeline for the person whose job depends on the car and a stopgap for someone who will be stranded again in three months either way — so what we should be asking is not how much they need or how they feel but what they will actually be able to do once we've helped. At our annual planning retreat in the spring we will have ninety minutes to settle this, and I don't want another circular debate.

Please design that ninety-minute session as a structured activity rather than a discussion. The setting is a church meeting room, twelve people of mixed ages, several of whom dislike anything that feels like being lectured. My thought is that the committee should be split into three groups, each given the same set of anonymized applicant files but instructed to rank them using only one question — the money gap, the felt situation, or what the person will be able to do afterward — with the same fixed budget, so that the three groups arrive at visibly different funding decisions and then have to face each other across the table. I can build composite files from real past cases, and if you think it helps, one of us can play an applicant who answers the three groups' different questions differently. Include the rules, the files I should write, the timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the exercise onto the three candidate answers to my unspoken question — especially the moment, if there is one, where a group discovers that its question changed what the applicant revealed. Then tell me honestly what will go wrong the first time we run it, and which part of the real problem no exercise with paper files can ever reproduce — I suspect it has something to do with the fact that the committee members themselves are not neutral observers of who is suffering, but I'd rather hear your view than trust mine.
