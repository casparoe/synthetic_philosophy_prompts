Our moral dilemmas reading group has hit a wall and I want a diagnosis of the wall, not a verdict on the debate. The setup, briefly: one side insists genuine dilemmas are impossible. Their story is that when two duties conflict, at most one of them survives as an actual obligation — the other just loses, the way it apparently does for Ross, where the conflict resolves into a single duty in the circumstances — and if that's right, the guilt you feel after choosing is just regret, not evidence of a duty you failed. The other side thinks that's defining the problem away: they point to the guilt in these cases, which feels like it tracks something left over, a remainder the first side has no room for, and they say that if obligation can't even in principle conflict, the concept has stopped doing any real work. It's also just weird that even Kant apparently couldn't stomach the idea of duties conflicting — which suggests the anti-dilemma side isn't being pedantic, they're in serious company. A year in, nobody has moved an inch.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? My half-baked theory is that it's verbal at one level and substantive at another: the two sides mean different things by "obligation" (all-things-considered verdict vs. something more pro tanto), so the definitional part is trivially resolvable — but the fight over whether the guilt is apt or merely understandable doesn't go away once you fix the words, which would explain why each side keeps feeling like it's winning on its home turf. Is that right? Or is the whole thing a semantic quibble dressed up as deep, or two groups answering different questions (one about the logic of "ought", one about which emotions are fitting after a hard choice) under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly whether anything — a sharper distinction, a test case, reformulating the question — could actually count as progress, or whether I should just accept this one stays stuck.
