My roommate is deep into the secession literature and she's got me stuck on an argument that I think proves way too much, but I can't find where it goes wrong. We were arguing about Scotland and Catalonia and she produced this:

(1) The whole point of a right to collective self-determination is that people shouldn't have to live under political institutions they can't realistically exit and don't endorse. It's basically the only rationale for a right to secede that survives any scrutiny.

(2) The unit entitled to self-determination is whatever group forms a majority on the territory in question. That's just how these things work — the 2014 Scottish referendum was decided by a majority of voters in Scotland, full stop, and if the Yes side had gotten 51% nobody thinks the 49% could have vetoed by pointing out they were a majority of their own street.

(3) But nothing in (1) fixes which territory is "in question" in the first place. If the Scots are entitled to opt out of institutions imposed from Westminster, then the exact same endorsement-based reasoning says any sub-territory with its own persistent majority is entitled to opt out of whatever the new arrangement is. This isn't hypothetical — when Scottish independence looked like it might actually happen, the Shetland council literally started exploring going its own way, and everyone knows the wealthy bits of an independent Catalonia would be the first to bolt.

(4) So the right applies recursively: regions, then sub-regions, then cities, then neighborhoods, then apartment buildings, until finally each individual is a majority of one within their own four walls, and personal secession is just... emigration or statelessness, which states obviously don't recognize as a right.

(5) A right of collective self-determination that, followed consistently, bottoms out in one-person states has stopped being collective self-determination entirely. It's just exit, which we already know isn't granted.

(6) Therefore either (1) is false — endorsement doesn't actually ground any right to secede — or (2) needs some principled way of fixing the unit that the endorsement story itself can't provide, in which case plebiscitary theory is smuggling in whatever fixes the unit and pretending it comes from the value.

She thinks this shows the plebiscitary theory of secession is basically self-refuting, and that only the remedial view (where you only get to leave if you've been wronged, like an occupied people) survives. That feels off to me too — the remedial view implies the Scots had no claim at all unless Westminster was oppressing them, which seems nuts given how the whole point is that they never consented to begin with.

My instinct is that there's a slide somewhere between "endorsement is a property of individual persons" and "the right is exercisable at whatever scale individuals happen to be locally in the majority" — like the argument treats the scale of the unit as morally inert when it obviously isn't. But I can't make that precise without just hand-waving about "viability" or "community," which she'll say is exactly the smuggled-in criterion from (6). Where does it actually go wrong? If I have to sacrifice a premise I'd rather keep (1) than anything else, since its the only justification I've ever found plausible, so tell me what keeping it costs me. And is there any fix for (2) — timing, viability thresholds, needing the sub-unit's grievance to be with the *parent* secession rather than the original state — that actually survives the apartment building case, or are those all ad hoc?
