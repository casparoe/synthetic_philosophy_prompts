At my school's ethics club we've been arguing about what I have been calling moral deals: agreements between two people who disagree morally, in which each agrees to behave as the other believes one ought to, in exchange for the other doing the same. I've collected my verdicts on a set of cases and cannot make them line up, so I would like to know what principle, if any, captures them — and which of my verdicts that principle forces me to give up.

The cases, with my verdicts. (1) My friend is vegetarian because she believes eating factory-farmed meat is a serious wrong; I eat meat, but I believe buying fast fashion is a serious wrong, and she buys it. We agree she will stop buying fast fashion and I will go meatless on weekdays. Verdict: clearly good — the world ends up with less of what each of us considers wrongdoing, and neither of us had to concede that we were wrong about anything. (2) A devout classmate believes mocking anyone's faith is wrong; I am an atheist and believe recruiting freshmen into his church's youth group before they can think for themselves is wrong. We agree I will stop making anti-religious jokes and he will stop the recruiting. Verdict: good, but with a residue I cannot shake — I feel as though I have signed my name to the claim that the jokes were wrong. (3) A classmate regards eating any animal product as comparable to murder; I regard playing music out loud on the bus as wrong. She offers to wear headphones without exception if I go fully vegan. Verdict: fine, and a bargain for me, though it also feels as though her intensity has let her buy a great deal with very little. (4) A classmate has read online that strangers' Halloween candy is sometimes poisoned and believes trick-or-treating is reckless; she offers to do my share of cleaning the club room for a month if I skip trick-or-treating entirely. Verdict: I waver. Nothing I would be doing is wrong by my own lights, but I would be profiting from a false factual belief of hers, and that feels different from profiting from a moral disagreement, which is what every deal here does. (5) A classmate believes, for religious reasons, that interracial dating is wrong. He offers to stop mocking people's weight — which he knows I consider a genuine wrong — if I stop dating outside my race while at school. Verdict: absolutely not, though I notice the world would contain less wrongdoing if I accepted. (6) A softer version of (5): the same classmate offers the same concession on weight-mocking if I merely stop contradicting him in public when he says mixed couples are wrong. Verdict: this is the case I keep going back and forth on. (7) My friend and I each believe the other's favourite charity does net harm in the world. We agree that for every hour I volunteer for his charity, he will volunteer an hour for mine. Verdict: bad, or at least pointless — though structurally it is just (1) with causes instead of habits.

My tentative principle is this: a moral deal is permissible when, by your own lights, what you are agreeing to is at most a sacrifice of convenience, and impermissible when your own compliance would be something you consider wrong. It handles (1), (5), and (7), since in (5) and (7) my side of the deal would be wrongdoing by my own lights. But it leaves the discomfort in (2), the wobble in (4), and quite possibly my resistance to (6) unexplained, since in each of those my compliance is merely costly. So: is there a principle that captures all or nearly all of my verdicts — perhaps my tentative one plus a clause, perhaps something altogether different? Please test the tentative principle against the cases, repair it or replace it, say plainly which of my verdicts cannot be accommodated and why, and state what the resulting principle predicts for cases I have not listed — in particular a deal struck with the school administration, which enforces rules nobody thinks it itself believes in, and a deal in which my compliance is only trivially wrong by my own lights, a white-lie-sized wrong, since that is exactly where (6) lives.
