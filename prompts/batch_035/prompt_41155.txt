I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and as part of my funding package I teach a section of introductory ethics. We've just finished the unit on personal identity — Part III of Parfit's Reasons and Persons — and I'd like to end the last lecture with a poem that does real philosophical work rather than name-dropping. Ideally the form carries the argument.

I'd like a sestina whose six end-words are: scanner, Mars, cells, memory, branch, and matter. The stanzas should follow Parfit's sequence of cases in roughly his order: the plain teletransporter, where the body on Earth is destroyed and a Replica with all my memories wakes on Mars; the branch-line case, where the one who stepped into the scanner lingers on Earth for a few days while the Replica gets on with life on Mars; and the division case, where two Replicas — or two transplanted hemispheres — are created, neither of them straightforwardly me, though Parfit's view is that the prospect is about as good as ordinary survival. The sestina strikes me as the right form for a Reductionist argument: the same six words return in rotation, in ever-new combinations, and no combination ever yields a further fact — which is, I take it, exactly the point. The envoi should end on "matter," so the poem closes on the pun between matter as substance and matter as significance, and the closing lines should enact the dissolution of the question rather than adjudicate it: somewhere in the final stanza, "will the person on Mars be me?" should lose its grip, the way it does in the division case, instead of being answered yes or no.

A few accuracy constraints. The poem should not imply that the Replica simply is the original, and equally should not treat the case as straightforward death — Parfit denies psychological continuity nothing; connectedness is precisely what he thinks is preserved. Please avoid stock sci-fi imagery (glowing pods, beaming up) and avoid the word "soul." Someone who knows the cases should find the poem right; someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem.
