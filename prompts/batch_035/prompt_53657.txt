After a career of some forty years in physics, most of it in statistical mechanics and the last fifteen in the study of disordered systems and combinatorial optimization, I retired two years ago and have since been reading philosophy in earnest: philosophy of mathematics, philosophy of science, and the literature concerned with physical systems as computers. I find myself repeatedly struck by a mismatch. Philosophers by and large discuss computation in the register of Turing machines — unbounded time, unbounded tape, no energy budget — whereas everything I know as a physicist says that the interesting line in nature is not between the computable and the uncomputable but between what can be done with the resources the universe actually issues us and what cannot. The philosophy of computational complexity seems to be the field that takes this seriously, and I should like to devote a substantial part of my remaining vigorous years to it. Before committing, I want an assessment of where the effort is best spent.

So my request is this: give me a prioritized account of the open questions in the philosophy of computational complexity — which matter most, which are upstream of which, and which deserve the least attention. To make the assessment concrete, let me fix the circumstances it is for. The resources are one researcher, aged sixty-eight, with a strong background in probability, statistical physics, and algorithm analysis, a working knowledge of complexity theory as used in the sciences, and only two years of self-directed philosophical training. The available horizon is perhaps six or seven years of serious work, and the aim is to produce a book or a body of papers that both physicists and philosophers of science would take seriously — not to survey the field for my own edification.

Among the questions I have encountered, and whose relative weight I cannot yet judge, are the following. Whether the feasible/infeasible boundary is a natural joint or an artifact of the Turing model — whether the class P tracks any physically robust distinction, or whether a resource-based notion that prices time together with energy and dissipation, of the sort a thermodynamic treatment of reversible computation suggests, would carve nature at the same place. Whether the physical Church-Turing thesis should be reformulated as a claim about feasibility, and whether any physics we can currently conceive of — analogue magnitudes, continuous dynamics, exotic spacetimes — could in principle breach it. Whether the ubiquity of NP-hard problems in nature carries epistemic significance: nature hands us protein folding and spin-glass ground states as data, and I wonder whether the fact that these are hard for us but were, so to speak, solved cheaply by the system itself tells us something about why prediction succeeds where it does. Whether the worst-case framing of complexity classes distorts a philosophical discussion that should be conducted in average-case or typical-case terms, since heuristics perform so well on the instances that actually arise. And whether there is a principled epistemology of truths that are in principle accessible but not in practice reachable within any human or cosmic budget, and whether that distinction does any epistemological work beyond the trivial.

I ask for a defended ordering, not a list. For each question you rank highly, tell me why it belongs there: what makes it important, how tractable it is for someone with my profile rather than for a trained metaphysician, and how much downstream work turns on it. Tell me also which of the field's alleged implications are overrated or simply do not follow — I have seen it claimed, far too quickly for my taste, that unproved separations between complexity classes would entail limits on human knowledge, and I should like to know which such inferences are sound and which are sleights of hand. Finally, tell me what developments, mathematical or empirical, over the next few years would move items up or down your ranking. Please reason from the substance of the issues; I am not looking for a reading list, and I would rather have your arguments than pointers to literature.
