I'm a journalist who writes long-form features on science and ideas, and I'm at work on a piece about the ethics of future people — a subject that has lately moved from seminar rooms into public argument, given the fights over falling birth rates, climate policy, and the fashionable claim that humanity's long-term future is what matters most. While working through the population-ethics debates, I've become stuck on the procreation asymmetry: the claim that it is wrong to bring into existence a person whose life will be worse than never existing, while there is no corresponding reason to bring into existence a person whose life will be happy. The claim strikes me as obviously right, and I've constructed an argument for it that I cannot myself break. But the argument seems to license far more than I bargained for, and before it appears in print I need to know where it goes wrong — or what it costs if it doesn't.

The argument runs as follows. (1) An act can be wrong only if it wrongs someone: only if there is a person for whom things are made worse than they would have been under some available alternative. (2) Creating a person whose life will be worse than never existing wrongs that person, since such a life is worse for them than the alternative of never existing. (3) Creating a person whose life will be well worth living wrongs no one — they are not worse off than never existing — and benefits no one either, since a person who is never created is not around to be deprived of anything. (4) So creating a miserable person has a wrong-making feature with no counterpart in creating a happy one, and, by the same token, creating a happy person has no benefit-making feature at all. (5) An act with a wrong-making feature and no compensating benefit is pro tanto wrong; an act with neither is a matter of moral indifference. (6) Therefore creating a miserable person is pro tanto wrong, and creating a happy person is not pro tanto good: the asymmetry.

Here is my difficulty. Premises (1) and (3) generalize beyond creation to any choice that determines who will exist, and applied that way they seem to license conclusions I cannot bring myself to print: that a policy which foreseeably leaves every future person's life barely worth living, when an alternative policy would have brought equally many people into far better lives, wrongs nobody and is therefore not wrong — each future person's life beats non-existence, and the better-off people of the alternative are never around to complain (I take it this is the non-identity problem); that whether it is wrong to conceive in circumstances guaranteeing a child a painful condition depends solely on whether the resulting life clears the worth-living threshold, so that the severity of the condition matters not at all beyond that; and that nothing of moral account is lost if humanity simply stops reproducing, provided the last generations are content. Worse, a colleague who let me try the argument out on her says that (2) can only work by treating suffering as bad however it comes about, in which case, by the same standard, happiness is good however it comes about, and (4) collapses back into the very symmetry the asymmetry was supposed to deny. For what it's worth, I would most like to keep (3) and the verdict that creating a miserable life is wrong while creating a happy one is at most optional, and I am prepared to see (1) go if it must. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked so convincing in the first place, and what each available rejection costs — including, if you think the asymmetry itself was a mistake, what I should tell my readers instead.
