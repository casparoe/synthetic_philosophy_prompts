I spent the better part of four decades in physical chemistry before retiring, and the last two years reading philosophy of mind, mostly on what a person is. The question I have arrived at is the one Dennett made famous: whether the self is best understood not as an inner object but as an abstraction the brain and society jointly posit, something like a center of mass — a center of narrative gravity, in his phrase, a point we attribute stability to because so much prediction and explanation runs through it. I find the analogy attractive, since my own field is full of load-bearing abstractions that no one mistakes for extra pieces of matter. But I have noticed that the discussion of such views proceeds without an agreed set of criteria, the way model evaluation in my old field did before we learned to write the specification down first. So that is what I want from you: the specification.

Write out the desiderata an adequate theory of the self has to meet, with the motivating case or consideration behind each, and mark which are non-negotiable and which reflect the commitments of one camp rather than the shared problem. The desiderata I would want you to address include, at least: explaining why the self is the kind of thing to which we can ascribe interests and rights, and whether a mere abstraction can be a bearer of those in a way a center of mass plainly is not — I suspect these two requirements, the self as useful posit and the self as bearer of moral standing, may not be jointly satisfiable, and I want your view on whether the list itself contains such a contradiction; explaining the characteristic ways selves go wrong, such as confabulation, delusion, and dementia, where the narrative is partly false or dissolving while our concern for the person does not dissolve with it; handling those who do not narrate — infants, the severely cognitively disabled, perhaps some animals — whom we treat as selves and bearers of interests despite their not telling any story; respecting the difference between a self's existing and its self-understanding being accurate, so that a theory does not imply that a person with a largely false autobiography is thereby less of a self; staying consistent with what is known about the distributed character of processing in the brain, without smuggling in a homunculus or a Cartesian theater; and making sense of the first-person perspective without granting it the epistemic authority the tradition of introspection claimed for it.

Then, briefly and without letting it take over, say how three contenders fare against the list: the narrative-gravity view itself, a Lockean psychological-continuity account, and animalism. Keep the weight on the criteria; the scoring interests me least. And if you conclude that some cluster of requirements cannot be met together, say so plainly and tell me which one you would drop — that, I take it, is where the real philosophical work in such a list lies.
