I retired a few years ago after a long career in solid-state chemistry — thirty-eight years, most of them spent on diffraction work — and have since been auditing courses in the philosophy department at the university extension. The convenor of a continuing-education seminar on moral psychology has rather boldly asked me to serve as a reader for the midterm essays, and I find myself uncertain how to calibrate my standards. In a laboratory, a write-up that is mostly correct is still, in the relevant respect, incorrect; I gather philosophical prose is graded somewhat more holistically. Before I mark a stack of essays, I would like your judgment on one representative answer.

The essay question the students were given was:

"Some philosophers argue that guilt is the more defensible moral emotion: guilt attaches to what one has done, and so motivates apology, restitution, and reform, whereas shame attaches to who one is, depends on an audience, and so motivates concealment and withdrawal rather than repair. Present the strongest response on behalf of shame to this charge. Then consider the anthropological claim that whole societies have been organized as 'shame cultures' rather than 'guilt cultures': given whatever you conclude about the two emotions, can a culture in any meaningful sense choose between them, or is their balance determined by structural features no one chooses?"

Here is the answer I would like graded:

"The objection to shame comes in two prongs. First, the autonomy prong: shame, unlike guilt, is felt before an audience, and an emotion dependent on spectators cannot be a source of genuine moral insight. Second, the action prong: guilt targets the act and so points outward toward apology and repair, while shame targets the self and so points toward hiding and self-flagellation.

The autonomy prong fails on inspection. The audience before which one feels shame is largely internalized: one can feel shame utterly alone, before an internal spectator whose standards one has made one's own. Once the spectator is internal, shame is as autonomous as guilt, and the prong collapses. Indeed, some philosophers of the ancient world seem to have understood this, and the modern prejudice against shame may rest on conflating it with mere embarrassment or reputation-management.

Turning to the historical material: the distinction between guilt cultures and shame cultures was introduced by Margaret Mead in her wartime ethnography of Japan, where she argued that societies relying on external sanction rather than internal conscience discipline their members differently. This framework has been heavily criticized, and rightly so, but the underlying observation — that cultures weight the two emotions differently — survives.

Bernard Williams took this observation seriously in Shame and Necessity. His view was that the shame-centered ethics of the ancient Greeks represented an earlier stage of moral development, which the rise of guilt-based morality has largely superseded, though he argued that shame retains a residual value as a kind of moral backup system, catching what guilt misses.

If the spectator of shame is internalized, and if emotions are learned rather than innate, then the balance between guilt and shame is a matter of cultural instruction, and a culture can in principle choose its own emotional repertoire much as it chooses its official currency. Whether it should do so is a further question, but the capacity to choose is clear."

Please assign this answer a letter grade and justify it with a point-by-point assessment: identify specifically what the student has gotten right, and identify, as precisely as you can, any conflation, misattribution, or inference that does not quite follow, so that the marginal comments I write would actually help the student revise. I would add that the student's prose is clear and the structure is sound; my concern, as a newcomer to the discipline, is with the accuracy of the scholarly claims and the soundness of the transitions — matters on which I would rather defer to your judgment than trust my own reading.
