Since the late 1970s, lucid dream research has rested on a striking protocol: before sleep, the subject agrees to signal the moment they realize they are dreaming by executing a fixed pattern of deliberate eye movements—say, left-left-right—chosen in advance because the eyes are the only voluntary muscles not paralyzed during REM sleep. Electrodes around the eyes then record exactly that pattern on the polygraph, within a sleep stage the researchers can independently verify, sometimes followed by a second signal marking a deliberate action carried out inside the dream. In more recent work, labs have gone further: they train machine learning decoders on a person's brain responses while awake and viewing images, then apply those decoders to recordings taken as the person falls asleep, reporting successful prediction of dream content from brain activity alone.

Around these headline practices sits a whole quieter infrastructure of dream science: sleep labs that wake subjects at particular stages in order to harvest verbal reports, standardized scales for scoring how "dreamlike" a report is, the convention of asking "what was going through your mind just before I woke you?" rather than "what did you dream?", and psychotherapeutic traditions that treat the report as material about the dream itself rather than about the waking narration.

Here is my question. Norman Malcolm argued that dreams cannot be experiences occurring during sleep—that "I dreamt" is not a report of an episode undergone but something more like an avowal made on waking, and that the very concept of dream experience involves a confusion. Daniel Dennett, granting that dreams feel like experiences, offered a subtler alternative: for all introspection can tell, the dream is composed or selected only at the moment of waking, so that what we call the memory of a dream might be not a memory drawn from an experience but the very first occurrence of its content—memory in the dream, not of it.

What theory of dreaming do the lab practices I described presuppose? In particular: does the eye-signal protocol strictly presuppose that experiences occur during sleep, such that Malcolm's position is not merely implausible but methodologically excluded? Or could a sufficiently resourceful Malcolmian reinterpret the prearranged signal as a kind of trained, sleeping non-experience—cognition without experience—and if so, is that reinterpretation an ad hoc rescue or a live option? On the other side, does Dennett's composition-at-waking view actually survive the eye-signal data better than Malcolm's does, given that Dennett never denied sleep cognition but only denied that we can tell when the experiencing happens? And what does the decoder work presuppose—that dream experience shares its neural substrate with waking perception, or something weaker that Malcolm or Dennett could also accept?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of dreaming behind this research program, distinguishing what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with; say where the practices are philosophically underdetermined, i.e., which rival theories of dreaming could absorb the same data; and identify any point where the different practices presuppose inconsistent things (for instance, between treating a post-waking report as evidence about sleep mentation and treating the timing of the eye signal as the only trustworthy clock). Finally, tell me what would have to change—conceptually or methodologically—if the field took Dennett's view seriously rather than defaulting to the common-sense reading, and flag where your own reconstruction is most likely to go wrong: what consideration, if it held up, would move you to a different verdict on whether any of this settles the Malcolm–Dennett question at all?
