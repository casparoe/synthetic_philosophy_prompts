Been lurking rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, so my instinct on epistemics has always been "argument screens off authority" — evaluate the claim, not the guy making the claim. Lately I've gotten into the reductionism vs anti-reductionism debate in the epistemology of testimony (roughly: reductionists say believing someone on their say-so is only justified if you can back it with reasons that aren't themselves testimonial; anti-reductionists say testimony has a default entitlement, like perception, until something defeats it) and I keep thinking this debate has a perfect real-world test case nobody ever uses: cold fusion.

Quick facts so we're on the same page — I'm doing this from memory, so correct me if I get something badly wrong, but don't quibble over trivia:

In March 1989, two well-respected electrochemists at the University of Utah, Martin Fleischmann and Stanley Pons, held a press conference announcing they'd produced excess heat in a palladium electrode sitting in heavy water — room-temperature fusion, allegedly. As I recall one of them was literally a Fellow of the Royal Society. They skipped peer review partly because they were scared a rival lab was about to announce similar results and scoop them. Within weeks physicists were shredding their calorimetry at a big American physics meeting, and within a year a Department of Energy review had concluded there was nothing there. By 1990 cold fusion was a punchline and these two had gone from credible scientists to cranks in the public eye.

Here's what I want. Analyse the cold fusion episode through the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate, and be really disciplined about the mapping:

1. Who is the testifier and who is the hearer at each stage? Ordinary people hearing the press conference; physicists hearing about calorimetry they never personally ran; Fleischmann and Pons themselves trusting their own instruments and each other's notes. Note that even the "direct evidence" here was an instrument reading, which for everyone else is testimony about an instrument — does that make the case unusually friendly to anti-reductionism, or is all of science like that and nobody admits it?

2. What was a scientifically literate layperson entitled to beleive in, say, April 1989, by the lights of each theory? The anti-reductionist says: two decorated experts sincerely reporting what they saw, no known defeaters yet, so default trust. The reductionist says: your trust must trace to non-testimonial ground, and for a layperson it basically can't, so suspend. Which one actually gets the epistemology of that spring right? And was the rational move the one that felt natural — provisional belief, fast updating when replications failed — and can either theory even describe that move, or do they both only have vocab for all-or-nothing trust?

3. Where does each theory go silent? Reductionism demands non-testimonial backup but gives the layperson no way to get it for any frontier science whatsoever. Anti-reductionism explains the initial trust but seems to have nothing interesting to say about why "two sincere experts" is worth so much less when they announce it by press conference instead of through peer review — is skipping the journal a defeater, or does it show the default entitlement was always mediated by the institution, which is a reductionist point wearing anti-reductionist clothes?

4. And what does the episode do to my slogan? Is "argument screens off authority" just reductionism about testimony in a trench coat, or does it survive this case fine, because the argument (checkable calorimetry, replication) genuinely did screen off the authority, and the people who lost the most epistemically were the ones who kept trusting the tellers after the argument came in?

One more thing I've been chewing on and want your take on: maybe the live distinction isn't reductionist vs anti-reductionist at all, but what you might call reductionism-in-principle vs reductionism-in-fact — trusting testimony only when it's backed by a checking system that could have caught it (peer review, replication, calibrated instruments), where the backing is itself mostly testimony, but *organised* testimony that has been wrong in detectable ways before and corrected itself. Does cold fusion support that reading, or is it just a sneaky way of being an anti-reductionist while feeling smugly rationalist about it?
