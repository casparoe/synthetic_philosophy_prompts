At my moral philosophy reading group we recently worked through Taurek's 1977 paper on whether the numbers count in rescue cases, and one member — I'll call her Nadia — has since worked out a set of verdicts on variants of the case that I cannot reduce to a single principle. Her record so far: five strangers versus one stranger, all equally unknown to her and in identical peril, she says she would settle by lottery rather than walk straight toward the five, because going to the five would treat the one as if he were not there at all; though she adds that she would not blame a rescuer who simply saved the five. Two strangers versus one stranger: still a lottery, and she remarked that the lone person should not come off worse merely for having no companions. Her close friend versus five strangers: she saves the friend without any lottery, and insists this is not unfair to the strangers. Five people in their nineties versus one ten-year-old: still a lottery — she refuses to let remaining life-years enter the deliberation at all.

Next week we are presenting her with three new cases, and I would like help thinking through them in advance. Case A: three separate groups — of one, of two, and of five strangers, all in identical peril, and she can attempt only one rescue. What does she do, and crucially, with what chances: does every person at risk hold one ticket (giving roughly 1/8, 2/8, 5/8), or does every group hold one ticket (giving a third each), and can her earlier remark about the lone person survive the second reading? Case B: a thousand strangers versus one. Does her position still hand the one a fifty-fifty ticket, and is that a bullet she would knowingly bite? Case C: five strangers versus one, except that one of the five is her brother. Does the special tie flow into the group's claim, or does partiality, for her, only ever license overriding a lottery rather than loading it?

What I want back is not just predictions but the analysis behind them: identify at least two rules that fit her existing record equally well (per-person tickets versus per-group tickets, permissive anti-aggregation with lotteries as a default, any threshold above which numbers start to count, and anything else you think is genuinely live), spell out what each rule predicts for each of A, B, and C, give your best guess on each with your reasons for preferring it, and then propose the single further case — beyond A, B, and C — that would best discriminate between the leading candidate rules. Please also flag any verdicts in her record that strike you as unstable, i.e., ones she might revise under pressure rather than defend.
