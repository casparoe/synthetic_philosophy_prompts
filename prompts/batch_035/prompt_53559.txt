I lead a small team of site-reliability engineers who debug distributed systems, and I've come to think that Dennett's three stances — the physical stance of tracing actual mechanics, the design stance of asking what a component is for, and the intentional stance of treating some part of the system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires — name something we already do implicitly, sometimes well and often badly. My juniors flip between them without noticing: they treat a cache's "belief" that a token is still valid as though it were a mechanism, or they refuse to say a load balancer "wanted" to shed traffic because "it isn't an agent," and the confusion costs us time during incidents. I want a procedure my on-call engineers can actually run in the first thirty minutes of a page: an ordered set of questions for deciding which stance to adopt toward a misbehaving component, what each stance licenses you to infer and predict, when a stance's failed predictions should force a switch to another one, and when the intentional stance is genuinely warranted toward a system that is not an agent in the ordinary sense. Ground it in the substance of the view rather than the labels: the idea that the stances are tools whose justification is predictive leverage, and Dennett's criterion from his work on real patterns, that a pattern is real to the extent that treating it as real lets you predict the data better than you could describing it point by point. Then run the procedure on three cases: a cache serving stale entries after a failover, a rate-limiting heuristic that filters out a class of legitimate users as if it believed they were bots, and an intermittent latency spike that resists both mechanical and functional explanation while the team drifts into anthropomorphizing it. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer engineers will not like, say explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and identify where it has to hand the decision to a person. Write it as continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you'd write a long email to the team. Be explicit about how confident you are in each part of the procedure, and single out the part you are least sure of.
