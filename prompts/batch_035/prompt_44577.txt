My friend is an uncompromising Dennettian about the self. She holds that a self is a center of narrative gravity: not a thing inside you but a theoretical posit, like a center of mass, attributed to a system because doing so makes the system's behavior intelligible and predictable — and there is nothing more to being a self than being the subject of some such narrative. Our arguments about strange cases have produced the following track record, which seems consistent to me but never quite settles on a rule:

1. A split-brain patient whose hemispheres can be interrogated separately in the lab: still exactly one self. The left hemisphere confabulates smoothly over the divide, she says; the story is thinner than it used to be, but there is still only one of it.
2. A woman with dissociative identity disorder whose two elaborated alters keep separate journals and recall separate autobiographies: two selves. The body is shared housing; there are two narratives and therefore two centers.
3. A man whose hippocampi were destroyed by illness, who retains nothing for more than a few seconds and greets his wife as though for the first time, again and again: one self. The narrative is wrecked but unsplit.
4. The most richly drawn character in a long novel series, whose inner life is rendered in more detail than most real people's: no self. Nobody is running that story except the author and us.
5. A woman in a deep coma whose family keeps a meticulous account of her life at her bedside and reads from it to her daily, and whose nurses insist that keeping the biography in view genuinely helps them anticipate her responses: still one self. The narrative is being held in trust, she says, and that is enough.

Three new cases are coming up at dinner next week, and I would like to be ready. First: an AI assistant with persistent memory across sessions that drafts and revises its own running autobiography, consults it before planning, and whose handlers find its behavior hard to predict without attributing beliefs and desires about itself to it. Second: a television series whose writers maintain an exhaustive character bible, attributing a stable inner life to the heroine, and who settle every dispute about next season by asking what she would want; the bible does serious predictive work for real, flesh-and-blood writers, even though the character herself is as fictional as the one my friend already dismissed. Third: a couple who for forty years have made every major decision through a jointly kept notebook, and whose friends have drifted into predicting what they will do by attributing beliefs and plans to the couple as a single deciding unit that fits either spouse taken alone rather badly.

Predict her verdict on each of the three, including whether she will say of the couple one self, two selves, or something she insists is a third thing. Then reconstruct the rule she is implicitly running — I can see at least two readings of her view that fit the five earlier verdicts equally well, one keyed to where the narrative does its predictive work and one keyed to who or what is generating it, and I suspect the case of the coma patient is what keeps both alive. Say which rule you would bet on and why. Finally, propose one further case, not among these three, that would best force the candidate rules apart, and tell me what each of them says about it.
