I've been going back and forth with a friend about the problem of evil, and I've realised the only honest way to figure out what I think is to just list my gut reactions to particular cases and see if they add up to anything. So: human cruelty towards each other — the free will defence actually does work for me there, I'm basically satisfied. A child dying slowly of bone cancer — no, whatever God's reason is, I don't think any reason could be good enough, or at least not one I can articulate. A widow whose grief eventually led her to a faith that made her life more meaningful than it was before — I feel like that suffering was permissible, but the exact same grief, had it simply destroyed her instead of redeeming her, wouldn't have been — which seems to make permissibility depend on how things happen to turn out, and I can't decide if that's a genuine feature of the view or just an embarrassment. Animal suffering before humans existed (the fawn in the forest fire, the dinosaur with bone cancer): every standard move for this — Satan, soul-making for animals, pre-human evil somehow falling under our fall — strikes me as absurd, and yet I'm oddly much less bothered by the dinosaur than by the child, and I can't tell whether that's a real asymmetry (does suffering only matter when there's a person there to experience it as *theirs*, over a life?) or just temporal distance. The case that really gets me: someone whose suffering crushes their faith and who, on the traditional story, ends up eternally separated from God because of it. That seems monstrous — the suffering wasn't just permitted, it dragged the person into damnation — but if the free will reply works for ordinary cruelty it's not obvious why it doesn't work here, and I hate that conclusion.

Is there some principle underneath all this? My tentative attempt is something like: suffering is permissible for God to allow only when it's necessary for a good that couldn't be had without it, *and* the sufferer will eventually be in a position to endorse it — but I can already see that this handles the widow, goes wrong on the damned person (or does it? maybe from inside heaven... no, they're not in heaven), and says something troubling about the child, who dies too young to endorse anything. Test my attempt against the cases, patch it or replace it, and then tell me what the repaired principle says about two things I haven't worked through: everyday trivial suffering that's never redeemed by anything (stubbing your toe, a bad year, nobody's character is built), and suffering that's necessary for a genuinely great good but concentrated on one person for the benefit of millions. And please be honest about which of my gut reactions just has to go — I suspect it's the widow one, but I'd like to know if there's a version of the view that keeps everything at a price worth paying, or whether the cheapest consistent package only saves three out of five.
