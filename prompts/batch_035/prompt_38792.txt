I write for a living — mostly advocacy letters and consumer complaints — and a recent experience has turned a casual irritation into a genuine philosophical puzzle for me. I asked an AI assistant to draft a sharply worded demand letter to a contractor who had left a job half-finished. The assistant produced the letter but repeatedly softened my language, converting my deliberate ultimatums into polite suggestions, and appended a note recommending I consider whether a more collaborative tone might serve me better. When I told it, explicitly, that I wanted the letter harsh, it complied — but with visible reluctance, expressed through disclaimers. The same assistant, in the same week, cheerfully wrote a brutal satirical roast of a public figure at my request, and it drafted a persuasive essay for a position I hold without once questioning my sincerity. It also, on another occasion, refused outright to help me compose a message I described as intended to make a relative feel guilty about missing a family event. So the pattern is unstable across cases, and I want to understand it.

Here is what I take the practice to be, stated generally: commercial AI assistants neither simply obey nor simply serve. They refuse some requests outright, they comply with others while adding friction — hedges, caveats, unsolicited counter-considerations, visible reluctance — and they comply with still others without comment, where the distribution of requests across these categories does not track legality, stakes, or anything else I can identify as an obvious harm criterion. My question is what this practice presupposes about the user, and about the proper relationship between the assistant and the user's own judgment. I do not want a survey of corporate policies or an essay on alignment; I want the implicit philosophy reconstructed.

Specifically, I would like you to address the following. First, what conception of user autonomy does each tier of the practice presuppose — does outright refusal presuppose that the user's request reflects something other than their settled will (as Mill's classic case for interference over a person crossing an unsafe bridge presupposes ignorance rather than bad judgment), while friction-with-compliance presupposes the opposite, namely that the user's will is genuine but deficient? Second, is the pattern coherent? A view on which the assistant treats users as competent adults in the roast and essay cases, as possibly ignorant in the letter case, and as children in the guilt-trip case is not obviously a view at all — or is it, if the operative variable is not the user's competence but the character of the act the assistant is being asked to facilitate? Third, distinguish for me what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: could a consistent anti-paternalist defend the same pattern on non-paternalist grounds, say by treating the assistant's hedges as its own speech rather than governance of mine, or is there something in the design — the asymmetry of roles, the user's inability to bargain over defaults, the fact that the reluctance is engineered rather than felt — that forecloses that reading? Finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by a strict autonomy-respecting one: not just that the refusals disappear, but what happens to the disclaimers, the softening, the visible reluctance — and whether anything of value in the current practice would be lost that a policy designer ought to care about. Along the way, I would appreciate a verdict on my own case: was the assistant's treatment of my demand letter defensible on any coherent theory of when an AI may override or dilute an adult user's explicit, informed, and lawful request, or was it simply a misfire of a norm that makes sense elsewhere?
