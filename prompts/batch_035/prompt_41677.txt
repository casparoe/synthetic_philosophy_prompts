I'm a novelist, about three chapters into a book where my protagonist undergoes gradual replacement — nanites swap her neurons for functionally identical synthetic ones over roughly two years, she's awake the whole time, keeps a diary about it. So naturally I went and read the philosophy to know what she'd actually be going through, and now I'm stuck on something that has nothing to do with plot. The gradual replacement debate has been going for decades and as far as I can tell nobody ever moves. The survival side says: every step preserves function, consciousness depends on function, so she survives, and the intuition that she dies is just substrate chauvinism — the same bias that would say a man with a cochlear implant hears less. The death side says: a copy is a copy, gradualness is a sleight of hand, the diary just gets continued by someone else with her memories, and the slow version works on the reader the same trick Parfit's teletransporter does, only with continuity-of-consciousness confetti sprinkled on top. And then theres a third bunch who say the question is empty — there's no further fact of the matter about whether she survives, "the same person" isn't a natural kind, and both sides are fighting over a ghost pronoun.

My question isn't "who's right." Here's what's actually bugging me. When I imagine the scene where she finishes the last neuron and looks in the mirror, I genuinely can't make myself feel that anything happened to her. My agent, who is not stupid and read the same chapters, says any reader will feel the exact opposite and will feel cheated if the book doesn't at least acknowledge that a death occurred. Two people, same thought experiments, opposite deliverances, zero movement. So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Substantive, verbal, or — my pet theory, tell me if it's nonsense — two different cognitive systems in the same head disagreeing, one evolved to track bodies and one that tracks psychological patterns, neither of which can be argued out of anything, with the philosophers just whichever intuition they got assigned at birth plus post-hoc scaffolding?

And because it matters for the book: if my diagnosis is even half right, is a novel about this just a machine for producing whichever intuition the reader walked in with, or can fiction do something an argument can't — like forcing someone to hold both intuitions about the same person at the same time? I'd also like an honest answer on whether anything would even count as progress in this debate, because if the answer is no I'd like to stop researching and get back to writing.
