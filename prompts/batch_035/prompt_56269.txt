As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I'm commissioning a short story, roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words and in American English, for a magazine issue devoted to the philosophy of time. The story should dramatize, through plot and character rather than lecture, the argument Borges pursues in his essay on the refutation of time: drawing on Berkeley and Hume, he observes that the mind never holds anything but its present contents, that the self of a moment ago is strictly someone else, and that if each instant is complete in itself, then succession — time itself — is a synthesis the mind adds after the fact and can, in principle, refuse. Yet he ends by conceding that the refutation proves too much: even one's disbelief in time is something that happens to one, in time. Build the story around an invented premise along these lines: a cognitive scientist who studies the specious present discovers that the brain encodes no succession whatsoever — only self-contained moments — with the felt flow of time a post-hoc editorial insert, like a preface stitched on after the book is finished. Let her live out the refutation, and let the plot supply the one datum her theory cannot absorb, so that the ending lands on the essay's own reluctant concession without ever quoting or naming Borges. Constraints: no direct quotations from any real text; a reader who knows the essay should recognize its spine, while a reader who doesn't should still be able to follow everything from within the story.
