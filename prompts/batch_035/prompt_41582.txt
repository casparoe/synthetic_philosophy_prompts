I teach an intermediate course on rational choice in a philosophy department, and every spring I hit the same wall. My students accept without complaint that a rational player won't choose a strategy strictly dominated by another pure strategy, and they'll follow iterated elimination for a round or two. But when I define dominance over mixtures — telling them that a strategy can fail to be dominated by any single pure strategy and still be strictly dominated by a randomization over two others — roughly half the class rebels. They object that a requirement which obliges you to compare your options against coin flips is rationality run amok, and that a player who must invoke randomizations to rule anything out has already conceded too much. I want a case that will make them feel the force of the mixed-dominance definition rather than merely memorize it.

Construct me a two-player game meeting the following specifications. First, it must be clothed in a mundane situation my students could imagine themselves in — colleagues dividing up grading, students choosing submission windows for a paper, lab members claiming equipment slots — with payoffs in ordinary goods (points, dollars, hours saved), presented both as a story and as an explicit payoff matrix. Second, each player should have at most four strategies, and no pure strategy may be strictly dominated by any other pure strategy, so that iterated elimination in the purely pure-straightforward sense never even starts. Third, once mixtures are admitted as potential dominators, iterated elimination must run at least two rounds and terminate in a unique surviving strategy for each player, and it may not be collapsible into a single step. Fourth, use only strict dominance throughout — no weak dominance — because with strict dominance elimination is order-independent, and I don't want the interest of the case to rest on order effects. Stipulating the conclusion rather than generating it from the payoffs is cheating: every elimination must be checkable arithmetic on numbers you give me.

After presenting the game, demonstrate that it meets each specification in turn: show each round of elimination with the actual numbers, verify that each eliminated strategy is dominated by a mixture and by no pure strategy, and identify the profile that survives. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the case if that feature is removed — for instance, if one payoff were adjusted so that every dominance relation held directly between pure strategies. Finally, and here I want you to take a side rather than survey the field: is it genuinely irrational to play a strategy that survives pure-strategy dominance reasoning but falls to mixed dominance, and should a rational player in your game in fact play the unique survivor? Give your reasons and confront the strongest objection — the student's objection that iterated mixed dominance presupposes both common knowledge of rationality across rounds and a comfort with randomization that no ordinary agent in an ordinary situation possesses. Close by telling me whether you would teach this case as a demonstration of the power of iterated dominance or of its limits; I intend to put it on the problem set either way, and I need to know which moral to write on the answer key.
