I'm on the governance committee of a forecasting community (~30,000 users) that recently converted into a member-owned cooperative, and we're stuck on who gets to vote in the upcoming charter revision. Currently only dues-paying members vote, but three groups are contesting that: long-time users who contribute forecasts and moderation work yet have never paid dues; members of two adjacent research communities whose projects our treasury part-funds; and developers who don't participate in forecasting at all but depend on our free API and would be directly hit by any change to its terms.

The dues-payers say voting rights should track contribution and financial stake, while the others keep invoking the claim that anyone materially affected by a decision should have a say in it. I have two worries I can't fully articulate. First, the affected-interests position seems to prove too much: in a networked community, nearly everyone is marginally affected by nearly everything, and I don't see a non-arbitrary cutoff for how much affectedness buys how much voice. Second, the contribution-based position looks self-serving in a subtle way: the incumbent members get to define which contributions count, and they'll naturally pick the ones they themselves happen to have made. A test someone proposed—would this decision have any content if the group in question were subtracted from the world?—seems to help but I'm not sure it isn't just a fancy way of favoring the status quo coalition.

Is there a principled way to draw the boundary here, or should we concede that any boundary will be somewhat arbitrary and focus instead on procedural safeguards (supermajorities, sunset clauses, an appeals process for excluded groups) that make the arbitrariness less dangerous? If you think the second, I'd like to hear how you'd distinguish legitimate safeguards from a ratchet that entrenches whoever holds power at incorporation time.
