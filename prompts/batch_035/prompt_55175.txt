I'm a junior on my school's ethics debate team, and I want help dissecting a proverb that keeps beating me. Last week I defended the following position in practice rounds: if any of us would wade into a shallow pond to save a drowning child, accepting muddy shoes as the cost, then distance and the number of other able bystanders cannot make a moral difference, and so we are equally obligated to give to effective charities that rescue faraway people in mortal need. The opposing side's captain ended every rebuttal with the phrase charity begins at home, delivered as though it settled the matter — and, judging by the nods from our coach, it did. I don't want to come back with mere annoyance; I want to understand what I was actually being told. What are the genuinely distinct precise claims that proverb could be advancing? I can see at least these candidates: that special obligations to family and local community legitimately outrank duties to strangers; that we can verify local needs and know our help actually arrives, while distant aid is opaque; that a dollar simply does more good nearby; that aiding outsiders beyond some threshold is praiseworthy but never required, so my claim of equal obligation rests on a mistake about what duty even is; or perhaps that the pond case fails because of some difference between rescue and contribution that I haven't isolated. For each reading you can construct, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive thesis worth taking seriously, or straightforwardly false; and — this is the part I most need — whether, even if true, it would defeat my argument or merely explain why people are psychologically reluctant to act on it. Please give me a numbered list I can bring to the next practice, ordered from weakest to strongest, plus a short note on which reading my opponent was most plausibly invoking, since I suspect she was shifting between several without noticing.
