I've been collecting my gut verdicts on acts of lawbreaking for a cause, trying to figure out if there's an actual principle behind them or if I'm just cheering for teams. Here's the list:

- The classic lunch-counter sit-in, in a segregated town: protesters sit where the law says they can't, stay when asked to leave, get arrested, dont resist. I think this is not just permissible but admirable.
- Same open, arrest-accepting tactics, but used by people blockading the entrance of an abortion clinic because they sincerely believe abortion is murder. My gut says this is NOT justified, even though structurally it's nearly identical to the sit-in. This one bugs me.
- A road blockade by climate protesters glueing themselves to the asphalt during morning rush hour: commuters delayed, ambulances rerouted. I wince at the collateral cost but I come down on permissible, though I'm less sure than I'd like to be.
- Basically the same blockade, but by truckers shutting down a border crossing for three weeks over vaccine mandates. I notice my reaction is much harsher, and I can't tell if that's the cause talking or something real, like the difference between disobeying one targeted law vs. inflicting diffuse pressure until the government caves on policy generally.
- Someone trespasses on logging land, openly, chains themself to a tree, gets arrested: fine by me. Someone sneaks in at night and sabotages the logging equipment so it can't run: not fine. Same goal, same law broken basically, but the secrecy and the destruction seem to matter.
- A government analyst leaks documents proving illegal surveillance, holds a press conference, accepts arrest: mostly fine. The same analyst leaks anonymously and then flees the country to avoid prosecution: feels distinctly worse to me, even though strategically it's smarter. Something about taking your punishment being part of what makes it legitimate?
- A war objector refuses to pay the portion of her taxes that funds a war she believes is unjust, sends a letter to the tax office every year explaining, pays the rest: I say fine, sort of touchingly fine.

So what's the principle? The obvious candidate is procedural -- openness, willingness to accept punishment, proportionality, no serious collateral harm. But that seems to vindicate the clinic blockaders, which I don't accept. The other candidate is content-based -- you may only disobey laws (or on behalf of causes) that are actually unjust -- but then I've handed a license to anyone sufficiently confident in their own moral judgment, which includes every fanatic. And notice my road-blockade pair suggests content is leaking in anyway.

Is there some principled way to split the difference, or do I have to give up one of my verdicts? And if there is a good principle, what does it say about two cases I keep going back and forth on: a juror who votes to acquit someone clearly guilty under a law she thinks is unjust (is that even civil disobedience, since jury nullification is itself legal?), and the climate road-blockers doing it again and again with no discernible effect on policy -- does futility matter, or is that the wrong question?
