my friend (medieval history phd, insufferable in the best way) and i spent like three hours at the pub arguing about the whole "AI is the printing press" thing. you know the version: just like gutenberg broke the church's monopoly on knowledge, LLMs will break the gatekeepers, democratize expertise, and after some chaos we get an enlightenment. he thinks this is a terrible analogy used to smuggle in optimism, and his counterargument was honestly annoyingly specific: he said the early print industry's real killer app was stuff like indulgences, form letters, and liturgical calendars — i.e. the press initially made money mass producing exactly what the establishment already wanted, not liberating anything. the reformation needed a Luther, political chaos, and a good century and a half of religious warfare to shake out, literacy rates barely moved at first, and he threw in the ottoman thing at the end, how they basically didn't allow printing in arabic script until the 1700s as if that settled it. his conclusion was that the press analogy, taken seriously, predicts AI's first big application is industrial-scale slop serving incumbents, that any genuinely disruptive moment will be as unpredictable and as ugly as the reformation was, and that the "democratization" people are confusing a side effect with the mechanism.

i want to actually adjudicate this rather than just concede because he knows more about incunabula than me. can you build the mapping out properly: what plays the role of the scribes, the indulgence, the vernacular bible, luther, the index of forbidden books, the thirty years' war? then tell me where the mapping genuinely breaks — im guessing things like marginal cost of copying vs cost of inference, whether the church even had the option to not adopt printing, the fact that a press doesn't improve itself, stuff like that, but do it properly. and then the key question: which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusions being drawn vs which are just pedantry. does his pessimistic reading survive his own scrutiny the same way he thinks the optimistic one doesn't, or is he making the mirror-image mistake? also if you think there's a better analogy from the same period (cheap paper? the rise of the clerical profession? postal systems?) i'd honestly like to hear it, because i get the feeling "the printing press" is doing way too much work for everyone in this debate and it'd be nice to retire it
