I am a novelist — four published books, fifteen years of drafts, an MFA's worth of workshop vocabulary worn smooth from use — and I have lately been reading philosophy of language as a civilian. Austin's lectures on performatives struck a nerve I want examined properly, because my working suspicion is that everything he says about speech acts is something working novelists have always known under other names, and also that he quietly insulted my entire profession in passing.

Here is what I want. Take Austin's framework — the performative utterance that does something rather than reports something, the felicity conditions under which an utterance like a promise or a naming or a bequeathing succeeds or misfires, the locutionary/illocutionary/perlocutionary trichotomy, and the eventual collapse of his own constative/performative distinction — and translate it, piece by piece, into the vocabulary I actually use. I mean the real craft vocabulary: scene objectives, subtext, beats, what a line of dialogue is doing in a scene as opposed to what it says, the difference between what an utterance does to another character and what it does to the reader, dramatic irony, free indirect discourse, the implied reader, the sense in which a scene can fail to have earned a line. Where he says an utterance misfires when the speaker has no authority to perform it, I suspect that is what I mean when I say a line of dialogue lands as false; where he distinguishes what is said from what is done in saying it, I suspect that is subtext; where he talks of effects on the hearer, I suspect part of it is effect on the reader and part is not, and I cannot tell which.

Do the translation honestly — do not smuggle in my vocabulary's assumptions and present them as his claims, and do not prettify the parts of his scheme that have no counterpart in mine. Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually care about. First: what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what cannot be said in a novelist's terms at all. I suspect the hardest case is the normativity of promising — Austin tracks something like a real obligation incurred by an utterance, and the nearest thing I have is the debt a promise on the page incurs toward the reader's expectations, the Chekhov's-gun logic of setups and payoffs. Are those the same phenomenon at different scales, or did I just equate two unrelated things because my vocabulary has only one word for debt? Second — and this is where I stop being neutral — Austin, in the course of setting up his theory, brackets utterances made by an actor on stage, introduced in a poem, or spoken in soliloquy as in some peculiar way hollow or void, not serious, parasitic on the normal use of language. That bracketed territory is my entire livelihood. In my fiction, every wedding vow, every christening, every bequest is spoken by someone who is, in his sense, an actor on a stage. And yet fictional promises plainly do things: they bind characters to each other, they create obligations the plot then enforces or punishes, they act on the reader's expectations with real force. So tell me whether the case Austin set aside as a degenerate instance is, from where I stand, the primary case — whether my vocabulary treats his exception as the rule and can say things his framework cannot — or whether my vocabulary is missing exactly the dimension of seriousness that made him bracket it. In short: when the translation fails, I want to know whether the residue indicts his framework, mine, or the intuition I started with.
