In my graduate seminar we spend two weeks on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and each year the discussion collapses in the same place. My own credence that the theorem, properly stated, establishes utilitarianism as a moral doctrine stands at roughly 0.3; a colleague in the economics department, who regards the result as a settled matter of consistency, puts it far higher — above 0.7, I should think.

I want your own number, but the number is the least of it. Decompose it into the sub-questions on which the figure turns, assigning each a probability and a single line of justification. The candidates I have in mind, though you may add to or reject them:

(i) Whether the von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities that the theorem sums across persons are interpersonally comparable in the relevant sense, or whether the theorem simply presupposes the comparability that utilitarianism needs and cannot itself supply it.

(ii) Whether the requirement that social preferences over lotteries obey the expected-utility axioms is a genuine constraint of rationality on social evaluation, or a substantive assumption that builds utilitarianism's indifference to distribution into the machinery from the start — this is where Diamond's old objection about fairness across persons bites, since two outcomes with the same utility sum can differ in what is owed to each individual.

(iii) Whether the theorem licenses anything more than a positive weighted sum of utilities, so that the equal weights of utilitarianism require a separate and independent argument — as I understand matters, Harsanyi's impartial-observer route delivers equal weights by construction, but that is a different argument, and conflating the two is the most common sleight of hand in this literature.

(iv) Whether what is derived is a doctrine about how persons are to be treated, or merely a representation theorem about the mathematical form a social ordering must take given certain axioms — in which case the theorem constrains without compelling.

Combine your probabilities into an overall figure, tell me which term the figure is most sensitive to, and identify the argument or consideration that, if it succeeded or failed, would move your number most. Then locate the disagreement between my 0.3 and my colleague's 0.7: I suspect it lives in (iii), or in the conflation that (iv) is intended to expose, but I should like to see whether your decomposition bears that out or instead places the gap somewhere I have not thought to look.
