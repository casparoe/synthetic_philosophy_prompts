In my ethics reading group we've spent three weeks on the demandingness objection to consequentialism, and I've come away troubled by a retort one member has now formalized — she calls it the reflexive version of the objection. Her claim is that the objection proves too much: it refutes not just consequentialism but any moral outlook containing anything like a duty of easy rescue, which is to say every moral outlook anyone has ever defended in earnest. The argument runs: (1) Consequentialism is false because it requires each of us to keep relieving preventable suffering until we reach the point of marginal utility — the point at which giving one more unit costs us as much welfare as it delivers to others. (2) Common-sense morality contains the rescue principle: whenever you can prevent something very bad at small cost to yourself, you must. (3) Taken case by case, the world's preventable suffering decomposes into an enormous sequence of rescue-principle cases, since each further small donation is, considered on its own, a small-cost prevention of something very bad. (4) What one is obligated to do is closed under conjunction: if you ought to do A and ought to do B, then you ought to do both. (5) So common-sense morality likewise requires giving to the point of marginal utility, and the demandingness objection, if sound against consequentialism, is fatal to common-sense morality too. (6) Since we are not about to conclude that common-sense morality is false, the objection is unsound — which is exactly what she wanted, being the group's token consequentialist.

I can see where I'd like to dig in: step (4) looks like the weak plank, and I'd much rather keep the rescue principle than closure under conjunction. But I can't make the denial principled. If obligations don't aggregate, then someone facing five hundred individually obligatory rescues seems permitted to perform only some arbitrary subset, and the only alternative I can see is introducing a threshold — do "enough" rescues and you're off the hook — whose arbitrariness strikes me as worse than the disease (why does doing rescues 1 through 47 discharge you when rescue 48 sits there, as cheap and as urgent as the first?). And when I floated a fair-share view — my duties cap out at my share of the total burden, conditional on others doing theirs — she pressed back: that makes what morality demands of me hostage to how badly everyone else behaves, which she claims is just consequentialist aggregation wearing a cooperative disguise. Meanwhile another member insists the real flaw is earlier, in step (3): a cost can be small when counted one rescue at a time and enormous when counted together, so the rescue principle, read naturally, isn't blind to the total burden the way her decomposition assumes.

So I want a diagnosis, not a team to join. Which premise or step should be rejected, and why did it look fine in the first place? In particular: is the closure premise actually false, or only false on some readings; is the "on its own" reading of the rescue principle an equivocation or a legitimate restriction; and what does each escape route cost — the threshold view's arbitrariness, the fair-share view's dependence on mass noncompliance, and whatever the holistic reading of cost has to pay? If you think the argument is valid and something plausible must simply be surrendered, say which premise is least costly to give up and what we lose with it.
