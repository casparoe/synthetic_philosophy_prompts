I'm preparing a seminar unit on the paradox of deontology: the objection, pressed most influentially by Samuel Scheffler, that agent-relative constraints are puzzling because they can forbid an agent from committing one violation of a constraint in order to prevent several further violations of that very constraint — so the deontologist must sometimes ensure that more of the forbidden thing occurs rather than less. What I want from you is not an essay on the paradox but a piece of experimental design: a matched set of scenarios that factorially separates the two variables that are usually confounded in discussions of it — the number of violations and whose violations they are.

Construct three thought experiments, each no longer than a paragraph, all set in ordinary professional life (a workplace, a hospital administration, a university department — no violence, no ticking bombs, no courtroom drama). In all three, the agent's only available means of preventing the further violations is to tell a single lie herself, and the lie produces no benefit other than preventing those further lies: no one is rescued, no advantage secured, no side-effect goods. The only thing at stake, in every scenario, is the total number of lies told in the world and who tells them. The lies prevented should be comparable to the lie told in kind and in harm; make the victims comparably situated; and, insofar as realism permits, make the further violations certain to occur if she refuses — if perfect certainty strains realism, say so explicitly and explain whether the residual uncertainty matters to the design.

The three scenarios should differ as follows. In Scenario A, the single lie would prevent five lies that five other, fully responsible people would otherwise tell. In Scenario B, the single lie would prevent exactly one lie that one other fully responsible person would otherwise tell. In Scenario C, the single lie would prevent five lies that the agent herself would later tell — she can foresee with confidence that if she does not close off the situation now, she will predictably succumb to pressure on five separate occasions and lie each time. So A varies both numbers and agency, B varies agency alone (the numbers are equal), and C varies numbers while holding agency fixed in one sense (all the violations are hers) but not in another (they are future rather than present). Part of your task is to make C realistic without making her future lapses seem like compulsion or impaired agency.

After presenting each scenario, demonstrate that the design works rather than merely asserting that it does. State what verdict an absolutist agent-relative deontologist must give, what verdict an act consequentialist gives, and what verdict a threshold deontologist with a catastrophe clause gives — and be explicit about the fact that the catastrophe clause should not engage at all here, since the harms at stake are comparable in magnitude, so threshold views collapse into the absolutist verdict in these cases. Say for each scenario which structural feature of the case is generating the verdict.

Then answer the diagnostic questions. First: does the sense that the deontologist's refusal is irrational survive in Scenario B, where the numbers are equal and only agency varies? If it does not, that is evidence that the paradox is fundamentally about aggregation rather than about the agent-relativity of constraints — say whether you think that is the right conclusion and what it would take to resist it. Second: what should the agent-relative view say about Scenario C? If preventing one's own future violations counts as a reason, the constraint begins to look like a rule about minimizing the total of one's own violations, which threatens to collapse into a kind of intra-personal consequentialism; if it does not count, the constraint looks oddly indifferent to whether one's own future self violates it five times. Press both horns.

Finally, give the defense its due. Show how a defender of constraints would attack the design itself: perhaps by denying that refusing to lie counts as allowing the other lies in any sense that matters; perhaps by appeal to the doing/allowing distinction as it applies to one's own future acts; perhaps by arguing that the clean-hands rationale for constraints, whatever its ultimate merits, simply does not generate a puzzle in the one-to-one case. Identify which of your three scenarios is most vulnerable to such an attack, and what happens to the design if that scenario falls. Bear in mind throughout what these cases are for: the verdicts must fall out of the theories applied to the scenarios, not be stipulated by the scenarios' framing — a case that merely narrates the deontologist's refusal as cold or self-righteous has cheated. If you find that no realistic scenario can meet my specifications, do not quietly lower the bar: tell me which specification fails, why, and what that failure itself reveals about how the paradox is usually presented.
