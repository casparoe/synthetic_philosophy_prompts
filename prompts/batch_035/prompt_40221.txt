I came to the Sleeping Beauty problem through the rationalist blogosphere rather than through the journals, and I've now followed it for well over a decade: the old threads where it was first fought under that name, Elga's original paper and Lewis's halfer reply, and above all the anthropics discussions where it gets re-litigated as SSA versus SIA. I know the moves by heart. Elga's indifference argument; Lewis's "no new information" reply and its notorious consequence about Monday conditionals; the Dutch book challenges to the halfer; the variant where Beauty is woken a million times under tails and intuition screams that heads is nearly impossible; Bostrom's Presumptuous Philosopher, which shows that each answer, scaled up, commits you to something outrageous somewhere else.

And I've concluded the debate has stalled. Every argument on both sides is at least twenty years old, each camp can state the other's case better than its own proponents can, and in all the threads I've read I can count the conversions on one hand.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement isn't about probability at all, but about what the number is a probability of. The thirders, I suspect, are answering a question about this awakening: what fraction of awakening-moments like this one are heads-awakenings? The halfers are answering a question about this run of the experiment, for which the awakening is merely the occasion on which she thinks about it. Same words, different referent — so the fact that both sides produce a number between 0 and 1 and call it "her credence that the coin landed heads" makes it look like a contradiction when it may just be an equivocation.

But I can already hear the objection, and it's why I distrust my own diagnosis: there are cases where the two readings come apart decision-theoretically, and the camps don't differ merely in bookkeeping. When a bet is settled per awakening, halfers and thirders accept different wagers, and it's hard to see how a purely verbal dispute could do that. So maybe it's substantive after all: a disagreement about whether the bare fact of being awake at all is admissible evidence — about whether self-locating information ("this is an awakening") can confirm or disconfirm a non-centered proposition ("the coin landed heads"), when the agent knew in advance that she would be in a state in which that self-locating fact obtains. And if it goes that deep, it may bottom out in bedrock intuitions about whether an experience you knew you would have can count as evidence at all.

So diagnose it for me. Is this verbal, substantive, or two different questions under a single heading? If verbal, you owe me an account of the per-awakening bets: does adopting that betting framing simply force the thirder reading on anyone who accepts it, or does the halfer have principled grounds for rejecting the framing itself rather than the conclusion? If substantive, say precisely where the evidential question turns, and whether anything could settle it — a formal result, an agreed-upon limiting case, a dissolution into decision theory, or a distinction no one has yet drawn. What I don't want is a survey of the positions; I know them. What I want is a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, and, given that diagnosis, a concrete account of what would count as progress.

One more requirement. I've watched confident diagnoses of this debate fail for twenty years, so hold your own to a standard: end by telling me what would change your mind about the diagnosis you've given, and identify the step in your reasoning where you think it is most likely to go wrong. If you can't find such a step, that itself is a datum about how these analyses work, and I'd like you to say so.
