I've been kicking around AI safety circles for a few years and I've pretty much internalised the iterated amplification and distillation picture: if you can decompose any question you can't answer into subquestions that a weaker, more trustworthy system (or a human consultant) can answer honestly and well, then recursively amplify and distil the results back down, both competence and alignment should ratchet upwards together rather than eroding with each step. Lately I've been trying to argue myself out of it and failing, which honestly makes me suspicious of myself. My standard move when a decomposition turns out subtly wrong is to say the decomposition was badly chosen, and the view can always say that, forever, about any failure. So I've possibly just insulated a thing I like.

So tell me straight: what would count against this position? Not just the standard catalogue — that humans can't decompose above their own competence level, that oversight is easier than generation until it suddenly isn't, that honest answers to subquestions can compose into a dishonest or dangerous whole. I want to know what observations would count as evidence the framework itself is broken rather than badly implemented. If someone actually ran the loop at scale, what would competence degradation look like in a way the framework predicts shouldn't happen — e.g. distillation repeatedly discarding exactly the judgement that made the amplified answer good, so the distilled student keeps passing evaluations while being worse in ways the evaluations can't see? What would alignment degradation look like that isn't just "the subquestions were loaded"? And crucially: which of these would a serious proponent concede as counting against the view, versus diagnose as a fixable bug? Is there a principled line between those two categories at all, or is the "bad decomposition" escape hatch enough to render the whole thing unfalsifiable — and if it is, is that fatal for a research programme of this kind, or is it just what you should expect from a framework rather than a theory, in the way "natural selection" survived lots of specific failures?

For context, the thing that got me worrying is an argument with a friend who does interpretability work. Her claim was roughly that decomposition can't preserve a certain kind of know-how: an amplified system may answer each subquestion correctly because the overall structure of the inquiry was set up well, but that structure is exactly what doesn't survive distillation, since the student only ever sees the question–answer pairs, never the reason the questions were asked in that order and at that granularity. If she's right, the competence loss isn't an implementation bug, it's structural — the thing that makes amplification work is precisely the thing distillation can't compress. I'm honestly not sure whether this is devastating or just the standard generalisation problem wearing a costume, and I'd like you to place it on the map of defeaters along with everything else: fatal if true, merely awkward, or something the position was never committed to in the first place. Rank the defeaters for me, and tell me which ones I should actually let move me.
