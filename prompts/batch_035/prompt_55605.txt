I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. At work, a few of us keep a fortnightly lunch slot that has drifted from distributed systems into philosophy of mind, and I've been assigned the meta-problem of consciousness for the next session — thirty minutes, a whiteboard, and an audience of engineers who have read a popular book or two at most. By the meta-problem I mean the move, associated with Chalmers's 2018 paper on the subject, where instead of asking what consciousness is, you ask why we talk and judge about it the way we do: why we insist there is something it is like to be us, why a mechanistic story seems incapable in principle of capturing that, why the intuition doesn't die when the mechanisms are described. My understanding is that Chalmers treats explaining all this judging as an "easy problem," in his sense — a problem about behavior and mechanisms like any other — and that's precisely the part my group will find either trivial or scandalous. I'm not asking you to explain any of this to me. I'm asking for the plan of the session.

Two things I want your judgment on. First, the order. My group has to be made to feel the hardness of the hard problem before the meta-question will even register — otherwise the whole thing looks like a category error — but if they sit with the hard problem too long, the flip to treating their own bafflement as data will feel like a dodge. Where is the pivot, and what has to be established on either side of it? Second, my stock example. I keep wanting to open with something from our world: a service that can produce fluent reports about its own states while having no access to its own implementation, so the reports are sincere, internally consistent, and incurably blind to their own causes. It plays well in this room, but I half suspect it's rigged — it builds in the conclusion that the blindness is the whole story, and I'd rather the group earn that conclusion than have it smuggled to them. Should I use it, repair it, or replace it, and with what?

Please structure your reply in the following named sections, in this order: "Session arc" — the sequence of ideas, what depends on what, and where the pivot falls, roughly 300 words; "The carrying example" — your verdict on my analogy and what any replacement must contain and avoid, roughly 150 words; "Misconception map" — the errors this audience will predictably form, in the order they'll surface, each with the point in the arc where it should be absorbed, roughly 250 words; "What to leave out" — and a line on why, roughly 100 words; and "The closing check" — a single question whose correct answer would show they've separated explaining the phenomenon from explaining the reports, roughly 80 words.
