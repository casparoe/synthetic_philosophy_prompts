I am a curator of prints and drawings who has spent the last two years following the philosophical debate about forgery and aesthetic value, and I have come to suspect that it is not going anywhere. As far as I can tell, the positions were laid out in the aesthetics journals within a few years of each other in the mid-1960s and have been restated, in roughly the same words, ever since. Camp one says: if a forgery is visually indistinguishable from the original, then whatever we lose when it is exposed — monetary value, historical testimony, the satisfaction of being in causal contact with the master — is not aesthetic value, and calling it that confuses the properties of the object with the properties of its production. Camp two says: knowing that a work is a forgery genuinely changes the experience of it, changes what we see in it, so the aesthetic difference is real and internal, not external; and in any case there is no sharp line between what we see and what we know while seeing it. Camp one replies that the "changes the experience" move proves too much, since learning that the painter was a scoundrel also changes the experience for some viewers, and nobody wants to call that an aesthetic defect. Camp two replies that the copying-versus-creating difference is not like the scoundrel difference, because it bears on how the visible features came to be as they are. And so on, for six decades.

Two further things make me suspect the impasse is structural rather than just hard. First, the cases we actually handle are nothing like the clean thought experiments: half the "genuine" works in any old-master collection are partly workshop, partly later hands, restored within an inch of their lives, and the debate's categories seem built for a purity those objects never had. Second, we now have images that are, in the relevant sense, nobody's — generated by models trained on everything — and when the camps apply their positions to these, both camps start splitting internally in ways their founders did not anticipate, which suggests to me that the disagreement is not one disagreement but several stacked on top of each other, possibly including a verbal one about what "aesthetic" is supposed to modify: the experience, the object, or the act.

So I want a diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict on which camp is right. Please structure your response as follows, in this order:

1. **The positions as each side actually holds them** (~150 words) — a fair, compressed statement of each camp, stated so that a member of that camp would accept it.
2. **Why the standard arguments bounce off** (~200 words) — an analysis of why each side's best move fails to touch the other's core, showing where each argument quietly presupposes what the other side denies.
3. **The diagnosis** (~300 words) — what kind or kinds of disagreement this really is: verbal, substantive, or several distinct questions travelling under one heading; and, importantly, whether the messy real cases (workshop pictures, heavy restoration, model-generated images) are noise obscuring the real debate or evidence that the debate was framed around an unreal object from the start.
4. **What would count as progress** (~200 words) — a concrete answer: what distinction, reframing, class of cases, or change in the question would actually move this, if anything could.
5. **An honest conclusion** (~100 words) — your own best judgment, with reasons, about whether this debate is resolvable in principle or permanently stuck, and why.

Throughout, please engage with the arguments rather than name-dropping authors; I have read the name-dropping surveys already.
