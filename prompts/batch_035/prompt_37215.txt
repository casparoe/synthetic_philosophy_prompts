I'm four hundred pages into a family saga and I've hit a wall that turns out to be philosophical rather than plot-shaped. The novel follows a family in an unnamed country whose fortune came from a quarry run on forced labor — the grandfather managed it and knew exactly what he was doing; the grandmother mostly kept the books and the blinds closed; the kids grew up with the money and no idea; and the youngest granddaughter, my POV character, finds the ledgers in 1998. My editor wants to know what the book "believes" about whether she owes anything, and I've realized I don't know what I believe — only that I have verdicts. So here they are, my gut on cases. The grandfather: guilty, obviously. The grandmother who kept the books and never asked where the workers went: guilty of something, though I'm not sure it's the same something. The son who inherited the business, found out what it was in his forties, and quietly sold it: I hesitate to call that guilt, but I do think he had a duty to make restitution — the money was poisoned in a way the guilt maybe wasn't. The granddaughter born decades after everything, who wears her great-grandmother's ring and loves the family house: I want to say innocent, and yet I'd think she was doing something wrong if she sold the house and pocketed every cent without a second thought. The family as a whole — still existing, still having reunions, still sharing the name: I'm comfortable saying the family bears *something*, shame at least, maybe debt, even though no individual member is guilty in the grandfather's sense. And then the country itself, most of whose current citizens were born long after: I half believe it can meaningfully be the subject of an apology, and half think that's just a confused way of talking about people who did nothing.

What I want from you is the principle, or the small set of principles, behind these verdicts. Someone in my writing group mentioned that Jaspers sorted German guilt into four kinds after the war, and I suspect my grandmother/son/granddaughter cases are tracking something like that split — but don't just recite categories at me, tell me what actually distinguishes the kinds, where exactly the line falls, and whether it can bear the weight of a whole family on one side of it. Then two test cases I genuinely need answered, because the plot forks on them: (a) the granddaughter gives the house away entirely and lives in a rented flat — has she done more than she owed, exactly what she owed, or the wrong thing in a different way, mortgaging her one life to a debt that was never hers; and (b) her cousin, whose branch emigrated before any of it, changed the family name in the fifties, and has spent fifty years loudly repudiating the lot of them — does that repudiation discharge the family-something, and if not, what would? I want a committed answer: pick your principle, state it as precisely as you can, run every one of my verdicts through it, and be upfront about which verdict it can't save. If one of them has to go, I need to know which one and why before I write the last chapter, not after — my editor will smell fence-sitting a mile off, and honestly so will the granddaughter.
