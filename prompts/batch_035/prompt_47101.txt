I teach a final-year undergraduate module in social choice theory, and I am setting this year's closed-book final examination. Last year's question on Arrow's impossibility theorem produced answers I could have written myself before the exam was sat: virtually every script recited the theorem, listed unrestricted domain, Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives and non-dictatorship, offered a sentence on why independence is meant to be attractive, and stopped. This year I want a question that cannot be answered by reciting the four conditions. I have a particular angle in mind — one we spent two weeks on in lectures — and I would like you to turn it into a proper exam question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The angle turns on an asymmetry between two ways of responding to the theorem by weakening a condition. If we weaken independence, possibility returns at once: the Borda count, for instance, satisfies unrestricted domain, Pareto, non-dictatorship and transitivity of the social ordering, and fails only independence. If instead we drop Pareto and keep the rest, we do not get possibility back. A corollary we proved in lectures, by running Arrow's argument on decisive coalitions, says that any social welfare function satisfying unrestricted domain, independence, non-dictatorship and transitivity of the social ordering — provided the rule is not imposed, that is, the social ordering is not simply fixed in advance regardless of what anyone prefers — must be an oligarchy: there is a group of individuals such that whenever all of its members strictly prefer x to y, society ranks x above y, and whenever any one of them strictly prefers x to y, society is at least indifferent between them. The members of the oligarchy are jointly decisive, and each individually holds a veto. So dropping Pareto buys almost nothing, while dropping independence buys a great deal, and I want the question to force students to confront that asymmetry and its implications for the fashionable reaction to Arrow that says, in effect, that the theorem merely demonstrates independence is an implausibly strong condition, and that relaxing it dissolves the problem.

The deliverables are these: one essay question, answerable in ninety minutes by a student who has attended the course but has memorised nothing beyond the lecture notes (a single prompt or two linked parts is your call); a model answer of roughly 600 words; and a marking scheme out of 20 that separates a first-class script from an upper-second by naming the specific moves each must contain. My expectation is that an upper-second answer should reproduce the corollary and the Borda point accurately, whereas a first-class answer should additionally diagnose why the asymmetry arises — I have my own view (roughly, that independence is a structural constraint on how the rule may use information about preferences, while Pareto is a substantive constraint on outcomes, and the proof shows the structural constraint does nearly all the work on its own), but full credit should be available for any well-argued diagnosis, not only mine — and should engage seriously with the "just reject independence" reaction rather than dismissing it. For each band, include a one-line note on the mistake students typically make at that point. Please also reserve at least two marks for noticing that the corollary as I have stated it needs its non-imposition proviso, since a permanently imposed social ordering satisfies unrestricted domain, independence, non-dictatorship and transitivity while making every voter irrelevant; a script that spots this unprompted, and can say what work the proviso is doing, is exactly what I want the question to elicit. Finally, no bibliography is required — award no marks for name-dropping references that are never used, and expect the model answer to stand entirely on its own.
