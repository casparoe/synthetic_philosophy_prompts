Long story short: I'm a retired physicist (solid state, 35 years) who's gotten way into philosophy since leaving the lab, mostly decision theory, and my old department talked me into chairing their "continuation review" panel — the group that decides whether the university keeps funding research projects that are over budget and behind schedule. We review maybe five projects a month, stakes run from $200k up to $40M, and every meeting goes the same way: the PI says they're 90% done, the panel feels like we've come too far to quit, and we fund it. I just read about the dollar auction and it scared me a little — the whole panel looks like the two bidders stuck at $4 and $5 fighting over a dollar, where each individual vote to continue seems locally sensible but the whole sequence is crazy.

Can you write a decision procedure the panel can actually run in a 30-minute review? Ground it in what's actually known about escalation of commitment, not just "don't fall for sunk costs" — I want the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the call to a human because it's a values question rather than a facts one. Then run it on three cases: (1) a detector upgrade that's six years in at 3x budget, where the next milestone would also serve a second experiment; (2) a migration of the department's old data system that's already failed twice, but the legacy system is now end-of-life so walking away isn't really on the table; (3) the dean's pet sky survey, politically untouchable, definately over budget — and be honest if the procedure says kill that one, since I won't like hearing it. Also tell me plainly where the procedure will misfire. Better I find out now than in the meeting.
