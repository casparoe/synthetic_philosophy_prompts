I'm on the standards desk at a mid-size news outlet and I keep having to referee sentences about genes that arrive from reporters who half-remembered something from a psych textbook. The claims come in two flavors lately: "twin studies show X is 60% genetic, so there's no point funding [intervention]" and the pushback "heritability numbers are meaningless, so the study says nothing at all." I know enough to know both are probably wrong but not enough to say exactly why fast, and I need something my copy editors can actually run in ten minutes at deadline without a stats person on call.

Write me a checklist or decision procedure my desk can follow for any sentence that leans on a heritability number or a "genes for" claim. Things it needs to catch, roughly in the order we'd hit them when reading a draft: whether the number is being applied to the same population it was measured in, whether the writer has slipped from "differences within this group are partly genetic" to "differences between groups are genetic," whether the trait was measured in an environment that's basically uniform (because that's exactly where heritability runs high and it tells you the least), and whether the writer turned "heritable" into "unchangeable." Also where the procedure should just tell the editor to strike the number entirely versus soften it, and where it has to punt to a human because the question is a values call or just genuinely empirical and over our heads. Be honest about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three real-ish cases from my queue: (1) a story saying since IQ heritability estimates run high in modern cohorts, Head Start-style programs can't do much and we should say so; (2) a story about a GWAS that found a bunch of common variants each explaining a tiny sliver of variance in educational attainment, with the reporter wanting to write that the "missing heritability is solved, genes for education found"; (3) a story about a rare genetic condition where carriers essentially always develop the condition if they live past a certain age -- I suspect our instinct to demand "environment matters too!" hedging is wrong here and the procedure should be able to say that cleanly.

One of those three should show the procedure giving an answer I won't like, and you should flag which. Also tell me what the procedure can't do -- like whether a heritability figure from one population and era can be extrapolated at all, and what to do when we genuinely can't tell. Please write in American English.
