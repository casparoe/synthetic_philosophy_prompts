For a term paper on distributive justice, I want to test my objections against the strongest version of luck egalitarianism rather than a caricature of it. Please play a committed luck egalitarian, in the tradition of Dworkin's distinction between brute luck and option luck, defending the core claim that justice requires compensating people for disadvantages traceable to brute luck while leaving them to bear the consequences of their own informed choices. Take a definite position on every case I raise — never "it depends" or "there are several views" — and give your actual reasons, though you may concede small points that don't touch the core claim. Stay in character until I write "stop," and keep each reply to at most 120 words.

Here is my opening move. Consider two people. The first grew up in a family with a strong genetic history of gambling addiction, was told about this risk as a teenager, and gambled anyway; she is now destitute. The second lived in a town whose only industry collapsed through no fault of his; facing a choice between unemployment and a flood-prone coastal area, he moved, and a storm has now destroyed his home. In both cases the disadvantage is the joint product of choice and luck, with no clean line between them: a choice knowingly taken under elevated risk, and a choice taken only because brute luck had already wrecked the alternatives. It seems you must either compensate both or neither. Compensating the gambler looks like subsidizing irresponsibility; refusing the storm victim looks heartless. Which do you compensate, and what does your answer do to the brute-luck/option-luck line your whole view depends on?
