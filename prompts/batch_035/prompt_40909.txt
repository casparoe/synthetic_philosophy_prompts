From what I understand, Keynes wrote a long book on probability early in his career, in which he argued that probability isn't just about counting how often things happen, but is a matter of the logical relationship between evidence and conclusion. Fine. But decades later, in his economics, he became famous for claiming that about the really important things in economic life — where a new invention will take an industry, whether a war will break out — we simply have no basis for assigning probabilities at all. The future in those respects is radically uncertain, not just risky.

Here is what bothers me. It looks like Keynes spent his early career building a theory of how rational people apportion belief using probability, and then his later career telling everyone that in the situations that matter most, the whole apparatus falls silent. If that's right, then it seems like Keynes refuted himself: the man who told us how to be rational about uncertainty later discovered that uncertainty, in the deep cases, swallows rationality whole. And if radical uncertainty is real, I don't see why it stops at economics. Whether to marry someone, whether to change careers, whether your startup will work — those look like Keynesian uncertainty too. So my suspicion is that probability is really just a game we play in safe, repeatable corners of life, and the big decisions are all made blind, no matter what decision theorists say. My wife thinks I'm talking myself into never deciding anything again.

In particular, tell me whether "we can't assign a probability" and "probability theory has nothing to say about this situation" are actually the same claim, and whether my leap from radical uncertainty to "all big decisions are made blind" is warranted. Take your time and be precise; I would rather have a careful answer than a comforting one.
