I'm a formal methods person -- my day job is writing specs in a proof assistant, babysitting SMT solvers, coming up with loop invariants, occasionally reading about soundness bugs in verification tools. Every so often at a party (ok, fine, on reddit) someone tells me Gödel proved that the mind is bigger than any machine, or that no system can understand itself, or that AI is doomed / constitutions are impossible / economics can't be complete, because incompleteness. I actually have a decent grip on the real theorems: I know the first theorem from the second, I know completeness of first-order logic vs. incompleteness of arithmetic are two different senses of the word and it bugs me when people mash them together, and I know arithmetization is the load-bearing trick. So pop-Gödel usually strikes me as hand-waving, but I've never carefully checked that instinct.

What I want is a translation exercise rather than a debunking. Restate the main extra-mathematical uses of Gödel -- the Lucas/Penrose anti-mechanist argument about minds outstripping any formal system, the fuzzy "no system can fully account for itself" version people apply to consciousness and self-knowledge, and the political-philosophical version where somehow constitutional rules or legal systems can't contain their own amendment -- in the vocabulary I actually use: soundness and completeness of a proof system relative to a semantics, what a proof checker can and cannot verify about itself, reflective towers of trusted bases, model checkers and their finite state spaces, the fact that we happily trust SAT solvers whose soundness we take on faith. Go use by use, and tell me for each: which claims translate cleanly into that vocabulary, which ones only fit if you bend them, and which have no counterpart at all in the world of verification.

Then give me the honest residue report. If something doesn't survive, is that because my vocabulary is missing something (it has literally no way to talk about a mind, or about what a proof "means" to the prover), or is it because the original claim was never as precise as it sounded? I have a going suspicion that everything interesting here collapses into "a sufficiently strong, sound system can't prove its own consistency, a stronger one can, and you mostly shouldn't care" -- tell me whether that's too quick, and be specific about what argument or consideration would change your verdict if you're wrong. Don't hedge into mush; I'd rather you commit to a reading of where the pop uses of Gödel go wrong (or where I'm being too dismissive) and flag the single point in your reasoning where you're most likely to have made a mistake.
