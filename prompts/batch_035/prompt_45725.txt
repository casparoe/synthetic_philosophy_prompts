My department has two people who have been sniping at each other for years about the St. Petersburg paradox. One is an orthodox expected-utility type: the theory says the game has infinite expected value, so any finite price is a bargain, and if you balk at paying $1000 for a play, that's a fact about your psychology or your nonlinear utility for money, not a defect in the theory. The other thinks it's a genuine reductio: no coherent agent can value anything infinitely, so unbounded utilities have to go and the paradox is the proof. They've finally agreed to do a real adversarial collaboration and I somehow got asked to draft the joint statement. Write it for me: the claims both can sign (about the math itself, about what the paradox does and doesn't show for finite games, and about which popular 'solutions' are non-starters for both of them); the precise disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording; the cruxes — I suspect one is whether diminishing marginal utility of wealth alone caps a rational willingness to pay, and another is what happens with repeated play, where the aggregation results show that even averages over many plays keep misbehaving; and for each crux a concrete case or thought experiment both sides agree bears on it (feel free to use the Pasadena variant, where the series only conditionally converges, as a stress test), with each side's prediction written down in advance. Also flag whether any part of their fight is really verbal — e.g. whether 'the rational price' means a one-off choice by a single agent or something about long-run aggregate behavior — and if so which part and why. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.
