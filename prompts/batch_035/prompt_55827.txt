I've been reading about Max Weber's old idea that the Protestant ethic helped bring about modern capitalism — the notion that certain religious attitudes, especially the Calvinist worry about salvation and the idea that relentless work in one's calling was a sign of election, turned a steady pursuit of profit from something morally suspect into a duty. I have no academic background; I'm just working through this on my own, so please bear with the amateur framing.

In my discussion group, one member defends Weber with a picture that everyone there seems to find convincing: a society's economic arrangements are its hardware, and its culture is its software. On this view, medieval Florence had excellent hardware — banks, double-entry bookkeeping, long-distance trade, accumulated capital — but it never produced capitalism because it was running the wrong software: values that treated the merchant's life with suspicion, that ranked the contemplative life above the active one, and that saw no religious significance in steady, disciplined accumulation. The Reformation, he says, was a software update, and capitalism is what the old hardware did once the new program was installed. He uses this to argue against the purely materialist story in which economic conditions cause everything and ideas are just a reflection of them: if culture is the program the economy runs on, then culture can genuinely be a cause, not merely an effect.

Another member thinks the picture is doing more work than the thesis. His objections, as best I can reconstruct them: hardware and software are separable by design, engineered that way on purpose, while economies and values grow together and shape each other continuously; a program is installed all at once and then runs unmodified, whereas the Protestant ethic spread unevenly, was reinterpreted as it traveled, and kept changing in response to the commercial life it was supposedly directing; and a program cannot rewrite itself to serve the interests of whoever runs it, but value systems seem to do exactly that — the people who preach them benefit from them in ways the software analogy quietly hides. It also seems to assume there is a clean layer below the culture that stays fixed while the culture varies, and I'm not sure history offers that.

Could you sort this out for me? Set out the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the hardware, the software, the update, the manufacturer — then identify where the mapping breaks down, and tell me which of the breakdowns actually matter for the causal claim and which are incidental. I'd especially like your judgment on whether the conclusion my friend wants from it — that culture can be a cause, and that Weber is vindicated against the materialists — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was being carried by the analogy the whole time. And if the hardware/software picture can't carry that weight, I'd like to know what a better picture would be.
