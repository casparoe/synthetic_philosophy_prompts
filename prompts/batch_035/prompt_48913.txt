My closest friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the idea — the one Dennett spent years popularizing — that Darwin and Turing each performed a strange inversion of reasoning: Darwin explained the appearance of design without a designer, competence without comprehension, and Turing explained comprehension by building it out of competence, mere rule-following all the way down. We ended the argument without either of us conceding much, and it left me with a pile of unresolved questions I'd like to sort out. I have a six-month sabbatical from my usual work and want to devote it to writing one substantial essay on this cluster, so I need to decide which question in it actually deserves the time.

Some of what we fought over, to give you the flavor of the cluster: my friend insisted that the two inversions are not really the same move — Darwin removes the comprehending designer from the explanation, while Turing lets comprehension back in at the end, reconstituted as an emergent product of uncomprehending parts — and that Dennett's habit of treating them as twins papers over a live asymmetry. I pushed back that the asymmetry may not matter if the deeper open question is whether comprehension is simply a certain kind of competence: one that is compressed, transferable, and counterfactually robust, so that the distinction dissolves rather than inverts. My friend also floated something I haven't seen discussed much: whether the inversion can be run a third time, on institutions — markets, bureaucracies, legal systems as competence without comprehension — and whether that extension illuminates the original case or trivializes it. And late in the night we got stuck on the question of what would even count as evidence of comprehension in a system whose behavior we can only probe from the outside, which is now the thing current language models are supposedly forcing everyone to confront.

What I want from you is a prioritization, not a survey. Which open questions in this area are most worth six months of conceptual work aimed at a serious long-form essay, and in what order? For each question you prioritize, tell me why: what makes it important rather than merely unresolved, whether it is tractable by careful thinking as opposed to empirical work, how many of the other questions in the cluster depend on it, and what would have to become clearer for you to move it up or down the list. Tell me also which of the questions we argued about are overrated or are really just restatements of each other in different vocabulary.

One request about intellectual honesty: please keep a clean separation between what is genuinely settled among people who know this literature, what is contested between identifiable camps, and what is open because nobody has yet framed it well. If you state a view that serious people disagree with — including anything in my friend's framing or mine — say that you are doing so rather than presenting it as the consensus. Where my framing of the questions above smuggles in a contested assumption, I'd rather know that than have you work inside it silently.
