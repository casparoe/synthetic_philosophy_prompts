I'm a graduate student in public history, and for a seminar paper I've been cataloguing the labeling conventions that large museums follow when they display narratives sitting somewhere between myth and history — the Trojan War, King Arthur, the Exodus. The pattern puzzles me. In the Bronze Age galleries, the Trojan War is introduced as myth, but the label goes on to mention that excavations at Hisarlik revealed a sizable city destroyed in the late Bronze Age, and that the war may reflect a real conflict, with heavy hedging of the kind that says a historical kernel may lie behind the story. In the medieval galleries, Arthur is called a legendary king, with a note that some historical commander might stand behind the legend — hedging about the existence of a person rather than of events. The Exodus is handled differently again: it is called a founding narrative of a tradition, with a note that archaeology has found no trace of any mass migration out of Egypt, and the label offers no suggestion at all that the event may have occurred. Meanwhile, two galleries over, a pharaoh's military campaigns are stated as flat fact on the basis of his own monumental inscriptions, with no hedging whatsoever, even though those inscriptions are self-serving propaganda and, in many cases, our only source. So one institution, presumably applying one policy, seems to issue several different verdicts on broadly similar evidential situations.

I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit epistemology of this labeling practice. What does the practice presuppose about what makes a narrative history rather than legend or myth — a threshold of independent corroboration, and if so, corroboration of what exactly, the events, the setting, or the existence of a central figure? Does the practice treat history as a property of the narrative itself or of our epistemic position with respect to it? Notice that the labels seem to vary with the size of the claim even where the direct corroboration is comparably thin: a story of hundreds of thousands of people wandering for decades gets no may-have-occurred at all, while a story of one city's fall gets a hedged maybe, and it's tempting to read this as the practice implicitly encoding scale-sensitive priors — the idea that a bigger claim would have left bigger traces, so silence in the record weighs more heavily against it. Is that a real feature of the implicit view, or just an artifact of curatorial caution? Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, tell me where it is underdetermined such that rival epistemologies would license exactly the same labels, and flag any incoherence in it — is it consistent to hedge the historicity of the war while asserting the campaigns unhedged, when the campaign evidence is narrower in source and more biased in provenance than the tradition behind Troy?

Finally, tell me what would actually have to change in the wall text if the museum replaced this implicit view with a straightforwardly probabilistic one, expressing credences instead of using the myth, legend, and history vocabulary. Would schoolchildren and casual visitors be able to read such labels, or does the old trichotomy do something a credence cannot, such as indicating what kind of access to the past each narrative claims to give us — communal memory, poetic tradition, documentary record — rather than merely how likely it is?
