I'm a PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on whether credibility *excess* can itself be a form of testimonial injustice -- Medina argues for something in this neighborhood, but don't worry about reconstructing his view letter-perfect, I care about the structure of the problem, not attribution. The thing I need is a case with a specific shape, and I keep failing to build it myself, so I want to see whether it can be built without cheating.

Here's the spec. Construct a realistic scenario -- a hiring committee, a hospital shift handover, a journal's peer review, whatever you like, but no sci-fi and no perfectly rational idealized agents -- in which a hearer adopts a *corrective* credibility policy: they deliberately extend more credibility to speakers from a group that is genuinely and systematically under-credited, and this policy is one they are right to adopt. Its expected epistemic and justice-theoretic value is higher than any alternative they could realistically implement, so the case can't cheat by making the policy a clumsy overcorrection. Yet in the scenario, the policy must produce a concrete outcome in which a *third party* is wronged qua knower -- someone whose testimony gets dismissed or discounted, or whose epistemic agency is sidelined, because the corrected speaker now occupies the evidential space. The wrong must land on a bystander, not on the person receiving the excess credibility and not on the hearer.

After the scenario, I need you to do the following walkthrough, in this order:

1. **The scenario** -- one paragraph, ~150 words max.
2. **The baseline injustice** -- ~100 words on why the uncorrected version of the case (no corrective policy) is straightforward testimonial injustice under the standard Fricker-style definition, i.e., a credibility deficit that tracks group identity.
3. **The new wrong** -- ~150 words on who gets wronged in the corrected version, in what respect, and why it's a wrong *as a knower* rather than mere bad epistemic luck or just being made worse off.
4. **Does the definition stretch?** -- ~200 words on whether the strict definition can even register this harm. The harmed third party isn't under-credited because of prejudice against their group, so either the definition has to be stretched to cover credibility excess with a downstream victim, or the harm falls outside testimonial injustice entirely. Tell me which way you think it goes and what the stretch costs us.
5. **What's doing the work** -- ~100 words. My suspicion is that it's the triadic structure, the wrong traveling through a middle speaker, but maybe you'll find the load-bearing feature is something else. Say what it is and what happens to the case if you delete it.
6. **The two defenses** -- ~200 words. Steelman the Fricker-style defender who says the corrective hearer does nothing epistemically unjust, and separately the Medina-style defender who says the excess itself, received by the beneficiary, is the real injustice and the third-party harm is a red herring. Then tell me which attack is stronger and where that leaves the case.

Two more constraints, and please take them seriously. First, the third party has to be wronged as a knower: it's not enough that a false belief circulates or that outcomes get worse -- someone's testimony has to actually be discounted or their epistemic agency damaged. Second, I want you to check whether the hearer has access to some refined policy that fixes the problem, and if they do, tell me what it is. If there's a dominating refinement, the case collapses into the hearer just having picked a suboptimal rule, and I need to know whether mine does that.
