I've been reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum since around 2015, and I thought I'd internalized every version of the race-dynamics worry — Moloch discourse, the phrasing about "racing to the bottom," the whole genre. But a user on a forum I frequent posted an argument that seems to prove dramatically more than any of the standard versions, and after a week of chewing on it I can't find where it breaks. Please act as a decision theorist with a serious sideline in game theory and AI strategy, think carefully before answering, and do some visible brainstorming first: sketch at least three candidate diagnoses before committing to the one you'd actually defend.

The argument, as I reconstruct it:

(1) Between any two actors racing to build transformative AI, the one that deploys first captures essentially all the value; there is no meaningful second place, and a lead of even a few days compounds, because earlier systems help build the later ones that beat you.

(2) Every voluntary safety measure — capability caps, dangerous-capability evals, red-teaming before release, interpretability work that delays deployment — either consumes resources or delays deployment. Call the resulting delay the alignment tax.

(3) In a winner-take-all race decided by arbitrarily small margins, any positive tax is decisive regardless of magnitude: paying it means losing by exactly that much, and no expected-value reasoning about "reputation" or "catastrophe risk" changes this, because catastrophes can only happen to actors who deploy at all — dead-last and safe is still dead-last.

(4) So a rational racing actor pays no voluntary alignment tax whatsoever.

(5) And the race frame is scale-invariant: mid-sized labs shipping products, startups chasing demos, and open-source collectives all face their own winner-take-most races with their own compounding leads.

(6) Therefore voluntary safety measures are impossible in principle at every level of the ecosystem, and the only levers that exist are externally enforced mandates and alignment techniques with literally zero deployment cost.

The poster draws the punchline that working on alignment is pointless unless you're either making it free or lobbying for mandates. Two things bother me. First, the conclusion seems to prove too much: mutatis mutandis the same reasoning would show that airlines should skip maintenance and pharma firms should skip trials whenever enforcement lapses, which they observably don't. Second, whenever I try to pin down the bad step, I find myself asserting something I don't believe in the other direction — e.g., that safety is usually free, or that races are almost never winner-take-all — and then the poster just points back at (1) and the compounding story.

So: which premise or inference should be rejected, why did it look fine to me (and to him), and what does rejecting it cost? If you think the argument is actually valid, say so and tell me which premise is least costly to give up. I'd also like to know exactly where the disanalogy with aviation and pharma enters — not merely that there is one — and whether the conclusion survives in some weakened form that a serious person should still worry about. Be rigorous and commit; don't give me a survey of possibilities.
