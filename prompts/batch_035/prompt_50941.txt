I'm in a philosophy of language course and we just spent two weeks on Moore's paradox (the "it's raining but I don't believe it" thing). My professor, who I'll call Dr. V, keeps issuing snap verdicts on example sentences, and I've been keeping a list because there seems to be a pattern I can't quiet pin down. Her rulings so far: "It's raining but I don't believe it" — absurd, cannot be sincerely asserted. "It's raining but I'm certain it isn't" — equally absurd. "It's raining but you don't believe it" — perfectly fine, even a natural way to talk someone into grabbing an umbrella. "It's raining but my downstairs neighbor doesn't believe it" — fine. "It rained all last night, but I don't believe it did" — fine, and she said the natural home for it is a puzzled historian looking at conflicting records. "Either it's raining or I don't believe it" — fine, and she made some offhand remark about how it can't be false without you being wrong about the weather. "John believes that it's raining and that he doesn't believe it" — she paused a long time on this one and finally said it's strange to attribute but not impossible for someone to believe. And "It's raining, though I could be wrong about that" — completely fine, apparently even virtuous.

Next week she's promised verdicts on a new batch, and a few of us have a bet going. The new cases: (a) "It's raining but I don't know that it's raining"; (b) "I don't believe it's raining, but it is" (note the flipped order); (c) "It's raining but I have no evidence whatsoever that it is". Predict her verdicts, but more importantly tell me what rule or rules she's actually running. My own guess was something like "asserting p commits you to believing p, and the second half cancels the commitment," but my friend swears its a knowledge-norm-of-assertion thing and that the rules will split on case (a). Are there genuinely multiple rules that fit every verdict she's given so far, or is one of us forcing it?
