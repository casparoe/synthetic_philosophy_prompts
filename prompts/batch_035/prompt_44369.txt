For roughly four decades, the debate between Molinists and their critics over the so-called grounding objection has struck me as completely stalled, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another installment of the arguments themselves. Let me lay out the dispute as I understand it, because the diagnosis needs to engage what the sides actually say. The Molinist holds that, prior to any decree to create, God knows the truth values of subjunctive conditionals of creaturely freedom — conditionals of the form "if this agent were placed in these circumstances, the agent would freely choose thus," where the choice is libertarianly free and hence not settled by the agent's nature or the circumstances. The objector asks what makes such a conditional true at that stage. There is no decision yet to serve as a truthmaker; the agent may not even exist; and it is obscure what in God or in the uncreated realm could ground a contingent claim about a particular creature's future free act. Robert Adams pressed a version of this objection in the 1970s, and William Hasker has developed it in an explicitly explanatory form: since nothing explains why a given counterfactual of freedom has the truth value it has rather than the opposite one, we cannot so much as rationally believe that it has one. The Molinist replies have stabilized into a few familiar moves: Plantinga's early line that these conditionals are simply, contingently true, without needing to be made true by anything — on the model, perhaps, of other contingent truths that plausibly lack truthmakers; Craig's insistence that the demand for a ground is an optional and tendentious theory of truth being foisted on the theist; and Dean Zimmerman's more recent attempt to ground the conditionals in tensed facts, hybrid facts concerning the times at which the decisions actually occur. The pattern since then is always the same: the objector says nothing settles the conditional's truth value, the Molinist says truth does not require settling, and nobody on either side budges. What is odd is how narrow the disagreement is: most objectors remain theists who accept omniscience, so the issue is not whether God knows such conditionals but whether there is anything there to know.

Here is my working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: the impasse is not really about middle knowledge at all, but about truthmaking and explanation in general, with the philosophy of religion serving as the battlefield. The Molinist and the objector disagree about whether truth requires an explanation, and they would disagree just as thoroughly if the subject were negative existentials or counterfactuals about indeterministic quantum events rather than free creaturely choices. But I can see two rival diagnoses. On one, the load-bearing assumption is libertarian freedom itself: if a choice is genuinely undetermined, perhaps there simply are no facts of the "would freely choose" form prior to the choice — that is the open theist's exit, and it suggests the Molinist and the grounding objector are jointly committed to a combination (indeterminism plus antecedent truth) that generates the puzzle, so that the debate is a symptom of an unstable position shared by both parties. On another, the two camps are answering different questions under one heading — the Molinist addressing whether God could have the knowledge the doctrine ascribes to Him, the objector addressing whether the doctrine is internally intelligible — so the debate is verbal in the way that makes each side's arguments sound obviously question-begging to the other.

I would like you to assess these diagnoses against what the participants actually say, and to tell me, given the best diagnosis, what would count as progress. Candidates worth weighing: a general account of truthmakers or "grounds" for counterfactuals whose absence is the shared presupposition; a worked test case on which the sides could be made to disagree about something other than Molinism, which would confirm or refute my hypothesis; or a reframing of the question that dissolves the impasse. I also want an honest answer about whether no progress should be expected — whether this is bedrock disagreement about the explanatory demands on truth, of a kind that no distinction or result could end — and, if so, why the participants keep writing as though an argument could. Please do not adjudicate Molinism itself; I am asking about the structure of the disagreement.
