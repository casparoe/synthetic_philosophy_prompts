hi, so i'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, mostly longform stuff, and I keep getting assigned pieces where some new tech is declared "inherently political" and then someone else in my DMs insists the tech is neutral and it's all in who uses it. I've written the blockchain-libertarian story, the biased-algorithm story, the "does social media make us worse people" story, and honestly each time I feel like im making it up as I go along, because nobody ever tells me what would COUNT as an artifact carrying politics in the first place.

So before I write another 3,000 words on this, I want the spec, not the takes. Write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of whether and how artifacts get to have politics has to deliver. The cases it must get right — I'd include Langdon Winner's old thing about the Robert Moses overpasses on the Long Island parkways that supposedly kept buses off, the way the same encrypted messaging app reads as a human-rights tool in one country and a harassment-coordination tool in another, and covid contact tracing, where the Google/Apple exposure-notification design baked a whole model of who you might have infected into the protocol itself. Also this one from my own life: my building swapped keys for a phone app that requires a card on file with the bank, which quietly decided that unbanked guests are less welcome. Distinctions it has to respect — built-in politics vs politics of a particular deployment, the artifact's politics vs the manufacturer's intentions, and honestly whether "political" here means partisan, distributive, or something about who has to adapt to whom. Results it cant contradict — everyday facts about adoption and repurposing, the way people misuse things, the fact that designs get reversed or worked around.

For each requirement, give me the case or consideration behind it. Then tell me which are non-negotiable and which are just what one camp (the STS people, the techno-determinists, the "it's just a tool, humans all the way down" crowd) treats as sacred. And the bit I most want: point out any pair of requirements that look jointly unsatisfiable — like whether you can respect the fact that users reinterpret everything and still say anything is built in at the artefact level. Finally, briefly: which single requirement does the most work ruling out the popular positions — Winner's, the neutrality view, and the "code is law" line. Don't score them all in detail, I'll do that in the pub.

Also please write it in British English throughout — behaviour, labour, organised, artefact (yes I know I spelled it the other way above, i've been in america too long), that sort of thing. And keep it at the level a working journalist can actually use, not a literature review — I'm not asking for reading lists or citations, just the thinking. cheers!
