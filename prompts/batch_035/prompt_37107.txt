I'm a novelist and I got roped into running a 45-minute session at a writers' retreat on how history actually turns — the contingency thing, "for want of a nail." My audience is other novelists, and the problem is that fiction trains you for inevitability: you plant the gun in act one and it goes off in act three, so these people instinctively treat the past as a well-plotted novel where everything was headed where it ended up. I want to plan the session, not have you explain contingency to me. What I need: the order the ideas have to go in — I suspect they can't hear about a lost horseshoe changing a kingdom until they've been disabused of hindsight first, but I might be wrong — one carrying example that isn't Franz Ferdinand (everyone's seen that one) but that a general reader will half-know, and a way to handle the pushback I know is coming, that the same outcome would've arrived by another route anyway, which is actually a real distinction I want them to leave understanding: some outcomes are overdetermined and some are knife-edge, and it matters which is which. Also: the misconception I'm guessing they'll form, that contingency just means chaos so nothing matters and no cause is ever bigger than any other, and where exactly in the sequence to head that off. And how to tie it back to their craft — when a plot turn should *feel* contingent versus when the reader needs it to feel fated — without the thing turning into a workshop on endings instead of a session on history. One question I can ask at the end to check whether they actually got the overdetermined-vs-knife-edge thing, and what I should openly refuse to get into (someone always brings up alternate-history novels, and I can feel the whole session dying when that happens).
