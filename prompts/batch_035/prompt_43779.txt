I'd like a systematic comparison of the self-sampling assumption (SSA) and the self-indication assumption (SIA) as rival treatments of anthropic bias: the question of how my evidence that I exist, and that I occupy this particular position among observers, should update my credences about the world. Please state each assumption in a precise enough form that the difference is visible in the arithmetic of the update — what is counted, what is conditioned on, and what measure is placed over observers — before applying them to four test cases.

First, the Carter–Leslie doomsday argument: show exactly how SSA lends it force and how SIA's bias toward hypotheses with more observers is supposed to cancel that force, and say whether the cancellation is principled or merely a convenient offsetting of two errors.

Second, Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher case, where SIA's preference for the theory on which more observers exist threatens to swamp any ordinary empirical evidence for the simpler theory. Assess how damaging this really is, and whether SSA's own embarrassments (especially the reference-class problem, i.e., the sensitivity of its verdicts to how the class of observers one might have been is drawn) are better or worse.

Third, the Sleeping Beauty problem, where the SSA/SIA dispute is often said to map onto the halving/thirding split. Say whether the mapping is exact or whether it imports extra structure.

Fourth, a case I have not seen in the literature, so please treat it as novel and reason it through from the assumptions rather than from precedent. Call it the Universe-Builder's Discount. A device flips one fair coin. On heads, it creates a single universe containing a billion observers. On tails, it creates a billion separate universes, each containing a single observer. Every observer in every outcome has exactly the same total evidence, including full knowledge of the setup. Since the total number of observers is equal on both sides, it seems both SSA and SIA must yield credence one-half in each outcome — yet on heads there are vastly more fellow observers in one's own universe, and on tails vastly more universes containing an observer with one's evidence. One natural reading is that the SSA/SIA disagreement is not really about observer counts at all, but about which base measure — universes or observers — is fundamental, with each assumption smuggling in a different one. Assess that reading: does the case expose a hidden parameter both assumptions share, and is there any non-question-begging way to fix it?

In comparing the two assumptions overall, weigh tractability, resistance to counterintuitive verdicts, and fit with ordinary Bayesian reasoning about cases where the number of observers is fixed. I do want you to reach a tentative verdict about which assumption (or which departure from both) one should adopt, but keep it calibrated: state how confident you are in it and identify the single point where a defender of the rival assumption has the strongest comeback.

Finally, a request about the exercise itself. If you believe my framing of any of these cases misdescribes what SSA or SIA actually entails — for instance, if SSA or SIA does not in fact deliver the verdict I have assumed for it, or if the two assumptions do not genuinely come apart somewhere I take them to — say so plainly and correct the setup before proceeding. I would rather have the cases fixed than have them answered as posed.
