**Exam question (25 pts):** "Hedonism about well-being says a life goes better to the extent that it contains more pleasure and less pain, pleasure being the only thing good in itself. The heterogeneity problem is this: consider the pleasure of a warm shower after shoveling snow, the pleasure of finally understanding a proof, the pleasure of an inside joke landing with an old friend, the pleasure of a back scratch, the pleasure of revenge served cold. If you try to name the one property all these episodes share, the obvious candidates fail: they aren't all bodily sensations (understanding the proof isn't); they aren't all intense; and 'they all feel good' either adds nothing beyond 'they're pleasurable' (circular) or seems just false for some of them. Philosophers have offered various unifying accounts: (a) the 'one common feeling' view — there is a single felt quality of pleasantness present in all pleasures, just a very thin one; (b) the attitudinal view — what unifies pleasures is not a feeling but a stance: in each case the subject takes a favorable attitude toward the experience itself, endorses it, wants it to continue; (c) the desire-satisfaction view — an experience is a pleasure just in case it satisfies a standing desire (for warmth, for comprehension, for the joke to land). Explain the problem, say what each of (a)–(c) gains and at what cost, and say which, if any, you find most promising and why."

**Student's answer:** "The heterogeneity problem is the problem that pleasures don't seem to have anything in common. The pleasure of a back scratch and the pleasure of finally understanding a proof feel completely different, so the question is what single property they share that makes them both pleasures. This was originally raised as an objection to Mill, since Mill was a hedonist, and Mill answered it with his distinction between higher and lower pleasures: intellectual pleasures rank above bodily ones. So the common-property question is answered by ranking — higher pleasures are better, and that is what matters for utilitarianism.

As for the three theories: (a) the common-feeling view says there is one thin felt quality present in all pleasures. Introspection supports this: when I attend to my various pleasures they do all have a certain something, even if it is hard to describe. Its cost is that specifying the something without just saying 'pleasant', which is circular, is difficult. (b) The attitudinal view gains unity by moving the common factor from feelings to a stance — in each case the subject endorses the experience and wants it to continue. Its cost is that attitudes can be unconscious: you can take a favorable attitude toward something without feeling anything, and since pleasure is by definition conscious, the attitudinal view can't be a theory of pleasure. So it collapses into (c), the desire-satisfaction view, which fails for the same reason: desires can be satisfied without the subject noticing, e.g. a standing desire fulfilled while you sleep.

The most promising view is (a). It preserves the datum that pleasure is felt, and the difficulty of specifying the common something looks like a job for psychology (finding some neural signature) rather than a fatal flaw for hedonism. So hedonism survives the heterogeneity problem."
