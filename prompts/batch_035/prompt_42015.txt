Write a satirical piece, roughly 650 words, in the form of an annual performance review issued by an outside consultancy for a fictional mid-ranked country — invent the name, and a few plausible-sounding but invented statistics — whose government has reorganized itself around a single KPI: its position in the annual World Happiness Report. The country is to be treated throughout as an underperforming employee, with sections for an Overall Rating, Key Accomplishments, Areas for Growth, SMART Goals for the Coming Fiscal Year, and a 360-degree review panel made up of neighboring countries plus the perennial top performer, who contributes only gracious, insufferable Nordic understatement.

The comedy must come from getting the measurement issues right rather than from invented absurdities. Each recommended intervention should quietly be a category error or a distortion of what the survey actually measures, and a specialist reader should be able to name the error without help:

- The headline number is a life evaluation: respondents place themselves on a ladder with the best possible life for them at the top and the worst possible life at the bottom. So the consultant's flagship recommendation — a national program of expectation-calibration seminars, framed under a rubric like raising ladder awareness — should let readers see that a country can climb the ranking by lowering what people believe is possible for them, without anything improving.
- Evaluative life satisfaction and day-to-day affect come apart, and the reviewer's enthusiasm for the former over the latter (day-to-day feelings are legacy metrics and hard to shift) should expose the choice of measure as itself a policy choice.
- At least one goal should quietly instantiate Goodhart's law — once the ranking becomes the target, it stops measuring what it used to measure — for instance a national initiative to remind citizens, at the moment of survey, how much better things are than five years ago.
- The closing Overall Summary should deadpan, without the reviewer noticing, the question the document never asks itself: whether the country exists to make its citizens' lives go well or to make the number go up.

Keep the register of corporate HR jargon deadpan throughout — no winking, no overt editorializing. The reader should come away having felt, rather than been told, the genuine issue: that a country optimizing its standing on a self-report measure changes what the measure measures. Optionally, include one parenthetical employee comment in which the country's own wellbeing statistician raises a substantive objection — say, about cultural differences in how people use the top end of a scale — which the reviewer misfiles under resistance to feedback.
