My friend and I have been arguing about the moral weight of suffering versus happiness for months, and I've finally assembled enough of her considered judgments that I think I can test whether she's running one consistent principle. Here is her track record. She refuses to trade one stranger's month of severe arthritic pain for a thousand strangers' months of pleasant dinner-and-conversation experiences, and she refused again when I raised it to a million; she says at some absurd number she'd "maybe" budge, but she can't picture getting there. Yet she is not a pure negative utilitarian: she thinks a world of mildly contented people with ordinary aches beats an empty world, so pleasure counts for something. She has no problem with one person accepting a mild headache so that a dozen people each avoid a migraine — arithmetic within the realm of pain is fine by her. And she's happy to let a loud party impose slight annoyance on a few neighbors for the sake of hundreds of guests' enjoyment, so she clearly thinks mild pleasures can aggregate against mild pains.

Next week we're going to argue about two new cases, and I want to walk in prepared. Case one: a treatment that would spare one patient a year of chronic pain that she herself would describe as "bad, but bearable — not torture," funded by money that would otherwise pay for deeply moving experiences — say, once-in-a-lifetime trips, weddings, concerts — for a hundred thousand people. Case two: the same year of chronic pain, but now weighed against giving five people a rare, genuinely transcendent experience of the kind that people say changed their lives. My suspicion is that her earlier verdicts are jointly consistent with more than one principle: a threshold view on which suffering above some severity can't be compensated by any amount of lower-intensity pleasure (with her "maybe" hedging just marking where the threshold gets murky); a massive-but-finite weighting view on which pain simply counts thousands of times more than pleasure; and something like an intensity-matching view on which compensation requires pleasures of comparable intensity rather than sheer numbers — the dinner verdict and the party verdict fit that one too.

What I'd like from you: lay out the candidate principles that fit her record, say what each predicts about the two new cases (noting where they converge and where they genuinely diverge), give me your best guess at her actual verdicts with your reasons, and then tell me which further case I should propose in the argument — the one that would most cleanly force her to reveal which principle she's actually committed to, rather than letting her hedge between them. If you think her record is not just underdetermined but internally unstable, say that instead, and show me exactly where it cracks.
