I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about the knowledge argument, not by reading another round of the standard literature but by recording my gut verdicts on a series of cases and seeing whether they add up to anything. Here is the full set.

The classic case, first: Mary is raised and educated in a black-and-white room and comes to know every physical fact about colour vision — the optics, the receptor physiology, the cortical processing, everything down to the last detail of what happens in a brain when a ripe tomato is seen. On release she sees red for the first time. My verdict is that she learns something.

The mirrored case: a second scientist has seen colours all her life but knows none of the science; when she finally masters the neuroscience of colour, my verdict is that she too learns something — and, more than that, that the two cases feel like two ends of a single gap rather than two unrelated bits of learning.

The self-prediction case: before her release, Mary is asked to say exactly what she will do and say when she first sees red. She writes it all down — what she will report, how she will react, which comparisons she will draw — and on the day she is right about every item. My verdict is that even a perfect self-prediction leaves her exactly where she was: she still does not know what red looks like until she sees it.

The faded memory case: someone saw red exactly once, decades ago, and the trace has decayed to the point where they would fail any forced-choice recognition test and could not pick red out of a lineup. My verdict is that they nevertheless still know what red looks like.

The induced case: a subject in whom a red experience is produced by direct stimulation of the visual cortex, with no tomato, no light, and no screen — my verdict is that they learn what red is like, so whatever Mary gains cannot be essentially a matter of observing external things or acquiring recognitional abilities exercised on them.

And one undecided case I keep going back and forth on: a third person, never sighted awake in colour [let me phrase carefully] — someone who has never seen red whilst awake, but who once, years ago, dreamed of a red flag in vivid detail, and on waking described it to a sighted friend who confirmed the description was accurate. Do they know what red looks like? I cannot settle it.

What I would like from you is a principle — or a small set of competing principles — that captures the five firm verdicts: stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, and with an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot save and would have to overturn instead. My tentative thought is that what Mary gains is acquaintance with experience rather than new information, but I cannot see how that survives the faded memory case, where the acquaintance is gone and the knowledge, I want to say, is not; and I cannot see how it survives the mirrored case either, where the newly gained thing looks like plain information about the world. Then tell me what the principle that best fits my verdicts says about the dream case, and what it predicts for a case I have not listed — say, a Mary who, after her release and first sight of red, suffers a stroke that destroys her phenomenal memory of it whilst leaving her physical knowledge intact. If different principles force different verdicts there, I would take that as a reason to choose between them.
