First, here is the essay question the student was given:

"Daniel Dennett has argued that the free will worth wanting is not the one incompatibilists insist we need. Explain what, on Dennett's account, the free will worth wanting consists in, and how he responds to the objection that if determinism is true, an agent could never have done otherwise. Then discuss whether Frankfurt-style cases support Dennett's position, and explain what, if anything, they leave unsettled."

And here is the student's essay, reproduced in full:

"Dennett is a compatibilist, which means he thinks free will and determinism are compatible: even if all of our actions are fully caused by prior events, we can still be free and morally responsible. He says the kind of free will that matters is not the libertarian kind that requires escaping causation, but a kind worth wanting. The idea that we should look for the free will worth wanting comes from Harry Frankfurt, who introduced it in his 1971 paper on second-order desires.

For Dennett, free will worth wanting is a matter of having the right kind of mind: the ability to anticipate the future, to respond to reasons, and to control oneself in the light of those reasons. A free agent is one whose first-order desires are endorsed by her second-order volitions — she wants her will to be the one it is. This is what distinguishes us from animals and from unfortunate humans like addicts who are not free. Dennett also argues that this kind of freedom is an achievement of evolution: organisms that could foresee consequences and inhibit impulses had a survival advantage.

Dennett answers the 'could have done otherwise' objection by saying it only matters in a conditional sense: what we care about is that the agent would have done otherwise if she had chosen to, or if circumstances had been different. If a madman locks me in a room and I decide not to leave, I didn't leave, but I could have if the door were open. So the absolute version — 'she could have done otherwise even given exactly the same past and laws' — is not required for responsibility.

Frankfurt's famous cases support all of this. In them, a neuroscientist is ready to intervene and force you to do X if you were about to do otherwise, but you do X on your own, so he never intervenes. Since you could not have done otherwise even in the conditional sense, yet most people feel you are responsible, these cases show that determinism does not threaten moral responsibility. Therefore Dennett's compatibilism is vindicated: we have the free will worth wanting, and the incompatibilist is simply insisting on a kind of freedom nobody ever wanted."
