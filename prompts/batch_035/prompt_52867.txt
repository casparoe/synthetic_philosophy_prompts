I'm a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas, and I've been assigned yet another piece pegged to the eternal "why do civilizations collapse" content, so naturally I've fallen down the Rome rabbit hole. Everyone from Gibbon onward has a favorite culprit -- barbarians, Christianity, lead pipes, some late-antique cooling event -- and then there's a whole other camp saying nothing much fell in 476 at all, the west just rebranded as "late antiquity" and people kept living their lives. What I can't figure out from the popular books is which of these claims are actually fighting words among specialists and which are basically settled. Like: is it settled that the western imperial apparatus genuinely collapsed and that measurable material life (pottery distributions, roof tiles, cattle bones, whatever archaeologists actually count) got worse in the western provinces, with the real fight only over causes? Or is even the material-decline part contested now? And how much of the "transformation not fall" framing is a defensible reading of evidence versus a polite fiction -- I've heard it argued that line partly reflects the moment it was written, the 60s and 70s, when people wanted to tell the story from the barbarians' side too, and I'd like to know if that's fair or cheap. Where does the Justinianic plague fit -- consensus ingredient in the story, or still fringe? Please separate what specialists actually agree on from what's genuinely up for grabs, don't present a contested take as the consensus, and be blunt about where your less sure. Keep it usable for a general reader, but I don't want to hand my editor something a classicist would laugh at.
