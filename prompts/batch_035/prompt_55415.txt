I run a philosophy of mind reading group that has spent two semesters on the eliminative materialism debate, and I've noticed that every session ends in exactly the same place. The eliminativist side holds that beliefs and desires are the theoretical posits of folk psychology — a folk theory, inherited and revised only casually over millennia, whose explanatorily stagnant record makes it fair game for elimination the way phlogiston or caloric were eliminated rather than smoothly reduced. The other side replies that intentional explanation isn't a theory in that sense at all: it's a constitutive framework, part of the background against which anything counts as a mistake, a reason, or an agent in the first place, and so it cannot be empirically refuted without undermining the very notion of evidence that a refuting science would rest on. The group can recite all the moves — the argument from the theory-ladenness of cognition, the analogies with obsolete science, the interpretationist counterattack — and then the discussion just stops, with both sides apparently convinced the other has begged the question rather than answered it.

My working hypothesis, which I want stress-tested rather than accepted, is that the two camps are not really disagreeing about whether beliefs exist but about what kind of existence claim a cognitive-state term makes. The eliminativist treats 'belief' as a natural-kind term whose referent is fixed by whatever empirical theory of the brain matures, so it can turn out empty just as a failed chemical kind can. The opponent treats it as closer to a functional or interpretive term, whose applicability is secured by the practice of giving and asking for reasons, so the question of empirical refutation cannot even be raised coherently. If that's right the dispute is partly verbal — but not merely verbal, because the natural-kind reading carries with it a substantive picture of what a mature cognitive science should look like, and the interpretive reading carries a rival picture of what the science of minded creatures is for. So my question is really about the structure of the disagreement: is it verbal, substantive-but-stalled, or two different questions travelling under one name — one about the ontology of a future science, one about the status of everyday explanation? I'd like a diagnosis grounded in what each side actually commits to, not a survey of positions I already know.

Two constraints on the answer. First, tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a distinction that would need to be drawn, a specific empirical development that would bear on one reading but not the other, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the two readings have insulated themselves from any common standard of adjudication. Second, take seriously a case my group keeps circling: suppose we eventually had a detailed neuroscience of decision-making, or an engineered system, that predicted and explained behavior at least as well as belief-desire attribution does — would that settle anything, or would each side simply absorb it, the eliminativist announcing that the new taxonomy vindicates elimination while the opponent insists it merely implements the intentional states that were never in doubt? I would rather have a clear-eyed diagnosis that ends pessimistically than a synthetic reconciliation that papers over why this debate has run for decades without anyone visibly changing their mind.
