A philosopher friend of mine — well read in Parfit, though she insists she goes by intuition rather than theory — has been working through cases about future people, and I want to anticipate her next verdict. Her rulings so far: (1) a woman who conceives while taking a medication she knows will give her child a serious but non-fatal condition, when she could easily have waited a month and had a healthy child, acts wrongly, even though the child's life turns out happy and worth living; (2) a national depletion policy that will leave the people of two centuries hence substantially worse off than they would otherwise have been — though their lives remain decent, and though the policy's economic side effects mean they will be different people altogether — is wrong; (3) a couple who conceive now, when waiting a year would have produced a somewhat better-off child, do nothing wrong; (4) a couple who could have a child predicted to be very happy but choose to remain childless do nothing wrong; (5) given the resources either to save the life of one existing stranger or to bring two new, very happy people into existence, one must save the stranger. Tomorrow's case: an agency is weighing a measure that would, as a foreseeable side effect, shift the timing of conceptions across an entire country, so that an entirely different hundred million people exist a generation later, each living a life marginally worse — no disability, no serious deficit, just a shade less well off — than the person who would otherwise have existed in their place. Predict her verdict on this case, in British English. Identify the rules that fit her track record equally well, say what each of them predicts here, and give your best guess at the one she is actually running, with your reasons. Then design the further case that would best tell those candidate rules apart, and explain what it would reveal.
