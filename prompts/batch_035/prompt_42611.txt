I coordinate a volunteer peer-support text line — not therapists, just trained volunteers who talk to people having a rough night. Last quarter we got a grant from a foundation that tracks two numbers for us: median time to first reply, and the share of conversations marked "resolved" within a single session. Ever since those became our reported metrics, the volunteers have gotten much faster at responding and way quicker to send people off to other resources — a hotline number, a clinic address, a link to a breathing exercise. On paper everything went up. The funders are thrilled and want to double our budget.

Here's my problem. Part of me thinks the volunteers are gaming it: "resolved" doesn't mean resolved, people are closing out conversations to keep the stat looking good, and the actual thing we do — sitting with someone through a hard couple of hours — is quietly disappearing. But another part of me wonders whether I'm just being romantic about the old way of doing things. Some of those quick referrals are genuinly good. A guy who needed a food bank got a phone number in two minutes instead of an hour of chatting with a volunteer who couldn't actually help him. Maybe the long, messy conversations weren't as valuable as I always assumed, and the numbers are just showing me that.

So is there a principled way to tell whether the metric is being gamed or whether it corrected something I was wrong about? And is it even fair to call it gaming, when nobody is lying and every number is real? I have to write our renewal narrative next month and I can't tell if I'm describing a corruption of the practice or an improvement to it.
