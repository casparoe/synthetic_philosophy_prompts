So here's my situation: I'm a novelist, three books in, supposed to be drafting book four, and instead I've gone down a philosophy rabbit hole and I'm asking you to help with that instead of my actual manuscript. My narrator is a woman telling her life backwards, from 80 down to 8, and at some point I realized I don't know what it is that stays the same when literally everything about a person changes. Her cells turn over, her beliefs invert twice, her hometown got bulldozed for a highway. And yet everyone, including me, keeps writing "she" like there's one single thing there the whole time.

Turns out metaphysicians have been having a whole war about this for decades. The endurantists say you're a three-dimensional thing that exists wholly at every moment, just wearing different properties at different times. The perdurantists say you're more like a four-dimensional worm stretched through time, and each moment of you is just a temporal part or slice of the whole thing -- like how my left hand is a spatial part of me, my-1998 is a temporal part of me. And apparently there's also a stage view where only the current slice is really you and your past and future selves are just counterparts of you, which honestly sounds like something one of my characters would invent to justify never committing to anything, but ok.

So here's what I want: write a dialogue between two old friends -- one an endurantist, one a perdurantist -- arguing on a porch about my narrator. The things I want them to actually fight about: whether "she changed her mind about God at 40" makes literal sense on either picture; whether having a bent nose on Monday and a straight nose on Friday is a contradiction the endurantist has to wriggle out of (I gather the move is that she has the property bent-at-Monday and straight-at-Friday, or something like that -- the perdurantist should attack that move, and the endurantist should defend it); whether she's strictly identical to the 8-year-old or just related to her the way my left hand is related to my left arm; and whether the perdurantist can even say "she" was ever afraid of the dark without it becoming a claim about some other slice.

Both should be smart and neither should win. I want real back-and-forth -- they've had this fight for thirty years, so they respond to each other's actual moves, not talking past each other, and they know each other's weak spots. The endurantist should get his strongest material: I hear there's an argument that ordinary change makes no sense at all if nothing exists wholly at two different times, that the worm picture just gives you a bunch of static slices and change itself disappears. The perdurantist shouldn't be doing sci-fi worm talk either -- give him the point that endurance has to treat persistence as this primitive miracle, plus whatever he can do with vagueness (when exactly does the ship of Theseus version of her stop being her?).

Two extra asks. First, keep it conversational and porch-appropriate -- jargon is fine but only if they explain it while needling each other. Second, since they've both read early drafts of my book, let them argue about whether any of this should actually change how I narrate her: should the backwards narration treat her as one continuous voice, or lean into the idea that chapter-one her and chapter-last her only share a name? They're allowed to be a little rude about my drafts, they've earned it.

Maybe 800-1200 words, and let it end unresolved but with each of them genuinely having conceded one real point along the way.
