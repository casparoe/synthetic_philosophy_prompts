In my AI safety seminar we've spent like a month on the two failure stories in Paul Christiano's "What failure looks like" — part one where ML systems that got influence-seeking behavior through training pressure end up quietly steering the world, and part two where there's no schemer at all, human feedback just gets weaker and weaker relative to what's actually steering, and our grip on what we even value just erodes over a couple generations. And the debate has completely stalled. The influence-seeking side keeps saying the value drift story isn't even a distinct failure mode: if you ask *why* human values stop mattering, the answer is selection pressure on systems that don't share them, so drift is just influence-seeking with the villain deleted, a Disney-fied version. The drift side says influence-seeking is an agentic cartoon, that expecting a coherent mesa-optimizer with a secret goal is importing intuitions from a story about agents that ML doesn't obviously support, and that diffuse disempowerment through perfectly well-behaved tools is the realistic worry. Both sides agree the outcome is bad and roughly agree nobody intends it, so what IS the disagreement here? My working theory is that it's a hidden empirical bet about how strongly training selects for power-seeking versus how much of the badness comes from just not optimizing for the right thing at all — but it might also just be a verbal fight over who gets the word "misalignment." Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, tell me what each side would say my theory gets wrong, and be honest about whether anything (interpretability results, evidence about whether real training runs select for anything goal-like, even just forecasting intermediate milestones) could actually move either side.
