I lead a small interpretability team at a mid-size AI lab (six people, two of whom are engineers with no philosophy or epistemology background, if that matters). Roughly once a week somebody comes to me with a candidate finding: an activation direction that separates "truthful" outputs from "confabulated" ones, an SAE feature that fires on our honesty eval set, a circuit that seems to implement "the model checking its work" — and I have to decide whether we sign off and put it in a paper or a safety claim.

The recurring worry, which we talk about constantly but have never actually operationalized: the thing we found might track *our labels* or the quirks of whatever dataset we tested on, rather than the concept we claim it implements. A "deception" direction that's really a "hedging" direction. A "the model knows X" feature that's really "X appeared in the prompt," or worse, some mix of both. Everyone on the team has seperate instincts about when a finding is solid, so the outcome depends on who happens to review it, which is obviously bad.

Please turn this into an actual decision procedure I can run on a candidate finding: the tests to run in order, what a passing result has to look like, where the procedure says "no, don't make this claim" outright, and where it stops and hands the call back to a person because it's a judgment call about what the human-level concept even is — e.g. whether hedging counts as part of deception is a question about how we carve up concepts, not something an experiment can settle. Constraints to respect: the full procedure has to be runnable in about a week of one engineer's time on a modest compute budget, several steps will necessarily be qualitative because some experiments are just too expensive, and it has to be legible enough that a skeptical external reviewer could follow the reasoning. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on two real-ish cases from our backlog:

1. A probe that classifies model outputs as "honest" vs "deceptive" with about 97% accuracy. Thing is, the eval set was annotated mostly by one contractor, who we later noticed tends to label hedged or heavily qualified outputs as deceptive.
2. A feature that fires strongly when the model gets hard benchmark questions right and weakly when it gets them wrong — the discoverer wants to claim it's a "the model knows whether it knows" feature, and this is supposed to be our shot at a headline result.

For at least one of these I expect the procedure to give an answer we won't like; show that honestly rather than fudging. And say what the procedure can't do — the failure modes it won't catch no matter how carefully we follow it, like the possibility that the concept itself doesn't cut the model's internal structure at its joints at all.

My own current rule of thumb is "does it survive paraphrase, translation, and re-collecting the dataset" — if you have a view on whether that belongs in the procedure as an explicit step or is already implicit in it, I'd take that too.

Keep the whole thing under 1000 words; I'd rather have tight than exhaustive.
