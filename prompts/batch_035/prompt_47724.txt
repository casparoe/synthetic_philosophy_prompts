Been trying to sort out what I actually believe about coherence arguments for EU maximization by just listing my gut reactions to cases, and the list is kind of a mess. Getting Dutch-booked — bets that each look good on their own but together guarantee I lose money: not fine, full stop. The classic money pump, paying a dollar to switch A→B, another dollar B→C, then another C→A till I'm back where I started minus three dollars: also not fine. Allais preferences though — taking the certain million over the gamble, and then taking the 10% shot at 5 million over the 11% shot at 1 million, which together break independence: fine honestly, I'd bite that bullet, people who dogmatically obey independence feel like they're optimizing for the axiom instead of the money. Refusing each bet in a series of individually +EV flips but happily taking all of them as a package: fine, even smart, thats just looking at aggregate risk instead of isolating each flip — even though I'm pretty sure it's also technically an independence violation. Being moved by framing, choosing differently when the same surgery is described as 90% survival vs 10% mortality: not fine.

So is there actually one principle behind all this? The obvious candidate is something like "a preference pattern is only irrational if it's exploitable, axiom violations by themselves don't matter" — that saves my Allais and my bet-bundling verdicts while keeping the Dutch book and money pump ones. But then framing sensitivity seems exploitable too (someone re-describes the same offer twice and charges me a penny each time I flip), so does it survive? And the case I keep going back and forth on: I would honestly pay a small fee to NOT be offered a bunch of even-money bets at all, since I know I'll take some and it'll eat my attention — but refusing to even hear an offer looks like it violates the "more options can't hurt" thing that falls out of EU. Give me the best principle or small set of candidates, say exactly which of my verdicts each one can't accommodate, and tell me what the winner says about the pay-to-not-be-offered case. Feel like everyone quotes the money pump theorem at me but nobody ever says which side of these lines they're actually standing on.
