I've drafted the following short essay, provisionally titled "The Unexamined Century," for a general philosophical audience, and I'd be grateful for your comments.

---

Since Zermelo and Fraenkel, no contradiction has been found in ZFC. This is often cited as our main reason for confidence in the consistency of classical mathematics. I want to argue that this fact is much weaker evidence than it is usually taken to be.

First, human proof search is not a random sample of proof space. Mathematicians and proof assistants explore derivations that are short, structured, and human-legible. But the length of the shortest contradiction in an inconsistent system, if one exists, is not guaranteed to be small. We know from history that foundational systems have turned out inconsistent precisely when their contradictions were short and near the surface: Frege's Basic Law V yielded to Russell's paradox almost immediately, and Quine's ML was shown inconsistent soon after publication. Had either system hidden its contradiction a few million symbols deep, the systems might have been used productively for generations. The absence of a discovered contradiction in ZFC is close to what we should expect whether or not ZFC is consistent, so long as any inconsistency lies beyond the reach of our biased search.

Second, the productivity of mathematics is not evidence of consistency. Naïve set theory did excellent work for decades before Russell. Moreover, ex contradictione quodlibet notwithstanding, mathematical practice does not actually explode when a background theory is inconsistent. No working mathematician derives arbitrary theorems from an available contradiction; proofs are relevance-constrained in practice. If a contradiction in ZFC were found tomorrow, we would quarantine the offending axiom or fragment and continue, much as analysis survived the foundational crisis of the calculus. So the fact that mathematics "keeps working" is nearly independent of whether ZFC is consistent.

Third, the positive evidence for consistency is thinner than it looks. Gödel's second incompleteness theorem blocks a direct proof within ZFC, so confidence must rest on the informal inductive argument just questioned. The genuine partial results — consistency proofs for weak fragments, relative consistency results for large cardinal axioms over ZFC — push the question back rather than answering it.

I conclude that we should hold a modest but nonzero credence that ZFC is inconsistent — high enough to matter for the philosophy of mathematical practice, though not high enough to change how anyone proves theorems on Tuesday.

Please comment on the essay with particular attention to the following: (1) its accuracy, including any overstatements about the historical episodes it invokes; (2) whether the claim that mathematical practice is "relevance-constrained in practice" is a fair response to the explosion problem, or whether it conflates what mathematicians do with what the logic of the situation licenses; (3) whether the argument overlooks structural sources of confidence in ZFC, such as the independent motivation of its axioms or the network of relative consistency results; and (4) whether the conclusion actually follows from the premises, or whether the essay slides from "we would not have found an inconsistency" to "we should be uncertain about consistency." Please also flag any passages that would confuse a reader unfamiliar with proof theory, and suggest where the argument is most in need of strengthening.
