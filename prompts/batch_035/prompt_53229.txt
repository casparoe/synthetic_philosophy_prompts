I'm a twelfth-grade student who runs the philosophy society at my school, and for our spring term I want to build a sorting instrument for the grandfather paradox debate. The society members all throw around labels — timeline branches, fixed history, time travel is impossible — but I suspect most of us haven't actually worked out which view we hold, and I want a tool that settles it by what people say about cases rather than by which label they like the sound of. So I am asking for a decision tree, and I would like it built in a particular order.

First construct the logical space, then place the positions in it. The branch nodes should each be a question about a concrete case that a member could answer without knowing which position it leads to, and no node may simply ask someone to pick a theory by name. Please include, in some form, the following cases among your nodes, since I have noticed these are the ones on which my members genuinely come apart. One: a traveler goes back, aims a carefully tested gun at his young grandfather, pulls the trigger, and the gun jams; then the gun jams again, and again, always for a different mundane reason. Two: a traveler hands a 1920s engineer the complete blueprint for a time machine, and it later turns out the engineer's famous machine was built from that blueprint, the blueprint having no origin other than the loop itself. Three: after doing something in the past, the traveler tries to return to the moment she left, and something in the past has visibly changed. Four: the Hollywood case, where the grandfather dies, history is overwritten, and the traveler somehow still exists with memories of both histories.

The leaves should be actual positions, each with its name if it has a standard one and the word unnamed if not. I want, at minimum, these to appear: the view that the grandfather paradox is a proof that travel to the past cannot happen in principle; the single self-consistent history view, on which the past can be visited but not changed, and something always stops the killing; the branching view, on which the traveler who kills his grandfather has not changed his own history but arrived at, or created, another one; the view associated with the philosopher David Lewis, on which the traveler can kill grandfather in the ordinary sense of can but simply never does and never will; and the fatalist view that the whole business shows our ordinary concept of choosing between open futures does not survive time travel even in one history. The Hollywood overwriting model should also appear as a leaf, but I want you to say explicitly whether it is an incoherent position, an unattractive one that requires giving up too much else, or merely underexplored, and to defend whichever diagnosis you give.

Two further requirements. First, where your tree cannot really separate two positions, say so plainly rather than inventing a fake distinction; my own guess is that the self-consistency view and the Lewis view are hard to pull apart with case questions, but work that out rather than taking my word for it. Second, if some combination of node answers has no occupant, note it and briefly diagnose why nobody stands there, distinguishing incoherent from unattractive from merely unexplored, just as with the leaves.

As to format: because I will read the finished tree aloud at a meeting rather than projecting it, please deliver the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter or a long email. No headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no diagrams; render the node questions and the branches as flowing sentences, using phrases like at the first fork and if you answered no to that to indicate structure. Please also do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; the tree should stand on its own, and I have no way to check references while running a meeting. Aim for roughly eight to ten node questions and six or so leaves, and keep the whole thing to a length I could read aloud in about fifteen minutes.
