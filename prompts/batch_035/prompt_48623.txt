I teach a graduate seminar on vagueness, and every year the same illusion survives the course: students arrive convinced that the dispute between epistemicism and supervaluationism is a dispute about whether there is a sharp boundary — the epistemicist saying yes, the supervaluationist saying no. By the end of term I want them to see that the boundary sentence is precisely where the two views do not divide, and that the real division surfaces elsewhere, in sentences nobody entered the dispute caring about. To that end I want a thought experiment built to the following specifications.

The setting must be realistic and institutional — no heaps of sand, no bald men, no sorites row presented as such. My preferred vehicle is an employment tribunal reviewing a dismissal for "persistent" lateness, where the tribunal is walked day by day through a two-hundred-day attendance record and asked, for each day n, whether by that day the employee's lateness had become persistent. If you have a better realistic vehicle — an insurer disputing "reasonable" wear and tear, a regulator policing a "low in sodium" label — use it, but keep it ordinary and preserve the forced-march structure. The scenario itself should run one paragraph.

The record must contain three zones: days on which everyone agrees the lateness plainly had not yet become persistent; days on which everyone agrees it plainly had; and, in between, days about which the tribunal could not justify a finding either way however carefully it reasoned. You may not stipulate any theory's verdict: every verdict you attribute to a view must be derived from its semantics, not written into the plot.

The specifications on the theories are these. First, the two views must agree on the truth of the sentence everyone takes to divide them — the boundary sentence, "there is a last day before which the lateness had not yet become persistent," or equivalently "there is a sharp cutoff." You must show why the supervaluationist is committed to it: the sentence comes out true on every admissible precisification of "persistent," and is therefore supertrue — a result my students find intolerable, so derive it carefully rather than asserting it. You must also show why the epistemicist holds it, which is the easier half.

Second, the views must diverge on at least one sentence about the same record that no one enters the dispute caring about. The obvious candidate is the atomic report of a single borderline day — "by day 97 the lateness had become persistent" — where the supervaluationist finds a truth-value gap and the epistemicist a hidden fact. But I would rather you locate a divergence one step further in: something in the neighborhood of "it is definitely the case that by day 97 the lateness had become persistent," or "the tribunal's finding at day 97 was either correct or incorrect," where the two semantics yield different verdicts for reasons that are not merely the gap re-described. If you conclude that no such further divergence is available, say so and defend the conclusion; do not manufacture one for my benefit.

After the scenario and the derivations, make three closing moves. Diagnose which structural feature of the case is doing the work — I suspect it is the interaction of quantification over precisifications with the forced-march series, but I want your diagnosis, not a confirmation of mine. Then tell me what happens to the agreement on the boundary sentence if the institutional context precisifies the predicate outright — say the employer's handbook had defined "persistent" as more than twelve late arrivals in any rolling ninety days — and whether the thought experiment collapses, and into what. Finally, give the supervaluationist's best reply to the charge that her view has quietly conceded the very sharp boundary it was invented to deny, and say whether you find the reply adequate.

Throughout, do not editorialize for either view. The case is for teaching, and a case that smuggles in its verdicts fails: I want the theories to generate the pattern, not the pattern to caricature the theories.
