My sister and I have an ongoing thing about whether I should want kids. I keep saying I just don't want them, and she keeps ending the argument with "you'd want them if you really thought it through." When I pressed her on how she could possibly know that, she said something like "everyone who really examines it deeply comes around," and then, when that didn't land, that she's not really talking about me as I am now — more like "me at my best." That last move got me thinking, because I've bumped into the same shape of claim in two other places recently: a philosphy podcast about ideal observer theories, and an article about AI alignment that said we should build the AI to do what humanity would want if we knew more and thought faster. Apparently there's a whole tradition of grounding what people *should* want in what some idealized, better-informed, calmer version of them would choose.

The more I chew on the sentence, though, the more it seems like "you'd agree if you really thought it through" is several very different claims wearing one overcoat. It could be a plain empirical prediction — that I, actually continuing to think, will in fact come around — which as far as I can tell is just sometimes false, since plenty of people examine the question hard and stay where they are. It could be a definitional move — that "rational agent" and "wants kids" go together so tightly that anyone who doesn't want them just hasn't *really* thought it through by stipulation — which feels circular and would make the sentence unfalsifiable in a way that should worry my sister. Or it could be a counterfactual about a version of me that knows everything relevant and is perfectly reflective — and then whether that person's preferences have any authority over me *now* seems to turn on stuff like: does the idealization hold my current values fixed and only add information, or does it let the values themselves drift? How far can you idealize me before it isn't me anymore and the result is just "some better person disagrees with you," which seems like it shouldn't count for much?

Can you lay out a menu of the distinct readings the one-liner could be taking — from the trivial or definitional ones, to the substantive and risky ones, to the ones that are probably just false — and give a verdict on each: whether it's plausible, and crucially whether it would actually support what my sister wants it to do, namely treating my current preference as answerable to the idealized one? A list format would help. And separately, tell me which reading you think people who use this line usually have in mind, and whether that's the same reading that makes their argument work — I have a suspicion those come apart.
