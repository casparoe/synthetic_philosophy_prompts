My friends and I have been debating whether "generations" like Millennials and Gen Z name anything real, and I've noticed that neither side argues directly — both reach for a comparison to something physical, and the comparison seems to be doing all the work. I'd like help auditing the two pictures.

The friend who thinks generations are invented uses a wave. A generation, he says, is like a wave passing across a lake: the water — the actual people — stays more or less in place, being born, living, and dying, while "Millennial" is just the label for whichever stretch of the population happens to be at a certain life stage right now, the way a wave is not an extra thing laid over the water but merely the water's temporary arrangement. On his view, asking "what do Millennials want?" is as confused as praising or blaming a wave for the condition of the lake.

My aunt, who takes generations very seriously, uses wine. A generation is like a vintage, she says: grapes that pass through the same weather in the same year acquire a shared character that stays with the bottles for life, and nobody thinks "a 2005 Burgundy" is a fictional category. On her view, people whose values form during the same economy, the same technology, and the same events pick up a durable common imprint, so "Millennial" names something about as real as a vintage.

Please examine both comparisons. First, set out for each what is meant to correspond to what — the water, the wave, the lake; the grapes, the weather, the year, the bottles, the cellar. Second, say where each mapping breaks: which features of waves does my friend's picture quietly import that human populations lack, and which features of wine does my aunt's picture import that cohorts lack (for example, vintages get exact, externally fixed boundaries, whereas everyone argues about where one generation ends and the next begins and redraws those lines after the fact). Third, I suspect the two pictures are secretly about two different questions — whether a generation is a persisting object at all, versus whether the label tracks stable shared traits — and that each comparison may be winning one of those while being used to settle the other; please say whether that's right. Finally, once the failed mappings are on the table, does either side's conclusion survive on its own, or was each conclusion being carried entirely by its comparison? And if both pictures are misleading, what comparison would fit better?
