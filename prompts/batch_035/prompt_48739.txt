My friend and I have been arguing for months about the ethics of having children, and I'm trying to write up our disagreements for a philosophy elective. Her position used to seem simple, but the more cases we go through, the less sure I am what rule she's actually running. Here is her track record so far:

1. A couple learns that any child they conceive will face unremitting pain with no compensating joys. She says conceiving would be clearly wrong.
2. A healthy couple could easily have a child certain to enjoy a wonderful life, but they would rather spend their twenties traveling. She is firm that declining to conceive is not wrong at all — not even slightly — and that the would-be child is not wronged or deprived.
3. She insists that a world containing that happy child is not morally better than a world in which it is never conceived, at least not in any way that gives the parents a moral reason to create it.
4. Yet she also says it is genuinely good that a would-be child doomed to nothing but suffering was never conceived: the avoided pain counts in favor of the empty cradle in a way that avoided pleasure does not count against it.
5. A couple conceives a child whose life will be well worth living but will include real suffering, say recurrent episodes of depression that are successfully managed. She says that is perfectly permissible.
6. When I pressed her on whether the happy child from case 2 would be benefited by being created, she hesitated and said she couldn't identify anyone who ends up better off.

I told her this sounds like Benatar's asymmetry — the claim that the absence of pain is good even when there is nobody to enjoy it, while the absence of pleasure is bad only when somebody is deprived of it — and she said she would have to think about whether that is really her view.

Here are next week's cases. (a) A woman is on a medication that guarantees any child she conceives this year will suffer intermittent joint pain for life alongside an otherwise very good life; waiting a year to switch medications would produce a different, pain-free child, but she conceives now. (b) A couple undergoing IVF has two viable embryos, one predicted to flourish and one predicted to have a decent but mediocre life; they choose between them at random and end up with the mediocre one. (c) A couple deliberately conceives a child they know will have a life only barely worth living, in a remote village, with no prospect of anything better.

Predict her verdict on each case. More importantly, lay out the candidate rules that fit her track record equally well — Benatar's asymmetry itself, some kind of person-affecting view on which no one is wronged by creation or non-creation, a threshold view on which it is wrong only to create lives below neutrality, or something else entirely — and show exactly where those rules agree and disagree on my three cases. Then give your best guess about which rule she is implicitly running and why you prefer it over its rivals, and propose the single further case that would most cleanly separate the remaining candidates.
