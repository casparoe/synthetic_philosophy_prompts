Take the role of a philosopher of physics who works on interpretations of quantum mechanics and thinks carefully about the structure of disagreements, not just their content.

The background, so you know where each of us stands: it seems obvious to me that Everettians owe us an account of why probability in a branching universe should be governed by squared amplitudes rather than by branch count or amplitude-weighted anything else, and that the main attempts — the decision-theoretic derivations in the Deutsch–Wallace style, and the self-locating uncertainty approach in which an observer about to branch doesn't know which future copy they will be — each just smuggle the answer in, either through the rationality axioms or through assumptions about how to distribute credence over branches. My friend's position is that the demand for a derivation is itself confused: once branching is a physical fact, "probability" just means "what a rational agent should expect," and the Everettian accounts show exactly why such an agent should weight outcomes by squared amplitudes; complaining that the axioms aren't derived is like complaining that Bayesianism doesn't derive its own axioms. We went around this loop for three hours without either of us conceding anything, and I've since realized I can't tell whether we disagree about physics, about what a "derivation" requires, or about whether the classical analogy I keep reaching for is even legitimate.
