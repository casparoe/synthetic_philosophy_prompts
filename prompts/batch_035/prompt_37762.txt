I'm a grad student in econ, and I keep bumping into the old fight about unrealistic assumptions in economics — the one Friedman kicked off in '53 with the claim that a theory should be judged by its predictions, not by the realism of its assumptions, and that wildly false assumptions can even make a theory *more* useful (what people later nicknamed the "F-twist"). The critics have had their say too: the reasonable ones concede that of course every model abstracts, and that the real question is which falsities are harmless — some are like frictionless planes, harmless neglect of small stuff; some are domain restrictions, quietly telling you where the theory stops applying; some are just fudges that let you derive whatever you want. And yet seventy years on, nobody has moved an inch: half my department shrugs "the model predicts, who cares about the mechanism," and the other half insists that a model with a false mechanism predicts well only by accident, and that luck isn't science.

I want you to act as a professional philosopher of science, and please brainstorm a bit before committing to a diagnosis. Is this a substantive disagreement about what explanation requires, a verbal one about what "realistic" and "assumption" even mean, or are the two sides just answering different questions — prediction versus explanation — under one heading? My own pet theory is that it's really a fight over whether economists are in the business of forecasting or of giving mechanisms, and that once you split those two jobs the whole thing dissolves; but I'm honestly not sure economists themselves could agree on which job is theirs, which makes me wonder if my diagnosis just relocates the fight instead of ending it. Diagnose the stall, tell me whether my theory is right, and be straight with me about whether anything — a sharper distinction, a worked case study, anything — could actually end this, or whether it's the kind of disagreement that just is bedrock. Please also tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis and which part of it you'd bet least on. And answer in American English.
