I've been trying to work out my actual view on what separates science from non-science by recording gut verdicts on cases, and I'd like to know whether any single principle captures them, or whether some of my verdicts have to go. A theory that makes a precise, novel, risky prediction, is proven wrong, and is abandoned: that's science at its best, even though the theory was false. A theory whose failed prediction is rescued by positing an as-yet-unobserved planet, which is then found: also clearly science. A theory of history that claims to predict revolutions, mispredicts each one, and after every failure reinterprets what it "really" meant to say: not science, even though it engages with evidence constantly. Astrology responding to failed horoscope tests by saying the full chart is too complex for the coarse study: also not science. But here are my problem cases. String theory has produced decades of work with no novel testable prediction, and I still want to call it science. Homeopathy makes risky, testable predictions -- specific dilutions curing specific symptoms -- which fail, so it is falsifiable and falsified, yet I don't want to call it science. Intelligent design occasionally stakes out an empirical claim, such as the irreducible complexity of the bacterial flagellum, which then gets refuted, and I refuse to call that science either. And I waver on rational-choice economics: unfalsifiable in practice because its parameters can always be re-estimated, yet I'd never say economists aren't doing science.

So: is falsifiability the principle behind my verdicts, and if so, what do I do about the fact that it seems to sort my cases wrongly in at least two places -- calling string theory non-science and homeopathy science? Is there some more complex principle, perhaps one that tracks how a theory behaves after failure rather than whether it could fail in the abstract, that gets every verdict? And is there a simpler principle that gets most of them and forces me to overturn one? Please state the principle or candidate principles precisely, show which clause each of my cases motivates, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate. I'd also like you to apply it to a case I haven't decided: a theory that made exactly one novel, risky prediction, saw it succeed spectacularly, and has produced nothing testable since -- and to say where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong, or what argument would move you to a different verdict.
