I hold epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness: qualia exist, are non-physical, and are causally idle — every physical event, including every utterance and every judgment, has a fully sufficient physical cause, and experience rides along without pushing anything. I'm aware of the standard embarrassment, the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if qualia do no causal work, my sincere report that the afterimage is green is caused not by the afterimage's greenness but by some physical state, so my apparent knowledge of my own experience needs a story. My current line is a common-cause story: the physical state that produces the experience also produces the report, and the report tracks the experience because the psychophysical laws happen to align the two. What I want is a ranked map of the view's exposure — not the textbook objections, but what would actually count against it, sorted by how decisive it would be, including things that could in principle already have happened. Specifically:

First, empirical exposure. The view predicts that physics will never turn up a causal anomaly: no measurement will ever require a non-physical input to close its books. Is there any finding, possible in principle, that would be fatal rather than merely awkward here? And conversely — since mainstream physics expects no such anomaly ever — has the empirical content of the view been drained to zero? Is that a problem for a metaphysical view of this kind, or is demanding empirical content here a category mistake?

Second, the alignment. My common-cause story requires the psychophysical laws to be, as it were, unscrambled: they could have mapped the physical states that cause pain-avoidance onto the feel of warmth, or onto nothing at all, and then my reports would not track my experiences at all, and I would never notice. So the view must treat the observed harmony — pain accompanying aversion, neural damage producing matching phenomenal deficits, my ability to notice and report structural features of experience in the first place — as a brute fact about which laws hold. What, if anything, would count against treating it as brute? In particular, would a case in which a judgment about experience is accurate in ways the concurrent physical state, on its own, underdetermines — the report carrying more grain than the common cause could carry — break the common-cause story, or can the epiphenomenalist always rescue it by positing finer-grained psychophysical laws, and is that rescue a qualification into unfalsifiability?

Third, collapse. If the laws are that fine-grained and that harmonious, some will say the causal idleness becomes a bookkeeping distinction: a view on which the laws non-contingently align everything starts to look like a dual-aspect or Russellian-monist picture wearing epiphenomenalist clothes. Under what conditions would that count as refutation — the view collapsing into a rival it was supposed to exclude — rather than as a welcome refinement? And say which intuitions my position actually stands or falls with: the conceivability of zombies, the causal closure of physics, the claim that phenomenal concepts pick out their referents directly. Which of these would committed epiphenomenalists themselves concede as genuine defeaters if push came to shove, and which would they dismiss — and where would you put me right, given the story I've told, to be most movably wrong?

Finish with the defeaters ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward, a note on which are live given what we actually know rather than merely logically possible, and an honest verdict on whether the view has been insulated by qualifications — and, if it has, whether that disqualifies it or is simply what a view of this kind costs.
