I teach a university elective on the ethics and governance of AI — about 18 students, one 90-minute seminar session, no machine-learning background required. Design an in-class game that makes them feel the force of what people in the field call the competitiveness constraint and the alignment tax: the thought that safety investment is a real cost, that in a race dynamic this cost puts the conscientious actor at a disadvantage, and that even actors who privately want to slow down get dragged along by whoever is willing to cut corners.

The game, not a lecture, has to produce the dynamic. So set it up roughly along these lines: four or five teams as rival AI labs; six rounds; each round a fixed budget of points to allocate between capability and safety; capability accumulates toward deployment, with winner-take-most payoffs depending on relative standing; safety levels set a probability of catastrophic system failure, checked by dice, with the failure consequences hitting the whole market, not just the failing team, at least some of the time. Work in a round where teams may sign a public but nonbinding safety pledge, and a later round where they can attempt a binding, monitorable agreement — so they discover for themselves why the nonbinding pledges unravel and what coordination would actually have to cost to work. And leave genuine uncertainty about whether the safety/capability tradeoff is even real: build in some chance, unknown to the players, that safety investment improves capability (a trust dividend, better reliability winning market share), because part of the real-world dispute is whether the tax exists at all. Keep the arithmetic simple enough to run without computers, and specify the materials.

For the debrief, map specific moments onto the concepts: that the tax is endogenous (it only exists relative to what others invest, so it's a property of the race, not of safety itself); the difference between a prisoner's-dilemma structure and an arms-race structure where second place is genuinely bad; whether the teams' revealed preferences matched their stated ones; and what an institution would need — detection, enforcement, participation — to change the equilibrium. Then give me an honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (teams that find the dice boring, a pledge round where nobody defects and the lesson misfires, a team that wins by pure luck and muddies everything), and a frank note on which features of the real constraint the game cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply in discussion — irreversible harms, existential stakes, and the fact that no one gets to observe the counterfactual where everyone slowed down.

Begin your response directly with the rules as the participants would receive them, with no preface, no restating of the assignment, and no commentary before the game itself; the facilitator notes and debrief come after.
