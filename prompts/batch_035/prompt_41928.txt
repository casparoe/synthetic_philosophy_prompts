I'm a philosophy PhD student working on value theory, and as a side project I've been informally interviewing my labmate — a sharp guy, no philosophy background — about which mental states count as pleasures, as a way of probing whether 'pleasure' even picks out a unified thing. His verdicts so far: the taste of good coffee, yes; the warm afterglow after a long run, yes; the rush of a rollercoaster, yes; the quiet contentment of sitting by a fire with nothing to do, yes; grief-matched sad music (the kind you deliberately put on while mourning), yes; consensual masochistic pain in the right headspace, yes. But: finally scratching a mosquito bite that's been driving him mad all day, no — he insists that's just relief from an aversive state, not a pleasure; deep flow while rock climbing, where he says he feels literally nothing in the moment and only calls it pleasant retrospectively, no; and the grim click of a grudge finally settling when the guy who wronged him gets what's coming, no — that's satisfaction, not pleasure, apparently.

Next week I'm going to run some new cases past him, and I've got a bet going with another grad student about his answers. The cases I'm considering: (1) the equanimity state serious meditators report, which they describe as the best thing they've ever felt but which supposedly lacks any felt hedonic quality in the moment; (2) the first sip of water after being parched for hours — instinctively that seems like coffee-taste pleasure, but structurally it looks awfully similar to the mosquito bite thing; (3) getting so absorbed in a crossword that you don't notice the mild enjoyment of it until you snap out three hours later and feel great. Predict what he'll say on each, lay out the rule (or rules) that best fit his track record, say which rival rules fit the same data equally well, and tell me which of the three — or a fourth case you invent — would best split the candidate rules apart, since that's where I want to put my money. Please answer in British English — my betting partner is at Oxford and she'll never let me hear the end of it if the spellings are American.
