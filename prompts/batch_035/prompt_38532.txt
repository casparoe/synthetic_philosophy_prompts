My cousin works on AI safety, and over Thanksgiving we got into it about whether lobsters can suffer — I'd read somewhere that Switzerland banned boiling them alive, and my position was that whether that law makes sense depends entirely on whether there's something it feels like to be a lobster. My cousin kept cutting me off with a line I've since heard everywhere: "Consciousness isn't an on/off switch, it's a dimmer switch." Everything — worms, crabs, dogs, us, maybe even simple robots — supposedly sits somewhere on a spectrum, and he said my whole question was confused because there's no sharp line where sentience switches on. The problem is that I can't tell what that one sentence is actually claiming, and the people who repeat it use it to do very different jobs. Sometimes it seems to just mean that animals have varying degrees of intelligence or responsiveness, which seems obviously true and useless for the lobster question. Sometimes it seems to mean something much stronger: that the felt quality of experience itself comes in quantities, so that a shrimp might have, say, a faint trickle of experience where we have a flood — not just less intelligence, but less of the raw "something it feels like" itself. And sometimes it seems to be making a point about us rather than about the animals: that our tools for detecting sentience are graded and blurry, which would be a claim about evidence, not about the dimmer.

I want to know what the distinct precise claims are that could be hiding inside "it's a spectrum," and what each one would actually do if we took it seriously. For each reading, I'd like to know whether it's plausible, whether it's a platitude or a genuinely controversial position, and — most importantly — whether it supports the move my cousin made with it, which was to wave away the question of whether the Swiss law rests on a mistake. Because it seems to me that even a true spectrum can have a zero on it, and a dimmer switch still has an "off" position: saying crabs are "somewhere on the spectrum" doesn't tell me whether they're at a positive value or at the floor. And there's a further wrinkle: maybe the different things we bundle together — perceiving, feeling pain, being self-aware, having emotions — are graded in different ways, so that "the spectrum" quietly assumes a single axis where there might be several that don't line up. Please lay out the readings as a clear list, in American English, with a verdict for each, and tell me which reading, if any, actually justifies treating "is it sentient?" as a confused question rather than a hard one.
