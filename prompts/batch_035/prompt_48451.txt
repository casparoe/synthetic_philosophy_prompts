I'm in a reading group that just spent two sessions on Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals, and I walked away with an argument I wrote down myself that seems to prove way too much, and I can't locate the seam. Here's the chain as I have it. (1) An indicative conditional like "if the die comes up even, it comes up six-or-four" is a proposition: at any possible situation it's either true or false, so it can be embedded — negated, disjoined, and crucially conditioned on. (2) The natural constraint on its probability is that the probability of "if A, B" equals the conditional probability of B given A — this is basically the ratio thesis everyone finds intuitive. (3) Since the conditional is a proposition, the law of total probability applies: P(if A, B) = P(if A, B | B)·P(B) + P(if A, B | ¬B)·P(¬B). (4) Now apply the constraint from (2) to the probabilities you get after conditioning on B — i.e., treat P(·|B) as just another probability function the thesis holds for — and you get P(if A, B | B) = P(B | A&B) = 1. (5) Same move conditioned on ¬B gives P(if A, B | ¬B) = P(B | A&¬B) = 0. (6) So P(if A, B) = P(B). (7) Combine with (2) and P(B|A) = P(B) always, for any A and B whatsoever. Which is obviously false — my credence that the die lands even given it lands six is 1, not 1/6.

The friend who walked me through this says the moral is just "indicative conditionals aren't true or false at all," full stop, and that Lewis proved it. That feels too fast to me, but I genuinely can't tell whether the problem is a hidden equivocation somewhere in (3)-(5) or whether the argument is valid and something in (1) or (2) has to give. For what it's worth, I really want to keep (2) in some form — the whole reason conditionals seem probabilistic to me is that "I'm 80% sure that if she takes the job she'll move" just sounds like P(move | takes job), and I don't want to be told that's a loose way of talking with no systematic content. But I also don't want to be a person who says ordinary conditionals are neither true nor false, because we argue with them constantly ("you said if it rained you'd come, it rained, you didn't come, so you lied").

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost? If the flaw is somewhere in my application of the thesis under conditionalization, I'd like to understand exactly why that application is illegitimate rather than merely inconvenient — what exactly changes between the unconditional and the conditionalized setting that lets me keep (2) in one and not the other. And if instead the right move is biting a bullet on (1) or (2), tell me what the standard escape routes are and price each of them: what do the people who keep truth conditions but restrict the probability constraint have to give up, and what do the people who keep the constraint but deny the conditional is a proposition say about the embedding behavior and about my rain-and-lying example? One more thing: please keep clear about what's actually settled versus what specialists still argue about — I know there's some real disagreement about how bad these results are and what they establish, and I don't want the friend's "Lewis proved conditionals aren't truth-evaluable, case closed" presented as the consensus when I suspect it isn't. Also if I've misstated the argument itself somewhere, say so, since I reconstructed it from memory and might have garbled a step.
