My mate Tom and I just spent basically the whole of Sunday afternoon arguing about internal vs external reasons for action — you know, the Williams thing — and we realised about four hours in that roughly half our disagreement wasn't substantive at all, it was us using "reason" to mean different things. So now I want to see if it's possible to write up what a proper adversarial collaboration between the two camps would produce, because I think the output would actually be embarrassing for both sides in a useful way, and I want to see whether a model can do the drafting work rather than just cheerleading for one team.

Here's the setup. Tom is a hard-line internalist: he thinks the statement "A has a reason to φ" is only ever true if φ-ing connects, through a sound deliberative route, to something in A's actual motivational set — and crucially for Tom, the motivational set is the one A has right now, warts and all, so if you genuinely couldn't care less about philosophy or your future self or whatever, there just is no reason for you to care, full stop. I'm more of a externalist (though honestly I wobble): I think there are at least some cases — the lazy man who is miserable and would be less miserable if he pulled his finger out — where we want to say the reason is there even if no sound deliberative route from his current set reaches it. But I'm not sure whether I want to say that because there's a fact of the matter about what he'd want under ideal conditions (a hypothetical internalism, which is still internalist in spirit and Tom says this makes my position collapse into his), or because reasons just aren't grounded in motivation at all.

So: write the joint statement the two of us could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want the following pieces.

1. A numbered section of claims both sides can accept. This should include the things that are actually uncontroversial once you state them carefully: that some reasons are plainly grounded in existing motivations and nobody disputes those; that the real fight starts with agents whose motivational sets are strange, inert, or self-undermining; that "he has a reason but couldn't arrive at it by deliberation" is a genuinely different claim from "he'd have different motivations if he deliberated better", and that a lot of the literature runs these together. Get the agreed claims to be non-trivial — don't pad them out with things like "reasons matter for action".

2. A disagreements table. Each residual disagreement should be phrased in wording its own side would actually sign off on — so Tom's row shouldn't say "external reasons don't exist" in a way that begs the question against me, and mine shouldn't smuggle in that idealised motivations still count as "internal" (that's a fight we had twice and I lost once and won once, which tells you the wording matters). Include in the table the verbal disputes we diagnosed: specifically whether the disagreement over the lazy man is partly just disagreement about which sentence "A has a reason" expresses, and say which part you think is verbal and why.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us. I suspect one is this: is the sound deliberative route requirement forward-looking or counterfactual about the agent's actual set? I.e., when we say the reason must connect to motivation "through deliberation", are we talking about routes the agent could actually travel from where he stands, or about what he would want if he underwent some idealisation (full information, vivid imagination, no depression, whatever)? Because if it's the latter, Tom's view starts absorbing cases he thinks it shouldn't, and if it's the former, my view has to bite the bullet on cases that sound pretty bad. Spell out the other cruxes you think are real — one candidate is whether the motivational set includes only present motivations or also dispositions and latent cares, since Tom and I never settled whether a dormant concern that would surface under reflection counts as "in the set" before it surfaces. That one might be the most underexplored bit of the whole debate, honestly.

4. For each crux, a concrete case or thought experiment both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Don't make these up out of thin air — construct them by varying the cases that are already standard in this literature (the man who doesn't care about his wife, someone indifferent to their own future, an agent whose only motivation is avoiding effort, an agent with a motivation set that's coherent but bizarre — say someone who cares intensely about the number of spoons in their kitchen drawer and nothing else). For each constructed case, say what Tom's internalism predicts he'll want to say about it, what my externalism predicts I'll want to say, and — this is the important bit — flag honestly wherever you think one of us would have to squirm, because a good joint statement names the awkward cases for each side rather than only the ones that flatter it.

Format: numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table, then cruxes each with its case and the two predictions. Keep the tone of a document two people who respect each other could actually put their names to — no strawmanning either side, and no pretending we resolved more than we did. If you think part of the residual dispute comes down to whether "reason for action" is being used to track justification or to track explanation, put that in the verbal-disputes part of the table rather than burying it.

One more thing: since the whole point of this exercise was that our argument kept stalling on words, I'll be judging the output mostly on whether the formulations are exact — whether each sentence of the disagreements section is one its own side would genuinely endorse, and whether the agreed claims are ones both would sign rather than vague enough to mean different things to each of us. A statement both of us can sign but for different reasons is a failure. Please take that seriously.
