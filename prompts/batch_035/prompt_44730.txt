I'm the teaching assistant for a second-year undergraduate course in philosophical logic, and I'm preparing a handout that sorts students into positions on vagueness by how they react to cases and arguments rather than by which labels they've already heard. I'd like you to write the handout's centerpiece: a decision tree that starts from the sorites argument and ends at a named position on vagueness. The tree must be built from the logical space first — the distinct escape routes from the paradox — and only then locate the actual positions inside that space.

The argument as we present it in class: (1) a pile of 10,000 grains arranged in heap-formation is a heap; (2) if a pile of n grains is a heap, then a pile of n−1 grains is a heap; (3) 9,999 applications of modus ponens; (4) therefore a single grain is a heap. In lecture we also introduce the contrast predicate: an amusement-park sign reading "Riders must be at least 48 inches tall," which is sharp by stipulation, and the ordinary predicate "tall," which is not. Students were genuinely split on whether the difference between the two is that "tall" has a cutoff we merely can't know, or that it has no cutoff at all.

Constraints on the tree:

- Every internal node must be a question a student can answer from the case alone, without knowing which position it leads to. Good candidates: what to say about "Either Jones is tall or Jones is not tall" when Jones is a borderline case; whether someone who is clearly tall is thereby clearly not short; whether "borderline" itself has borderline cases; whether one grain of sand could ever make the difference between a heap and a non-heap, even unknowably; whether a claim about a borderline case has a determinate truth value, a partial truth value, or no truth value at all.
- The first branching should be over which escape route from the sorites argument the student takes: denying the tolerance premise outright, denying some instance of it while keeping most, accepting the conclusion that classical logic simply fails here, or accepting the whole argument and living with its conclusion. Positions, not names, should fall out of the branches; only at the leaves should the names appear.
- The leaves must include epistemicism, supervaluationism, at least one degree-theoretic view, at least one truth-value-gap view, at least one contextualist view, and at least one leaf for views that revise the logic itself. You may add further leaves if the space requires them.
- Note which combinations of node-answers are unoccupied, and diagnose each briefly: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive for a reason you should state, or because it is merely unexplored?
- Flag honestly any pairs of positions the tree cannot separate — that is, two named views whose defenders answer every node the same way. Please do not resolve this by sneaking an extra question onto one branch whose only purpose is to distinguish them; if they cannot be separated by the cases, say so.

Two requests about your own confidence. First, for each leaf, state in a sentence how confident you are that the position you've placed there really belongs there, and what a student's answer to which further case would move it elsewhere. Second, tell me where you think the tree is most likely to misroute someone — which node is easiest to answer in a way that doesn't reflect the student's real commitments — and what would convince you to restructure it. I'd rather hear that a branch is fragile than have you present the tree as if the positions it carves were more cleanly distinguished than they are.
