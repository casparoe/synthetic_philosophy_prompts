Next month I present a paper to a workshop on Hellenistic ethics and its afterlife. The audience is mostly ancient philosophers, including two who have written extensively on Epictetus, plus a couple of moral psychologists and one cognitive scientist who studies the contemporary therapeutic Stoicism revival. My thesis is that the dichotomy of control, properly read, is not a distinction between what we control and what we do not, but between the present exercise of assent and everything that depends on further conditions unfolding over time — health, reputation, external goods, and even one's own future character. My sketch of the argument is this: (i) what is up to us, for Epictetus, is not the faculty of choice as a whole, which can be corrupted and hard to move, but the individual act of assent or withholding, which is always available in the moment; (ii) this reading explains why Stoic training, habituation, and rehearsing anticipated misfortunes matter — they shape which impressions arise and how vividly, not what is up to us; and (iii) the modern self-help version of the doctrine, which tells people to focus on what they can control, achieves its motivational force only by quietly re-enlarging the category of the controllable to include long-run outcomes, which is precisely what the strict reading forbids. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the six or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by the damage they would do, phrased in the vocabulary of the people likely to raise them — including, from the ancient philosophers, the regress worry about whether withholding assent itself requires a prior assent, and, from the cognitive scientist, the objection that assent looks far less voluntary once you take automaticity and implicit processes seriously — each with a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, what I should concede up front, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
