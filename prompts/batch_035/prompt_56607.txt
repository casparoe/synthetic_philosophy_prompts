I sit on the grants panel of a national research council that funds economic policy modelling, and we must decide between two rival proposals for a five-year programme of advice on labour-market policy. The disagreement on the panel rehearses a famous methodological dispute, and I would like the memo to face it head-on rather than around it.

Proposal A extends our existing large-scale macroeconomic model in the representative-agent tradition: a single household with rational expectations, prices and wages that clear all markets continuously, unemployment modelled as the outcome of workers' optimal search effort, and a government assumed always to be credible. Its proponents argue the model has tracked the broad direction of aggregate outcomes for two decades, and that its simplifications are precisely what make it tractable, estimable, and comparable across policy scenarios.

Proposal B would replace it with an agent-based model: many heterogeneous, boundedly rational firms and workers, rules of thumb for pricing and vacancy posting, network effects in hiring, and no equilibrium imposed from outside. Its proponents argue that the first model's assumptions are not harmless simplifications but falsehoods — and falsehoods in exactly the places where the policy question lives, namely how wages and vacancies actually adjust.

Half the panel holds that the realism of assumptions is irrelevant to a model's worth so long as its predictions hold up — the position associated with Milton Friedman's 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics, often caricatured as the claim that the more significant the theory, the more unrealistic its assumptions may be (a line of argument critics have nicknamed the F-twist). The other half holds that when a model is deployed to give direct policy advice, an assumption that is false in the very mechanism under discussion cannot be insulated by predictive success in aggregate series.

Our council's funding guidelines require only that models be 'sound' and their assumptions 'appropriate' — words the guidelines leave undefined, which is why the memo must do philosophical work rather than cite procedure.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's sitting. It should recommend a concrete and actionable outcome — fund A, fund B, fund both in some proportion, fund one with conditions, or commission something else first — with reasoning the losing side could respect. In particular, it must give the panel a workable distinction between (i) an unrealistic assumption that is a harmless idealisation of the frictionless-plane or ideal-gas sort, which buys tractability at little cost, and (ii) an unrealistic assumption that misrepresents exactly the mechanism the model is being asked to speak about. It should weigh what predictive fit is worth in a model used prospectively for advice, where the future has not yet had a chance to confirm it. And it should state honestly the strongest consideration on the losing side, rather than straw-manning it.

Please write the memo in British English (e.g. 'modelling', 'labour', 'programme', 'behaviour').
