For the past decade I have written about science and ideas for a weekly broadsheet, and the beat has left me with a conviction I have never quite dared to put in print: that the covering-law model of explanation, which Carl Hempel first advanced for history and then generalised to the sciences, is basically right about everything, including history. When a historian explains why the Weimar Republic collapsed, or why a commander took a particular decision, I take this to be, at bottom, the same operation as a physicist explaining the deflection of light: subsumption under general laws, except that in history the laws are left tacit, hedged with implicit ceteris paribus clauses, and rarely rise above the statistical. Hempel's own admission that historical explanations usually arrive only as sketches, to be filled in by later research, strikes me not as a retreat but as an accurate description of most working explanation anywhere outside the physics textbook.

Two objections I have come across seem to me the strongest, and I would like them taken seriously rather than treated as warm-up material. The first is William Dray's old criticism that historians frequently explain an action by citing the agent's reasons, and that citing a reason renders the action intelligible without invoking any law at all — no generalisation from which the event follows, just the fact that, given what he wanted and believed, this was the thing to do. I know the standard rejoinder, that reasons are causes and that there must be some tacit generalisation linking reasons to actions, and I want to know whether that rejoinder has real content or merely postpones the day of reckoning by leaving the generalisation unspecified for ever. The second is W. H. Walsh's idea of colligation: the claim that historians do irreducibly explanatory work by organising events under a single concept — the Reformation, the Cold War, the slide into war — in a way that is neither subsumption under law nor mere bookkeeping. If that is right, my view has a hole in the middle of it; if it is not, I want to know what colligation reduces to on a covering-law account, and whether the reduction would be illuminating or merely verbal.

So: tell me what would count against the covering-law view of historical explanation. Rank the defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and say in each case whether a committed Hempelian would concede the point as genuinely counting against the view or would instead explain it away — and, where the response is to explain it away, whether the move is legitimate or a sign of trouble. Be candid, finally, about whether the view, once it has absorbed Dray, colligation, and the persistent elusiveness of its laws, has been qualified into something unfalsifiable — and, if so, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or simply the ordinary fate of a philosophical programme. I am prepared to be moved: I would rather not defend a tidy line in a column that philosophers of history would rightly call complacent, but I would equally rather not abandon it on the strength of objections it can actually answer.
