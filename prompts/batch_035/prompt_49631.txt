I lead a small book group of retired teachers that has spent the last year reading popular and semi-academic history—this fall we did a stack of books on the outbreak of the First World War, and previously we did the Reformation and the fall of Rome. A pattern keeps coming up that I can't find a name for, and it's making our discussions circular. When historians are asked what caused these events, they nearly all refuse to name a single cause; they give a list—nationalism, the alliance system, mobilization schedules, the assassination, the personalities of particular leaders—and they clearly regard singling one out as vulgar or old-fashioned. But then, elsewhere in the same books, they do single one out, usually with a hedge like "the deepest cause" or "the underlying cause," and two historians who agree about every fact in the case will single out different items from the list. What strikes me is that their disagreement isn't about what happened; it's about which jointly present factor deserves to be lifted out of the pile, and the implicit standard for lifting seems to shift: sometimes it's whichever factor was most abnormal against its background (the assassination), sometimes whichever was most entrenched and thus hardest to imagine away (the alliance system), sometimes whichever best supports the lesson the historian wants to draw.

So the thing I want a name for is not the situation of many causes being present—everyone just calls that multi-causality, and that word doesn't help—but the selection problem: the practice of choosing one condition from a set of conditions that were individually insufficient and (arguably) jointly necessary, plus the shifting criteria (abnormality, contingency, removability, depth) used to justify the choice. It's related to overdetermination as I understand that term from a bit of philosophy reading, but in the classic overdetermination cases each cause is sufficient on its own, whereas in the historical cases I mean no single factor would have done the job alone, so "overdetermination" doesn't quite land either. I've also seen philosophers mention that there's something called an INUS condition, but that seems to describe each item on the list rather than the picking.

I'd like to know: does this selection problem have an established name or literature (I gather philosophers have written about "causal selection," but I'm not sure whether that's the same thing or something adjacent)? Please tell me what the nearest existing concepts are, how each differs from what I've described, and which one fits best; and if the fit is imperfect, coin a term of your own with a definition and a couple of boundary cases—e.g., does it apply when a scientist picks one gene out of a network as "the gene for" a trait, or only in retrospective historical explanation? I don't want just a label; I want to understand what exactly the practice is and where its edges are. Please answer in American English.
