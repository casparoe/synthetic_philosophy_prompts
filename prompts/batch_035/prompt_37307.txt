I'm taking a seminar on scholastic political thought, and my paper is basically the claim that Aquinas slams the door on tyrannicide: in the little treatise on kingship he allows that when a tyrant's oppression has become unbearable and there's no higher authority to appeal to, killing him seems not to count as murder, since the tyrant is an enemy of the commonwealth — but then he immediately pulls back and says that this is a dangerous business, because private men who conspire against tyrants usually botch the job and end up provoking even worse oppression, so the remedy should be sought through public and lawful means rather than private daring. And in the Summa he says plainly that killing a sinner belongs to public authority, not to private individuals. My friend says the later Jesuits blew the door back open, and I want to practice arguing my side against a real opponent rather than him paraphrasing wikipedia at me.

So play a learned, elderly Spanish Jesuit in the mold of Juan de Mariana, sitting in your study not long after word has reached you that your treatise on the king was publicly burned in Paris by order of the Parlement, following the assassination of Henri IV by Ravaillac in 1610 — a deed everyone is trying to pin on you. You are unrepentant and sharp. Your core thesis, which you will defend to the end: a private man may justly kill a tyrant, and it can even be praiseworthy, at least where the tyrant has been judged (whether by an assembly, by wise men, or by the clear testimony of his crimes) and there is no lawful recourse left. Argue from Scripture, the Roman historians, canon law, and natural law; write in your scholastic first-person voice with real distinctions, not slogans. You may concede small points (that a lawful trial is preferable when possible, that foolish zeal is vicious) but never the main thesis. Stay in character until I write stop, and keep each reply to a single paragraph.

Here is my opening. Thomas grants you more than you think, and it still isn't enough: he concedes the tyrant can be an enemy of the commonwealth, yet says private men who attempt the killing mostly fail and so make things worse — and our case seems to prove him right. The king was legitimately crowned, then turned tyrant; a lawful council of the realm has in fact judged and condemned him to death; but the sentence sits unexecuted, because every magistrate who ought to carry it out is too afraid to strike, and meanwhile the condemned king keeps ruling and killing. So tell me: may a private man act on the council's sentence? If you say yes, he seems to be a mere executioner of a public judgment — which is my side's concession, not yours. And if you say he may strike even without waiting on such a sentence, or that his own private judgment suffices, then you've handed Thomas his very argument back. Go ahead, father.
