I'm preparing a two-hour seminar for final-year PPE students on collective decision-making, and I'd like to close with a diagnostic exercise rather than another lecture. The topic is the discursive dilemma and its aftermath: by the end of the session the students will know the three-judge court example, and they'll have seen that majority voting on each proposition separately can yield a collectively inconsistent verdict even when every individual's set of judgments is perfectly consistent. What I want from you is a decision tree that starts from that starting point — 'I accept that majority voting on interconnected propositions can produce an incoherent collective judgment' — and leads, via branch nodes the students can answer without knowing any of the labels, to a leaf naming a position on what committees, panels and juries should actually do about it.

A few constraints on how I'd like the tree built. First, please build it from the logical space rather than from a list of school names: the branch nodes should be questions like whether the collective outcome must itself be consistent as a matter of principle, whether the aggregation rule should treat every proposition on the agenda the same way regardless of whether it is a premise or a conclusion, whether it should be sensitive to how individual voters arrived at their verdicts, and whether it is acceptable for a single member's judgment to settle a proposition for the group. Second, the leaves must include at least the following: the view that bites the bullet and accepts occasional collective inconsistency as the price of equal votes; the premise-based procedure (decide the premises by vote, derive the conclusion); the conclusion-based procedure; some form of oligarchy or weighted voting; outright dictatorship as the limiting case; epistemic deference to the member or members judged most competent on the issue; and the retreat that gives up the demand that the collective judgment be complete rather than consistent. Third, please note any combinations of branch answers that are coherent but that nobody seriously defends, and say briefly whether you think that is because the position is unattractive, unstable once spelled out, or merely unexplored.

Two further requests. Some of these leaves are notoriously hard to prise apart in practice — for instance, restricting the agenda to premises can be observationally indistinguishable from the premise-based procedure, and a high enough oligarchic threshold on an issue with few informed members can look exactly like epistemic deference — so flag any pair your tree cannot genuinely separate rather than pretending the branches do the work. And since the students will answer the nodes in real time before seeing the leaves, phrase every branch question as something a person can answer from their own reactions to concrete cases (a hiring committee, a parole board, a grant panel, a church synod debating doctrine) rather than from theory. Please write the whole thing in British English, and aim for something I could actually print as a two-page handout: roughly eight to ten branch questions and no more than ten leaves, with one or two sentences at each leaf on the position's most awkward commitment.
