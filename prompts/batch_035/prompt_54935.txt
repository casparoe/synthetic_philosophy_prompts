I am at work on my fourth novel, in which a slow epidemic robs a small coastal town of phenomenal consciousness while leaving memory, speech, and conduct entirely intact. The afflicted maintain, fluently and quite sincerely, that they are exactly as aware as they ever were; my narrator, a neurologist who has read her philosophy, cannot determine whether this testimony is evidence or the very thing her theories instruct her to discount. She is to spend the middle chapters arguing with her brother-in-law, a retired philosopher of mind who holds the epidemic to be conceptually impossible, and I have reached the point where I cannot write their arguments until I know what they should be demanding of the theories they trade in.

So, rather than another rehearsal of the standard positions, give me the specification: the desiderata that any adequate account of the relation between the physical facts and phenomenal consciousness must meet. Which cases must it get right — my qualia-less but otherwise unimpaired neighbours, the inverted spectrum, Mary in her black-and-white room and whatever does or does not change upon her release, blindsight-like dissociations in which discrimination survives experience — and what must it do about the conceivability of a complete physical duplicate of the world with no experience added: explain the intuition away, accommodate it, or show it confused, and without either debunking wholesale what the intuition seems to teach or quietly building the dualist conclusion in as an assumption. Which distinctions must it respect — between the conceivability of such a duplicate and its possibility, between the water-is-H2O style of identity and the claimed identity of experience with a brain process, between the narrator's third-person evidence and the first-person reports she can no longer trust — and which of these demands are genuine desiderata rather than the prejudices of one camp. If any pair of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, flag the tension inside the item where it bites, since that impasse is precisely where I intend to end the book. Do not score the familiar contenders against the list; the neurologist will attempt that herself, and get it wrong in ways I need her to.

Give me the response as a numbered list of exactly five desiderata, each a sentence or two, with nothing before the first item or after the last.
