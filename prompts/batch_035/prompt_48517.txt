I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and our department's postgraduate work-in-progress series is hosting a paper defending the parity thesis: that lying and deliberately misleading someone are morally on a par, so that whatever makes one wrong makes the other equally wrong. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press where the literature presses, rather than inviting a restatement of the view. At minimum I want one question on which direction the parity is meant to run — whether misleading inherits the wrongness of lying or vice versa, and what follows for the murderer-at-the-door cases that motivate Kantians to care about the distinction in the first place; one on the suggestion that misleading is not equal but worse, since it recruits the hearer's own cooperative reasoning and makes her complicit in her deception; one on whether the thesis is hostage to contested semantics, e.g. whether one can lie while saying something true if what matters is asserting against one's beliefs rather than asserting what is false; and one on the agent who scrupulously avoids lying while engineering false beliefs — whether that effort shows greater deliberateness (aggravating) or genuine respect for some norm (mitigating), and what to make of her retrospective appeal to being 'technically true'. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply the author is most likely to give, and close with the single case you think a defender of parity should find hardest to digest.
