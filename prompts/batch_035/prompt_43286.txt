Analyze the era of atmospheric nuclear testing, and its 1963 ending, as a case study in multipolar traps and races to the bottom. By way of fixing the salient facts so that the analysis does not turn on contested details: between 1945 and 1963, the United States and the Soviet Union — joined late by Britain — conducted hundreds of nuclear tests in the atmosphere and underwater. The fallout was a global commons burden rather than a bilateral one: strontium-90 turned up in milk and in children's teeth, became a public controversy, and gave everyone, including the superpowers' own publics, a shared interest in ending the contamination. In August 1963, shortly after the Cuban missile crisis, the three powers signed the Moscow Treaty banning tests in the atmosphere, outer space, and underwater, with underground testing permitted to continue. I know the standard arms-race framing and the textbook prisoner's-dilemma treatment; what I want is the multipolar-trap analysis specifically — the one on which competing agents are individually pushed toward actions that are jointly worse, because unilaterally abstaining cedes an advantage to rivals — run in both directions, theory illuminating the episode and the episode testing the theory.
