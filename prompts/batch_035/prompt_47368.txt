I've been reading up on these "idealized preference" accounts of wellbeing, where what's good for you is what you'd want under some improved conditions (smarter, better informed, etc.). Rank these four on how well they'd actually pin down a determinate answer for a given person, with a sentence on why each lands where it does:

(a) Brandt-style cognitive psychotherapy: strip out desires that depend on irrational beliefs, re-form the rest given the facts
(b) Railton's ideal advisor: your fully-informed, vividly-appreciating self, but still recognizably you, advises your current self
(c) Yudkowsky's CEV-style extrapolation, pushed as far as it goes (coherence, knowledge, growing up)
(d) a variant I've been toying with myself: form a committee of your fully-informed selves at every age you'll ever be, one vote each, equal weights, and take what the committee settles on

Be honest about which ones run into the circularity worry — where the idealization changes the person, which changes which idealization applies, and so on — and whether the averaging in (d) even helps or just blurs things. Also tell me what would make you re-rank: which assumption, if it failed, would move which item the most.
