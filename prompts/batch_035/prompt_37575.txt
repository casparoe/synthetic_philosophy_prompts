I'm a retired physicist who has taken up philosophy of mind in retirement, and I keep running into a methodological stance I can't put a name to. In consciousness research, some writers proceed as follows: collect subjects' verbal reports about their experience, transcribe them, and then treat the transcripts not as windows onto experience but as data about what the subjects believe about their experience — the way a historian treats a partisan chronicle, as indispensable evidence about the chronicler rather than about the events. The reports are neither trusted nor discarded; the theorist reconstructs from them how things seem to the subject, and then explains that seeming, possibly away, in neural terms. It reminds me of how I treated a miscalibrated instrument back in the lab: the readings are data, but about the instrument–world system, not about the world directly. Does this stance have an established name, and is it associated with a particular author? If so, how does it differ from behaviorism, which also declined to take reports of inner life at face value, and from ordinary scientific skepticism about introspection? And a further point that bothers me: if my subject's reports are just texts awaiting interpretation, so are the theorist's own, including any on which the interpretation itself rests. Has anyone argued the method is circular or self-undermining in this way, and does it quietly presuppose that consciousness is exhaustively describable from the third person? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my questions.
