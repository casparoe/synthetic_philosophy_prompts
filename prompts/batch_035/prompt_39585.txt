"The covering-law model, developed by Karl Popper in his early work in the philosophy of history, holds that to explain a historical event is to subsume it under a general law. The historian cites a set of initial conditions together with a law of the form 'whenever conditions of type C obtain, an event of type E follows,' and the description of the event to be explained is then deduced from these premises. Because the derivation has exactly the same logical form as a prediction, the model treats explanation and prediction as structurally symmetric: an ideal historian who could not have foreseen an event could not, on the model's terms, explain it either. The strongest objection is that history contains no laws of this sort: there are no exceptionless generalizations about revolutions, inflations, or the decline of empires, and since the model requires laws to be strictly universal, no merely statistical or probabilistic regularity can do any covering work. From this, the narrative school drew the natural conclusion. William Dray argued that, subsumption being unavailable, historical explanation dispenses with general and causal claims altogether: the historian simply relates events in their chronological order, and it is the coherence of the story, not any appeal to causes, that does the explaining. Dray's further point was that this is not a weaker substitute for explanation but a different logical operation altogether — a mode of understanding that subsumption could never even approximate."
