Write a dialogue between two epistemologists: one who accepts the closure principle for knowledge and one who, following Nozick's tracking theory, denies it. The dialogue should be a genuine argument, not a lecture; each participant should press the other's actual commitments and be given a competent reply.

Scene and stakes: the two are standing before a pen at the zoo, in the spirit of Dretske's zebra case. Looking at the animal, it is obvious to both that they know it is a zebra. The closure defender argues that, since they know it is a zebra, and since its being a zebra entails that it is not a cleverly painted mule engineered to look just like a zebra, they ipso facto know that it is not such a mule — and that anyone who grants the first knowledge but withholds the second has abandoned a highly intuitive principle (known propositions entail their known consequences) merely to avoid a theoretical cost.

The tracking theorist should respond on Nozick's behalf: knowledge requires the belief to track the truth — roughly, if the proposition were false, the subject would not believe it. The belief that the animal is not a disguised mule fails this, since if it were a disguised mule, the subject would believe just as confidently that it was not. Sensitivity is not, in general, preserved across entailment, so closure has to go. The closure defender should then press the standard costs: rejecting closure licenses allegedly "abominable" conjunctions of the form "I know that p, but I don't know that q," where q is an obvious consequence of p (or of p plus admitted background facts), and it threatens to leave the tracker unable to know disjunctions of things she knows, or the conjunction of two known propositions.

Both parties should also address, at some point: (a) whether the tracker can soften the cost by distinguishing which alternative propositions are "properly raised" in a context — e.g., whether the mule hypothesis is a live alternative in the zoo but not in a farmyard — and whether that contextualist move is really available to Nozick, whose sensitivity condition is supposed to be a substantive counterfactual fact about the subject rather than a fact about the conversational setting; and (b) how each side handles a lottery case, where a ticket-holder believes, on overwhelming statistical grounds, that her ticket has lost, since these cases pull against closure-friendly accounts (which must say she knows it lost, or find some way to deny knowledge) and against tracking accounts alike, and so test whether either side's story about the zoo transfers.

Neither participant should be a strawman: the closure defender should not be allowed to treat "closure is intuitive" as sufficient, and the tracking theorist should not be allowed to assert that sensitivity obviously matters without defending why preserving a substantive connection between knowledge and how the world would have gone is worth more than preserving closure. End the dialogue without a forced resolution, but with each character stating clearly what they take the single strongest consideration on their side to be, and what would make them abandon their position.
