Kavka's toxin puzzle has our reading group completely stuck, and I want a diagnosis of the stuckness, not a verdict on whose right. Setup so we're on the same page: a billionaire will pay me a million tomorrow at noon if, at noon, I intend to drink a mildly nasty toxin the day after. I know that once the money is mine there's no reason left to drink, and I know I won't drink. Half the group says the intention is impossible or irrational because intending to A requires not believing you won't A — call them the belief crowd. The other half says intention is a commitment-state, not a prediction, so I can intend now even while forecasting that I'll renege; one of them even invented an escrow variant where a third party administers the toxin unless I phone by noon to cancel, and asked whether the belief crowd thinks the intention not-to-cancel is impossible too — and they just changed the subject to Gauthier and assessing rationality over dispositions or policies instead of individual acts. And there it sat, for the fourth meeting in a row.

My hypothesis is that the two camps aren't actually disagreeing about a psychological fact at all. One side is answering 'what is intention' — a question about the state's commitment conditions — and the other is answering 'what should rationality be assessed over', and the phrase 'can you intend' lets each of them hear their own question. Is that right? If it is, tell me whether anything would count as progress here: a sharper distinction, a better thought experiment, maybe even empirical work on whether people who sincerely intend are actually any good at predicting their own follow-through. Or is this one of those impasses we should just expect to stay stuck forever, and if so, why — because the question is ill-posed, or because it's hard in a way no distinction can touch?
