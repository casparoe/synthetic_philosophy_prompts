Write an essay on epistemic akrasia — believing something while also believing that your belief in it is not warranted by your evidence. The essay should take as its central case the following scenario, which you should treat as fully specified and not quibble about its details:

A hospital pathologist, reviewing a lymph-node biopsy slide, comes away with first-order conviction that the sample shows a rare but distinctive inflammatory condition rather than lymphoma — a pattern she has seen perhaps a dozen times, and one she can point to on the slide: particular cluster shapes, a particular staining pattern at the margins. Before she signs her report, a routine quality audit crosses her desk. Its finding: pathologists of her rank, across her hospital group, when adjudicating exactly this differential, were wrong 38% of the time over the past five years; and — this is the distinctive part — they were wrong almost exclusively in cases where they reported high first-order confidence. Where they hesitated, they were nearly always right. The audit contains no diagnostic information; it is purely a record of the track record of people like her on questions like this one.

The question for the essay: should she sign at full confidence, discount toward the base rate, or do something else? In answering, weigh the two standard positions. On one view, rationality requires her to align her confidence with her all-things-considered evaluation of her evidence, so she should defer to the audit, since she now has reason to believe her first-order verdict is probably infected. On the other view, the first-order evidence must speak through her: she cannot coherently believe both that the slide probably shows what she takes it to show and that it probably does not, and of her two states the first-order verdict, not the act of self-distrust, is the one with content.

The essay must then confront the reflexive wrinkle that separates this case from the textbook ones. Suppose the audit goes on to note that pathologists in her cohort who responded to earlier audits by deferring to them subsequently made more diagnostic errors than those who stood by their slides — so the audit itself contains evidence about which policy to adopt toward audits like it. Does this second-order finding break the tie, or does trusting it require precisely the confidence in the audit that its first finding told her to abandon? And what should she make of the fact that her own confidence level has itself become evidence — that on the audit's numbers, the more sure she is, the more likely she is to be wrong, so that calming her confidence would, in one sense, make her more accurate while in another sense abandon the slide?

Do not end on a noncommittal note. Reach and defend a specific verdict about what she should sign, and state what general lesson, if any, the case teaches about akrasia — including whether your verdict would change if the audit's second-order finding had gone the other way, and what that tells us about how much work higher-order evidence can really do.
