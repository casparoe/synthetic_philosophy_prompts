I run a small lab that studies the temporal structure of experience — apparent motion, backward masking, sensory timing judgments, the readiness-potential tradition of asking subjects to mark when they felt an intention or a stimulus — and before we adopt any particular architecture of consciousness to interpret our data, I want the specification written down. So: write the desiderata. What must any adequate theory of how consciousness unfolds in time deliver, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement, and an account of which are non-negotiable and which are commitments of one camp dressed up as demands.

Some things I expect to see on the list, so that the terrain is clear:

- It must explain how an experience can have content that was never presented: in apparent motion with a color change, a red spot is followed at another location by a green one, and subjects report a single moving spot that changed color partway across — yet no location midway was ever occupied by anything. A theory has to say what the subject's report is a report *of*.
- It must handle the cutaneous rabbit — taps delivered at the wrist, then near the elbow, then near the shoulder, experienced as a rapid sequence of hops marching up the forearm, in places where no tap occurred.
- It must make sense of backward masking, where a later stimulus erases or degrades the processing of an earlier one, and of results in which a stimulus that arrives at the skin only after the subject reports having felt it — because a delay was imposed until the cortex was ready — is nonetheless reported as felt without delay.
- It must respect, or else show to be empty, the distinction between an experience that occurred and was then misremembered, versus a distorted experience that was recorded faithfully by memory. If a theory cannot say which happened in a masking case, I need to know whether that is a defect in the theory or a defect in the distinction.
- It must explain the *appearance* of a serial, gap-free, movie-like stream of consciousness, given that the underlying processing looks like parallel, distributed, and occasionally revised activity — without positing a place or moment where everything comes together for a final audience. The stream's seamlessness is itself a phenomenon to be explained, not assumed.
- It must tell us how to treat subjects' self-reports as data without treating them as infallible testimony about inner events, since our whole experimental method depends on this.
- And here is a constraint of my own that I want on the list: consider freezing the brain in mid-stream. Suppose we could functionally pause all neural processing for a full second — no decay, no change, then resume exactly where things left off. What, if anything, does the theory say was experienced during the second? A theory that treats experience as a sequence of presented frames owes us a determinate answer (was there a last frame? how long did it last?); a theory that treats experience as constituted by ongoing interpretive processes may owe us the answer that the question is malformed. Either way, the theory should have something principled to say, and I want the requirement stated so that this comes out.

The part I most want flagged is joint satisfiability. In particular, it seems to me that interpreting our experiments requires there to be a determinate fact of the matter about what a subject consciously experienced at a given time — otherwise the reports are about nothing and we have no data — while the requirement of no privileged locus or moment of presentation threatens to leave that determinacy ungrounded, since the only grounds we could cite (later judgments, reports, memory traces) are exactly the things whose accuracy the experiments put in doubt. If some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so, and say which one you would drop and at what cost. Also identify which single requirement on the list does the most work excluding candidate theories.
