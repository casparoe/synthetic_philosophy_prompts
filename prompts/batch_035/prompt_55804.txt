I'm taking a seminar on the ethics of war and we just finished the module on whether combatants on both sides of a war are moral equals (Walzer says yes sort of, McMahan and the revisionists say no way). I mostly side with the revisionists on the theory but my gut verdicts on actual cases are a mess, so I want to hire you as a just war theorist for this exercise: think hard, maybe brainstorm a couple candidate principles before settling, and then give me a real reflective-equilibrium treatment — a principle (or two competing ones), stated precisely, with each clause motivated by one of my cases, and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts you had to overturn and why.

Here are the verdicts. A conscript into an unjust war of aggression, who fights hard and kills three defenders after his country invades: I don't think he's a murderer, and I don't think the defenders were wrong to kill him — those both feel right at the same time, which is already weird. A volunteer pilot in the same unjust war who bombs a military airbase and kills mechanics: he's culpable, though less than if he'd hit a school. A mercenary hired by the just side to defend against that same aggression: feels dirty somehow, but I can't find anything wrong with him. On the just side, a soldier who volunteered specifically because he was eager to kill: fine, killing enemy combatants, even though I hate his motive. Allied bomber crew over Dresden in an otherwise just war: very wrong, as wrong as the unjust pilot maybe, which is awkward since the bomber crew's war is just. A fourteen-year-old rebel child soldier: excused, obviously, but I notice I excuse him more easily than I excuse the conscript, which can't be right since the conscript faced prison and the kid faced worse. A combat medic on the unjust side who never fires a shot: not liable to anything, though he's doing more good for the unjust cause than some riflemen. And the pair that keeps me up: I think a defender in a just war may permissibly kill an attacking conscript, AND I think that conscript, if he survives and is captured, deserves to be released at war's end — but I can't hold both without thinking permissible killing can target someone who isn't culpable, which sounds like I'm collapsing back into Walzer.

My tentative principle is something like: you're liable to defensive harm only if you (a) pose a threat, (b) had a reasonable opportunity to know the war is unjust, and (c) weren't coerced to a degree that excuses — but I suspect (b) and (c) together excuse nearly everyone in modern conscript armies, which would mean the revisionists are right in theory and wrong about every actual war. Test the principle against my cases, patch or replace it, and then tell me what it says about two cases I haven't listed: a private military contractor guarding a munitions factory on the unjust side, and a voluntary human shield standing in front of that factory who's unarmed but knowingly keeping the bombs at bay. Which verdicts of mine do you have to throw out to make it work?
