A recurring idea in interpretability-based safety evaluation is mechanistic anomaly detection: run a model on inputs you consider benign, learn a characterization of its internal activations, and flag any later input whose internals fall outside that distribution — the bet being that when a model does something dangerous, it is usually implementing a different mechanism than the one you characterized, not just the familiar mechanism in a bad mood. It strikes me that this whole research program quietly presupposes a theory of when two computations count as the same mechanism, and that nobody states the theory. So: what criterion of mechanistic identity does the practice actually need — same parameters doing the same thing, same circuit topology, same causal role in producing behavior? Do these criteria come apart in realistic cases, e.g., a dangerous capability that reuses the same features with different downstream wiring? Does the presupposed view face a vagueness problem when benign and harmful behavior share most of their circuitry, with only a few links re-purposed? And is the practice philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that several rival criteria of sameness would license the same detector, or does it genuinely commit to one? Finally, tell me what would have to change in the methodology if we gave up on mechanistic sameness entirely and flagged anomalies only relative to input distributions.
