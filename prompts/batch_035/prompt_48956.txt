I cover city hall for a small daily in a midwestern town of about 11,000, and twice a year we get what my editor has taken to calling fence season — the council's ordinance cleanup session where they go through the town code rule by rule and decide what stays. She means Chesterton's fence, obviously: don't go pulling down a fence till you've figured out why somebody put it up in the first place. I've got a column due Friday and I want to get ahead of next month's big one instead of just reacting to it, so I'm trying to work out what these people are actually doing before they do it.

The track record from the last few cleanup sessions:

- Kept the 1924 backyard chicken ban. Noone could find any record of why it was passed — the minutes from that year are just gone. One councilor said that was exactly the point.
- Removed the Prohibition-era rule barring liquor licenses within 500 feet of a church. Everyone knew exactly why that one existed, the reason was plainly dead, and zero people showed up to defend it.
- Removed the 1955 overnight parking ban on Main Street. It was originally for the dawn street-sweeping machine, which the town sold off in the eighties. Again, no defenders.
- Kept the requirement that downtown shopfront signs be painted letters, no plastic. The reason on the record is some 1930s dispute with an out-of-town sign company, long irrelevant — but they kept it anyway, and a councilor said the rule is basically the look of the town now. She's not wrong, it is.

Next month's headline case: a developer wants to override the two-story height limit downtown to put a third floor of apartments over the pharmacy. The limit's original reason is documented in the minutes — 1948, fire ladder reach — and the fire chief himself testified that his truck handles four stories easy. But alot of residents have already turned up to say the low skyline is the whole character of the place, it's why people stop and walk around.

So here's what I want from you. Predict the vote, sure, but what I actually need for the column is the principle they're running. Because it can't be the plain Chesterton rule — keep the fence till you know why it went up, take it down once you know the reason's gone — because the sign vote breaks that one: they knew the reason was gone and kept it anyway. Maybe it's the more Burkean version, where a fence counts as having found a new job it was never hired for, so it stays even after the old job ends. Or maybe it's something less flattering — that they remove whatever nobody bothers defending and keep whatever gets an angry turnout, and the sign rule only survived because people like the way it looks. The height limit is interesting precisely because it's the first case where those readings split: the documented reason is dead as the liquor one, but there's a real constituency like the sign rule had.

Tell me which principle best fits all four of their past votes, what it predicts for the height limit, and your best guess at the outcome with your reasoning. Then give me the case that would settle it — some future ordinance question where the fence-for-a-new-job reading and the angry-turnout reading (or whatever else you think is live) come out differently, so I know what to watch for at the session after this one.

And one thing: if you think I'm over-reading a pattern onto nine people who are mostly voting vibes, and there is no principle there to find, tell me that straight instead of building me an elegant theory my editor will have to retract later. I'd rather write the column about the vibes.
