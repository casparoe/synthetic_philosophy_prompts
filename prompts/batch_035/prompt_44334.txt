I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on aesthetics, and our final unit covers the British empiricists on beauty and judgment. I am writing the take-home final, and I want one essay question on Hume's "Of the Standard of Taste" (1757) on the question of whether beauty is objective — a question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard summary of the essay. Students who have merely memorized the five marks of the true judge, the opening concession that all sentiment is right, and the observation that Hume's test ranks one long English epic far above another mediocre one should find that none of this alone earns credit.

The question must be built around a distinction I want to test, and the question text must state it plainly so that no background in philosophy of science is presupposed: the difference between a constitutive standard and an epistemic one. Consider the old platinum-iridium bar kept at Sèvres, which once defined the metre: something was one metre long if and only if it matched the bar, so the bar could not be wrong about the metre — it fixed the fact rather than tracking it. A well-calibrated thermometer is different: the temperature of a room is what it is independently of the instrument, the thermometer is at best a reliable indicator of it, and it can err, be corrected, and be compared against rivals. A constitutive standard makes a verdict infallible by definition; an epistemic standard makes a verdict defeasible but creditable, because it points at something beyond itself.

Students must decide which kind of standard Hume's joint verdict of qualified judges is supposed to be — or argue that the essay slides between the two — and say exactly what hangs on the choice for the objectivity of beauty. If the verdict of ideal critics is constitutive, then a work's being beautiful just is its being approved by them, and the objectivity Hume secures is cheap: he would have defined the standard into existence, and the skeptic's challenge would be met by stipulation rather than argument. If it is epistemic, then beauty must be a fact about works that is fixed independently of the critics, and Hume owes us an account of what that fact could be — a burden his sentiment-based metaphysics of beauty (certain qualities in objects fitted by nature to produce pleasurable feelings) makes awkward to discharge.

The question must also require students to do each of the following:

1. Treat Hume's remark about the poet who was admired in the ancient world and is still admired in modern capitals as an empirical convergence claim — a prediction about durable agreement — and say what it would show about the standard if a work admired for many centuries were to fall permanently out of favor. They should consider whether such a reversal refutes the standard, merely reveals that the earlier judges were not qualified, or shows the standard was constitutive all along and the work's beauty changed with our nature.

2. Address the circle in the essay's procedure: qualified judges are identified partly through their verdicts on works we antecedently admire, and the standing of those works is then vindicated by the verdicts of the qualified judges. They must say whether this circle is vicious, and whether Hume's independently stateable marks of the true judge — delicacy improved by practice, extensive comparison across works and ages, freedom from prejudice, good sense — break it or merely relocate it.

3. Take seriously the fact that the essay's title promises a standard of taste, not a standard of beauty. They should address whether whatever objectivity is won attaches to the verdicts of critics rather than to the works themselves, and whether that is objectivity about beauty at all, or merely intersubjective convergence dressed up.

Please provide three deliverables. First, the question exactly as students will see it, including the constitutive/epistemic distinction as stated above, in a form they could answer in roughly 90 minutes. Second, a model answer of about 650 words that itself takes a definite position rather than surveying the options. Third, a marking scheme out of 20 points: five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, a one-line note after each on how candidates typically lose the marks, and a closing paragraph naming the specific moves that separate an A answer from a B answer — the A answer should do something the B answer cannot, and you should say what it is.
