My uncle received a COPD diagnosis this spring after fifty years of smoking, and at a recent family dinner he announced that he intends to keep smoking: he enjoys it, he is seventy-four, and a somewhat shorter life spent as he pleases strikes him as a fair trade. The family has since split into two camps that re-litigate this at every gathering, and because I have been deputized to draft a letter to him, I need to know who is actually right about what before I put anything in writing. My cousin Ruth, who is finishing medical school, says Uncle Ray has overwhelming reason to quit, full stop: health is a genuine value, its claim on him does not depend on his caring about it, and whether he recognizes a reason is a separate question from whether he has one. My aunt Carol, who read philosophy as an undergraduate and will not let anyone forget it, says Ruth's claim is empty unless it connects to something already in Uncle Ray's motivational set — his attachments, projects, and cares as they actually stand. On Carol's view, a reason, in order to be a reason *for him*, must be capable of showing up in his deliberation; otherwise saying he has it does no explanatory work and could not ground any advice we could actually give him. Ruth answered with the case of a paranoid man whose psychosis is the only thing standing between him and the medication that would restore his life: we all say such a man has reason to take it, so reasons evidently do not require the agent's current endorsement. Carol said that case is easy for her — psychosis distorts desires, and once the distortion is corrected, the medication connects to things the man already cares about; addiction works the same way, which is why it remains worth talking to Uncle Ray at all. Ruth replied that this was cheating twice over. First, the reason in Uncle Ray's case is generated by facts about his lungs; the connection to his existing cares is a persuasion strategy, not what makes it a reason. Second, even Carol's own view lets her reach beyond what Ray currently wants to what he would come to want under full information and fully coherent deliberation — and at that point the idealized Ray has been quietly rebuilt in the image of Ruth's values, so the internal reason is just externalism wearing a disguise. Carol responded that full information is not the same as new values: the idealization only extends reasoning Ray himself already endorses, and if a fully coherent Ray who understood exactly what COPD involves still preferred the cigarettes, then — hard as it is to say — there would be no sense in which he was wrong about what to do, only a sense in which he was unlucky in what he wanted. Ruth said that consequence refutes the whole view: someone who knowingly trades his grandchildren's remaining years with him for cigarettes is not merely unlucky. They have now asked me to referee, and I honestly cannot. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say who is right about each specific point as it was actually made, whether Carol's final concession is survivable or fatal for her position, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
