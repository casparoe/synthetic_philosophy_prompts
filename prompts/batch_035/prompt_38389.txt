I teach ethics and political philosophy, and the later Wittgenstein is an area I have not worked through seriously since graduate school. At a recent faculty reception, a colleague from the psychology department, who has been reading some popular philosophy of mind on the side, offered me his understanding of the private language argument, and I found myself unable to say with confidence exactly where he goes wrong, though I am confident he goes wrong somewhere. Since I would like to reply to him by email and be of some use rather than merely suspicious, I would be grateful for a careful audit.

His account, reconstructed as faithfully as I can manage: Wittgenstein wanted to show that a language only one person could in principle understand is impossible. Imagine you keep a private diary and decide to write the sign S every time a particular sensation of yours occurs, intending thereby to record that sensation on future occasions. Because nobody else could ever check your entries, the only court of appeal is your own memory of what you decided S meant — and memory is fallible, so you can never be sure on a later occasion that you are using S correctly rather than merely believing you are. But a word that cannot be used correctly or incorrectly is not functioning as a word at all, Wittgenstein concluded; correctness of application presupposes publicity. This is why, on his view, sensation words like pain mean what they mean solely through their role in our public, shared practices — and it is also why he introduced his famous beetle in a box analogy: since the private inner item can never enter into the public use of the word, it plays no role in the word's meaning and might as well not exist. So in the end the inner sensation itself drops out of the picture, and our talk of sensations is really talk about the patterns of behavior and circumstances in which we use sensation words.

I would like you to go through this claim by claim and tell me what a Wittgenstein scholar would accept as a reasonable simplification, what they would regard as subtly but importantly wrong, and what they would simply reject. In particular, I have three suspicions I would like checked. First, it seems to me that the diary example is presented as turning on the ordinary fallibility of memory, whereas my recollection is that the argument was meant to establish something stronger about there being no criterion of correctness at all — and that these are not the same point. Second, my colleague runs together a language that happens to be spoken by one person alone and a language whose words refer to what only the speaker could in principle know, and I suspect Wittgenstein's target is only the latter, which would matter for whether his conclusion really applies to a lone survivor on an island who continues speaking a language she once learned. Third, the slide from the beetle analogy to the claim that the inner sensation might as well not exist, and from there to the suggestion that our sensation talk is really just talk about behavior, strikes me as the kind of behaviorism I vaguely recall Wittgenstein distancing himself from — though I cannot reconstruct how he did so. Please also tell me whether the strong conclusions my colleague draws — that inner experience is in some sense illusory, and that privacy of the mental is refuted — follow from any accurate parts of his summary, or whether they require the distortions. I do not need citations or references to the secondary literature; what I need is an accurate reconstruction of the argument and a clear verdict on each of his claims, so that my reply to him is both generous and correct.
