I'm a grad student TA-ing an undergrad course on museums, art, and colonial legacies — next week's unit is restitution, centered on the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles, and I want to open my discussion section with the museum scene from Black Panther, since basically everyone in the class has seen it and it's the rare blockbuster that actually stages this exact debate on screen. If you need it refreshed: early in the film Killmonger wanders through a thinly-veiled British Museum stand-in with a curator, asks about a particular African artifact, is told the collection isn't for sale, and then asks her how her ancestors got all these things in the first place — whether they paid a fair price for them or just took them. Then it turns out he's there to steal one of the items, with help from someone on the inside. Read the film through the lens of the restitution debate: the argument from unjust acquisition, the cultural patrimony claim that these objects belong to the people whose ancestors made them, and the "universal museum" defense that world collections belong to everyone now.

But I also want you to take seriously the ways the film complicates its own museum scene, because that's where I think it gets philosophically interesting. Wakanda is a never-colonized African nation that hid itself and hoarded its resources — and Killmonger, the character who delivers the anti-museum speech, is a descendant of the diaspora whose claim on Wakandan wealth is exactly the kind of contested claim restitution arguments raise: is restitution owed to the nation-state, to the cultural community, or to the scattered descendants of the colonized and enslaved? Meanwhile T'Challa, the hero, responds to all this not by returning anything but by opening an outreach center in Oakland. So does the film ultimately endorse the restitution argument, defang it by putting it in the villain's mouth, or replace it with something softer? And is there something queasy about a Disney blockbuster profiting from the aesthetics of the very cultures whose looted objects are at issue?

At least one of the five should be a place where you think the honest reading goes against the pro-restitution grain of the film's most famous scene, and at least one should say what the film dramatizes about this debate that the academic arguments by themselves don't.
