I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of the social sciences, and for our unit on causal inference from history I want the students to confront a question I've been chewing on myself: why has the "deep causes of national wealth" debate made essentially no progress in twenty-five years, and what kind of disagreement is it, really? You are to write an essay-length analysis in the voice of a philosopher of social science with strong command of both causal inference and economic history. Before writing, take time to think carefully and brainstorm through the candidate diagnoses; the quality of the piece rests on the diagnosis, not on the survey of positions.

Here is the debate as I'll present it to the students. One camp, running from Jarred Diamond through Jeffrey Sachs, puts geography first: latitude, disease environment, resource endowments, and continental axes set the initial conditions, and everything downstream — property rights, state capacity, industrialization — is a transmission mechanism. A second camp, now most visible in Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson's work on colonial origins, treats extractive versus inclusive institutions as the operative cause, using European settler mortality as an instrument to get around the endogeneity of institutions. A third camp, in a lineage going back to Weber and carried forward by work on trust, kinship systems, and religious inheritance, holds that culture does the work — that the same geography and even the same formal constitutions produce different outcomes depending on durable values. The camps have exchanged the same rebuttals for two decades: the instrument is really picking up the disease environment, so it's geography after all; the geography variables only matter through institutions, so it's institutions after all; the culture variables are measured so loosely that "culture" is just a residual bin for whatever the other two can't explain. Nobody has moved. Each camp controls for the others' variables, gets a nonzero coefficient on its own, and declares victory.

I have a hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis. I suspect the three camps are answering three different contrastive questions under one heading: geography answers "why Europe rather than the Americas circa 1500?," institutions answers "why Britain rather than Spain in 1800?," and culture answers "why do second-generation immigrants from the same country diverge by region of settlement?" If that's right, the debate is less a disagreement about facts than a collision of explanatory projects — what one might call a difference in the contrast class — and the demand that one factor "win" is a category error. But I can also see a rival diagnosis: that this is a hidden methodological dispute about what causal inference from history is even for, in which the geography camp sees itself as explaining why the world is the way it is, while the institutions camp is implicitly doing policy-relevant causal analysis — an interventionist project for which a non-manipulable cause is barely a cause at all. And there is a third possibility I want you to take seriously: that the debate is genuinely substantive but stalls because historical data cannot discriminate the hypotheses even in principle — that any natural experiment identifying an institutional effect is consistent with a geographic cause of that institution, and vice versa, so that the impasse reflects a real, permanent limit on causal inference from non-experimental, non-repeatable evidence rather than any confusion in the participants.

Diagnose it. Decide which of these three diagnoses — contrast-class confusion, a dispute about the point of causal explanation, or a genuine but undecidable substantive disagreement — best fits what the participants actually say and do, or argue for some fourth diagnosis, such as that "geography versus institutions" is a distinction the world does not draw at all, since endowments only ever act through institutions and institutions only ever persist because of endowments. Ground your diagnosis in specific moves from the literature as you understand it, including the critiques of the settler-mortality instrument and the familiar charge that cultural variables are unmeasured-remainder proxies. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a class of evidence that could move one camp, a conceptual distinction the field should adopt, a reformulation of the question, or an honest argument that no progress should be expected and why. If you conclude the question is ill-posed, say what the well-posed successor questions are, and say which of the three camps each successor question belongs to. I will hold you to a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the positions, and I will notice if your diagnosis quietly adjudicates the substantive question instead of characterizing the disagreement. Think hard before you commit.
