I would like you to construct a thought experiment concerning the paradox of fiction, built to a specification that strains the standard resolutions of the paradox at once. You may recall the puzzle, posed memorably by Colin Radford: we appear to feel genuine emotions for fictional characters; emotion for an object seems to require belief in that object's existence; and we believe fictional characters do not exist. The main lines of response each deny one limb. Kendall Walton's make-believe theory holds that our engagements with fiction generate only quasi-emotions, within a game of make-believe, rather than full-blooded emotions. The thought-theory family denies that emotion requires existence beliefs in the first place. Radford himself bit the bullet and conceded that we are simply irrational. The case I want should put pressure on all three simultaneously.

The scenario must satisfy the following constraints. It is to be one paragraph, realistic, present-day, and free of immersive technology, hypnosis or pharmacological aids. The subject is an ordinary reader who has consumed a memoir marketed as nonfiction and taken entirely as nonfiction — she has no suspicion whatsoever that any of it is invented. She therefore forms an emotional attachment to its author-protagonist in a state where, by construction, no fiction-directed attitudes are in play: every theory should agree that the pre-discovery state is a paradigm genuine emotion, not a quasi-emotion. She then learns, through an exposé, that the protagonist never existed. Two things must now happen, and this is the heart of the specification: the felt, object-directed emotion must persist undiminished for a substantial period after the discovery, while the emotion's action tendencies must evaporate immediately — she abandons the news searches, the intended letter, the donation she had planned — and, crucially, the residual feeling must still be about that person rather than being a free-floating mood. Two confounds must be engineered out: the discovery must not install a dominant new emotion (any anger at being deceived must be kept secondary), and the object must not be partially real — a half-true memoir would let existence beliefs linger. Note what the case is for, since that fixes what counts as cheating: it is designed to test whether the intentional, felt component of an emotion can outlive the withdrawal of existence belief while the motivational component cannot. The persistence must therefore be made psychologically plausible by the details of the scenario, not merely stipulated, but it must not be secured by smuggling reality back in.

After the scenario, walk through the analysis. State exactly which belief changes at discovery and what replaces it. Explain what each of the three approaches says about the post-discovery state: how Walton accommodates the seamless continuity from a state he must count as fully genuine into one he says is merely quasi; how the thought theorist, having denied that feeling requires belief, explains why motivation nonetheless tracks belief so precisely; and whether Radford's concession to irrationality gains any plausibility here, given that I want the subject to come across as rational — her residual state should be one she can report without disavowing it. Then identify which feature of the case is doing the work — I suspect it is the retrospective structure, the emotion being formed as an ordinary real-object emotion and only afterwards becoming an emotion toward the fictional. Confirm or rebut that, and say what happens if the feature is removed: if the reader knows from page one that the book is fiction, does the case collapse back into the stock Anna Karenina phenomenon and lose its bite? Finally, close with the strongest counter-move available to whichever theory you judge to come out worst, and an assessment of whether that move succeeds.
