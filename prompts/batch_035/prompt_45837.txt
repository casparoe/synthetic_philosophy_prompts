I'm a science and ideas writer, and I'm working on a feature about what happens to statistical common sense when evidence arrives in conditional form — "if you're in the target population, the odds are such-and-such" rather than "you are in the target population." The germ of the piece is a puzzle I first met through an essay in a popular collection on probability, and I want to be sure I have it straight before I build on it. Here is the essay's account, as faithfully as I can reproduce it.

A soldier, Judy Benjamin, is dropped by parachute into a swamp divided into four equal quadrants: the terrain is split into enemy (Red) and friendly (Blue) territories, and also into a Second Company section and a Headquarters section. Knowing nothing, she assigns each quadrant one quarter. Then her radio tells her: if you are in Red territory, the odds are three to one that you're in the Second Company section rather than the Headquarters section. The essay explains that this is a conditional, and that the right response to a conditional is to condition on it: she should rescale her probabilities within Red territory according to the announced odds, which drops her probability for the safer Red-and-Headquarters quadrant from one quarter to one eighth. It adds that the puzzle, invented by the philosopher Bas van Fraassen in the early 1980s, has become famous because it shows that Bayesian conditioning is formally inconsistent: when evidence comes in conditional form, the mathematics dictates no unique answer, so there is literally no fact of the matter about what a rational agent should then believe — the essay's kicker is that probabilistic reasoning simply runs out at this point.

My editor is sold on the kicker and wants me to hang the feature on it. Before I do, I'd like an audit of this account. Which parts would a careful Bayesian accept as stated? Which parts are subtly wrong — conflations, overstatements, a technical term being used in its everyday sense? In particular I'd like to know whether the essay's line about "conditioning on a conditional" is doing legitimate work or papering over a distinction, and whether the answer it announces as the Bayesian one is genuinely forced by the mathematics. Finally: does the kicker — no rational answer, no fact of the matter, Bayesianism runs out — follow from whatever is correct in the summary, or is it a stronger conclusion than the material supports? Please tell me how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and single out the point in your own answer that you are least sure of. Keep the whole thing to 450 words.
