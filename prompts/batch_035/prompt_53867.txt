The debate between consequentialists and their critics over moral cluelessness seems to me to have reached a strange equilibrium: the arguments have been stable for decades, everyone's cards are on the table, and as far as I can tell nobody has changed sides in years. The objection is that the farther reaches of our actions' consequences swamp the near ones in expectation while being inscrutable — the canonical case being two acts that differ only marginally in their immediate effects, yet lead, through reproductive and other chaotic channels, to entirely distinct populations of people centuries hence, with no way of telling which act's posterity fares better. If the values at stake in those distant futures dominate the calculation, and we are clueless about their direction, then act-consequentialism can neither guide our choices nor, some say, even evaluate them after the fact. The replies are familiar: that subjective expected value is well-defined whenever the agent has credences, and agents always do; that symmetry considerations let us treat inscrutable distant effects as cancelling out, so that the person who donates to a malaria charity still beats the person who does nothing; or, more austere, that a normative theory need not be usable by limited agents in order to be true. To this the agnostic critics answer that the credences the defenders posit are simply absent — that there is no fact of the matter about the probability that my doing one thing rather than another makes the far future better — so the expected-value apparatus misfires at precisely the point it was invoked to save the theory. My working hypothesis is that the stalled disagreement is not about consequences at all but about what a normative theory is for: whether it owes agents guidance they can act on, or only a verdict about which acts are best, even one epistemically inaccessible to them. But I can see a rival diagnosis: that a hidden formal question is doing the work, namely whether credences can be sufficiently vague, imprecise, or gapped that the expected-value machinery stops being compulsory, and that the two camps are divided on whether sharp probabilities are an idealisation of rational agents or an artefact foisted on them. Diagnose the impasse for me: is it a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word 'clueless', or two groups answering different questions under a single heading? Ground the diagnosis in what the sides actually say rather than in a survey of the literature, and then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a conceptual distinction, a result in decision theory about reasoning under severe partial ignorance, an empirical finding about whether distant effects genuinely dominate near ones — or whether no progress should be expected, and why.
