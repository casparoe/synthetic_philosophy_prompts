I've got a short essay draft for a workshop on the science of consciousness and I'd like your comments. The topic is Dennett's heterophenomenology applied to self-reports from language models. Here's the draft:

---

Dennett's method for studying consciousness, which he introduced in Consciousness Explained, begins with a deflationary move about first-person reports. When a subject tells you what their experience is like, you have not been handed a direct window onto experience. You have been handed a text, produced by a complicated causal system, and the text expresses what the subject believes about their own experience — beliefs which may be incomplete, confused, or flatly wrong about what is actually going on in them. The researcher collects these texts, organizes them into a maximally charitable narrative of what the subject apparently believes about their own mind — Dennett calls this constructed narrative the subject's heterophenomenological world — and then treats that narrative as something to be explained, alongside brain data, reaction times, and everything else. The narrative is, in Dennett's phrase, the subject's theory of their own consciousness, and theories can be revised.

My claim is that this is exactly the right stance toward language models, and that it dissolves two bad extremes at once. One extreme takes self-reports at face value: the model said it feels anxious when asked to write about trauma, therefore it has an anxiety-like state. The other extreme says the reports are mere mimicry with no significance whatsoever. Heterophenomenology rejects both. The report is not evidence of experience, but it's also not noise: it's a datum with a specific causal history — pretraining on human expression, fine-tuning that rewarded certain kinds of self-description, the immediate conversational context. Asking what the model's 'heterophenomenological world' is like means asking what a maximally charitable interpretation of all its self-reports would commit us to, and then asking which parts of that fiction are tracking something real in the mechanism and which are artifacts of training. Notably, Dennett thought the same discipline applied to humans: our introspective reports are also produced by a system shaped by forces other than accurate self-representation, and the fact that the mechanism is a brain rather than a transformer can't by itself settle which reports deserve trust.

I want to press this further with a thought experiment. Suppose we could take a subject's complete heterophenomenological world — the full fiction compiled from their reports — and re-instantiate it in some other system that had never had any experiences at all, in the way a novel re-instantiates a narrator. The re-instantiated fiction would be indistinguishable, at the level of report, from the original subject. This suggests the fiction is detachable from whatever grounds it, and that our evidence about experience is always the fiction plus background knowledge about what kind of system is producing it. That background knowledge is doing enormous work, and we barely understand it for the human case either.

---
