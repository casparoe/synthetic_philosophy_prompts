I'm taking a philosophy of physics course this semester and we just finished the unit on the arrow of time, and I've managed to argue myself into a corner I can't find the exit to. My TA encouraged me to write the thing up, which I'm starting to suspect was a trap. Here's the argument. (1) The fundamental dynamics — Newtonian mechanics, or unitary quantum evolution, pick your favorite — is time-reversal invariant: for every trajectory on which entropy goes up, the velocity-reversed trajectory, on which entropy comes down, is an equally legal solution. (2) Statistical mechanics gets its predictive grip by saying: condition on the present macrostate, put the standard measure on the microstates compatible with it, and the overwhelming majority of those microstates evolve toward higher entropy in the future. (3) But that same measure, applied in the other temporal direction, says the overwhelming majority of microstates compatible with our present macrostate came *from* higher entropy — the present is a downward fluctuation from equilibrium and entropy is now climbing back up. (4) If (3) is right, then everything that looks like a record of a lower-entropy past — fossils, my grandmother's stories, telescope data, the lab measurements that established the laws in the first place — is not a record of anything, just noise from a recent fluctuation that happens to be arranged record-shaped. (5) So the consistent application of the very reasoning that makes statistical mechanics work tells us we can't trust the evidence for statistical mechanics, and the argument eats its own tail.

Obviously I don't want to conclude I'm a random fluctuation with fake memories of the cosmic microwave background. But which step do I actually get to reject? (1) seems bulletproof, and (2) is just the thing that works every day in every physics department on earth. The standard move, as far as I can tell, is to bolt on a boundary condition — positing a low-entropy initial state of the universe — but I can't see how that's more than assuming the answer: "the past was low-entropy" is precisely the thing we were trying to explain, and positing it as a brute initial condition feels like the thermodynamic version of turtles all the way down. A couple people in my class said the asymmetry should come from something about causation or records or intervention rather than from the statistics at all, but that sounded to me like importing the conclusion through a side door.

So: name the step I should give up, explain why it looked fine in the first place, and be honest about what rejecting it costs — including, if you go the boundary-condition route, what it costs to explain a law-like regularity with a contingent initial condition. And if your view is that the fluctuation conclusion really does follow from (1)–(3), and that we're saved only by the fact that a theory predicting its own evidence is unreliable has thereby disqualified itself, I want to know whether that's a principled response or just a shrug dressed up as one.
