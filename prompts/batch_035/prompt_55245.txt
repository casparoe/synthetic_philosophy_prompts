Having spent a career in statistical physics, I have lately turned to philosophy and its adjacent territory in theoretical computer science, and I have become preoccupied by a question I could not quite pose in the physics vernacular. In my working life we constantly reasoned as if independent what was not independent: mean-field theory neglects correlated fluctuations and works beautifully above the upper critical dimension while failing quietly below it, and the broader culture of Fermi estimates, dimensional analysis, and random models of complicated systems rests on treating unknown quantities as if they were uncorrelated — a presumption we could never justify in advance, only check after the fact. There is now a line of work, coming out of the AI alignment community, on formalizing "heuristic arguments" and in particular the presumption of independence: the idea being to capture, in a formal system, the license to treat quantities as approximately independent without literal independence, much as error bounds formalize approximate computation. Write an essay weighing whether this project is coherent. Is the presumption of independence a genuine domain of validity that could someday be charted as we charted the applicability of mean-field theory, with failure modes as diagnosable as critical dimensions — or is it an irreducibly informal convenience, doomed to dissolve the moment one demands rigor from it? In weighing this, consider test cases of your own choosing, such as the random model of the primes, which is known to fail in specific and instructive ways, and the practice of derandomization, where pseudo-randomness sometimes stands in for genuine independence. Reach and defend a verdict; a carefully hedged conditional answer is acceptable, but do not end on an equivocal note.
