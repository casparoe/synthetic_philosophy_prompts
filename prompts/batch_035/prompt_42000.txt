My car's been making this clunking noise from the front left when I go over bumps, but only when it's cold, and I've been going back and forth with my father-in-law about it. My mechanic says it's the sway bar bushings; my father-in-law is adamant it's the strut top mount, and here's the annoying thing: every observation we've come up with so far — when it happens, when it doesn't, how it sounds from inside vs outside the car — fits both stories fine. He's recently got into philosophy of science (retirement hobby) and keeps telling me the evidence underdetermines the theory, so his view is exactly as good as the mechanic's, and honestly I can't immediately say why he's wrong. So I've been trying to work out what I actually think about underdetermination, going by my gut on cases:

Two hypotheses that fit everything observed equally well but would diverge if we got new evidence (say we could get the car up on a lift and poke around): genuine rivals, but until that new test happens, I think each is as likely as the other. Coin-flip territory.

But the mechanic's story is the known failure mode for this exact model with this mileage, and my father-in-law's is based on something he swears happened to a Ford Sierra in 1989, and my gut says the mechanic is way more likely even before any new test. Which doesn't sit well with the coin-flip verdict above, because it's not like we ran a new test in between those two thoughts.

Matrix mechanics and wave mechanics back in the 1920s, before anyone proved they were mathematically the same: people talk about that as underdetermination, but I don't think it counts at all — people thought there were two seperate theories, but really it was one theory wearing two coats. So sometimes "two hypotheses, same evidence" isn't even a case of two hypotheses.

Lorentz's ether theory vs special relativity, which as far as I can tell made all the same predictions at the time: I think physicists were right to prefer Einstein, and not as a coin flip they happened to win. But I can't see what, if not evidence, was doing that work — which is precisely the thing my father-in-law says can't happen.

And the theory my nephew offered, that the car makes the noise exactly when the gremlins under the bonnet get hungry, and they get hungry exactly when the car is cold: fits the data too, obviously doesn't get equal billing. Everyone just says "that's silly", but I'd like a better reason than that.

Is there a principle that captures all or most of this — something about when "the evidence fits both" does and doesn't leave two hypotheses genuinely on a par? My tentative stab is that evidence can't break ties but things like fit with background knowledge and track record can, though I'm not sure that doesn't just amount to relabelling "evidence" to include stuff my father-in-law would exclude from it. Please test my tentative principle against the cases, repair or replace it as needed, and tell me what the resulting principle says about a case I haven't mentioned: whether rival interpretations of quantum mechanics, all fitting the same experimental results, are coin-flip rivals or not.
