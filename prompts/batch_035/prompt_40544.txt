You are a professional philosopher of religion with a specialty in divine providence, libertarian freedom, and the semantics of counterfactuals, and you also care about film as a vehicle for doing philosophy rather than merely decorating it. I'd like you to write a careful reading of Frank Capra's It's a Wonderful Life (1946) through the lens of Molinism and the doctrine of middle knowledge — and I'd like you to begin with a short brainstorming pass before committing to an interpretation, thinking through at least three different ways the film could relate to the doctrine before settling on the strongest one.

For context: Molinism holds that God possesses middle knowledge — knowledge, logically prior to any decree of creation, of what every free creature would freely do in every possible circumstance, the so-called counterfactuals of creaturely freedom. This is supposed to reconcile meticulous providence with libertarian free will: God chooses which world to actualize knowing exactly how each free agent would respond in each situation. The standard objections you should keep in play are the grounding objection (what makes these would-statements true, if the agent never actually faces the circumstance?) and the would/might problem (perhaps there simply is no fact of the matter about what a libertarianly free agent would do — only what they might do — leaving God with less than Molinism claims).

The film's middle stretch is an almost perfect thought experiment for this: Clarence the angel shows George Bailey a world in which he was never born, and this counterfactual world is presented as fully determinate and richly specific — Bedford Falls has become Pottersville, and the lives of people George touched unfold in exact detail. Here are the questions I want your reading to take seriously, and I'd like you to add others of your own:

1. Whose counterfactual is this, and whose knowledge is being dramatized? The counterfactual world depends not only on George's absence but on the free choices of many other agents — Mary's withdrawn life, Violet's trajectory, Ernie's and Bert's fates, Harry's death without George's rescue. On Molinist terms, Clarence is displaying a web of true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom concerning dozens of agents. Does the film, by making this vivid and particular, function as an intuition pump against the grounding objection — suggesting that there really is a fact of the matter about what Mary would have done — or does it smuggle in the conclusion by fiat, since a screenwriter wrote Pottersville rather than any free agent generating it?

2. Is Clarence's presentation the closest world without George, or just one world among many? Molinists distinguish worlds God could actualize from the world God does actualize; a critic could say the film stacks the deck by showing the worst plausible counterfactual. Does the film acknowledge anywhere that other versions of the no-George world are possible, and does that matter theologically — since on Molinism, our world is itself the one God selected from among alternatives?

3. George's role in the actual world looks structurally like the Molinist picture of providence: his small, seemingly insignificant free choices (staying in Bedford Falls, delaying his honeymoon, guarding the deposit) turn out to be load-bearing for an entire town, a web of dependence no one could have predicted. Is the first half of the film an argument that the value of free action is invisible to the agent at the moment of choice — and does that support the Molinist claim that only a being with middle knowledge could see it?

4. Where does the film resist the Molinist reading? George is shown the counterfactual and responds to it — he isn't merely an object of God's knowledge but an agent whose freely chosen despair and freely chosen return are, on the film's own account, what matter most. Does the ending, with its prayers affecting events, fit better with a simple foreknowledge view or even with open theism, where God genuinely interacts with and responds to free decisions, than with the static logical order Molinism posits?

Please be faithful in both directions: don't reduce the film to an illustration of a scholastic debate, and don't distort the doctrine to fit Capra's sentimentality. Identify specific scenes and story beats that do the most philosophical work, say plainly where the analogy breaks down, and close with your assessment of whether the film dramatizes something about counterfactuals, providence, and the hidden significance of ordinary choices that the technical literature on middle knowledge manages to obscure even while getting it right. Around 1200 words is fine, and please structure the essay with brief headings rather than answering my numbered questions one by one.
