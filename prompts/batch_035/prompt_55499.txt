I'm writing a term paper for a graduate seminar on the politics of memory, and my topic is the question of whether comparing atrocities — ranking, relating, situating historical evils alongside one another — is a legitimate intellectual operation or something structurally obscene no matter how carefully it's done. The case study I want to use is the West German Historikerstreit of 1986–87, but I keep getting stuck on a suspicion I'd like you to help me work through: that everyone in that dispute was doing several quite different things under the single word "comparison," and that the meta-question gets muddled unless those are pulled apart.

Let me seed the facts as I understand them so we're working from the same base. In May 1985, Reagan and Kohl held a commemorative ceremony at the Bitburg military cemetery, where Waffen-SS members are buried, and this was widely read in West Germany as a step toward "normalizing" the Nazi past. Against that backdrop, in June 1986, the historian Ernst Nolte published an essay in the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung arguing that the Holocaust was not unique in the way his critics claimed: the Nazi crimes should be understood as part of a European civil war, causally downstream of Bolshevik terror — a defensive and imitative, if escalated, response to what the Soviets had already done and what Germans feared was coming. Habermas and others responded that this was relativization in the service of restoring a positive German national identity, and the argument ran through the German press for the better part of two years, with one of its live questions being, explicitly, whether the Holocaust ought to be studied comparatively at all. Roughly a decade later, the same fault line reappeared in France: the Black Book of Communism (1997) drew a storm of criticism over its editor Stéphane Courtois's introduction, which placed communist and Nazi crimes on the same moral scale and tallied communism's dead at on the order of a hundred million — a figure attacked as inflated even by the book's own contributors, some of whom publicly distanced themselves from the comparison.

Here's the analysis I want, and it should run in both directions. First, through the lens of the theory: distinguish the different operations that "comparison" can name — causal explanation (common structural causes of mass killing), scale-fitting (placing one regime's dead count against another's), contextualization (situating one atrocity in a century of political violence), and competitive ranking (implicitly or explicitly asking whose dead matter more) — and locate Nolte's move precisely on that map. Was his sin the comparison itself, the causal conclusion he drew from it, or the function it served in Kohl-era West Germany? Then in the other direction, tell me what the episode shows about the anti-comparativist position: doesn't the symmetry problem cut against it, since a uniqueness claim is itself a comparative predicate — Habermas's side was comparing constantly, just to a different effect? Finally, I want an honest account of what a non-obscene comparison would have to look like: what norms it would need to satisfy, who bears the burden of justification, and whether the direction of the comparison matters (deflating the Holocaust to relativize German guilt versus elevating communist crimes to relativize nothing). And is the 1997 Black Book case the same structure, a mirror image, or genuinely different — and if it's different, does the difference vindicate the view that it was never comparison that was obscene, only apologetics?
