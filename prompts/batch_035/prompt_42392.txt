I'm assembling a satirical product-launch email for a fictional startup, and I want the philosophy to be load-bearing rather than set dressing.

The background: Samuel Scheffler, in *Death and the Afterlife* (2013, based on his Tanner Lectures at Berkeley), argues that much of what we value in our own lives — our projects, our loyalties, even our day-to-day emotional investments — depends on a background assumption that we rarely notice we hold: that other people will go on living after we die. Scheffler calls this the "collective afterlife." His thought experiments are meant to bring the assumption into view: in his "doomsday scenario," everyone on earth dies shortly after you do (though you live out your natural life), and in his infertility scenario, everyone remains healthy but no children can be conceived. His claim is that for many of us these scenarios would drain the point not just from our grand projects but from ordinary pursuits — saving for retirement, medical research, caring about how things turn out — more thoroughly than our own mortality does. The assumption is largely non-occurrent: it does its work in the background, without being consciously entertained or believed with any effort.

The startup: call it Continuity (or a better name if you find one). It sells, by subscription, the assumption that humanity will continue after the subscriber's death. The satire's target is not Scheffler but our complacency about the assumption he describes: we insure compulsively against personal death while the collective afterlife goes entirely uninsured, and a venture-backed company would be delighted to sell us back the confidence we already had.

Write the piece as a product-launch email or landing page with a tier table and an FAQ, 650–800 words, in a deadpan, upbeat Silicon Valley voice. Suggested tiers: a free tier pegged explicitly to the doomsday scenario (humanity ends a few weeks after you — no, the free tier can't be upgraded later); a mid tier guaranteeing a couple of generations of onward rush; a premium tier with indefinite continuation and "legacy analytics." The humor must come from the actual structure of the conjecture, so please work in, without lecturing: (1) that the product runs in the background with "zero cognitive load," since the assumption was never consciously held anyway; (2) that what is being sold is other people's futures, not the customer's survival — the terms must state plainly, in fine print, that the subscriber still dies, with no refund scheduled for that event; (3) that customers cannot tell whether the product works, because the assumption was presumably doing its work all along before they subscribed — the FAQ should field this complaint and answer it with startup aplomb rather than resolving it; (4) an infertility-scenario tier or add-on, for customers whose unease is specifically about the onward rush stopping rather than stopping soon; and (5) one testimonial from, say, an oncologist who found her research pointless for the months she doubted anyone would survive to benefit from it, and who now credits her subscription. The piece should stay deadpan throughout, and it should never state the philosophical objection that we're paying for something we already had; the reader should feel it.

Fairness constraint: the exaggerations should be of features Scheffler actually claims or explores, not invented ones, so that someone who knows the book finds the satire accurate rather than a strawman. Where the piece bends his view, it should bend it visibly — the free tier, for instance, can caricature the doomsday scenario's role in his argument as a device for revealing the assumption, not as a prediction.

Since a satirical piece has no verdict to give, append a short note (roughly 150 words) doing the nearest thing: identify where the piece takes liberties with the conjecture — for example, whether Scheffler claims the assumption is necessary for our activities to have value or merely that it matters to us in ways we don't notice — which jokes lean on a contested reading, and which single consideration a defender of the conjecture would most likely invoke to say the satire misses the point.
