The exam question was: "Economists say a rational agent ignores sunk costs. Is honoring sunk costs always irrational? If you think there's a case where it's rational, give one and defend it."

Here's one student's answer:

"Sunk costs are costs that have already been paid and can't be recovered, so a rational decision should look only forward at marginal costs and benefits going ahead. The fallacy, made famous by behavioral economists like Richard Thaler, is letting what you already spent influence what you do next. But it's not always irrational to honor sunk costs. Take a student two years into a PhD deciding whether to quit. Since they've already sunk two years into it, quitting would waste those years, and that is a real cost of quitting, so finishing is the rational choice. So honoring sunk costs is sometimes rational. Also, Kahneman's research on regret shows that in the long run people regret the things they didn't do more than the things they did, so the student who quits will regret it more later, which is another reason honoring sunk costs is rational in this case. Finally, any agent that honors sunk costs can be money-pumped by a clever trader, since its preferences are inconsistent, so the strict view has to say it's irrational -- though this mainly shows that our standard of rationality is too narrow."
