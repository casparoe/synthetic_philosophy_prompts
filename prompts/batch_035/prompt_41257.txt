I'm a high school senior taking an elective called Great Philosophical Ideas, and over winter break I read a pop-philosophy book I picked up in an airport, written by a science journalist rather than a philosopher. It has a chapter on what it calls "the strangest idea in twentieth-century philosophy," and I've been turning it over ever since. Here is how it summarizes David Lewis's modal realism, as best I can reproduce the passage:

'Lewis's idea is that every way a world could possibly be is a way some world really is. These possible worlds are not stories or pictures in the mind — they are concrete physical places, every bit as real as our own world, just unimaginably far away, so distant in space that no signal or traveler could ever cross the gap. On these worlds, history plays out every alternative it logically could: on one of them you became a dentist, on another Napoleon won at Waterloo, and on a third everything is exactly as it is here except that one coin somewhere came up heads instead of tails. Since all these worlds are equally real, Lewis concluded that our own world is nothing special — just one suburb of a vast multiverse — and that which world you wake up in is pure luck. Most philosophers today regard the whole scheme as a fascinating dead end, since positing unobservable worlds to explain everyday talk of "might" and "could" is exactly the sort of extravagance that science forbids.'

Later the chapter draws a moral that I found genuinely unsettling: if Lewis is right, then whatever you might have done, you did do, in some other world — so regret is irrational, because you did become the dentist, just not here — and our world has no claim to being the real one. I'm inclined to trust the journalist's picture, since it's vivid and the rest of the book seemed careful, but a friend who has read actual philosophy insists the chapter "gets Lewis wrong in ways that matter," and I don't have the background to referee between them.

Could you go through the summary carefully and tell me which parts a philosopher who studies Lewis would accept, which are simplifications a defender might grudgingly let pass, and which parts Lewis himself explicitly denied — and then whether the chapter's later conclusion about regret follows from whichever parts are accurate?
