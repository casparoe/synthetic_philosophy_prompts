I'm a graduate student writing the position memo for a decision theory proseminar, and I've hit a snag that has suddenly stopped being academic. Last month my mother tested positive for the Huntington's disease expansion, and the genetic counseling center has offered me predictive testing. Whether I take the test has become tangled up with the very commitments I'm supposed to defend in the memo, and I've realized I can't tell whether the package I've written down even hangs together. Before I circulate anything to my cohort, I'd like an audit of the following, which is as honest a statement of where I stand as I can manage:

(1) I accept Good's result on the value of information — the principle of total evidence: for an agent with a fixed utility function who updates by conditionalization and maximizes expected utility at the point of choice, receiving a truthful, costless message about the state of the world can never lower her expected utility, assessed ex ante in expectation over what the message might turn out to be. I find the proof convincing and accept its scope conditions as stated.

(2) I believe this result supports a general norm of rationality: one should be a free-information taker — never pay to avoid evidence, never decline a truthful answer to a question one has deliberated on, never forgo a costless observation. I take this to follow fairly directly from the theorem.

(3) I think Allais-type preferences are not irrational, and I reject the independence axiom as a requirement of rationality; rationality, in my view, does not demand full conformity to expected utility.

(4) I think Ulysses was fully rational, and not merely prudent, to bind himself to the mast and stop his crew's ears: an agent can correctly arrange in advance to be deprived of information she knows her later self will handle badly by her current lights.

(5) I one-box in Newcomb's problem, I would pay in the counterfactual mugging if I had committed ex ante (and I think I should so commit), and more generally I believe the rational choice at a node is sometimes dictated by the policy I would have chosen before the node, even when that means declining to condition on information in hand. I suspect there are cases in which I would rationally pay never to receive a signal at all.

(6) In the absent-minded driver problem, I hold that the driver would rationally pay for any device that told him which intersection he was at, and I take this to raise no issue for anything above: self-locating information is simply information, and information, on my view, helps.

(7) I think the value of information is to be assessed ex ante relative to the agent's current probabilities: an agent who regrets, after the fact, a test whose result came back badly has not thereby made any rational error.

My cousin, who has read roughly half of this, says the package is fine because (1) is a theorem and the rest are just applications of it. My advisor says the package is not obviously consistent but wouldn't say more. I have gone through the list several times myself and each time the inconsistency, if there is one, seems to slip away just as I think I've located it. Which of these commitments are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and how severe is each tension? Please rank the genuine ones by severity, say for each what would have to give and what giving it would cost me, and tell me whether the single cheapest revision would clean up the whole list. I'd also like to know, if you can say, whether anything here blocks or licenses taking the test — though I recognize that is the memo's problem and not mine.
