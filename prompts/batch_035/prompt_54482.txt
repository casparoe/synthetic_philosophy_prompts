I've been reading a lot of popular history lately, nothing serious, documentaries and a couple of fat paperbacks -- and I keep running into something that feels like it must have a name. Over and over, the story a country or a people tells about itself, the one that gets the monuments and the holidays and the school lessons, isn't a victory. It's a disaster. The British go misty over Dunkirk, which was a lucky escape from a beating. The Serbs built basically their whole national identity on a battle in Kosovo in 1389 that they lost. Jewish tradition treats Masada, a fortress that fell with everyone inside dead, as this huge symbol. And in the American South there was a whole intellectual industry built around the "Lost Cause" -- they were proud of losing. And here's the part that really gets me: when I dig in a little, the actual event is often smaller or stranger than the myth. At Kosovo apparently that battle wasn't even the decisive one, and the Serbian state carried on for decades after. Dunkirk only worked because the German army paused and let most of them get to the boats. So its not like these groups are celebrating genuinely glorious near-wins. It's closer to: the more completely the thing failed, the better raw material it makes.

And there's a twist I can't put my finger on. It seems like the defeat ends up doing a job a victory couldn't. It hands the group a moral identity -- we are the noble ones, the world did us wrong, our day is coming -- without ever having to deliver the actual political result, the kingdom, the secession, whatever it was. Nobody has to compromise or share power, because the story is about purity, and purity is only really pure when you lost. So the defeat quietly becomes the cause. Is there an established concept for this exact mechanism? I know "lost cause" is a term, but it seems stuck to the specific Confederate thing, and "founding myth" doesn't fit either because founding myths are usually about winning. What I want is the general move where commemorating a defeat substitutes for the cause succeeding.

If there is no established term, coin one and define it properly, because I don't just want a label. Draw the edges: does it count when the group eventually did win later, like a war of independence that started with a rout and ended fine -- or does later success kill the phenomenon? Does a defeat that's commemorated mainly as a "never again" warning, the way some countries treat WWII occupation, belong inside this or is that a different animal? And please don't just hand me a list of candidate names and leave me to choose. Pick the label you actually think fits best, say why it beats the others, and tell me straight if you think my "substitution" idea is overreading the cases.
