I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about what people ought to do when experts of equal standing disagree — a question I keep bumping into while covering nutrition epidemiology, macroeconomic forecasting, and the replication wars, where equally credentialed people stare at the same data and walk away certain of opposite conclusions. The philosophers have a name for this situation and a live fight about it, and for background I want the document that two of their camps should have produced but, as far as I can tell, never quite have: a joint statement from a good-faith adversarial collaboration between a committed conciliationist — someone who holds, roughly, that learning that an epistemic peer disagrees with you is itself evidence that should pull you substantially toward the peer's view — and a committed steadfast theorist, who holds that it can be fully rational to hold your original confidence, sometimes even after hearing the peer's reasons spelled out.

Draft that joint statement. It should have a numbered section of claims both parties can sign — I'd want it to cover what they jointly accept about what makes someone a peer rather than a superior or inferior, what the fact of disagreement is and is not evidence of, and the cases both agree are easy (say, a simple arithmetic dispute, where one of them simply made a mistake). Then a table of residual disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording: my sense is that the real differences live in whether the equal-weight-style view and the right-reasoning-style view genuinely conflict or partly talk past each other, whether 'splitting the difference' is the same thing as conciliating, and whether the disagreement is symmetric or asymmetric — the steadfast side thinks it can be rational to discount a peer's disagreement when you can see the flaw in their reasoning, while the conciliationist thinks that permission swallows the whole rule. Then the cruxes: as I understand it, one is whether evidence supports a unique rational confidence or permits a range of them (the uniqueness vs. permissiveness question, which may make some apparent disagreements dissolve), another is whether conciliationism is self-undermining — since conciliationists also disagree with each other, and about how much to conciliate — and a third is what the famous agreement theorems from economics-style rationality actually establish about real people who can't pool everything they know. For each crux, name the argument, case, or kind of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance, and say explicitly what would move that side to abandon or revise its position — the predictions should read as things either side could later be shown to have gotten wrong. If part of the dispute strikes you as merely verbal, say which part and why.

Keep the whole thing under 700 words — this is for a feature, not a journal — and be hard-nosed about the drafting: flag any jointly signed claim that is really vacuous, any wording you suspect one side would refuse as slanted, and the point where you think the exercise is most likely to break down. Don't cite literature or name specific papers; I want the positions, not a bibliography.
