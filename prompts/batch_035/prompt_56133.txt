A pop-psychology book I picked up for a long flight — it was recommended by a friend doing a clinical psychology PhD, and its thesis is roughly that religion is not going away, so the New Atheists and their ancestors got the prognosis wrong — devotes a whole chapter to Freud as the founding figure of the view that religion is pathology. The chapter's summary of Freud goes something like this: Freud saw religious belief as a collective mental illness. On his account, faith is a delusion in the clinical sense — the believer is out of touch with reality, holding convictions that contradict the evidence, so religion is something to be diagnosed rather than debated. The belief originates in infantile helplessness: adults, like small children facing a world they cannot control, project a cosmic father figure modeled on their own fathers, and because the projection springs from need rather than evidence, Freud took himself to have shown that religious claims are false. His diagnosis was sweeping — every believer in every tradition counts as neurotic, and religion as a whole is the obsessional neurosis of humanity, complete with compulsive rituals and guilt over forbidden impulses. And Freud was sure that as science and education advanced, this neurosis would dissolve, and humanity would exchange illusion for the stern consolations of reason, with psychoanalysis itself hastening the transition. My friend says this chapter is basically accurate, and the book uses it to argue that since religion has not in fact withered away, Freud's whole account stands refuted.

Something about it struck me as subtly off — I dimly recall that Freud drew a careful distinction somewhere between two words that the chapter seems to run together, and I also have a vague memory that Freud admitted somewhere that his own arguments might be motivated by wishes, which would be awkward for a man declaring everyone else's beliefs wish-fulfillment. But the book is by a professional science writer with a clinical background, and I don't know enough to say where it goes wrong. Please go claim by claim through the summary: which parts would a historian of psychoanalytic thought accept as faithful, which are simplifications a Freudian might tolerate, and which are genuine misrepresentations? In particular, I'd like you to be clear about what exactly Freud meant when he classified religion in the relevant category, whether he thought that classification settled the truth or falsity of religious doctrines, whether he really held that every believer is neurotic, and whether his actual prediction about religion's future was as confident as the chapter makes out. Finally, does the book's conclusion — that the persistence of religion refutes Freud — follow from the parts of the summary that are accurate, or only from the parts that aren't? Please answer in American English.
