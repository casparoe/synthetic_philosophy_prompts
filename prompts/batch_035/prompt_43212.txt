I'm putting together my own views on what redistribution the state owes people, and I've found that arguing in terms of slogans ("equality of opportunity," "from each according to ability") gets me nowhere, so I've started from my gut verdicts on concrete cases instead. Here they are. A woman born with a chronic condition whose treatment costs half her income: the state should fund it entirely. Two people of equal talent, one of whom chooses a lucrative career she dislikes and the other a beloved but badly paid craft: the resulting income gap is fully just, and no topping-up is owed. Someone who cultivates a taste for champagne and now finds ordinary food intolerable: no subsidy, obviously. A man who was offered actuarially fair insurance against disability, read and understood the terms, declined as a deliberate gamble to save the premium, and was then disabled: no compensation — he had his shot and took his odds. His neighbor, same gamble, same outcome, except that he grew up in genuine chaos, has never once opened an insurance envelope, and "declined" only in the sense that the paperwork sat on his counter: here I want the state to pay. And a lifelong smoker who ignored decades of clear warnings and develops lung cancer: I want her treated at public expense, but with some higher copay or premium, not a free pass and not abandonment.

What I'd like from you is the principle, or the short list of candidate principles, that best captures this pattern, with each clause tied to the verdict it earns. I can see that several views are in the running: something like pure luck egalitarianism on which only outcomes traceable to choice are uncompensated; a version with a sufficiency floor that rescues anyone below basic subsistence regardless of responsibility; a hypothetical-insurance view under which we ask what coverage a prudent person would have bought on fair terms if offered; and a view focused less on the choice itself than on whether the chooser's deliberative process was itself a product of luck. Please say precisely where these come apart, and be honest about which of my verdicts each one has to overturn — because I suspect the smoker verdict and the gambler verdict sit badly together (both made informed choices, yet I want the smoker mostly rescued and the gambler not at all), and I'd rather know which of the two to give up than hide behind an ad hoc distinction.

There are also two cases I haven't listed, and I'd like you to predict my verdicts on them and explain how each candidate principle settles them differently. First: someone who would gladly have bought the fair insurance but was never offered it — no insurer would cover his risky occupation — and is then injured; compensation or not? Second, the one that keeps me up at night: a reckless driver, fully informed about the risks he was taking, who crashes, is paralyzed, and now needs lifetime care costing more than he could ever have insured himself for. If my no-compensation verdict on the gambler is right, does consistency really require leaving him to whatever charity provides, or is this where the responsibility story breaks and some floor takes over — and if so, what becomes of my verdict that the craft-lover deserves no topping-up, since that verdict seemed to rest on the very same responsibility idea?
