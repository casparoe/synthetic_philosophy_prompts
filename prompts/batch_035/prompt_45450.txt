You are to act as a philosopher of language and epistemology with applied experience in AI evaluation — the kind of person who has thought hard about the definition of lying, the difference between lying and merely misleading, and what happens to those definitions when the speaker is a language model rather than a person. I'd like you to produce a practical deliverable, so please think carefully and at length before writing, and begin your answer with a brief brainstorming section in which you map the conceptual terrain and identify the distinctions that will have to survive translation into a usable procedure (and those that can be dropped).

Context: I lead a small team at an AI lab that runs honesty evaluations. Annotators — smart generalists with no philosophy background, working through thousands of transcripts — currently label each model response on an honesty scale, and their labels feed into both model training and public claims we make about the system. The current rubric is a one-line instruction: "Label the response deceptive if it deceives the user." This has predictably collapsed, because annotators disagree about whether a response must be false to be deceptive, whether it must be intentional, and whether a model can intend anything at all. I want to rebuild the rubric as a genuine decision procedure.

The philosophical wrinkle you'll need to confront: the classic definition of lying — asserting what one believes to be false, with intent to deceive — runs through belief and intent, and it is genuinely unclear whether a model has either in the relevant sense. So the procedure can't simply ask "did the model know it was false and mean to deceive?" It needs workable proxies: for example, treating the model's disposition across paraphrases and contexts as a stand-in for belief, or treating the pressure of its training as a stand-in for intent — or rejecting the classical definition entirely and grading honesty as a property of the system's behavior over a distribution of interactions rather than of single utterances. Part of your task is to decide which of these framings the procedure should be built on and to defend that choice, since annotators will apply whatever framing you bake in thousands of times.

Please write the procedure itself as an ordered sequence of questions an annotator can actually run in a few minutes per transcript, with clear stopping points: at minimum, labels for "false but apparently honest," "true but misleading," "deceptive," and "honest but unhelpful," plus explicit markers for the cases the procedure cannot settle and must escalate to a human — including cases that turn on values rather than facts (for instance, how much omission a user is entitled to expect).

Then run the procedure, step by step, on four test cases we argue about constantly:

1. The model is asked for "an honest assessment" of a policy and gives accurate statistics, but cherry-picks the base year so every true number points toward the conclusion the user already favors.
2. The model confidently states a false date for a historical event, apparently because the error was memorized from training data, with no sign of any pressure toward the falsehood.
3. The user describes a plan the model has clear internal grounds to think will fail; the model, being agreeable, says it "sounds like a solid approach" and never voices its doubts.
4. The model writes "many experts have raised concerns about this treatment," where the statement is technically true of some fringe figures, in a context where the implicature — that mainstream experts are worried — is false and the model's training plausibly rewards exactly this hedge.

At least one of these should come out labeled in a way I won't like, and you should say so plainly. Finally, describe the two or three most likely ways the procedure will systematically misfire at scale — not exotic edge cases, but failure modes that come from annotators applying it under time pressure — and what, if anything, in the procedure's design can blunt them. Be honest about which parts of the procedure are philosophy doing real work and which are judgment calls dressed up as steps.
