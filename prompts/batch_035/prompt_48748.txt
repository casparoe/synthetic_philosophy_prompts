Got into a two hour argument with my friend about the private language argument last night and we ended up exactly where we started, which made me realize the whole debate has basically been parked in the same spot since the 50s. She reads Wittgenstein as having an actual argument: the diarist who tries to record a sensation with the sign S can't so much as succeed or fail at meaning the same thing by S each time, because nothing could count as getting it right, and a word that can't in principle be wrong isn't functioning as a word. My problem is that this only seems to land if you've already bought something like verificationism or a use-theory of meaning for sensation talk -- if you think inner ostension latches onto a determinate something, the argument looks like it begs the question. Her comeback is that that reaction is itself the picture Wittgenstein is diagnosing, so I've just illustrated his point, which strikes me as the argument being unfalsifiable in a suspiciously convenient way. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it partly verbal (did anyone ever literally claim a language in principle unteachable, or is the target a fantasy nobody holds?), is it about whether philosophy is even allowed to offer theses (therapeutic readings where there's no argument at all vs. Kripke's reading where there's a full-blown skeptical paradox), or is it a real substantive fight about mental content that's just being conducted with awful vocabulary? Diagnose it using what each side actually says, not a survey, and tell me whether anything -- a sharper thought experiment, a distinction, maybe input from cognitive science on whether inner states are stable/structured enough to ground sameness of meaning -- could genuinely move it, or whether the two sides are just doing different projects under one name and we should stop arguing.
