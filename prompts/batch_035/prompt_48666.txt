I am a second-year philosophy undergraduate taking a seminar on free will and moral responsibility. Our set text is Dennett's Elbow Room, and our instructor treats its framing — that the real question about free will is which varieties of it are worth wanting — as a master key. Last week a classmate pressed the hard incompatibilist point that if our actions are products of causes we did not choose, nobody ultimately deserves blame or credit for anything, and the instructor waved this off on the grounds that the variety of free will the objection targets is not one worth wanting: losing it costs us nothing, while the varieties worth wanting are ones we plainly have on any scientific picture of ourselves. Something in that dismissal struck me as too quick, but I could not say what.

My suspicion is that the slogan compresses several distinct claims, running from near-trivial to substantive and disputable. For instance, it might amount to no more than a report about what the term "free will" ambiguously tracks; or to a claim about which capacities are in fact required for the practices of praise and blame; or to a normative proposal to refashion the concept so that the term survives determinism; or to something stronger still — a verdict that the desert-based worry never was a variety of wanting at all, whatever people who voice it say. There is also, I think, an unstated question about the standpoint of the wanting: whether it is the agent's own valuing that counts, or the theorist's, and whether wanting is even the right attitude — perhaps some capacities are worth wanting without being worth having, or worth having only in the way an appetite is.

Could you lay out, as a numbered list I can bring to seminar, the distinct precise theses the worth-wanting framing could be asserting, ordered from weakest to strongest? For each reading, please say whether it is plausible, and — separately — whether it actually supports using the slogan to close down the desert-based objection, since I can imagine readings on which the slogan is true but the dismissal still fails. Please also flag any reading on which the framing begs the question, quietly helping itself to a compatibilist conclusion under cover of a preference.
