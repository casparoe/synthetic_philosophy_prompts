I volunteer at a prison education program and I've been asked to lead one session (about 70 minutes, no required reading, whiteboard only) on the idea that evidence might not be enough to settle what's true -- underdetermination of theory by evidence. The guys in the group are sharp but have zero philosophy background; some have strong opinions about science being either completely solid or completely rigged, and honestly those two poles are exactly why the coordinator picked this topic.

Don't explain underdetermination to me, I know it. What I want is a plan for the session: what has to be established first, in what order the pieces need to come so the claim lands as interesting rather than as "so science is fake," and what should be deliberately held back for a later session.

A few things I want your plan to hit specifically:

1. The dependency structure. I can think of at least three things that could go in the first ten minutes -- the idea that theories face evidence as whole bundles rather than one claim at a time, the idea of empirically equivalent rivals, and the idea that induction itself has a gap in it -- and I genuinely don't know which one needs to come first and which one is actively harmful if it comes first.

2. The carrying example. It has to be something the guys in the room could have lived through or reasoned about themselves, not continental drift or something from a textbook. I'm inclined toward a forensic one -- like two different reconstructions of a crime that both fit every piece of physical evidence, where the difference only shows up in motive and mechanism. Is that the right kind of example, does it need any specific property to work (e.g., should the rivals disagree about something *testable in principle but not in practice*?), and is there a way it could backfire by making the whole thing look like it's just about criminal trials?

3. The misconception map. The ones I anticipate: (a) underdetermination means all theories are equally good so evidence doesn't matter at all; (b) it's just the same thing as "science has been wrong before"; (c) the stronger reading -- that for any theory there's ALWAYS a rival that fits the data, by construction -- is obviously true and uninteresting, rather than a substantive philosophical claim that people disagree about. Where in the sequence does each need to get cut off, and which one is most dangerous if it goes unaddressed for even ten minutes?

4. A way to tell at the end whether the idea actually landed versus them just nodding along. Something better than "ask them to define it back."

5. One question I should openly refuse to get dragged into at this level, and how to say I'm not answering it without seeming evasive -- my worry is the relativism stuff ("so truth is just whatever you can get away with") will come up in minute five and eat the whole session if I let it.

Also, if it matters for your plan: I'd rather the session end on what underdetermination does NOT destroy than on a tidy resolution, if that's a real choice to make.
