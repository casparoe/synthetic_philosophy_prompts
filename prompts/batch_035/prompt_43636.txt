I teach a small seminar on the ethics of belief, and every year we do the Clifford/James exchange the same way: Clifford's shipowner, James's defence of religious faith, some classroom heat, and then we move on to Descartes or whoever. This term it went differently. My students kept dragging in cases that don't sit in either camp cleanly, and when I tried to say which ones I actually thought were okay, I noticed my own verdicts were all over the place. So I've been writing them down, trying to work out whether there's a real principle somewhere in me or just vibes. Here are the verdicts. Believing my oldest friend is innocent of the fraud he's been charged with, months before any trial, on the strength of twenty years of knowing him: fine, maybe even required of me. An anxious student who talks herself into believing she'll pass an exam she keeps flunking in practice, where the belief itself steadies her enough to study properly: fine, and I'd probably nudge her towards it. A doctor who notices five patients doing unusually well on a new drug and starts telling further patients it works: not fine. The doctor who first suspected that medical students' dirty hands were killing new mothers and acted on the suspicion decades before anyone could explain the mechanism: fine, actually admirable — and I cannot for the life of me see how that's structurally different from the drug doctor. A manager who believes his struggling team is secretly excellent, conveys that confidence until it partly comes true, and ignores the numbers showing it isn't: not fine, though it's the same self-fulfilling shape as the anxious student. Letting myself expect the 10:40 will turn up even though it's late half the time: fine, barely even registers as a belief. And the referee who rejected my last paper — I cannot make myself stop believing he misunderstood it, even though on sober reflection he probably got it exactly right, and I don't feel one bit guilty about that one.

My tentative principle was roughly: believing ahead of the evidence is permissible when the belief helps cause the thing believed, and impermissible when acting on it shifts risk onto others. That sorts the anxious student from the drug doctor, and sort of handles the manager, but it stumbles badly on the hand-washing doctor, who absolutely shifted risks onto other people (his colleagues, their reputations, the mothers if he'd been wrong) and whom I want to praise, and it's not even true that his belief helped cause its own truth. I also keep going back and forth on the classic case: someone who talks themselves into religious belief partly on Pascalian grounds — safety if it's true, nothing much lost if false — which feels worse than the anxious student even though you could argue it's live, forced and momentous in exactly James's sense, i.e. precisely the kind of case he built his view to protect.

So, what I'd like from you: tell me whether there's a single principle that captures this pattern — some refinement of Clifford, or James with extra clauses, or maybe something about the difference between permissibility and blameworthiness that explains why the referee case doesn't feel guilt-worthy even though it's belief squarely against the evidence. State the principle (or two rival candidates) precisely, clause by clause, and say which of my cases motivates each clause. Then be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and which one I ought to give up — my own suspicion is that the hand-washing doctor is load-bearing and the manager isn't, but argue me out of it if you can. Finally, apply the principle to the Pascalian case and give me a verdict, because that's the one my students care about most and I genuinely don't know where I land. Two seperate things at the end: write the whole answer in British English, and don't feel obliged to be even-handed — I'd much rather get a worked-out view, even a defensible Cliffordian one that overturns half my list, than a survey of positions.
