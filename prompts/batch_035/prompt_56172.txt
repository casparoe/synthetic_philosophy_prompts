I'm a senior analyst at a foreign-policy think tank, and honestly a huge part of my job is analogies. Every week some editor or deputy director asks me whether X is "another Munich," whether we're "getting dragged into another Vietnam," whether this rivalry is a "Thucydides trap," and whether that framing belongs in the piece or the briefing. Sometimes the analogy is doing real work; sometimes it's just a mood. The problem is we have no shared way of telling which is which, so the arguement happens over vibes, and whoever invokes the most vivid image usually wins the meeting.

So please turn whatever philosophy of history and reasoning actually has to say about this into a procedure my team can run on any proposed analogy before it goes into a memo or a briefing. Questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure outputs one of: drop it, keep it but as rhetoric only (and flagged as such in our internal notes), or it's doing evidential work and here's what it can actually support. It should distinguish analogies used to predict (this will follow the same trajectory) from analogies used to warn (this could go the way of...) from analogies used to justify (we must act now so as not to repeat...) — I suspect those carry different burdens, and the procedure should say what the burden is for each. It should also separate wether an analogy is warranted from whether it's persuasive, since those come apart more than we like to admit and I only care about the first one for internal review.

Then run it on three cases. (1) A colleague wants to open a memo about a great-power rivalry over some new technology with the claim that we're in a Thucydides trap and that war is basically the default outcome. (2) Another wants to compare a limited military intervention to Vietnam on the grounds that it'll be popular at first and then grind us down over years — she cares about the trajectory, not the opening move. (3) I want to use Munich, specifically the lesson that appeasing an aggressor feeds his appetite rather than sating it, to argue against concessions in a negotiation. I'd like to know whether the procedure lets me get away with that, though I have a feeling it shouldn't, at least not the way I wanted to use it.

For context on constraints: these memos reach people who make actual decisions, we get a couple of analogy questions a week, and the whole check has to be runnable in about twenty minutes by someone who knows the history but has zero philosophy background. So the procedure itself should keep the philosophy invisible — the team will not sit through a lecture — but the distinctions underneath should be the real ones, not dumbed down to uselessness. And be honest about the spots where a judgment call can't be proceduralized and has to stay with a human.

Last thing: tell me where the procedure misfires. Specifically, are there cases where following it would have blocked an analogy that in hindsight turned out to be right, and if so, is that a bug or just the price of the discipline? I want to know what this procedure can't do before I sell it to my director as the answer to the vibes problem.
