I facilitate a monthly discussion group that meets in the back room of a bookshop -- about ten regulars, mostly non-specialists: a retired schoolteacher, a nurse, a software engineer, a lawyer who reads a lot of pop decision theory. We've done Newcomb's problem and one-boxing, basic expected value, and a session on Pascal's mugging that got a bit heated. Next month I've volunteered to lead a session on acausal trade, and I'm realizing it's the hardest thing we've attempted, because the whole point is invisible: nothing happens between the parties in any ordinary sense. For ninety minutes, whiteboard only, no math beyond expected value. Here's how I currently understand the idea, so you can correct me where I'm off: two agents stand in no causal relationship whatsoever -- no signal can pass between them, ever -- but each can model the other's decision-making, and each has a stake in what the other does. If each can benefit the other at some cost to itself, and each has reason to expect the other to reason in a similar way, then each may have grounds to act as if trading: to pay the cost, with no communication, in the hope of the other doing likewise, the whole arrangement hanging on the correlation between their decisions rather than on any influence one exerts on the other. The members who grasped one-boxing will be okay with the flavor of this; the ones who dug in their heels on causal reasoning will find it maddening.

Don't explain acausal trade to me -- I need a plan for explaining it to them. What has to come first, and what depends on what: should I warm up with Newcomb, or the prisoner's dilemma where you're told you're playing against a copy of yourself, or something else entirely, and what does each warm-up secretly import that I'd then have to undo? What carrying example should run through the whole session -- I've seen suggestions ranging from two civilizations in causally disconnected patches of an expanding universe, each able to cheaply advance the other's values, to agents in different simulations run by a common ancestor -- and what must the right example have and lack so that no causal channel sneaks in through the back door of the story? Then the misconceptions: I'm bracing for the software engineer insisting it's just ordinary game theory with correlated equilibria, the lawyer saying that without consideration there's no contract so the word trade is a fraud, and several people quietly concluding that information must be traveling somehow -- faster than light, or backwards in time, or through the agents' simulations of each other, which feels like a loophole the moment anyone says the word simulate. Where in the sequence should I head each of these off, and which are actually worth taking seriously rather than defusing? Finally: one question I can ask at the end that would show whether they've got the idea rather than merely tolerated it, and what I should openly refuse to cover in ninety minutes -- I assume anything like the formal machinery behind decision theories that take this seriously, and probably the more speculative applications, but tell me where you'd draw the line and how to say so without the group feeling like I've withheld the interesting part.
