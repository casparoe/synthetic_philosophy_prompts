I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy, and my supervisor is a committed particularist in the tradition of Jonathan Dancy: she holds that moral reasons are holistic, so that what counts in favour of an act in one case may count against it, or make no difference at all, in another. I have never managed a decisive objection to her, but last term I realised that the standard route to her conclusion, stated as a strict argument, appears to prove a great deal more than either of us is prepared to accept. I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis of it: which step should be rejected, why that step looked acceptable, and what rejecting it costs.

The argument as I have it:

(1) For a general claim to be a genuine moral principle, rather than a record of coincidences, it must hold good in every case that falls within its scope: if it says that a feature F counts in favour of an act, that must be so in each case in which F is present and the principle applies.
(2) For any candidate F, there is a possible case in which F is present, the principle applies, and yet F does not count in favour of the act: that the act would keep a promise does not count in favour of it when the promise was extracted by a threat to torture a third party unless it was given; that it would relieve pain does not count in favour of it when the pain is a punishment the person undergoing it has insisted on completing; that it would give pleasure does not count in favour of it when the pleasure is taken in a rival's humiliation.
(3) So, by (1) and (2), no general claim of this form is a genuine moral principle.
(4) Whatever is not a genuine principle can at most be a summary of what has usually happened, and a summary of what has usually happened has no authority over a new case.
(5) Therefore moral thought is not guided by principles: each case must be judged by discerning, within it, which features matter here.

My supervisor regards (1) through (5) as a fair statement of her position. What alarms me is that the pattern does not stop at morals. Substitute laws of nature: for any proposed law there is a regime in which its antecedent holds and its consequent fails — Newton's mechanics holds to a point and then gives way near very strong gravitational fields — yet nobody concludes that there are no laws of nature, only case-by-case prophecies. Substitute grammar: the generalisation that attributive adjectives precede their nouns in English fails for a handful of borrowings, yet English grammar is not on that account a record of coincidences. Substitute epistemology: any rule of belief-formation — ceteris paribus, trust the apparently reliable witness — can be defeated by an unnoticed bribe, yet we do not conclude that there are no epistemic principles and that each belief must be formed by discerning, in the situation, what counts in its favour. Run the same five steps in each domain and the argument sweeps away generalisation as such: no laws, no grammar, no canons of evidence. That is more than I am prepared to pay, and I suspect more than my supervisor realises she is committed to.

I therefore have three questions, in order of importance. First, which step of the argument do you reject — and does the same rejection cover the parallels, or must the moral case be handled differently from the rest? Second, is there a disanalogy that would let the moral case go one way and the others another — moral principles are supposed to guide action, whereas laws of nature merely describe, and it is not obvious that grammar guides at all — and if so, does the disanalogy in the end help the generalist or the particularist? Third, once the faulty step is rejected, what must be put in its place, and at what price? In particular, I want to know whether the generalist who repairs the argument owes us a way of distinguishing a genuine defeasible principle from one that has been gerrymandered into unfalsifiability — a 'principle' that holds except where it doesn't. If she cannot draw that line, I am not sure the repair is worth much.
