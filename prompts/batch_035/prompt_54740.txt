A tech newsletter I read had a piece last month on algorithmic fairness, and something about it felt off but I can't put my finger on what. The gist of it was: "Back in 2016, journalists showed that a criminal risk algorithm used in bail decisions (COMPAS) was flagging Black defendents as high-risk more often than white ones at the same actual reoffense rate. The company that made it replied that the scores were calibrated, meaning equally accurate for both groups, so no bias there. Then mathematicians stepped in and proved something much bigger: the two sides were both right, because the two fairness measures they were using are mathematically incompatible. This was the first of several impossibility results showing that no algorithm can be fair by every reasonable measure at once — pick any two fairness metrics and they will conflict. The exceptions (perfect prediction, or identical crime rates across groups) are useless in practice, so fairness is mathematically impossible, and auditing these systems for bias is mostly a waste of time since any audit will find a violation somewhere no matter what the algorithm does. That doesn't mean we give up — it means fairness is a political choice about which trade-off to make, not a technical fix."

I'm inclined to think the "impossibility" framing is at least sort of real because I've seen it a few places, but the "don't bother auditing" bit sounded dumb even to me. Can you go through the paragraph piece by piece and tell me which claims a fairness researcher would accept, which they'd wince at, and which are just wrong? And separately, does the political-choice-not-technical-fix conclusion at the end follow from whatever parts of the summary are actually true?
