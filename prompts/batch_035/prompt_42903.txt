Germany shut its last reactors in april 2023, in the middle of an energy crisis, and kept its lignite plants mining and burning the whole time. France next door, with the same climate laws and the same grid decarbonization targets, kept its fleet running and ordered new builds. I keep trying to figure out what theory of risk the German exit actually presupposes, and I can't make it come out consistent. Help me unpack it.

Here's the shape of the puzzle as I see it. Coal in Germany kills a lot of people every year, fairly predictably — particulates, mining, the rest — and the German state treats those deaths as an externality: something you price with carbon markets and manage downward over decades. A reactor meltdown would kill far fewer people in expectation, but it's treated as intolerable in principle — the response to Fukushima wasn't "tighten the safety case," it was "exit the technology." So the practice seems to presuppose that low-probability catastrophic harms are categorically different from steady statistical harms, some kind of maximin or threshold view where certain possibilities just dominate expected values. But the same state kept burning lignite, which feeds the one risk that's both catastrophic and irreversible (climate change), and kept doing it *while* shutting down zero-carbon plants. So the catastrophe-threshold reading seems to fail too.

So my questions: what view of risk — or of harm's distribution, reversibility, controllability — does the exit strictly presuppose, versus merely fit well with? Is there any coherent theory that licenses both the exit and the lignite, or is that just where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, with the real driver being something that isn't about risk at all (institutional trust, who visibly bears the danger, that sort of thing)? And the flip side — what would the policy have looked like if the presupposed view had been straightforward expected-harm probabilism instead? The France comparison seems like it should settle something, same evidence and opposite practice, but I can't tell whether it shows that risk theory underdetermines energy policy or just that France quietly presupposes its own view (state-run utility, domestic waste sites, decades of normalization).

One more detail that bugs me: they extended the last plants by a few months in winter 2022 when the gas situation got scary, then shut them anyway in April. What does that say about what the risk was a view *of* — the technology, or something more like the optics of the technology? If tolerance for catastrophe turns out to be negotiable when heating bills spike, is it really a categorical view of risk that the practice presupposes, or something else wearing that costume?
