I'm a philosophy professor who somehow got roped into co-teaching a seminar with people from the CS department, and the same fight breaks out every year when we get to AI safety. One side says the "competitiveness constraint" basically decides everything: if making a system safe costs anything at all in capability, speed, or money, then in any competitive market that cost gets competed away, labs will cut corners on alignment, and so meaningful safety is only affordable if you're a monopolist or you have hard external enforcement. The other side says the "alignment tax" isn't a fixed feature of the world but a cost of current techniques — that safety and capability aren't inherently opposed, and that you can in principle drive the tax to zero or below, at which point the whole racing-dynamics argument dissolves. Both sides agree on the game theory, both agree safety is worth something, and nobody has budged in three years. My working hypothesis is that this isn't really a philosophical disagreement at all but an unmeasurable empirical one about the sign and size of the tax, which both sides paper over with a priori arguments about idealized firms — but I'm not sure, since there's also a quiet normative split about how much risk a lab is entitled to impose on everyone else. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Diagnose it, and tell me honestly whether anything — a measurement convention, a worked example, a clearer distinction between the tax and the constraint itself — could actually move it, or whether my colleagues are just going to keep having this fight in front of my students forever.
