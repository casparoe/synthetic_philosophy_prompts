I advise a small foundation that funds work at the interface of philosophy of science and environmental governance. Our next cycle can support about two three-year projects — a postdoc or senior fellowship each, plus modest workshop money — and the board has settled on the Anthropocene debate as the portfolio's theme. For context: after the 2024 rejection by the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy of the proposal to formalize the Anthropocene as an epoch with a mid-twentieth-century golden spike at Crawford Lake, the term's formal fate within geology is in limbo, even as it keeps spreading through earth system science, the humanities, and policy language.

Help me decide which questions in this debate deserve the funding. The ones I can see include at least the following, though tell me if I'm missing a better candidate:

1. The definitional question: when the Anthropocene began — the 1610 Orbis spike associated with the Columbian exchange, early-industrial dates, the mid-twentieth-century radionuclide signal — or whether it is better classified as a diachronous event than an epoch at all; and what kind of dispute this is: an empirical one about the rock record, or a conventional one about the rules of chronostratigraphic classification.
2. The jurisdictional question: who should decide whether and how the term is formalized — stratigraphers working through the golden-spike procedure, earth system scientists who have used the term informally since Crutzen popularized it, or the historians and social scientists who worry about what the term does once it enters politics.
3. The normative question: whether formalization helps or harms environmental politics — the "good Anthropocene" optimism of the eco-modernists versus critics who hold that a geological-epoch framing universalizes responsibility for changes driven overwhelmingly by wealthy societies.
4. A more metaphysical one: whether this whole dispute should be read monistically, as a disagreement about what the world is like, or pluralistically, as legitimate classificatory schemes serving different interests.

What I want is not a survey but a defended ordering. For each project you would fund, explain what makes it worth three years of someone's time rather than one good article: whether it is upstream of the others, so that progress on it changes what the others can even ask; whether it is tractable with philosophical and historical methods rather than field geology; and whether it is neglected, since some of these questions already attract crowds of competent people. Please also tell me which of the alleged stakes are overrated — for instance, would it really make much difference to policy or public understanding whether geologists formalize the epoch, or is that largely symbolic? And close by saying what developments over the next few years — a revival of the formal proposal, further spread of the term into law and education — would move an item up or down your ranking.
