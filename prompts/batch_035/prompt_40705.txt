I'm taking a political psychology course built around moral foundations theory, and last week our study group got into an argument I can't stop thinking about. One member, who is politically conservative, was presenting Haidt's framework: roughly five evolved foundations — care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and sanctity/degradation — that function like taste receptors, detecting morally salient features of situations and triggering intuitions. He then noted the familiar finding from the American survey data: liberals rely almost entirely on the care and fairness foundations, while conservatives respond to all five at roughly comparable strength. From these premises he derived what he called an "epistemic advantage" argument for conservatism, and though the rest of us pushed back, none of us could say exactly where it slips. Here it is, as he formulated it: (1) The moral foundations function as perceptual receptors for morally relevant features of the world. (2) In contemporary Western populations, liberals exhibit strong sensitivity to two foundations, while conservatives exhibit sensitivity to all five. (3) A perceiver with more functioning receptors is in a better epistemic position with respect to the domain those receptors cover than a perceiver with fewer — someone who sees and smells the wine is better placed than someone who only tastes it. (4) So conservatives are in a better epistemic position with respect to moral questions than liberals. (5) Ceteris paribus, a party in a better epistemic position has beliefs more likely to be true. (6) So on contested moral questions, conservative positions are more likely to be true than liberal ones.

The details of the exchange that I want your help with are these. When a libertarian in the group pointed out that Haidt himself had to add a sixth foundation, liberty/oppression, to capture people like her, our conservative friend said this only strengthened the argument: more foundations, more receptors, more moral perception. When someone else raised the is/ought gap, he replied that his argument never crosses it — (3), he insisted, is not a moral premise but an epistemic one, of the same kind we would accept about eyesight or hearing. And when I pressed him on where it stops, he cheerfully granted that it commits him to saying that any society which moralizes more domains than ours — one that treats obedience to the state, or ritual purity of bloodlines, as moral matters — would ipso facto have more access to moral reality than we do. He regards that as a cost he is happy to pay; I regard it as the moment the argument started proving too much, but I can't locate the flaw rather than just the smell of one.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep (1) and (2) if possible, since they look like ordinary empirical psychology, which makes (3) the natural target — but I want to know exactly what the wine analogy smuggles in, whether the friend is right that (3) is epistemic rather than moral, and whether rejecting it means giving up anything we want to say about perception generally. And tell me whether the argument can be repaired in a weaker form: our friend's fallback is that even if the reasoning doesn't settle who is right, it at least shows that conservatives see more of the moral terrain. Does even that modest version survive, or does it collapse back into the same mistake?
