I teach mostly ethics and the history of philosophy, and I am preparing a general-education course that touches on the philosophy of physics. In the course of my reading, I have come across the following characterisation of the Humean view of laws of nature in a popular book on the foundations of physics, and I would like a careful audit of it, since the contemporary metaphysics of science lies well outside my area:

"On the Humean picture, the world is just a vast mosaic of particular local facts — this particle here, moving that fast — with no necessary connections binding them together. Laws of nature do not make anything happen; they merely summarise the patterns we happen to find in the facts. Nothing forces the apple to fall, and nothing in today's facts guarantees anything about tomorrow, so for the Humean the success of prediction is a piece of cosmic luck rather than a reflection of how things must go. Since the laws are nothing over and above the facts, they could have been different while everything in the world stayed exactly the same. Modern Humeans dress this up with the so-called best system analysis: the laws are whatever axioms appear in the deductive system that best balances simplicity and strength in systematising the facts. But this only makes matters worse, because simplicity is relative to us and to our language, so the laws end up mind-dependent — a conclusion so unacceptable that most philosophers of science have abandoned Humeanism. The rival view takes laws seriously as genuine necessities that compel events, which is what physics presupposed all along."

How much of this should I trust? Please go through the passage piece by piece and tell me which claims a contemporary Humean in the Lewisian tradition would accept, which they would accept only with heavy qualification, and which are straightforward misrepresentations. I have two specific suspicions I would like you to address. First, the claim that the laws could have been different while the facts stayed the same strikes me as in tension with the Humean's own insistence that the laws are fixed by the facts; have I understood this correctly, or is there a reading on which the book's claim is defensible? Second, is the charge of mind-dependence a knock-down objection, a live point of debate that Humeans have tried to answer, or a confusion about what the best-system analysis is meant to be relative to?

Beyond the audit, the book draws a further conclusion: that if Humeanism were true, the predictive success of science would rest on faith rather than knowledge. Does that conclusion follow from whichever parts of the summary survive correction? Finally, since I will need to present both sides fairly to my students, please tell me whether the summary's portrait of the anti-Humean alternative — laws as necessities that compel events — is itself accurate, or whether it papers over real disagreements among anti-Humeans, for instance about whether the necessities in question are contingent or hold as a matter of essence. I am not looking for citations or a reading list; a clear account of the positions and the arguments is what I need.
