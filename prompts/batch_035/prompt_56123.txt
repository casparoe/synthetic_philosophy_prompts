I spent the better part of four decades as a physical oceanographer, mostly on the slow dynamics of thermohaline circulation, and since retiring I have been working through the historians. I have just finished Braudel's Mediterranean, and I keep returning to his division of history into three durations: the nearly motionless time of geography and social structure, the intermediate rhythm of conjunctures -- economies, states, the slow rise and fall of prices and empires -- and the short time of events, which he treats as the surface agitation of a sea beneath which the real currents move. As someone who has spent a career on seas of exactly this kind, I find the analogy almost embarrassing in how well it seems to fit, and I would like to make something of it. In dynamical systems theory we have a standard apparatus for multiple timescales: fast variables relax to quasi-equilibrium in the field of the slow variables, so that on the long timescale the fast variables are effectively functions of the slow ones -- the fast motion is causally real in the moment but leaves no independent trace on the slow trajectory. Read this way, Braudel's dismissal of events looks like a theorem. But the same theory has well-understood exceptions: near a bifurcation point of the slow system, the separation of timescales degrades, critical slowing down sets in, and small fast fluctuations can push the system across a threshold it was already approaching. This suggests a half-formed idea I would like your help developing: perhaps a "great event" in history is precisely a fast fluctuation that happens to act on a slow system near such a threshold, so that the event-centered historian and the longue durée historian are not rivals but are each blind to what the other sees -- one to the gradient, the other to the fact that gradients occasionally concentrate into conditions where perturbations matter.

What I would like from you is a proposal for a substantial essay -- think of a long journal article or a book chapter, written for readers in both history and the sciences -- rather than a defense of any conclusion. Please state the question precisely, in a way that could in principle be answered; explain why the obvious existing positions leave it open (on one side the structural historians for whom events are epiphenomenal, on the other the event-centered and biography-centered historians, and somewhere in between the people who insist contingency and structure are "dialectically related," a phrase that has always struck me as a way of not answering the question); lay out what the argument would actually do and what evidence or case material it would need, given that I am a scientist and cannot simply become a credentialed historian of, say, the seventeenth-century crisis; and be specific about what would count as the project failing -- including the possibility that the dynamical-systems framing is decorative, a metaphor doing the work a metaphor cannot do, and how one would tell.

Two things I want you to take seriously as you draft this. First, the proposal should press on the middle level, the conjuncture, which seems to me the least theorized of Braudel's three durations: I suspect it is exactly the region where the timescales are not cleanly separated, where the slaving of fast to slow weakens, and that calling this a distinct level of history rather than a failure mode of the separation is where the real conceptual contribution might lie. Second, be honest about scope: I am a retired man with a good library and no laboratory, so tell me which parts of this ambition are realistic for one person in, say, eighteen months, and which parts would require collaboration with an actual historian that I do not yet have. Do not supply a bibliography or citations; I will fill in references myself, and I am more interested in the structure of the argument than in the literature review. A former colleague of mine dismisses all of this as physics envy, and while I do not believe that is what it is, I would rather the proposal were built so as to prove him wrong than built so as to avoid the question.
