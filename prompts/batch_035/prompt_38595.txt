I'm about four-fifths of the way through a draft of my novel and I've written myself into a corner that I suspect is actually a philosophy corner, so I could use help from someone who thinks about this stuff for a living. Quick setup: it's 1919, my narrator is a British deserter hiding out in Damascus. Early on, an old fortune teller in the souk tells him, very matter-of-factly, that she has seen how he dies — and she names the place, a specific courtyard in the old city, which she describes down to the lemon tree. He laughs it off, but in the world of the novel her predictions have a real track record: everything else she forseen for other characters came true exactly, and my narrator knows this about her. Much later, he gets word that a man he betrayed during the war has tracked him to Damascus and is coming to kill him. So: does he run to Aleppo that night, or does he stay?

Here's why I'm stuck. If she can genuinely see how he dies, then surely the death she saw already takes his decision into account — if she saw him dying in that courtyard, then fleeing is exactly the sort of thing that puts him there (the killer follows, or the road does it, or he turns back). But if he treats her vision as fixed no matter what, then nothing he does matters, which sounds like a reason to just do whatever he pleases, including staying put and facing the man with the gun. And there's a third voice in my head whispering that the whole setup is rigged: if the prediction really is infallible, then "what should he do?" might not have an answer at all — which would be a cop-out ending for a novel, but might just be the truth of the situation. My agent wants the ending where he flees because it's more dramatic, my writing group is split about fifty-fifty, and I keep flip-flopping depending on my mood.

What I need is not a polite tour of how different people might think about this — I want a committed answer. Should he flee or stay, and what does the decision look like from the inside, given what he knows about her track record? If you think the setup only supports one ending, tell me which one and why, and be willing to say outright that my agent's preferred ending is the wrong one for this book. If you think there's a version of the fleeing ending that stays coherent, spell out exactly what would have to be true in the story's world for it to work (what the prediction is actually tracking, how the infallibility is doing its job). Take a position and defend it — I can always overrule you, but I want to be overruling an actual verdict, not choosing from a menu. And please write your answer in American English.
