I sit on the ethics advisory panel for a national health service in a mid-sized country — think a place with one public insurer, a fixed budget, and a formal process for deciding what gets covered. Over the next few years we're going to get flooded with borderline cases and everyone on the panel keeps reaching for the therapy/enhancement distinction as if it settles something: GLP-1 agonists prescribed for people who are technically not obese, stimulants for healthy adults who just want to focus, embryo selection for non-disease traits, cochlear implants and gene therapies that arguably don't just restore function but overshoot "normal," prophylactic drugs for people with risk factors but no symptoms. Half the panel thinks the distinction is philosophically bankrupt, the other half thinks it's the only thing standing between us and covering whatever the loudest patient groups ask for. I want your help thinking through where the real work is.

You're a professional bioethicist who's spent decades on medicine and human enhancement — please don't give me a survey of the standard debate, I've read all that. What I want is a prioritization: which *open* questions in this area actually matter for coverage decisions like ours, ranked, with the ranking defended. Some candidates to consider (and feel free to add others): whether the distinction can be rehabilitated in some modest form (statistical normalcy vs. species-typical design vs. functioning-based accounts) or whether we should ditch it and argue directly about need, opportunity, and cost-effectiveness; whether some enhancement benefits are *positional* — where if everyone gains, nobody's better off, like a kind of arms race — and whether that changes the justice story compared to non-positional gains like healthspan or pain relief; whether "normal species functioning" can even survive as an anchor once germline editing makes the statistical baseline a moving target we partly choose; and whether the right frame is individual liberty (let people buy what they want privately, keep it out of the public basket) or equality of access (subsidize enhancements for the worse-off the way we subsidize education). For each question you prioritize, tell me why it's upstream of the others, whether it's tractable through conceptual work alone over a couple of years or really needs empirical input we don't have, and what would count as progress.

Also, and this is important: tell me which questions in this debate are overrated — the ones people love to argue about that wouldn't actually move a single coverage recommendation if they were settled tomorrow. I'd rather we spend our panel's limited attention on two or three things that genuinely change what we advise. Start with some brainstorming before you commit to the ranking, think carefully, and be willing to say the distinction itself should be deprioritized if that's where the reasoning leads — just defend it against the obvious rejoinder that politically we can't just ignore it.
