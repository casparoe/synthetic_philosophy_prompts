My team at work has this slow-running argument about how cautious AI assistants should be, and my coworker Talia keeps winning rounds with what she calls the locksmith analogy. Her version: a locksmith doesn't ask why you want into your own house. She doesn't run a background check on your reasons, doesn't refuse because you seem upset, doesn't worry the spare key might enable a bad habit — she does a minimal check that you plausibly have a claim to the door (and often not even that, per her) and then does the skilled job you're paying for. Expert service to a competent adult, zero paternalism. Talia says this is the correct model for AI assistants: help with what's asked, refuse only what's illegal or literally impossible, and stop the whole apparatus of evaluating whether a request is good for the person making it. She thinks an assistant that hedges or refuses on things like drafting an angry letter to a landlord, writing a persuasive essay the user will submit as their own, or explaining lockpicking is playing nanny in a way no actual service professional would recognize, and that the nanny behavior is mostly corporate ass-covering dressed up as ethics.

I want the analogy actually audited rather than just cheerled or dismissed, since I half buy it and can't tell which half. Can you give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the locksmith, the door, proof of ownership, the trade license, the shop policy reserving the right to refuse service, the fact that the customer is standing right there in front of you — then the places the mapping breaks. I'm especially interested in which disanalogies matter for her conclusion and which don't: the locksmith sees one customer at a time across a counter, can size up whether it's a drunk teenager or a harried homeowner, carries personal liability and a local reputation, and works in a trade whose norms took a century and a bunch of lawsuits to settle. The assistant gets a couple of ambiguous sentences from an unknown party, at a scale of billions of requests, where it also can't tell the savvy user asking about lockpicking for their hobby from someone with very different plans. Do those differences actually overturn the "just do the skilled job" conclusion, or only trim it — e.g. maybe the locksmith picture survives for requests where harm requires a lot of downstream steps, and fails only near the refusal-happy end Talia is complaining about? And if the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, is there a better-fitting professional comparison (the pharmacist? the lawyer who's a zealous advocate but not a pawn? the hardware store clerk who sells rope and doesn't ask), or does the honest answer end up somewhere no single profession sits?

One more thing: as you go, flag how confident you are in each major judgment and tell me which point in your audit you're least sure of — I'd rather know where the reasoning is soft than get a tidy verdict that pretends it isn't.
