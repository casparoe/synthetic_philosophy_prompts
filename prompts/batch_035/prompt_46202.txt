Setup in case I'm mangling it: imagine two AI systems (or an AI and a human) bargaining over some resource. Each can make commitments — binding, irreversible decisions — and there's a strategic advantage to committing early and hard, e.g. publicly locking in "I will never accept less than 70%," so the other side rationally backs down. But committing early also blinds you: you can't react to new information, and you might lock in a threat that both of you would be better off never having made. The worry is that overly eager committers end up in wars of attrition or mutual destruction that a more cautious agent would have avoided — but an agent that never commits gets walked over, so "just don't commit" isn't obviously the answer either.

My question: what's your credence that "commit early and hard" is actually optimal in expectation for agents like these, versus some moderate policy that staggers commitments or caps how much you can lock in? Decompose it — how much turns on whether agents can read each other's source code, how much on whether threats can be verifiably rescinded, how much on whether the situaton is one-shot or repeated, plus any other terms you'd add. Give a rough probability for each with one line of justification, then combine. And the part I care about most: keep straight what's actually settled game theory / decision theory versus what's speculative extrapolation by people worrying about future AI — I've noticed people slide between those without saying so, and I don't want you presenting some LessWrong-ish take as if it were textbook. Flag which parts of your decomposition are the speculative ones, and tell me which single sub-question would move your number the most.
