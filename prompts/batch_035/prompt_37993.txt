My labmate, a cheerful and absolutely unbudging expected-value maximizer, has an argument that our entire animal-welfare charity budget should go to insects — and then, in the very next breath, to entities even she hesitates to describe out loud. It runs as follows. (1) Whether a being's suffering counts morally depends on its phenomenology, not on its taxon or substrate. (2) We cannot rule out insect sentience; researchers who study invertebrate nervous systems genuinely disagree, so we should assign it some small nonzero probability — she says take 1% to be conservative. (3) By some estimates there are on the order of ten quintillion insects alive at any moment, and human agriculture and pest control kill comparably vast numbers of them, often in ways that would be agony in any creature with a functioning nociceptive system. (4) Expected suffering is population times probability times severity, so at 1% probability the expected quantity of insect suffering exceeds the expected suffering of all vertebrate livestock by many orders of magnitude. (5) Therefore expected-value reasoning requires that essentially all animal-welfare resources be redirected toward insects — humane insecticides, crop-choice reform, and the like. (6) And the same computation applies wherever numbers are astronomical and sentience is merely possible: nematodes in the soil, plankton in the oceans, and, given premise (1)'s substrate neutrality, the vast numbers of software agents already being spawned in industrial-scale simulations, each with some tiny nonzero probability of experience. (7) So nearly all moral effort should be aimed at beings whose inner lives we cannot presently investigate at all. She accepts the conclusion without flinching.

When I pushed back that we have no idea how to help insects, she replied that the argument concerns what matters, not which interventions work — priority-setting comes before efficacy. When I complained that ten quintillion times a 1% probability feels like a trick with numbers, she asked which factor I reject: the tiny probability, the huge population, the multiplication, or premise (1). And that's exactly where I get stuck, because each ingredient looks defensible on its own — moral status shouldn't track taxon, serious people do disagree about invertebrate consciousness, and the arithmetic is trivial. Yet the conclusion seems to prove too much: it turns any system that is merely numerous and merely possibly sentient into a moral black hole that swallows everything else we care about.

Tell me where this argument breaks, if it does. Is it invalid — is something smuggled between the steps, some equivocation in how "expected" or "possible" is being used that the premises don't license? Or is it valid, and the real question is which premise is cheapest to give up? I'd most like to keep both the thought that moral status doesn't track biological pedigree and the thought that you cannot conjure astronomical obligations out of microscopic probabilities — but I suspect I may have to choose, so I want to know precisely what each choice costs, both within this argument and for the rest of my moral picture.
