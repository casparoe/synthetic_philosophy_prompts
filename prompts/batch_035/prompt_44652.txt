I'm taking a seminar this semester on decision-making under moral uncertainty and my term paper (roughly 6000 words, due in about ten weeks) is going to be about the importance/tractability/neglectedness framework that effective altruism types use for comparing causes. The standard pitch is that you score a cause on three more-or-less independent dimensions and multiply or otherwise combine them to get a rough priority ranking. My half-formed worry is that the three factors aren't independent at all, and in a sneaky way: neglectedness is partly a *consequence* of tractability, because tractable problems attract money and attention, so a cause can look maximally neglected precisely because everyone who looked at it concluded it was intractable (or not important). If that's right then the framework doesn't just give noisy estimates, it systematically favors causes that are neglected for bad reasons — the correlation between the factors isn't random noise you can average out, it's a selection effect.

There's a second, related knot I want to fold in: who counts when you measure how "crowded" a cause is. If a government or a for-profit is pouring resources into something, that's not neglected in the everyday sense, but from the point of view of an altruist with different values it might still be wide open, and conversely a cause can be full of well-meaning actors whose work partly cancels out (two lobbying efforts pulling opposite directions means lots of "work done" and roughly zero progress). So neglectedness seems to need a reference class of agents, and the choice of reference class can flip the ranking.
