I'm a high school senior taking an elective in epistemology, and I'm preparing a presentation on whether practical stakes can affect whether someone counts as knowing. I've written six pairs of situations, each involving the same person holding the same belief on the same evidence, but under two levels of stakes. For each pair, please give a rating from 0 to 10 of how much the difference in stakes should plausibly shift the verdict on knowledge — where 0 means the stakes make no difference (if the person knows in the low-stakes version, they know in the high-stakes version too) and 10 means the low-stakes version is clear knowledge while the high-stakes version is clearly not. Rate the size of the shift, not how likely the belief is to be true, and please don't just say "it depends": the comparative ordering across my cases is the point of the presentation, so I need numbers that could come out different for different pairs, with a sentence of justification for each.

After the ratings, please do three further things. First, identify which pair was hardest to rate and explain what made it hard. Second, take the pair you rated highest and consider whether the shift you found is best explained by the subject's practical situation (the version of the view usually called pragmatic encroachment, on which whether someone knows depends in part on what is at stake for them) or by a change in the conversational context of the person doing the attributing (the contextualist idea that "knows" itself shifts its standards with the attributor's situation) — that is, say whether it matters to your verdict who is asking about the knowledge claim and why, and what would have to be added to the case to pull the two explanations apart. Third, tell me whether any of my pairs could be tweaked so that the stakes rise sharply and yet the person still knows, because the evidence is overwhelming — and explain what such a case would show against a crude version of the view that raising stakes always destroys knowledge.

The six pairs:

1. Mara believes the bakery on Oak Street closes at 7 p.m. Her evidence: she looked at the bakery's website once, last week. Low stakes: she wants a croissant after practice and would just be mildly annoyed if it were closed. High stakes: she has promised to pick up the cake for her sister's wedding reception this evening, the bakery is a forty-minute drive away, and there is no other bakery that could produce a replacement in time.

2. Dev believes the 6:12 train runs on Saturdays. His evidence: he checked the official timetable app this morning. Low stakes: he is meeting friends for lunch and the next train is twenty minutes later. High stakes: he is traveling to a scholarship interview that has no rescheduling option, the next train is three hours later, and missing it means losing the interview entirely.

3. Priya believes a particular dish at a new restaurant is vegetarian. Her evidence: the waiter, who seemed attentive, told her it contains no meat stock. Low stakes: her guest avoids meat out of preference and would simply be disappointed. High stakes: her guest avoids meat for religious reasons, and serving them the dish would be a serious matter for the guest and an embarrassment for Priya, though no physical harm is possible.

4. Luis believes his passport is in the desk drawer at home. His evidence: a fairly vivid memory of putting it there last month, and he has had no reason to move it since. Low stakes: he plans to renew it next week at leisure. High stakes: his flight leaves in two hours, he is at home right now, and the desk is in the next room, so checking takes ten seconds — but if he is wrong, he misses the flight and it cannot be rebooked cheaply.

5. Nora believes the rope will safely bear the relevant weight. Her evidence: a qualified instructor inspected and tested the setup within the last hour, in the same conditions. Low stakes: the rope is holding up a hammock in a garden. High stakes: the rope is what a person will hang from, at a height where a failure means serious injury. In both versions the setup and inspection are identical.

6. Sam believes the answer to a moderately involved arithmetic problem is 84. His evidence: he worked it out by hand twice, getting the same result both times, and the two calculations were independent. Low stakes: it is a homework problem. High stakes: he is volunteering for a charity and the figure goes into a document that, if wrong, would misdirect a real sum of money owed to someone.

Please treat the pairs as fixed: if you think a pair is badly constructed for this purpose, say so briefly, but still give it a rating. I would also appreciate, at the very end, one candid sentence on which single feature across all the cases — strength of evidence, cost of being wrong, cost of checking, or the kind of belief involved — did the most work in your ordering.
