I run a monthly philosophy night at a local bar — about a dozen regulars, sharp people but no formal training — and next month I've rashly promised them a session on the internal/external reasons debate. My confidence comes mostly from a four-hour argument I had last weekend with my friend D., which ended with both of us more entrenched and me less sure I understand the terrain than when we started. The trigger: D.'s uncle, a lifelong gambler who has burned through his savings and his family's patience, and who says, quite calmly, that he has no reason to stop — it's his money, he enjoys it, the losses don't touch anything he cares about. D. said that he plainly *does* have reasons to stop, whatever he says, and that a theory that denies this is a theory about nothing. I took the internalist side on instinct, then found myself conceding more and more strange things to keep it up — at one point I was arguing that the uncle has a reason because if he knew the facts and reasoned well, he'd see he had one, and D. pounced: so the theory wins every case by stipulating that anyone with a "real" reason would be moved by it, which makes it unfalsifiable and, worse, quietly smuggles in the externalism it claims to reject. We never even got to blame, which I understand is where the real trouble lies.

Here's what I want from you: don't give me the explanation — give me the teaching plan. Fifty minutes, a bar, non-philosophers, and I want to know (a) the order the pieces have to go in — what has to be established before the distinction can even be stated so it doesn't collapse into the difference between strong and weak reasons, or between reasons and motives; (b) the one carrying example that should bear the whole session — and I'd like your view on whether the obvious candidates are wrong: the amoralist strikes me as too loaded for an opening move since the audience will just hear "monster" and stop listening, and Owen Wingrave, Williams's own soldier example, needs a plot summary before it does any work. D.'s uncle is available and has the virtue of being real to these people, but I suspect he arrives pre-contaminated by the argument I just lost; (c) the misconception map — I can already predict half of them (that external reasons are just very strong reasons; that internalism means "if you don't care, nothing's wrong"; that "sound deliberation from one's existing motivations" is a get-out-of-jail card, which is D.'s pounce and the one I least know how to answer) — but I want to know *where in the sequence* each one will form and where it should be headed off, not just that it exists; (d) the check: one question I can ask at the end of the night that would show they actually have the distinction rather than a vague feeling of having been argued at; and (e) what I should openly refuse to cover and say so — I'm guessing the obscurity-of-blame material and the virtue-ethical rescue attempts belong on that list, but tell me if something else is a bigger trap for a well-meaning intermediate explanation. Assume the audience is bright, slightly drunk, and constitutionally unable to tolerate a distinction that doesn't cash out in a story about someone.
