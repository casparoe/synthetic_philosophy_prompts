I'm on our county's citizen art panel — all volunteers, we pick which pieces get bought or commissioned for the new courthouse annex and a couple of branch libraries. Six of us. Something came up that started as a scheduling headache and turned into a genuinely nasty argument, and I keep going back and forth on it at night, so I want a memo I can circulate.

One of our members, Doreen, had a stroke in the fall. She's recovering well but can't travel to the walkthroughs, so she's been voting based on notes and photographs that Mark, another panelist, sends her. Mark's a retired art teacher and writes incredibly detailed descriptions — honestly probably better than standing in front of some of this stuff yourself. Our chair, though, says a vote based on Mark's writeup isn't really a judgment about the art at all, it's a judgment about Mark. He wants a rule that you have to have experianced the piece firsthand (in person, or at minimum the video walkthrough for the installation stuff) for your vote to count. Doreen is understandably hurt, and the rest of us are stuck because I can see both sides. If we pass the rule, we're effectively kicking her off the panel until she can travel again, maybe for good. If we don't, we're saying her secondhand experience counts the same as ours, and I'm not sure I believe that when a vote comes down to one piece by three to three.

Here's what I keep chewing on. The chair has a point — if Mark tells me a piece is haunting and I just take his word for it, it kind of feels like I've learned something about Mark's vocabulary, not the painting. But I've also definitely trusted a friend's movie recomendation, loved the movie, and it seems obnoxious to insist I had no real reason to expect it'd be good. And there's a middle thing too: does it matter that Mark is describing the piece to Doreen and not just asserting it's great? And should it matter that Doreen has seen thousands of pieces over the years and can sort of reconstruct what Mark's describing in her head? Maybe that makes her situation different from a total novice voting off a stranger's blurb.

Draft me the memo for our March meeting. I want a concrete recommendation — pass the chair's rule as stated, drop it, or something in between (like her vote counting for less, or her voting on some categories but not others) — with the reasoning written out plainly enough that both Doreen and the chair can see their side taken seriously, not just overruled. Don't split the baby without saying why the split lands where it does. About a page, and please write it in American English.
