I run a graduate seminar on the evolution of cooperation, and for the final unit I want a thought experiment that breaks the textbook moral students take away from the famous computer tournaments of the early 1980s — the moral being that Tit-for-Tat's success shows you should be nice, provocable, and forgiving in roughly that order of emphasis. The case I want built inverts the usual threat model.

Construct a scenario in which Tit-for-Tat is driven out of an evolving population, and the strategy that displaces it is strictly more cooperative than Tit-for-Tat — it sometimes cooperates where Tit-for-Tat would defect, and never defects where Tit-for-Tat would cooperate. The displacement must not be brought about by defection. If pure defectors profit anywhere in the final state of your scenario, you have failed the brief.

Constraints:

1. The advantage of the more cooperative strategy must be generated by a structural feature of the environment that is independently realistic — the kind of feature that plausibly obtains among bacteria, bats, or nation-states, not something invented for the occasion. The feature may not be anything that improves the strategies' information about each other, and there may be no external enforcement, no growing shadow of the future, and no ability to read or simulate another player's code.
2. Payoffs must be a standard prisoner's dilemma matrix throughout, and interactions must be long enough per pairing for cycles of retaliation to develop and feed back into the score.
3. The scenario must be honest about mechanism: exhibit, round by round for at least one representative pairing, how Tit-for-Tat's punishment machinery turns against it, and show that the more cooperative strategy's forgivingness is what breaks the cycle rather than merely absorbing losses.

After the scenario, provide the following, in order:

(a) A step-by-step account of why strict Tit-for-Tat loses here — the specific sequence of events in which its greatest strength in the tournament setting becomes its greatest liability in yours.

(b) A characterization of the displacing strategy — you need not use a name from the literature, but specify its behavior exactly, including any randomness in it — together with an assessment of whether it resists invasion by strict Tit-for-Tat and by pure defectors, and an honest statement if it can hold off one but not the other.

(c) An identification of which single feature of the environment is doing the work, and an account of what happens to both strategies when that feature is removed — I expect the scenario to collapse back toward the familiar tournament result, and I want that demonstrated, not asserted.

(d) A response on behalf of strict Tit-for-Tat's defenders: the minimal modification they would propose to rescue it, whether that modification preserves what made Tit-for-Tat attractive in the first place — above all its simplicity and clarity — or whether it quietly concedes the deeper point that the original moral was an artifact of an idealized environment.

A scenario that stipulates its outcome rather than generating it from the mechanics of the strategies and the environment fails, as does one whose displacing strategy wins by being stupider rather than nicer. The deliverable is the scenario plus a demonstration that each specification is met. Length: whatever the demonstration requires, but the scenario itself should be describable in one paragraph.
