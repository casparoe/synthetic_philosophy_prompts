I'm drafting a chapter arguing that Dennett's hierarchy of minds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures — is an ordering of capacities rather than of performance: each level is supposed to subsume the powers of the level below, yet I want to claim that in the right environment a lower creature can reliably outperform a higher one. Construct a thought experiment to my specifications. The setting should be realistic (a trade workshop, a lab, a chess club — your choice) and involve two agents of equal native talent: one a pure Popperian, who simulates candidate actions in an internal model before acting but possesses no culturally transmitted mind-tools, and one a full Gregorian whose additional capacity consists entirely of inherited tools — maxims, notations, mental tricks — drawn from a wider culture in which those tools are genuinely optimal. Yet in the specific environment of the scenario, the Popperian must reliably do better, and the Gregorian's failure must flow from what its tools do to its search, not from stupidity, laziness, or any misuse of them: a well-supplied mind should be defeated by its own supplies. After presenting the scenario, show rather than stipulate the verdicts: walk through how a Darwinian creature, a Skinnerian creature, the Popperian, and the Gregorian would each fare in that environment, so the whole ladder is visible; identify precisely which feature of the environment is doing the work; and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed. Finally, tell me how a defender of the hierarchy as a strict ordering should respond — whether to bite the bullet and accept that a well-tooled Gregorian can be worse off than an unaided Popperian in a hostile niche, or to argue that my distinction between capacity-ordering and performance-ordering collapses into something the standard picture already allows.
