Two of my colleagues have been arguing past each other for a year and have finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration. One is a lexical threshold negative utilitarian; the other is a commensurating hedonistic utilitarian. I need you to draft the joint statement they should be able to sign.

To be sure the document is self-contained, here is how each party understands her own view:

- The negative utilitarian (call her the threshold theorist) holds that suffering above a certain severity is lexically prior to pleasure: no amount of happiness, however vast, distributed across however many lives, can compensate for a single instance of above-threshold suffering. Below the threshold, she accepts ordinary trade-offs — mild discomfort can be outweighed by sufficient enjoyment. She is emphatically not the caricature who would destroy the world painlessly to eliminate suffering; she thinks that follows from no plausible version of the view.
- The utilitarian (call her the common-scale theorist) holds that pleasantness and unpleasantness are opposite directions on a single dimension of valence, so that aggregate welfare is a sum over all experiences. She accepts that intense suffering requires an enormous quantity of pleasure to outweigh, but insists the quantity is finite: for any suffering, there is some world of enough joy that the trade comes out net positive.

Draft the document they should have produced, with these components:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. These should include what they agree on about the badness of suffering, about the practical cases where their verdicts coincide, and about what a moral theory owes them (e.g. completeness, action-guidance in tragic cases). Include at least one agreement that is substantive rather than diplomatic filler, and at least one agreement that might surprise readers who know only the caricatures.

2. A disagreements table. Each row states one proposition on which they differ, with two columns: the formulation of her own side's position that the threshold theorist would accept, and likewise for the common-scale theorist. Beware slanted wording: "suffering cannot be bought off" begs the question just as "no amount of pain is beyond compensation" does. Find phrasings each would actually endorse.

3. A cruxes section. I suspect at least three: (a) whether valence is genuinely one dimension or two independent processes — the common-scale theorist may care about whether pleasure and pain are mediated by distinct systems, since if they are, summation may be ill-defined rather than merely controversial; (b) whether commensurability, even if granted as a matter of description, settles anything normatively — whether an aggregate good has the right to spend one person's above-threshold suffering on others' pleasure; (c) whether the dispute is, in part, verbal — perhaps about what "compensate" means rather than about what to do. You are not bound to these three; revise as the material demands, but say which residual disagreements, if any, you judge verbal and why.

4. For each substantive crux, a case or argument both accept as bearing on it, with each side's commitment recorded in advance — not a prediction about the world, since these are normative views, but the verdict each would render on the case. Where a genuinely empirical crux exists, use a fixed hypothetical and stipulate both outcomes, writing down what each side would conclude in each. You may find it useful to work with stipulated cases such as: a person who, fully informed, voluntarily accepts an hour of suffering she herself would later call unendurable in exchange for a century of the best life available to her; and a world in which one above-threshold instance of suffering is the price of a large but finite gain in pleasure for any number of others. Feel free to design your own cases instead, but they must be precise enough that neither side can dodge by re-describing them.

Throughout, keep both parties intellectually honest and sophisticated: the threshold theorist must face the question of whether she can give any principled account of where the threshold sits and why a hair's breadth on either side of it changes everything; the common-scale theorist must face the question of why a number gets to decide when torture becomes worth it, and whether she can say so without flinching.

Finally, append a short confidence note: indicate how confident you are in each section, and single out the one formulation in the document you are least confident either party would actually sign, explaining your doubt.
