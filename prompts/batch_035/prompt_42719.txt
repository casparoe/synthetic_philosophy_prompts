Six months ago I joined the board of a small family foundation whose founding charter directs us to fund work that advances "the widening of human moral concern." The phrase has become a live dispute at our meetings. One faction wants the entire endowment redirected toward farmed-animal welfare, on the grounds that extending concern to animals is the obvious next stage of the widening; another faction insists the next stage is distant poor humans, since most people already extend some inchoate concern to animals while remaining unmoved by strangers abroad. Rather than referee the slogan directly, I have been testing my own judgments about historical cases everyone already agrees on, and my verdicts do not line up the way the slogan predicts.

Abolition of slavery: moral progress, and plainly the circle widening. Nineteenth-century animal cruelty laws: progress, and widening, extended to beings that cannot reciprocate. Women's suffrage: clearly progress, but I resist calling it widening — even the opponents of suffrage acknowledged that women had interests; the dispute was about standing and power, not membership in the moral community. The growth of concern for distant strangers through famine relief: progress, but the mechanism looks like television and cheap transport rather than moral argument, and I cannot decide whether that matters. Dueling's disappearance: unambiguous moral progress, yet nothing expanded — a permitted practice simply contracted. Corporate legal personhood: the circle of entities with legal standing literally expanded, and no one I know calls that moral progress.

Is there a principle that captures this pattern? I can dimly see a candidate on which progress consists in the removal of morally arbitrary distinctions rather than in the growth of a boundary, but I cannot tell whether it handles the animal-cruelty case. Please tell me what principle, or small set of candidate principles, best fits my verdicts; what the strongest candidate implies for the causes we might fund — the interests of people not yet born, ecosystems regarded as wholes rather than collections of individuals, perhaps future artificial minds; and, honestly, which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and would force me to give up.
