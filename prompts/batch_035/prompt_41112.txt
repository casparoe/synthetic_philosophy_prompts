I'm a novelist, about a third of the way through a book where a woman wakes up in a hospital after a car crash and, in the space of about an hour, has to give consent twice: once for surgery, where a nurse walks her through the risks and checks she understands and can refuse, and once for the hospital's patient portal app, which requires tapping "agree" to a wall of terms before she can see her own test results. Nobody reads that one. I don't think anyone is even meant to. It occured to me while drafting the scene that we run two basically different ideas of consent side by side, same hospital, same patient, same hour, and I can't tell whether that's hypocrisy or some actual principled distinction.

So here's what I want to figure out, because my character is going to push on it and I'd like her push to be philosophically load-bearing. What theory of valid consent does each practice actually presuppose? Does the tap-to-agree model presuppose that consent is just an act of will -- the button was pressed, done -- and that understanding is irrelevant? Or does it presuppose something about the stakes being lower, so the bar drops, which would imply consent comes in degrees scaled to risk? And is that even coherent, since the whole point of the surgical model seems to be that risk raises the requirements rather than just the stakes? Then again the surgical model isn't as clean as it looks either -- she's signing while groggy on painkillers, which can't be full competence, so maybe what the medical practice really presupposes isn't comprehension but some mix of protection and liability allocation, which the click-through could share.

What I'd like you to do: reconstruct the implicit theory behind each practice, tell me which parts are strictly presupposed and which parts are just things the practice happens to fit with, and say where each practice is underdetermined -- where several rival views of consent (deception, coercion, competence and all that) would license the same behavior, so you can't read the theory off the practice. Then tell me whether one coherent theory could cover both, or whether they genuinely conflict. And finally, for the plot: if the app had to meet the same consent standard as the surgery -- real disclosure, real voluntariness, real capacity -- what would concretely have to change about how it works? I'm not after citations or a reading list, just reasoning solid enough that a consent theorist wouldn't wince when my character delivers it.
