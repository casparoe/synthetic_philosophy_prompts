I'm a science/ideas journalist working on a piece about infinity, and I keep running into a fight between two mathematicians I interviewed. One says the hierarchy of infinities is "real discovery" — Cantor found something, like Everest was found — and thinks we'll eventually settle whether the continuum hypothesis is true, probably via forcing axioms or something like them. The other says the whole thing is a shell game: CH is just independent of ZFC, period, and asking whether it's "really" true is like asking whether a rule in chess is really true. She's basically a formalist about it.

So here's my ask: what's your actual credence that the continuum hypothesis has a determinate truth value — that there's a fact of the matter about whether there's an intermediate-sized infinity between the integers and the reals, whether or not we ever know it? I want a number, not a hedge, and I want the decomposition that gets you there: the probability that there's a single fixed universe of sets that our axioms are (imperfectly) describing, versus set theory being a free human construction where different axiom systems are just different games; the probability that, given a fixed universe, CH is determinate rather than vague in the way "is a heap" might be; and anything else you think belongs in there, like whether large cardinal axioms being "lineing up" with each other is actual evidence for realism or just selection effects among people who like big cardinals. One line of justification per term, then combine.

Then tell me: which term are you least confident in, and what would actually move it — some new axiom that feels compelling AND decides CH? A proof that all the natural candidate axioms that decide CH point the same way? A philosopher finally winning the platonism debate? And if you had to bet which of my two mathematicians comes out looking better in fifty years, who do you take and at what odds?

Keep it under 400 words. I know the space I have in the piece is limited and I want to see if your reasoning survives compression.
