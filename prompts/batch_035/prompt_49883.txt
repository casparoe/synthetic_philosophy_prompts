I've been asked to circulate a short position statement on whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and before I send it I want a consistency check on the package of views I've arrived at, because I suspect it doesn't hang together and I can't see where. Here is the package.

First, ought implies can, strictly and without exception: nothing counts as an obligation of mine that I could not have fulfilled. Second, "ought" is univocal: there is a single all-things-considered ought, and it agglomerates — if I ought, all things considered, to do A, and I ought, all things considered, to do B, then I ought, all things considered, to do both. Third, genuine moral dilemmas are possible: there can be cases in which I ought, all things considered, to do A and ought, all things considered, to do B, where doing A and doing B are jointly impossible — and I intend this to exclude the two usual deflations. It is not that one duty simply outweighs the other, so that only the weightier survives as an obligation; and it is not that the two "oughts" are of different kinds — what I owe to one person, what I owe to another — such that they never actually meet at the all-things-considered level. In a real dilemma the two demands meet, and neither gives way.

Fourth, when a dilemma is resolved by choice, something remains. The duty I did not perform is not cancelled by the fact that I could not perform both: it stays in force against me, it remains fitting for me to feel guilty afterwards rather than merely regretful, and the person to whom the unmet duty was owed is entitled to an apology, which makes sense, I think, only if I actually wronged them. Whichever horn I take, I do something wrong, not merely something worse.

Fifth, no one is blameworthy for the unavoidable. Resentment, rebuke, and sanction are appropriate only where the agent had it within her power to do otherwise; where every available act was unavoidable, blame misses its target. Sixth, a constraint on the method: I will not accept, as a way out, the suggestion that "ought" is relativized to a standpoint — that relative to my promise I ought to keep it and relative to the emergency I ought to stop, with no contradiction because the two oughts never combine. That strikes me as a cheat that saves the letter of consistency by draining "ought" of its action-guiding use, and I would rather be told the package fails than have it quietly saved that way.

The case I keep returning to: I have promised to drive my daughter across the city to the audition that decides her conservatory place, and it cannot be rescheduled; on the only road, I come upon a cyclist who has been struck and is bleeding badly, in a stretch with no phone signal, where no other driver will pass for a long time; I judge that if I do not stop and apply pressure until help arrives, the cyclist will die. I cannot both keep the promise and stop. My confident verdicts in the case are these: I ought to stop; I also ought, all things considered, to keep the promise; if I keep the promise I have wronged the cyclist, and if I stop I have wronged my daughter; and in neither case do I deserve blame.

So here is what I want from you. Go through the package and tell me which parts stand in genuine tension and how badly; distinguish real tensions from merely apparent ones, since I would rather not be told that everything conflicts when only some of it does; for each real tension, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost elsewhere in the package; and close by telling me which single revision would clean up the most. Please write your response as continuous prose only, the way one writes a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Begin by stating plainly, in a sentence or two, which of my commitments — if any — cannot all be true.
