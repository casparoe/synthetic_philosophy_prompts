Me and two friends run a little decision theory reading group and I've been asked to write up my actual positions so the others can pick at them. Before I send it around I want a consistency check from you -- think of yourself as a hard-nosed decision theorist who's seen every version of these fights and isn't impressed by labels. Don't just give me a list where every pair is "possibly in tension", actually work out for each pair whether it's a real clash or just a vibe of one, then rank the real ones by severity and tell me what the cheapest repair would be in each case.

Here's where I've landed. (1) The Allais preferences are rational, full stop: I take the sure million over the gamble in the first choice, I take the bigger gamble over the safer one in the second choice, and I will not call that a mistake or something I need to be cured of. (2) I'm on board with Buchak-style risk-weighted expected utility: rationality doesn't require the independence axiom, only the weaker axioms, and what I'm doing in Allais is exactly what a strictly concave risk function r looks like -- utility stays linear in outcomes and all the action is in how weights get assigned by rank. (3) Ambiguity aversion is rational too. In Ellsberg's two-urn setup I bet on the known 50/50 urn over the unknown one every single time and I refuse to call that a bias, and my hope is that REU is one framework covering both Allais and Ellsberg -- same kind of story, risk over rank, no need for extra machinery like sets of priors. (4) Stochastic dominance is untouchable: if one act is at least as good in every state and strictly better in some, preferring the dominated one is irrational, period. (5) Free information can't have negative value -- if someone offers to tell you how a coin landed before you place your bet, and the peek costs nothing, you never do strictly worse by looking, since worst case you ignore it. (6) Framing invariance: two presentations that describe literally the same states and the same outcome-contingent acts demand the same choice, so if you dress a compound gamble up as a two-stage tree versus just writing out the reduced distribution over outcomes, my choice shouldn't budge.

So that's the package. Which of these actually conflict, which only look like they conflict once you think it through for more than two seconds, and where is the cheapest place for me to give something up? Be blunt -- if my hope in (3) that one framework does both Allais and Ellsberg is dead on arrival, say so and tell me exactly which commitment has to go and what giving it up costs me elsewhere.
