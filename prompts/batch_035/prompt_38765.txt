I teach a graduate seminar on historical method, and each year I find myself lecturing on the distinction between pseudohistory and merely contested or erroneous history as though it were obvious. It is not, at least not to me, and I would like help bringing my own judgments into order. Below are my verdicts on a set of cases and a tentative principle that captures some of them. What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough to be applied — and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts it cannot save.

The cases, with my gut verdicts:

1. Fomenko's New Chronology, on which most of recorded history before roughly the second millennium is a phantom duplicate and events are redated by statistical and astronomical correlations between texts: pseudohistory, and about as clear a case as there is.
2. Von Däniken's ancient-astronaut claims, in which monumental architecture and ancient art are read as records of extraterrestrial contact: pseudohistory.
3. Illig's phantom-time hypothesis, on which several early medieval centuries were invented by court chroniclers and never happened: pseudohistory, though I notice it is at least a checkable claim of a familiar historical kind — forged documents and inserted reigns are real phenomena — which makes me unsure why it still feels hopeless.
4. The claim, argued at book length by two credentialed archaeologists, that Upper Paleolithic Solutrean people crossed the Atlantic and contributed to the Clovis culture: not pseudohistory. Wrong, in my judgment, and maintained after the genetic evidence turned sharply against it — yet I hold that it is bad archaeology rather than pseudohistory, and I cannot yet say what entitles me to that.
5. The nineteenth-century scholars and antiquarians who read the Vinland sagas as literal travel accounts and combed the New England coastline for Norse remains: not pseudohistory, even though it was speculative, even though the consensus of the American academy was against them, and even though their most cherished candidate structure turned out not to be Norse at all. The eventual excavation of a genuine Norse site in Newfoundland strikes me as vindication of their form of inquiry even where its particular conclusions failed.
6. The colonial-era attribution of Great Zimbabwe to Phoenicians or Sabaeans: pseudohistory, even though this was the respectable, institutionally backed position of its day and the archaeologists who established the African origin of the site were treated as troublemakers. So the consensus cannot be the criterion — in case 5 the fringe was doing history while the consensus dragged its feet, and in case 6 the consensus was the pseudohistory.
7. Bernal's Black Athena: not pseudohistory, in my view, though much of it is wrong and some of it inflammatory. It was argued by ordinary scholarly means, with the possibility of failure visible throughout.
8. A colleague's thesis that the timing of the Industrial Revolution turned decisively on the geographic accident of accessible coal: ordinary history, possibly mistaken.
9. Holocaust denial: pseudohistory, obviously — but I am suspicious of my own grounds. I notice that I would want it classified as such even before examining its methods, simply because of what it is, and a demarcation principle that runs on repugnance rather than method would misclassify case 7, whose motives were also advocacy. This is the case I keep going back and forth on.

My tentative principle is this: a claim counts as pseudohistorical when its proponents maintain it insensitively to counterevidence — they would go on believing it whether or not the evidence against it existed — whereas ordinary bad history is merely wrong given the evidence there actually is. This handles cases 1, 2, 7, and 8 tidily. It strains on case 3, misfires on case 4, whose proponents look exactly as insensitive as Fomenko's followers, and it says nothing at all about the consensus, which my verdicts on cases 5 and 6 suggest matters in some way I cannot articulate.

So, three deliverables. First, repair my principle or replace it, and motivate each clause with the case that forces it. Second, tell me its verdict on two cases I have deliberately withheld my own verdicts on: a retired naval officer's mass-market claim that Chinese treasure fleets reached and mapped the Americas in the early fifteenth century; and a county historical society that has concluded a medieval charter in its possession is a forgery because it conflicts with the town's founding legend, and now re-dates the local abbey on that basis. I will tell you afterward whether your principle's verdicts match mine. Third, be candid: which of my nine verdicts does your principle overturn, and why should I let it? And say whether the principle generalizes to demarcating pseudoscience as a whole, or whether it is specifically historical — and, if it does generalize, whether it has become so abstract that it no longer explains what made the Fomenkos and the von Dänikens of the world feel like a kind rather than a coincidence.
