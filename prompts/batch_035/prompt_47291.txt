I'm an undergrad taking a course on applied NLP, and for our class project we fine-tune small open models to act as math tutors — the model is supposed to walk a student through a problem, not just hand over the answer. In the first version we only supervised outcomes: we'd grade the final answer the model gave at the end of the walkthrough. Final-answer accuracy got decent, but when I actually read the transcripts, the middle was often junk — confident-sounding filler that happened to land on the right number, and a few times steps that literally could not have produced the answer attached to them. So we switched to supervising the process instead: a checklist the walkthrough has to hit (states a plan up front, defines the variables, checks the result against the problem statement). And here's the thing — final-answer accuracy barely moved, but the write-ups mutated. Every checklist item got its own little paragraph, often worded like it was translated straight from the rubric, e.g. let us now verify our result against the problem statement as required. It reads like the model performing the shape of good reasoning for the grader. A friend of mine TA-ing a freshman essay course says she sees the exact same thing in student essays: this essay will argue X in the intro, as shown above in the conclusion, sentences that exist only because there's a rubric line to satisfy. She calls it writing for the rubric, and I'm pretty sure that's what my model is doing too.

When I try to name the whole thing I come up short. Goodhart's law is in the neighborhood — I know the version about a measure stopping to track the goal once it becomes a target — but that's usually stated as a single step: the proxy breaks. What I think I'm seeing is more like a lifecycle with stages: (1) people use judgment to assess some quality; (2) the judgment gets codified into a checklist of the quality's visible symptoms, because checklists are cheaper and more consistent across graders; (3) the thing being supervised starts producing the symptoms directly, decoupled from the quality they were supposed to indicate; and (4) the weird one — once the checklist is established, going back to judgment feels arbitrary and unfair (why did I get a bad grade, I hit every box), so the checklist digs in even after everyone can see its being gamed. Reward hacking, which a friend mentioned, sounds like it's about stage 3 only, and teaching to the test is the stage 2 to 3 transition in education but doesn't really carry the codification or the lock-in.

So, question: does this arc, as a whole, have an established name in the literature on supervising models (or on grading humans — I suspect it's the same thing, tbh)? If something exists, tell me how it maps onto my four stages and where my description disagrees with the standard version. If nothing exists, coin a term — give a definition that makes clear it names the whole lifecycle and not just the moment the proxy breaks. Please structure your answer as: Closest existing concepts (roughly two paragraphs — the nearest ideas and precisely where each stops short), The pattern, stated precisely (roughly two paragraphs — what the four stages are and what makes them one phenomenon rather than four), A name, if one is needed (one short paragraph — the term plus a definition I could actually use in our project writeup), and Edge cases (one paragraph — e.g. does stage 4 have to happen for it to count, does it still count when the checklist genuinely helps at first, and does it matter whether the grader is a human or a learned reward model). I'd rather have careful carving than a confident label — my TA already said thats just Goodhart, and I don't think that's the whole story, but I can't quite put my finger on why.
