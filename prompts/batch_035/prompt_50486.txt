Film night for my seminar on autonomy and precommitment, and we're doing Kubrick's A Clockwork Orange. I want to frame the discussion with the Ulysses contract idea — binding your present self so your future self can't act badly, the same logic people invoke for constitutional constraints on future majorities. But here's what's bugging me: Ulysses ties himself to the mast, whereas Alex gets tied down by the state, for the state's purposes and without his consent. Is the novel actually about precommitment at all, or is it a category error the Ulysses analogy commits? I'd like a reading that says (a) whether the story shows that precommitment preserves autonomy only when it's self-imposed, (b) how the famously-cut final chapter — Alex just growing out of ultraviolence — fits, since it reads like the book ditching binding altogether in favor of ordinary maturation, and (c) whether the analogy to constitutional precommitment survives: if a polity can bind future majorities who never consented, why can't the state bind Alex? Tell me where the novel resists the reading, and what it shows about imposed binding that the precommitment literature doesn't.
