I've been having this running argument with my housemate about statues and street names and renaming things, and I've noticed my own gut verdicts are kind of a mess, so I want to lay them out and see if there's an actual principle in there. Confederate general on a courthouse square, put up in the early 60s pretty obviously as a message to Black residents: take it down, no hesitation. A statue of a similarly awful guy that's been sitting in a city museum for a century as an artifact of the era: I'd leave it, maybe with better labeling. A street named after a town founder who turned out to have made his fortune in the slave trade: rename it, the disruption to businesses is real but not decisive. The same founder's name on a mountain that locals have called by his name for 150 years, where basically nobody even remembers there was a person: here I weirdly resist renaming, even though it's the same honoring mechanism, just worn smooth. A Nobel-winning chemist whose private letters reveal he held racist views: keep his name on the lecture hall. The same chemist, except it turns out his famous experiments were run on unwitting human subjects: strip his name off everything.

Then there are the two that really bother me. Ordinary soldiers' gravestones in an old Confederate cemetery: I want those left alone, removing them feels like desecration, even though I was happy to remove the courthouse statue and both are ways of marking the dead. And a donor's name on a business school building, where the donor is later convicted of massive fraud: rename instantly, I don't even have to think about it — which is strange, because the building name was never an endorsement of his character, just a thank-you note for money, so why is that the easiest case in the whole list while the racist chemist stays up?

So: is there one principle, or maybe a couple, that generates all of these? Something about who's being honored and for what, or about what the name has become vs. what it started as, or about time and uptake? I'd like you to state the principle (or candidate principles) precisely enough that I could apply it to new cases, tell me which of my verdicts it just cannot save and should overturn rather than patch with ad hoc clauses, and then run it on a few cases I haven't mentioned: whether a theorem should stop being named after a mathematician who did something bad, whether renaming an entire college is the same as renaming one building of it, and what to do with a town that's fully absorbed a bad founder's name into its identity. Please write in continuous prose only, no headings or bullet points or anything like that — just plain paragraphs like an email.
