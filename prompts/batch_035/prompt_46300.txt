I'm a PhD student in political theory and I'm running a screening + discussion section next week for our intro course on concepts of liberty. We've just done Berlin's two concepts and the Pettit/Carter republican alternative (non-domination vs. non-interference), and I want to use The Shawshank Redemption as the case study, mostly because everyone's seen it so the screening is basically an excuse for pizza. The students will know the three-way distinction; what I want is the film read properly against it, not just name-dropped.

A few things I specifically want covered. Brooks post-release seems like the puzzle case — under a pure non-interference view he's about as un-interfered-with as it gets (no guards, no rules, nobody watching him at all), yet he hangs himself, and noone in the room will want to say he was freer outside than in. Does that actually support Pettit, or is it cheating — trading liberty for wellbeing/belonging? Then there's the warden's treatment of Andy: Andy gets the library, the beer on the roof, protection from the sisters, but all of it exists at Norton's whim and can be revoked the second Andy crosses him, which looks like domination dressed up as generosity. Is that the cleanest dramatization of arbitrary power in the film, and would an interventionist-but-predictable warden actually score better on Pettit's own metric than a lazy non-interventionist one? And Red's final parole hearing, where he stops performing rehabilitation and just tells the board the truth — is that liberty in any of the three senses we've covered, or is it autonomy or dignity masquerading as liberty?

I'd also like an honest section on where the film resists the republican reading, because I suspect it partly does. The escape itself feels like a negative-liberty fantasy — one man, one wall, noone to stop him — but he only gets there via twenty years of invisible tunneling under conditions of total surveillance and a rock hammer that works precisely because it's beneath notice. Maybe the film's real concept of freedom is closer to hope (the thing Andy tells Red about in the yard, and Red's whole arc with it) than to non-domination — is hope a concept of liberty at all, or is that just the film being sentimental and my section overreaching?
