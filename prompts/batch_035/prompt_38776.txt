I've read LessWrong and its satellite blogs since roughly the days when the sequences were still being posted, and the corner of that world I've never stopped chewing on is commitment races: the argument that agents who can bind themselves irrevocably face pressure to commit early, before they know much about the other side's dispositions, because the party that commits first to a hard line may extract concessions from everyone else — but commitments made blind can leave you locked into mutually destructive strategies against a counterparty who would have cooperated, and a race between agents to lock in commitments before understanding each other can end in a world none of them wanted. The canonical discussions run on hypothetical superintelligences deciding whether to precommit during a standoff. What they almost never do is ask what any of this looks like when the agents are human, fallible, and partly crazy.

I recently rewatched Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove and realized it might be the best commitment-races thought experiment ever filmed, so I'd like a serious reading of the film through that lens. The Soviet doomsday machine is, in the decision theorist's vocabulary, a perfectly credible commitment: an automated system that will extinguish human life if the USSR is attacked, constructed so that it cannot be reversed or disarmed once in place. It fails as a deterrent for precisely the reason Strangelove spells out in the war room — the Soviets had completed it but not yet announced it, so the Americans had no way of knowing they were deterred. And the triggering attack doesn't come from a rival superintelligence weighing its options; it comes from a rogue general acting on a paranoid delusion about the contamination of his bodily fluids — an irrevocable commitment issued by a malfunctioning agent against an enemy who isn't even playing the game the commitment is aimed at.

A few questions I'd like the reading to take on, though I'd welcome others:

- Is the doomsday machine actually an instance of commitment racing, or just a Schelling-style commitment device that failed for an information-theoretic reason? The races literature emphasizes committing before you know whether the counterparty is cooperative; the film's machine is built before either side knows whether the other side's chain of command can keep one madman from acting. Does that make the race framing apt, or does it quietly change the subject?

- Ripper's attack order is in effect a self-binding made under delusion rather than strategy. Does the film dramatize the gap between an idealized agent's self-binding and the human kind — and does that gap strengthen or undermine the rationalist case that early irrevocable commitment can be rational?

- The machine fails through lack of common knowledge rather than lack of credibility. Is the film making a point about publicity and timing that the commitment-races essays miss, or the same point under different vocabulary?

- Where does the film resist the decision-theoretic reading entirely? The final montage and the war room's mineshaft proposal strike me as jokes the framework has nothing to say about, and I'd like to know whether that's a limitation of the reading or of the framework.

I'm writing a long post for my blog arguing about whether irrevocable self-binding is ever rational for bounded agents like us, and I want to use the film as my centerpiece without embarrassing myself in the comments. So be faithful in both directions: don't reduce Kubrick to an illustration of decision theory, and don't bend the decision theory to fit his jokes. If the honest conclusion is that the film isn't really about commitment races at all, say so plainly, and tell me what the reading obscures.
