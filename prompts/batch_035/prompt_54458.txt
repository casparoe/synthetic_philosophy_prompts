I'm the deputy director of a small evidence-review unit at a regional health authority. Every few weeks a coverage or procurement decision lands on our desk where someone — a clinician, a patient group, a manufacturer, our own modellers — insists the decision be delayed pending more evidence, and someone else insists the delay itself is costing lives or money. I want a standing procedure, not case-by-case judgment, that one of my analysts with master's-level statistics can run in half a day, and whose output will go to a coverage committee that distrusts black boxes, so every step needs to produce a reason the committee can check by hand.

The anchor is a result I've only partially understood: Good's theorem, which I'm told shows that for an ideal Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, no observation can have negative expected value. If that's right, it sounds as though it licenses "always gather more evidence," which I distrust: I've watched delays do harm, and I've seen trials commissioned that everyone involved knew would not change the decision. So build the procedure so that it preserves what the theorem actually establishes, while being explicit about where its assumptions fail and the procedure has to say no, or hand the question back to a human being because what's left is a value judgment.

The procedure should be a numbered sequence of questions, each with a short note on what counts as a sufficient answer and where the chain stops with "decide now" or "commission evidence." It must handle at least these hard cases explicitly: (a) evidence costs money and time — where in the sequence does the cost of information enter, given the theorem assumes it's free; (b) the prospective evidence cannot change the decision because the decision-maker will not or cannot act on it — a contract is signed, the committee has effectively pre-committed, the manufacturer will withdraw the product if a trial runs; (c) the act of gathering evidence changes the thing being studied — patients and clinicians know the trial is running and behave differently; (d) the numbers needed for an expected-value-of-information calculation don't exist — no agreed prior, no defensible estimate of the probability that the new evidence flips the decision — and the analyst must fall back on something structured rather than a bare guess. Say plainly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as arithmetic.

Then run the procedure on three cases, a short paragraph each. First: whether to delay for eighteen months a coverage decision on a new cardiac device supported by one small trial, when the device is already in informal use at two hospitals in our region. Second: whether to fund a two-year outcomes study of an off-label use of an existing drug that a patient organization is campaigning to have covered immediately. Third: whether to commission a large trial of a screening program that our own modeller estimates has roughly a one-in-ten chance of changing the recommendation, at a cost about equal to the program's entire annual budget. I expect at least one case where the procedure returns "commission the evidence" and I won't like the answer, and at least one where it returns "decide now" against the campaigners — give those answers straight rather than smoothing them over.

Finish with a brief section on what the procedure cannot do.

Format: the procedure as a numbered list with concise parenthetical notes, then the three worked cases, then the limits section. Total length no more than 900 words.
