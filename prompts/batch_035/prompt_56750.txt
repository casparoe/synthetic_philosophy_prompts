I keep running into the same move in online arguments and, more worryingly, in philosophy-of-biology seminars: someone points out that DNA is a code, that the genetic code maps codons to amino acids, and that all the codes we know of outside the cell — Morse code, ASCII, the Zwicky-sort of cipher — have coders, so the cell should have one too. The standard rebuttal is to say the word code is only a metaphor here, and the standard rebuttal to that is to point at the codon table, which looks about as arbitrary and mapping-like as anything could be. I want to referee this properly, and I've realized I can't do that until I know what an adequate theory of genetic information is actually supposed to deliver. Before I go back into the literature, write me the desiderata.

Specifically: the requirements any account of DNA-as-code (or DNA-as-information) must meet to count as adequate, with the case or consideration motivating each. To give you some of the ground the list has to cover, here are the phenomena I'd want handled, though you should add whatever I've missed: the degeneracy of the codon table, where several codons map to the same amino acid without any of them meaning anything less; the near-universality of the code across life combined with its occasional deviations, as in some mitochondrial and ciliate lineages; the fact that the mapping itself is physically realized in the tRNA and aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase machinery, so that the code has a material substrate distinct from the messages it encodes; the transposability of the whole system, as when a jellyfish gene, codon-optimized, can be read out in a bacterium and still produce a working fluorescent protein; the stretches of the genome that regulate rather than encode; and the bare causal fact that DNA is, on one description, just a polymer whose shape and charge interact with other molecules, no semantics in sight. For each requirement, say what forces it on us, and be explicit about which requirements are non-negotiable and which are, on inspection, prejudices of one camp smuggled into the spec — for instance, whether arbitrariness of the mapping is essential or incidental, and whether anything should require that information be semantic at all rather than merely correlational in the Shannon sense.

Two things I especially want flagged. First, if any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — if, say, full-blooded arbitrariness plus evolutionary origins plus universality can't all be had at once, or if the demands of treating the code as a real mapping conflict with the demands of treating DNA as a causally efficacious molecule — say so plainly, because the unsatisfiable core would itself be the answer to the argument that started this. Second, tell me which single requirement, on your list, does the most to exclude the popular candidates, and which popular positions sail through precisely because the popular lists leave that requirement off.

Then, briefly and only briefly, run three rough pictures against the list so I can see it bite: the bare causal-dispositional account on which DNA carries information only in the way smoke carries information about fire; the account on which the genetic code is a genuine arbitrary mapping, realizable in different substrates, but with no need for intentional origin; and the teleosemantic sort of view that ties the meaning of a sequence to what it was selected to do. The scorecard should be short — a few sentences each — because the thinking, as far as I'm concerned, is all in the requirements.

Please write your answer in British English.
