Me and my friend Marcus just got out of a three-hour argument (over dinner, it got a bit heated lol) and I think it was actually about something bigger than what we were officially arguing about, so I want to know if philosophy has a name for the thing we kept circling around.

The argument: Marcus has decided he's going to believe in an afterlife. Not because of evidence -- he admits there isn't any good evidence either way -- but because, he says, the belief will make him less anxious about dying, and beliefs are basically free, so it's rational to pick this one up. His framing was something like: if believing p is good for me, that's a reason for me to believe p, full stop, and if you deny that you're just being dogmatic about what counts as a reason. I kept saying he's pointing at facts about his believing (that it'll calm him down) and treating them like facts about the thing believed (that it's likely). He said there's no difference, since the belief is numerically the same belief either way, and honestly I struggled to say what the difference is beyond "it just is", which definately lost me the argument.

Since then I keep seeing the same shape everywhere and I'm starting to think it's one thing. A colleague of mine openly "admires" her advisor because being seen admiring him gets her onto the good projects -- the admiration is real, she'd pass a polygraph, but its grounds are all about what the admiration does for her, nothing about him. My uncle says he "trusts" a certain politician because trust is good for the country's mood, so he claims patriotic reasons to trust. A friend swears she loves her boyfriend partly because being in love makes her more productive, and she thinks that's a perfectly fine reason for love. Same structure every time: the attitude is genuinely held, the person isn't lying or confused, but every reason they cite is about the effects or benefits of having the attitude, and none of them are about the object of the attitude. It feels like those reasons are somehow off -- like they don't touch whether the belief is accurate or the admiration deserved -- but I can't say what's off about them, which is exactly where I got stuck with Marcus.

Some complications I noticed, which is where it gets interesting:

1. Sometimes the benefits of an attitude DO seem to count in the normal way. Looking forward to a trip is pleasant, and that seems like a perfectly ordinary reason to look forward to it. Same attitude-type (anticipation), same style of grounds (the pleasantness of the anticipating itself), and yet my gut says this one is fine and the Marcus one isn't. Is the difference just that anticipation isn't in the business of being right about anything?

2. Marcus's belief would also be weakly supported by the usual stuff people cite (near-death experiences etc.). Does the usefulness add anything on top of that, or does it just not count at all?

3. Some attitudes you can half-choose -- you can work yourself into awe at a mountain, or into gratitude after a near miss. If the choice is open, don't the benefits of the attitude become straightforward reasons to choose it, same as choosing a hobby? Marcus says obviously yes, I say it still feels wrong, and I can't defend that.

So, questions. Does this distinction -- reasons that come from the consequences of holding an attitude versus reasons that come from the accuracy/object side -- have an established name in philosophy? If yes, what is it, and how does the literature actually draw the line, especially for my complications 1 and 3? If there's no single name, I'd bet it's a few neighbouring concepts -- in that case tell me what they are, how they differ from each other, and which one fits my cases best. And if nothing established fits, coin a term for it, give a definition, and say where its edge cases fall. Please don't just hand me a one-word answer and move on -- what I want is the carve-out, not the label.

Since you're acting as a professional philosopher working on reasons, rationality, and the norms governing belief and other attitudes: think carefully before answering, and start with a short brainstorm where you list the nearest candidate concepts and say for each one why it's close but not exact. Then give the full answer. Also feel free to tell me Marcus is actually right and I'm the confused one -- I can take it, but you'd have to explain away complication 1 to do it.
