For roughly three years now, our philosophy of biology reading group has kept returning to the adaptationism debate, and I have begun to suspect that we are not so much debating as reenacting. On one side we have the Gould and Lewontin line, from their 1979 paper on the spandrels of San Marco: that many biologists treat every trait as if it were an optimization, cycling through adaptive explanations for a feature until one seems plausible, without seriously considering alternatives — genetic drift, allometry, developmental constraints, or features that are by-products of selection on something else, the spandrels of the title. On the other side, the adaptationists, in the spirit of Dawkins and of Dennett's defense of him, reply that cumulative selection is the only known process that can build anything as intricate as an eye, that critics rarely produce a worked-out non-adaptive explanation of their own, and that proposing an adaptive hypothesis first is simply good scientific practice — you test it, you refine it, you move on. Everyone in the group can recite these moves by heart, and the result is that nobody has changed their mind in over a year, which is what bothers me.

I have a hypothesis about what kind of disagreement this is, but I want it diagnosed rather than assumed. My thought is that it is really a dispute about default hypotheses and burdens of proof: the adaptationist treats an adaptive story as the presumption to be overturned, while the Gould–Lewontin side treats it as a claim requiring independent support, so each side finds the other's practice irrational while both can agree about any particular well-studied case. A second member of the group disagrees; she thinks the dispute is straightforwardly empirical — a disagreement about what fraction of phenotypic traits are in fact adaptations, which we are currently just bad at measuring — and points to comparative methods and work on developmental constraints as the way out. A third member thinks much of it is verbal, and backs this up with a distinction he found in a paper by the philosopher Peter Godfrey-Smith, between empirical adaptationism (most traits are adaptations), explanatory adaptationism (selection is what makes evolution interesting and needs explaining above all), and methodological adaptationism (always try the adaptive hypothesis first). On his reading, Gould and Lewontin attacked the first, Dawkins and Dennett defend the second, and most practicing biologists hold the third, so the two camps keep talking past each other, each refuting a position the other side never occupied.

Given all this, I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict on who is right. Is the disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of two camps answering different questions under a single name? Where exactly does the burden of proof fall, and is there anything principled to say about that, or is it a standoff of research tastes? And, given your diagnosis, what, if anything, would count as progress — a sharper taxonomy of the positions, some class of empirical results about constraints or drift, a formal framework for partitioning the causes of a trait, or is this one of those disputes that mostly dissolves once the questions are separated, and if so, which parts genuinely remain? One more thing that puzzles me and might matter for the diagnosis: both camps seem to think they won. Gould considered the debate a success that changed how biologists practice, while the adaptationists claim they never denied constraints and that the critique hit a strawman. What does it say about a debate when each side's victory condition is so easily met on its own terms?
