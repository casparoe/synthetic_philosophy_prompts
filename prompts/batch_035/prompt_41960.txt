My two flatmates, both graduate students, got into a drawn-out argument the evening before a cycling trip, and asked me to referee — though I suspect I did a poor job of staying neutral, because I couldn't follow all of it. Maya had checked the forecast and reported a 30% chance of rain for tomorrow. Jonas, who studies statistics, said that strictly speaking there is no such thing as a 30% chance of rain tomorrow: tomorrow occurs exactly once, probability is long-run relative frequency, and the most the forecast could mean is that rain falls on about 30% of days with conditions like tomorrow's — a claim about a class of days, not about tomorrow itself. Maya answered that this is a dodge: the forecast is bought and used as a statement about tomorrow — it is what makes her take a rain jacket — and if the number weren't about tomorrow it would be a mystery that it helps her decide what to do tomorrow. Jonas replied that what helps her is her credence, her degree of belief, which is a private psychological state: two people with the same evidence can hold different credences about tomorrow's weather and neither is simply wrong, which shows credences are not probabilities in the objective sense that frequencies are. Maya then turned the tables on him: even a coin toss is a one-off event until someone chooses to embed it in a sequence, and there are many candidate sequences — tosses of this coin, of any fair coin, of tosses performed on windy afternoons — so by Jonas's own lights the objective probability of heads is not well-defined either; the reference-class problem infects his stock examples just as badly as it infects her forecast. Jonas's last word was that the reference class for a coin is fixed by the coin's physical symmetry, not by an arbitrary choice of sequence — and then, catching himself, he added that he realizes this makes it sound like probability is a physical disposition of the coin rather than a frequency, which is exactly the sort of unobservable, ill-defined magnitude he thinks probability theory was supposed to have cleaned out of science.

Please adjudicate the exchange round by round: at each step say who is right about the specific point at issue, and in particular whether Jonas's days-with-conditions-like-tomorrow's gloss in his first move is a coherent rescue of frequentism or a quiet retreat toward something that is no longer a frequency at all; whether his appeal in the third move to the possibility of reasonable disagreement between credences actually establishes that credences are not probabilities (or only that they are not frequencies); and whether Maya's reference-class objection succeeds against coin tosses specifically, given his final attempt to pin the class down by symmetry. Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing, and who comes out more right overall.
