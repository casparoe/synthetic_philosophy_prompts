I spent the better part of four decades as an electrophysiologist recording from single cells in visual cortex, and since retiring I have fallen into reading about mechanistic interpretability of artificial neural networks. To my surprise, the field appears to run on an analogy I know intimately: understanding a network is treated as a kind of neurophysiology — find the units, ask what each responds to, attribute function where the probe says it sits. It is Hubel and Wiesel with electrodes, except that this time the experimenter holds the entire connectome, every weight, and can intervene on any unit at will. I also recall how that program went in the actual brain: the simple and complex cells were real enough, but decades of single-unit work still left my discipline arguing about grandmother cells, sparse coding, and distributed representations. I gather interpretability has now hit a version of the same wall, which its practitioners call polysemanticity, and I have watched them respond roughly as we did: by moving from the single unit to population-level and dimension-reduction accounts.

What I want from the discussants in the field, and now from you, is an audit of the analogy rather than a repetition of it. First, a correspondence table: in the comparison between interpreting a deep network and doing neurophysiology, what plays the role of the neuron, the electrode, the lesion study, the behavioral readout, and the species gap between the cat's cortex and ours? Second, the disanalogies — and here I ask you to sort them, since not all of them bear on the conclusion the analogy is being used to support. Some in the field take the correspondence to license a strong realist claim: that because a function can be attributed to an identifiable unit or circuit, the network genuinely has representations, located where the probe finds them. The obvious disanalogies — linear scalar units rather than spiking ones, no dendritic computation, no timing code, no embodiment, training by gradient descent rather than development — may or may not touch that claim. I am especially curious whether white-box access is a difference in degree or in kind from the epistemic situation I was in with a micropipette, since that, more than anything, is what the neurophysiology analogy imports. Third, does the realist conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does it turn out that the term representation was carrying the argument all along — a word we neuroscientists never quite pinned down, inherited by the interpreters along with our methods? And finally, if the analogy does more harm than good at this point, what would you put in its place: a better-matched source domain, or the admission that none is needed?
