I've been commissioned to write a long-form feature on whether philosophy has anything to offer the physical sciences, and I've arranged the centerpiece: an adversarial collaboration between two researchers who have been publicly sniping at each other for years. One is a working theoretical physicist squarely in the Weinberg tradition — he holds up Steven Weinberg's chapter "Against Philosophy" in Dreams of a Final Theory as gospel, delights in reminding people that Hawking opened The Grand Design with the announcement that philosophy is dead, and is fond of the old ornithology jibe about how philosophy of science is about as useful to scientists as ornithology is to birds (he attributes it to Feynman, though he concedes under pressure that the attribution is shaky). The other is a philosopher of physics who has spent her career on the foundations of quantum mechanics and bristles at the suggestion that her field is a museum of beautiful, useless abstractions. Both, I should stress, are arguing in good faith, know each other's literature, and are tired of talking past each other at conferences.

They have agreed to let me publish the joint statement that emerges from their collaboration, and I need you to draft it. Please structure it as follows: a numbered section of claims about philosophy's relationship to science that both could sign their names to — I want it to include what they can agree on regarding actual historical episodes (they've fought, for instance, over whether philosophical scrutiny of quantum foundations was a genuine contributor to Bell-era progress or just a spectator sport that happened to run alongside it), and what they can agree is merely verbal in their dispute, such as what each of them even means by "philosophy" and by "useful"; then a disagreements table laying out the precise points where they part ways, with each disagreement phrased so that the physicist would accept the wording of his position and the philosopher would accept the wording of hers — nothing slanted, nothing vacuous; then, for each genuine crux you identify (I suspect at least one is whether the historical cases where philosophy mattered are representative or cherry-picked, and another is whether there is a class of questions science cannot ask itself without outside help), a concrete case, episode, or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about it recorded in advance, so my readers can later check who called it correctly. Keep the total under 900 words: my editor has been firm about this, and both parties have agreed that a document they can defend at that length is a document worth signing.
