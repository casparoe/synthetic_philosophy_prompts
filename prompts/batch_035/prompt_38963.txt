I teach a film-and-philosophy elective, and next term's unit on deception pairs Kant's 1797 reply to Benjamin Constant — the notorious essay in which he insists there is no right to lie even to the murderer at the door, and adds that if you lie and things go badly, the consequences are imputable to you, whereas the truth-teller, whatever happens, is not the author of the harm — with Schindler's List. The obvious classroom move is the debate-club one: Schindler lies to the Nazi regime for years, the lies save over a thousand people, therefore Kant is refuted and we can all go home. I want to hand my students something more careful than that, and I'd like you to write it: a reading of the film through the Kantian material that puts pressure on Kant without distorting him.

A few threads I'd want you to take up, though you can add or drop some. First, Schindler's deceptions are not the one-off panic lie of the murderer-at-the-door hypothetical; they are long cons, sustained over years, built on a manufactured persona — the war profiteer, the good party member, the fixer with the cognac and the bribes. Does that structural difference matter to the Kantian analysis, given that Kant's argument about imputed consequences is framed around a single lie told in a moment of crisis, and given that a long con arguably makes the liar responsible for consequences in a way that fits Kant's diagnosis better than his critics expect? Second, the film seems to stage a distinction between the web of lies Schindler spins for the regime and a truthful core he maintains toward the people in his factory — the list itself is, on its face, nothing but a true record of names. Is the film suggesting that the lies are parasitic on a preserved space of truth, and is that closer to Kant's own thought that lying corrodes the possibility of truthful statements in general than the standard anti-Kantian reading admits? Third, the sequence in which the women's transport is misrouted to Auschwitz and they are herded into what they — and we — have every reason to believe is a gas chamber, which turns out to deliver water: for those minutes the audience genuinely cannot know whether it is watching a mass murder. Is that scene the film's own construction of the murderer at the door, and does it do anything to the Kant-versus-consequences debate, or is it just a set piece?

Please also be honest about where the film resists the reading. Is Spielberg actually interested in the ethics of deception at all, or does the film treat lying as a morally transparent tool that only a Kantian would pause to examine? And close by telling me what, if anything, the film adds to the lying debate that the canonical exchange and its modern commentators do not give us — one or two argued points rather than a survey. The students will have seen the film and read the Kant; what I need from you is a critical essay I can circulate as a model of taking both sides seriously.
