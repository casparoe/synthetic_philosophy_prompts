I lead a platform-reliability team at a mid-sized company. Whenever a production incident comes in, an on-call engineer has roughly twenty minutes, partial logs, and a customer asking questions, and has to commit to a root-cause hypothesis that determines the next action — roll back, restart, page a vendor, or tell the customer it's their code. Right now we run on intuition and the reflex that the last deploy must have caused it, and we've burned entire nights on that reflex. Write me a triage procedure grounded in inference to the best explanation as philosophers actually analyze it: the explanatory virtues (scope, mechanism, unification, fit with background knowledge), and the standing worry that the most lovely explanation is not always the most likely one — that the two come apart exactly when it matters. The procedure must say what to ask, in what order, what counts as enough of an answer to move on, and when to stop and escalate to a human judgment call; and it must be honest about where it is a judgment call dressed up as a checklist. It also needs to take a definite position rather than offering a menu: commit to a priority ordering among the virtues for when they conflict, and defend that ordering — my senior engineers will immediately ask whether this is all just Bayesian reasoning in disguise, so answer that objection head-on rather than dodging it. Then run the procedure on three incidents from our postmortem backlog: a latency spike immediately after a config deploy that turned out to be an upstream DNS provider's outage; intermittent 500s affecting a single customer that turned out to be our own connection-pool exhaustion; and an incident that resolved itself before we confirmed any hypothesis. At least one of these should come out with the procedure confidently pointing the wrong way — show exactly where it goes wrong, and whether the procedure contains any step that would have flagged the danger in hindsight.
