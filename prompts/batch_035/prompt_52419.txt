For my senior capstone project, I have to moderate a written exchange between two mentors of mine who have been disagreeing for two years and have mostly been arguing past each other. One is a comparativist: she thinks cross-case comparisons of atrocities are not only possible but morally required, because prevention and priority-setting (which atrocities deserve more commemorative resources, which cases get funding for documentation, which risk factors researchers should study first) already involve implicit rankings, and it is better to make those rankings explicit and accountable than to leave them hidden. The other is an anti-comparativist: he thinks ranking historical evils is both conceptually confused and obscene — confused because atrocities differ in mechanism, intent, and victim-experience in ways that defeat any common scale, and obscene because "less severe than" slides so easily into "less important than," which is exactly the move apologists and denialists make. I have to produce the document they should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, and I want it to be a document they could actually both put their names to, not a bland split-the-difference.

Please draft that joint statement. I need the following components:

1. A numbered section of claims both could sign. Push past platitudes: include things like whether atrocities differ in morally relevant respects that careful language can name, whether implicit comparisons are already embedded in law and policy (reparations negotiations, sentencing for mass crimes, which conflicts tribunals choose to prosecute), whether some comparisons function as apologetics, and whether "uniqueness" claims can also be misused — say, to dismiss or minimize other cases.

2. A disagreements table. For each remaining disagreement, phrase it twice — once in wording the comparativist would accept for her own view, once in wording the anti-comparativist would accept for his — with neither phrasing slanting toward the other side. Distinguish, where relevant, between ordinal claims ("this case was worse than that one"), cardinal or aggregative claims ("three times as bad"), per-victim severity versus total magnitude, and comparing within one category versus across categories. If some of their two years of arguing turns out to have been verbal — for instance, if he means "incommensurable" and she hears "incomparable," or if he means "no common cardinal scale" and she hears "no true ordinal comparisons" — say which parts are verbal and why.

3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. I suspect at least one crux is whether ordinal comparison across cases can be made rigorous without importing a full cardinal metric, and another is whether comparisons inherently relativize the suffering of the victims in a way that other forms of historical writing do not. But identify the real ones, including any I have not guessed.

4. For each crux, a test, argument, or concrete case both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Feel free to be creative: blind vignettes where case descriptions are stripped of group identities, the question of whether the anti-comparativist can consistently endorse triage in disaster medicine or humanitarian priority-setting, whether a court's finding of comparative severity counts as evidence for the comparativist, whether "whataboutism" is a feature of comparison as such or only of bad-faith uses of it.

Please keep the register serious and precise throughout; this will be read by my faculty advisor, who has warned me against both false balance and sneering at either side.
