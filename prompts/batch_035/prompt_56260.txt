I teach epistemology and the philosophy of economics, but before academia I spent the better part of a decade making markets in index options, and my intuitions about markets were formed on that desk, not in seminars on Aumann. So do me a translation. Restate the philosophical literature on agreement and no-trade — Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result, the common prior assumption it rests on, the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem and its descendants, and the broader debate about whether prices in a market aggregate dispersed information — in the vocabulary I actually used every day: two-sided quotes and the bid–ask spread, a market maker's edge living in the spread rather than in any view about the underlying, adverse selection as the cost of filling the orders of better-informed counterparties, inventory risk and hedging it off, stale quotes being picked off, the gap between the mid and what you actually think fair value is, order flow that arrives for liquidity or rebalancing reasons rather than informational ones. Translate as faithfully as you can, and do not quietly substitute my desk's premises for the philosophers' and present the result as translation.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which of the philosophers' claims survive the translation cleanly? Which survive only distorted, and in which direction does the distortion run? And which have no counterpart in the microstructure vocabulary at all — does "common prior" have any real analogue in how a book is run, or is it a term of art with no home on a desk? Is the "trade" of the no-trade theorem even the same act as the trade that crosses my spread, given that a healthy fraction of my counterparties were not trading because their posteriors differed from mine but because they needed to be somewhere else by the close? Where something cannot be said in my vocabulary, I want your judgment on why: does the residue point to something the desk vocabulary cannot express — something the philosophy is genuinely about — or does it show that the debate is thinner than it looks once you have watched prices actually get made?

Push back on me where I deserve it. I am aware that my implicit premise — that having run a book entitles me to find these puzzles inflated — may itself be the confusion, and if so, say so directly rather than accommodating me. I will confess that I have never understood whether the no-trade theorem's conclusion is supposed to surprise anyone who has made markets for a living; if the honest answer is that I am missing what the theorem is actually about, I want that stated plainly, not softened.
