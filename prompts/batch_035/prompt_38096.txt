Over the past year, largely through a seminar on the philosophy of language and a lot of independent reading, I've settled into a deflationary view of truth: that truth is not a substantive, metaphysically deep property of propositions or sentences, and that everything there is to say about the concept is captured by the equivalence schema — each instance being of the form: it is true that snow is white if and only if snow is white. On this view the truth predicate earns its keep in a purely logical way: it lets us endorse patterns we could not write out explicitly, such as affirming that everything in the archive is true, or that at least one of the theorist's disjunctive claims is true, without having to enumerate endlessly many instances. Truth, in the deflationary picture, is less like a property that sentences have in the way copper conducts electricity and more like a device for generalizing.

I'd like a serious map of what would count against this position — not a survey of the debate, but its exposure. Which objections should genuinely move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and which ones a committed deflationist would rightly wave away? In particular I want your treatment of: the semantic paradoxes, especially liar-style revenge cases and whether deflationism's minimal apparatus makes them worse rather than better; the normative role of truth — the idea that belief aims at truth and assertion is governed by a truth norm — and whether a thin, purely logical concept can carry that weight; the explanatory demand, i.e., whether a substantive truth property is needed to explain anything, for instance why successful prediction tends to cluster with truth, or how our words manage to be about the world at all; and the worry that a deflationist cannot coherently state the truth-conditions of whole languages without quietly reintroducing a heavier notion. For each, say whether deflationists themselves concede it as a cost, whether the standard replies (hierarchies, fixed points, treating truth as an expressive device rather than a predicable property) amount to real answers or to insulation — and whether, if the view has been qualified to the point where nothing could refute it, that is a defect for a view of this kind, which is after all conceptual analysis rather than empirical theory. Finally, tell me what would have to be true for me to be pushed toward a rival — a substantive correspondence property, or pluralism about truth — rather than merely annoyed: is there any development, in logic, semantics, or elsewhere, that would make the heavier views worth their cost?
