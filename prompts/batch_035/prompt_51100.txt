A magazine I write for ran a back-page column explaining pragmatics to a general audience, and I want to fact-check it before we get letters. The column says roughly this: 'Grice showed that conversation runs on an unwritten contract: be as informative as the situation requires, don't say what you believe false, be relevant, and avoid obscurity. When a speaker appears to break these rules, the listener reasons back to what the speaker must have meant — so if I say "some of the committee members read the report," you infer that not all of them did, because otherwise I would have said so. That inferred layer is the implicature, and it sits entirely outside of meaning proper: the literal content of the sentence is the only thing actually communicated by meaning, and everything else is inference layered on top. Grice's larger lesson was that meaning itself is conventional — words mean whatever the dictionary and grammar assign them, and the hearer's task is to decode the sentence first and only then consult context. This is why a witness who answers a question with "I have no recollection of that meeting" strictly asserts something quite innocent: the misleading suggestion is a mere implicature, and since it is not part of what was said, he cannot be held responsible for it.' My own grasp of Grice is thin, but I suspect the column has him backwards in places — I have read that he wanted to analyze word meaning in terms of what speakers do, not the other way around. Please go through the passage and tell me what a linguist or philosopher of language would accept as standard, what is a harmless simplification for lay readers, and what misrepresents Grice's actual position; and separately, tell me whether the column's conclusion about the witness follows once the account is corrected.
