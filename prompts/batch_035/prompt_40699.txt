I'm a school psychologist, two years into the job, and part of my work is interpreting heritability statistics for people who have to act on them — parents of struggling readers, administrators deciding what a response-to-intervention program can realistically deliver, teachers who hear that a trait like reading ability or ADHD symptom severity is, say, sixty or seventy percent heritable and conclude that the fixed portion is a ceiling on what instruction and intervention can do. When I went looking for guidance on what these numbers actually license, I fell into a dispute that seems to have been running in essentially the same shape since the 1970s. On one side stand the critics, a line running from the Harvard population geneticist Richard Lewontin's famous critique that analyzing variance is not the same thing as analyzing causes: heritability is a ratio of population variances, defined for one population under one distribution of environments, and it says nothing about what causes a particular individual's trait, about how malleable the trait is, or about what would happen if the environments themselves changed. On the other side stand the behavior geneticists, who keep producing heritability estimates — first from twin and adoption designs, lately from DNA-based methods — treat these estimates as among the most replicable findings in psychology, and respond that they have always disclaimed the individual-causation reading, so the critics are attacking a claim nobody makes. The striking thing to me is that the fight has absorbed half a century of new data without either side moving. The genomic era was supposed to settle it: genome-wide methods now yield their own heritability estimates, these come out well below the twin-study figures for the same traits, the gap is discussed under the label of missing heritability, and yet the molecular behavior geneticists read the new numbers as confirmation that genetic influence is real and pervasive, while the critics read the same numbers as evidence that the twin designs were inflated by assumptions — equal treatment of identical twins, assortative mating, gene-environment correlation — that were never innocent to begin with.

My working hypothesis, from sitting in the middle of this while writing reports for school districts, is that this is not really a dispute about the statistics at all — both sides agree on the arithmetic — but about what a statistic has to do to earn the right to appear in a report that parents and administrators will act on. That is, it is a disagreement about the standards of evidence and the burden of translation, dressed up as an empirical one, and it persists because each side answers a different question: one side asks what heritability can mean in principle within quantitative genetics, the other asks what it can do in practice in the environments we actually have, where schools, neighborhoods, and families are not randomly assigned. Is that diagnosis right? Or is there a genuine hidden empirical question here — for instance, whether the causal structure of a trait is stable enough across realistic environmental ranges that population variance ratios track anything like individual developmental potential — that twin data, molecular data, or some synthesis of them could actually settle? And is the apparent stalemate just what we should expect when one side is answering an in-principle question and the other a practical one, or does the persistent 50-year pattern of each new method being interpreted as vindication by both sides tell us something deeper — that the disagreement is a proxy fight over how malleable human outcomes are, and that no amount of data will touch it?

I want a diagnosis, not a survey of the positions: tell me what kind of disagreement this really is — verbal, methodological, evidential, or a clash of starting intuitions about malleability that neither side can argue for directly — using what each side actually maintains, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis. That might be a better statistic or causal model, an agreed convention restricting the claims heritability figures are licensed to support in educational contexts, a result that would force one side to concede, or an argument that the impasse is structural and that reports like mine should simply stop quoting heritability numbers at all. Be honest about which of these it is, because I have to write the next report either way.
