My roommate and her brother have been going at each other for weeks now, ever since he got back from a two-year work posting in Singapore. He came home genuinely impressed: spotless streets, basically no violent crime, most people in public housing they're proud of, and a government that just... delivers. And from there he slid into a bigger claim: a state that takes a country from post-colonial poverty to first-world incomes within a generation has earned the right to rule it, and Westerners who sneer at one-party rule there are just exporting their own historical hangups as if they were universal principles. She thinks this is exactly backwards and it's become a whole thing at their family dinners, so they've sort of drafted me as referee, and honestly I can't call it.

The exchange as it actually went: (1) He said prosperity and order are the most defensible basis for legitimacy there is — the state exists to make people's lives go well, and this one demonstrably does, better than plenty of democracies. (2) She replied that delivery is a reason to keep a government, not a reason to remove your ability to remove it — a landlord who fixes the pipes doesn't thereby acquire the right to your apartment, and 'we delivered' is what every successful autocrat says, including ones who delivered with one hand and jailed opponents with the other. (3) He answered that democracies stake their legitimacy on delivery too: every incumbent campaigns on the economy, voters punish governments for recessions, so elections are just a slow, noisy performance review, and Singapore's system is at least honest about what the review actually measures. (4) She said there's a categorical difference — in a democracy you can fire the government without a crisis, through a routine procedure nobody treats as treason, and that standing to remove is what consent actually means, not the growth rate. (5) He said her firing power is one vote among millions every four or five years, mostly along identity lines, and she wouldn't call an employee free who can only quit at fixed intervals by participating in a mass vote with everyone else — and anyway, he added, Singapore has a performance clause too: if the PAP ever presided over collapse it would lose power, everyone knows it, which is precisely why it governs well. (6) She closed by saying that's the whole problem, not a defense: legitimacy that rests on performance is borrowed, it lasts only as long as the good decades, and a regime that has no answer to a bad decade except nationalism and scapegoats was never legitimate in the first place — and China, she said, is about to run that experiment for everyone to watch.

Can you go through their six moves and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? I'd especially like to know if move (5) actually works — whether 'they'd fall if they failed' really is a kind of accountability — and whether her last point about the fragility of performance-based legitimacy is a knockdown argument or just a separate observation about stability dressed up as one about legitimacy. And if there's something both of them are missing entirely, that would help too, because they keep arguing past each other about whether 'legitimacy' even means the same thing to each of them.
