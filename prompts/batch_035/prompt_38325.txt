So I'm a journalist, I cover science and ideas for a mid-sized outlet, and while reporting a piece on the science of happiness I've fallen down a philosophical rabbit hole that I now need help climbing out of. The debate about the heterogeneity problem for hedonism seems to have been going in circles for decades (arguably since the ancients), and I want to understand why it never moves.

Quick setup for anyone who knows the territory: hedonism about well-being says a life goes well to the extent its filled with pleasure and lacks pain. Fine. But then someone asks what makes all the wildly different things we call pleasures into pleasures -- good coffee, finishing a marathon, an evening of real conversation, an orgasm, reading philosophy. They don't feel remotely alike, so what's the one thing they share? As far as I can tell every answer on offer either (a) goes so thin it amounts to "experiences the subject is positively oriented toward," which looks circular since the positive orientation is just... pleasure again, or (b) retreats to listing candidate features (a distinctive phenomenal feel, wanting it to continue, not wanting it to stop) that each fail to cover some of the cases, or (c) just shrugs and says pleasure is primitive. The literature has been cycling through versions of these moves for ages and nobody seems to change sides.

My working theory, and I hold it loosely: the two camps are answering different questions under one word. One side treats "pleasure" as a natural-kind term whose extension psychology is supposed to fill in -- if it turns out there's a unified affective/neural state common to all the cases, hedonism survives; if pleasure fragments into liking, contentment, excitement, absorption, etc. with nothing shared but the label, it dies. The other side treats "pleasure" as a folk concept whose job is already done once it carves out the experiences we welcome, so no experiment could ever count against them either way. If that's right, the heterogeneity debate isn't a substantive empirical dispute at all, its a quiet fight about what kind of concept "pleasure" is, and neither clever thought experiments nor neuroscience will move it.

So: is that diagnosis right? Or is there a genuine substantive question here -- whether there really is a unified state the heterogeneous cases share -- that's simply very hard, in which case stalling is just what hard looks like? Diagnose it using what each side actually says rather than the labels, and be honest about which camp is doing which job, because from the outside they all sound the same. And then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, a specific type of empirical finding, a change of question, or an argument that we shouldn't expect progress and why.

One more thing, since I'm a science journalist and this is where I keep getting sucked back in: should the liking-vs-wanting work in affective neuroscience matter here at all? I'm thinking of the finding that rats whose dopamine systems are knocked out stop working for sugar but still make the pleased face when they taste it -- wanting and liking seem to come apart. Some philosophers wave this around as if it settles something about pleasure's unity, but I can't tell whether it actually bears on the heterogeneity problem or whether its just a cool finding people borrow to sound scientific. If it does bear on it, tell me how, and if it doesn't, tell me what kind of finding actually would.
