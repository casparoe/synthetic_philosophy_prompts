I teach a philosophy of history course and every year we do a unit on societal collapse, so I finally wrote out my own position to hand around, and now I want to poke at it before the students do. Roughly where I've landed: (1) collapse is a real phenomenon and not just a label archeaologists slap onto change they don't like — societies can genuinely fail, not merely transform; (2) the Classic Maya "collapse" in the southern lowlands was mostly political fragmentation and reorganization — the population didn't vanish, and the Maya are obviously still here; (3) the Easter Island ecocide story is basically a myth — yes there was deforestation, but the society didn't crumble on its own before European contact, and the popular version is mostly colonial storytelling; (4) the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, by contrast, was a genuine collapse — the palace economies, the trade networks, even the writing systems, went down and stayed down for centuries; (5) I think the right definition of collapse is demographic — massive death or dispersal, not institutional simplification; and (6) despite all that, collapse remains a legitimate and important concept for thinking about modern risks like climate change. Does this hang together? Which of these are actually in tension, which only look like it, and if something has to give, what's the cheapest revision and what does it cost me? Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all flat.
