I'm a science-and-ideas journalist who has been invited to guest-run a 75-minute session for twenty undergraduate journalism students on how reporters come to know what they publish. Design a newsroom simulation in which the students act as reporters under deadline, receiving a stream of tips from sources with differing track records, and must repeatedly decide whether they know a story well enough to run it or merely believe it strongly enough -- with a scoring scheme that pays off for publishing true stories but penalizes both false publications and missed true ones, so that justification and truth pull apart in their incentives. Build in at least one case where a tip from a source with a perfect record turns out to be self-interested or planted, and where the story is nonetheless true for an entirely unrelated reason the students only discover in the debrief. Then give me a debrief that maps their own publish/spike verdicts onto the difference between justified true belief and knowledge, and asks them to locate the moment they started treating the source's reliability as the story's truth. Include rules, materials, a timeline, an honest note on which feature of the epistemology a simulation with staged evidence cannot reproduce, and a candid account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it.
