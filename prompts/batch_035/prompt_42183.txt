I am a staff software engineer at a company that builds a large consumer mobile application, and I sit on the small internal council that owns our recommendation system's objective function — the formal quantity our models are trained to maximize. The council revisits the objective every eighteen months, and the upcoming review has split it three ways, with a decision required before the next planning cycle.

The first faction wants to keep optimization on behavioral signals alone: clicks, watch-through rate, session length. Their position is that behavior is the only data that isn't cheap talk — whatever users say about themselves, what they actually choose under real conditions reveals what they prefer, and survey responses are contaminated by what people wish to believe about themselves.

The second faction wants to move a substantial part of the objective onto stated preference: a periodic in-app survey asking users how satisfied they are and whether the product serves them well. They argue that our behavioral metrics have been Goodharted — that engagement measures attention, not benefit — and that a user who binges for two hours and then reports feeling worse is revealing a preference through a different channel, one our click data structurally cannot capture.

The third faction — a single principal engineer — claims the whole dispute is verbal. On his view, there is no fact of the matter about what users "really want" independent of which measurement we happen to choose, and the council should simply pick whichever proxy best predicts retention and stop arguing about preference.

I have to write the memo that settles this. Draft it for me: a recommendation the council can act on (for example, a specific weighting of the objective, funding the survey infrastructure, or deferring with a specified study and decision criteria), the reasoning behind it, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument on each losing side. Two things the memo must take seriously. First, whether observed behavior genuinely reveals preference in our setting, given that the recommender curates which alternatives a user is ever shown — we are partly training on behavior we ourselves caused, so the "choice" we observe is made from a menu we wrote. Second, whether my colleague is right that the disagreement is merely definitional, or whether there is a substantive question about what our objective should track that survives even once everyone agrees on what the measurements say. Assume no philosophical background on the council; define any terms of art you use.
