I write a column on science and risk for a general readership, and I've been sitting on a puzzle I can't resolve to my own satisfaction. One half of my beat is safety and environmental regulation, where agencies cheerfully multiply small probabilities by large losses: they price a marginal reduction in mortality risk as a value per statistical life, and approve or reject projects on the arithmetic of expected costs and benefits. The other half is insurance and catastrophe finance, where nothing of the sort prevails. Insurers refuse to write coverage without an upper bound on payouts; the state steps in as insurer of last resort precisely for the risks too large to price; several countries cap nuclear operators' liability by statute regardless of the harm actually done; and solvency regulation typically demands only that a firm survive, say, a one-in-two-hundred-year loss, with nothing required beyond that horizon. As far as I can tell, institutions that multiply small probabilities by large losses in one context quietly stop doing so once the tail gets long enough — and that is exactly where the St. Petersburg gamble lives. Anyone who took a decision-theory course knows the candidate resolutions: Bernoulli's move of capping the utility of wealth so the expectation converges; the decision to rank gambles by the median, or by some other functional, rather than the mean; plain risk aversion; or the observation that no real counterparty could honor the long tail anyway, so the infinite expectation was a bookkeeping fiction from the start.

Here is my question. Which, if any, of these resolutions do our actual practices presuppose? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of value and risk under which the observed pattern — expected-value bookkeeping for small risks, truncation and statutory caps for long ones — makes sense, and say whether it does make sense or whether the two halves of the pattern rest on flatly inconsistent commitments. Then distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely tolerates: is there one resolution of the St. Petersburg paradox that our institutions are implicitly committed to, or are they underdetermined among several? And what would a regulatory regime that took the untruncated tail seriously have to change — not just in insurance, but in the cost-benefit half of the pattern, where the same expected-value arithmetic is applied without apology?

Two requests about how you answer. Since I'd be publishing a version of this, I need to know where the argument is fragile: please say explicitly which consideration or argument would most plausibly change your verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. And close the response with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing, in a few words, the single commitment the practice most needs — for instance 'bounded utility' or 'none: underdetermined' — so I can extract it mechanically.
