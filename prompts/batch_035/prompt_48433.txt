I'm a novelist, and I've become stuck on a problem that began as research for my third book. The book concerns a museum conservator who, over the course of thirty years, quietly replaces the paintings entrusted to his studio with copies of his own manufacture -- copies so faithful that no examination, technical or connoisseurial, ever detects the substitutions. The central irony I'd planned is that the visiting public continues to weep in front of the canvases exactly as before, and I wanted this to be an image of self-deception. The trouble is that the more I worked on the manuscript, the more I found myself believing the conservator is not deceiving anyone at all. I've arrived at the view that a work's aesthetic value supervenes entirely on what can be encountered in it -- on the perceptible and intelligible surface, in the broadest sense -- so that a perfect forgery, once no one can tell the difference, is aesthetically worth precisely what the original is worth. Origin matters to provenance, to biography, to the market, and to the history of technique; it does not matter to the value of the experience the work affords. My art-historian friend tells me this is a pose, and presses me with cases: the van Meegeren Vermeers, which critics praised extravagantly until the disclosure and then somehow could no longer see the same qualities in; Borges's Pierre Menard, whose word-for-word identical Quixote is nonetheless supposed to be a richer text than Cervantes's; even my own craft, since a plagiarized paragraph seems to read differently the moment one knows -- and she asks whether an experience that is altered by knowledge of origin is not itself aesthetic data, in which case my supervenience claim quietly concedes everything.

I'd like someone to treat my position as a genuine metaphysical commitment and tell me what would count against it. I don't want only the intuitive revulsion or the familiar thought that we value originals the way we value the actual sword rather than an identical replica; I want to know which arguments, which kinds of cases, and which empirical results would genuinely damage the view, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. I'm especially interested in the challenge that artworks are achievements rather than mere objects, so that a forged painting is like a plagiarized novel -- a false record of what was risked and done -- and in the thought that there can be no such thing as an actually undetectable forgery, since the very act of forgery leaves its genealogical traces, which would make my position empirically empty rather than false. I'd also like to know which of these defeaters committed defenders of my side would themselves concede as counting against it, which they would dismiss as confusion between aesthetic and other kinds of value, and whether the standard retreat from "no one can tell" to "no one could in principle tell" insulates the view against all possible evidence -- and if it does, whether that is a fatal problem for a view of this kind or simply the ordinary condition of a supervenience claim. Please also address the novelist's version specifically: whether the intuition that a stolen paragraph is aesthetically worse than the same paragraph honestly written is a datum my view must explain away, or a crack in its foundation.
