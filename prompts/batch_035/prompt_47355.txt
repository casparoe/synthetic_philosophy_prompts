My closest friend and I have a long-running argument about Hofstadter's superrationality — the idea he introduced in his Metamagical Themas columns that in a one-shot dilemma among parties who are all rational and know they are reasoning about the same situation in the same way, each party may treat its own choice as a sample of what all the others will choose, and pick the strategy that is best on the assumption that everyone chooses alike. In a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma this yields cooperation where textbook game theory says defect; Hofstadter even staged a contest for his magazine readers, which he called the Luring Lottery, with a million dollars divided by the number of entrants as the prize, to see whether real people would reason that way. My friend is a convert. His position, as he puts it, is that whenever the people involved are "basically alike" — comparable intelligence, comparable values, facing the same choice — each of them does better to assume the others will reach whatever conclusion they themselves reach. My suspicion is that the "basically alike" clause is quietly carrying the whole argument, and I want to put that to a proper test instead of trading intuitions across the dinner table.

So: construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. Two people should apply superrational-style reasoning — each assuming the other will arrive at whatever conclusion they arrive at — and end up worse off than plain self-interest would have left them. The constraints: a realistic setting (a family decision, a workplace, nothing science-fictional); no perfect predictors, no literal clones, no telepathy — the assumed like-mindedness must be the ordinary, plausible sort my friend actually has in mind; the agents must be intelligent and decent, so that the damage comes from the structure of the reasoning rather than from anyone's stupidity or malice; and the situation should be one in which the best available outcome requires the two to choose differently, since that is where I expect the alikeness assumption to break. Then walk me through it: the scenario itself in one paragraph; what straightforward self-interest recommends for each person; what superrational-style reasoning recommends and where the two recommendations part company; which feature of the case is doing the work; and how my friend would most plausibly attack it — I anticipate he will say that superrationality was only ever claimed for agents symmetric in payoffs as well as in reasoning, so that my case marks the idea's boundary rather than refuting it — together with your assessment of whether that reply is a principled domain restriction or an immunizing maneuver, and what happens to the scenario if full symmetry is restored.

A final request about how you handle the assignment. The case exists to test the claim my friend actually makes, so it fails if the bad outcome is stipulated rather than derived from the reasoning itself, or if the agents turn out to be alike only where it sets the trap and unlike everywhere else — please flag it if you catch yourself doing either. And if you conclude that the specifications are jointly unsatisfiable — that any case in which this style of reasoning backfires will, on inspection, turn out to be a case where the reasoning never genuinely applied, so that the scenario cannot be built without cheating in one of those two ways — say so plainly and explain where the tension lies, rather than handing me something that merely looks like it meets the spec. I would much rather learn that my test is ill-posed than win the argument with a defective case.
