I'm a portfolio manager -- long/short equity, about fifteen years in, CFA, the usual -- and a college friend of mine has gotten deep into effective altruism and keeps telling me they decide where to donate using a framework called importance, tractability, neglectedness. As best I understand it: pick problems that are big, solvable, and that not many people are already working on. He claims this is why he thinks AI risk beats funding mosquito nets in poor countries even though the nets have decades of actual evidence behind them, and that the framework is basically the same reasoning I use every day, just about doing good instead of making money.

Maybe he's right, but I want it done properly. Restate the ITN framework in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can -- importatance relative to market cap vs. addressable market vs. magnitude of the mispricing, tractability as something like capacity to deploy capital and implementation risk, neglectedness as undiscoved alpha or as crowding/alpha decay, and whatever else maps. Then give me the residue report, because that's the part I actually care about: which of their claims translate cleanly, which only translate if you distort them, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all -- and for each piece of residue, whether that's because finance is missing something or because their claim is fuzzier than it sounds. The thing I keep snagging on is the crowding mechanism. When a trade gets crowded, the price moves and the edge is gone -- that's *why* we care about crowding, it's priced in automatically. When a cause gets "crowded," what exactly happens? Does anything move? If there's no price, is neglectedness tracking marginal impact at all, or just virtue-signalling about being early to something? And the nets-vs-AI comparison looks to me like the nets are the trade with a real backtest and AI risk is the narrative stock with the huge TAM, which in my business is a classic way to lose money.

So: translate it honestly, tell me what doesn't carry, and if the whole comparison just breaks down at some point, say so plainly instead of forcing it into a nice story. Same goes for my own framing -- if my "narrative stock" jab is doing work it shouldn't, or if I'm smuggling in assumptions from markets (like that opportunities get priced efficiently) that don't apply to philanthropy, flag that too. I'd rather find out the analogy is bad than feel clever about it.
