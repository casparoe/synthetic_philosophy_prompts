I host a philosophy podcast aimed at graduate students and technically minded listeners, and next week's guest is a decision theorist -- I'll call her Dr. L., since the episode records under a pseudonym she uses for her more polemical work -- who has spent her career arguing that money-pump arguments are wildly overrated as a foundation for expected-utility theory. Her line, in brief, is that money pumps show at most that a rational agent needs some protective apparatus -- commitment devices, decision procedures, awareness of the pump's structure -- and not that the underlying preferences themselves must be transitive, complete, and independent in the EU sense. Her view is not that cyclical preferences are fine; it's that the standard arguments for their irrationality equivocate between properties of preferences and properties of the agent who holds them, and that once you see this, the normative force of the pumps largely evaporates.

Draft nine to eleven interview questions for this guest. The questions should be written to be asked aloud, so keep them under about eighty words each, but they should be substantively demanding -- the listener has taken a course in decision theory, and my audience writes in angrily when I lob softballs. Specifically:

1. At least one question pressing the distinction between one-shot and sequential money pumps: if the exploiter must run the cycle repeatedly to profit, does anything follow about the rationality of the preferences exhibited on a single pass, and can she say what does follow without simply biting the bullet that one-shot intransitivity is harmless?

2. At least one question on sophisticated choice: the agent who foresees the whole pump can presumably refuse the trades, but if the standard reply is that foresight restores consistency only at the level of plans and not of preferences, what exactly does she think is wrong with requiring consistency only at the level of choice?

3. At least one question on resolute choice in roughly McClennen's sense -- the agent committing in advance to a whole plan and not re-evaluating at each node -- and in particular whether her position secretly depends on denying the separability of successive decisions, which many decision theorists regard as the point of the whole exercise rather than an optional extra.

4. At least one question about the construction she and I have sparred over before, which she calls the "conditional-preference gap": the claim that an agent facing the second trade of a pump is entitled to refuse it on the ground that their preferences over the remaining options differ from their earlier conditional preferences, so that the exploiter's ledger never actually closes. Ask her whether this reply is anything more than the observation that agents can be stubborn, and what she says to the charge that it makes rationality unfalsifiable.

5. At least one question on whether any of this survives when the preferences in question violate the independence axiom rather than mere transitivity -- for instance, Allais-type preferences, where the pump's profitability seems to depend on facts about the agent's response to certainty, so that pumping the agent may require offering exactly the sort of sure thing whose availability changes the preferences being exploited.

For at least five of your questions, add a one- or two-sentence follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, given her published positions as I've characterized them; where her likely answer is genuinely uncertain, key the follow-up to the more defensible of the two branches. Finally, include one closing question that asks her to state, in a form a listener could act on, what diachronic rationality would look like if she is right -- what would remain of the idea that we should keep our preferences in order over time, once the money-pump argument is no longer doing that work. Please present the questions in the order you would actually ask them, with any bridging remarks you think the host needs, and keep the whole set answerable by someone who knows the literature but has no prepared talking points.
