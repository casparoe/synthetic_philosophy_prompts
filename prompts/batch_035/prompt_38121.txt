I'm drafting the methodological chapter of my dissertation in consciousness science, and before I commit several thousand words to it, I want an audit of the package of positions that chapter presupposes. Here is the package:

1. Dennett's heterophenomenology is the right methodological template for the field. Subjects' self-reports are treated as texts to be interpreted against surrounding theory; their accuracy is neither assumed nor denied wholesale but left open, to be settled case by case; and the resulting catalogue of what subjects apparently believe about their own experience stands to consciousness science roughly as raw data stand to any other science.

2. Introspection has no special epistemic authority. The confabulation literature — choice blindness, subjects' reports of visual richness in the periphery that their own performance shows they lack, misordering of their own mental events — persuades me that people can be wrong not just about the causes and correlates of their experiences but about the phenomenal character of the experiences themselves.

3. I remain a realist about phenomenal consciousness. There is a fact of the matter about what a subject's experience is like, and that fact, not the reports about it, is the explanandum of the science; the reports are at best evidence of it.

4. The science's remit extends to subjects who cannot report — pre-linguistic infants, non-human animals, aphasics — and this is not a courtesy metaphor. Their conscious lives are within the science's purview in principle, not merely extrapolated by analogy from those of us who can talk.

5. A system whose introspective reports matched human reports in every possible conversational and experimental context — imagine a very large language model trained to convergence on all of our introspective-reporting behavior — would not thereby be conscious. A science that certified such a system as conscious purely on that basis would have lost its subject matter rather than extended it.

6. Physicalism: whatever the phenomenal facts turn out to be, they are physical facts.

Some of these I have held for years and some are recent conversions, and I no longer remember which commitments are load-bearing. Tell me which pairs or larger subsets are in genuine tension, which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and, for each genuine one, what the cheapest revision is and what that revision costs elsewhere in the set. Rank the tensions by severity rather than giving me a flat list, and tell me which single change would clean up the most. I would also like to know whether the tensions are artifacts of my phrasing or would survive rewording — that is, whether the package is unstable in itself or merely as I have stated it — since that determines whether the chapter needs a rewrite or only a careful paragraph.
