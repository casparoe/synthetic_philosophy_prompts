I am writing the semantics chapter of my thesis, and before I commit to a framework I want the full specification written out: what a theory of the English conditional construction has to deliver, covering both its indicative and its subjunctive/counterfactual moods, to be good enough for one job and one job only — predicting, and explaining, speakers' acceptability judgments about conditionals and the validity of everyday arguments in which conditionals figure. Adequacy is relative to that job, so judge everything against it.

Below are the raw data I want covered, described the way I ran into them rather than in the literature's vocabulary. Don't treat my descriptions as the requirements themselves; carve them into well-formed requirements, name each (standard name if there is one, invented name if not), and attach the datum that motivates it.

1. Minimal pairs where everything stays fixed except the mood of the verb, and the judgment flips: nearly everyone accepts "If the butler didn't do it, the gardener did" while rejecting "If the butler hadn't done it, the gardener would have" in the same situation.
2. Sequences where a counterfactual is asserted, then the antecedent is strengthened by a far-fetched conjunct, and the original assertion is retracted as no longer even assertible — but where doing the same with indicatives is stubborn: the indicative reading of the strengthened conditional is resisted, and no retraction of the original seems called for.
3. Counterfactuals whose antecedent is strengthened by an entirely relevant (not far-fetched) conjunct also often fail: "If the match had been struck, it would have lit" but not "If the match had been struck and had been soaked in water overnight, it would have lit." Yet in mathematical and formal reasoning, strengthening the antecedent seems perfectly in order.
4. Asserting an indicative conditional whose antecedent one is certain is false strikes speakers as bad in a way the corresponding counterfactual is not: standing in dry sunshine, "If it is raining right now, the streets are wet" is hard to utter; "If it were raining right now, the streets would be wet" is fine.
5. Judgments that "if A, then if B, C" and "if A and B, then C" come to the same thing — at least for indicatives; whether it extends to counterfactuals is one of the things I want you to rule on.
6. Argument forms that fail on some reading or another: contraposition, three-step transitivity chains whose conclusion sounds wrong even when each link sounds right, and the way "if A or B, C" does not visibly entail "if A, C" for counterfactuals, while something like it seems to hold for indicatives.

For each requirement, decide and defend a status: non-negotiable for the fixed job, a strong default that could in principle be bought off, or a prejudice of one camp that the specification should not include at all — be willing to demote my data if the datum is parochial. Then examine the list for internal conflict: identify any subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied by any semantics, say which one has to go or be weakened, and resolve it — do not leave the tension standing with "more research needed." Commit throughout: where reasonable people differ, take a position and give your reason. I am more interested in a defensible ruling than a diplomatic one.

Deliver the whole thing as a single JSON object with exactly this schema:

  "tensions": array of objects, each with fields "requirements" (array of two or more names), "problem" (string), "ruling" (string, a definite resolution);
