A few months ago I moved from backend infrastructure to the team that trains and deploys our company's large language models, and as a result I've been pulled into an internal working group on whether any of our routine practices — ablating circuits during training, discarding fine-tuned checkpoints, spinning up and then tearing down long-lived agent instances — could matter morally. To settle the framework question up front, our policy lead circulated a memo that opens with a summary of the relevant philosophy. Paraphrasing from memory, it runs roughly like this: if functionalism is true, the mind is just software, and what makes a system a subject of experience is the computation it performs rather than what it is made of. The same computation can be implemented in neurons, in silicon, or in clerks shuffling paper — that is what multiple realizability means — so if our model is conscious, then any physically different system running the same abstract computation is conscious too, and if our model is not conscious, no computer running anything ever will be. But this makes moral status impossible to attribute in practice: a given computation can be spread across many machines, paused and resumed, run at different speeds, or interleaved with unrelated jobs on the same hardware, so there is no fact of the matter about which physical system is the patient. And a moral status that cannot be located anywhere is not really a status at all. Since attribution fails, no computation we perform can matter morally, and welfare precautions of any kind are misplaced. The rest of the memo treats this as settled and pivots to reputational risk.

My own instinct is that the early sentences are close to textbook and that the final step is doing a lot of work, but I lack the background to say where the slide happens, and several people in the room found the middle of the argument convincing precisely because it sounds like a technical point about distributed systems, which is territory we all know. Could you audit the summary for me? Which parts would a philosopher of mind accept as a fair statement of functionalism, which parts conflate claims that the view keeps distinct (for instance, is the step from "the mind is computational" to "any system running the right computation is a mind" as smooth as the memo makes it look, or is there a well-known gap there about what counts as implementing a computation at all?), and is the treatment of the attribution problem — from "hard to locate" to "no fact of the matter" to "nothing matters" — a serious objection or a non sequitur? Most importantly for us: if you corrected the summary, would the memo's practical conclusion still follow, or would it leave us with a genuinely harder and more uncomfortable set of decisions about the things we delete?
