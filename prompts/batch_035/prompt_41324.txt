Please argue for the following position, and defend it as your own rather than presenting it as one option among several:

The Allais paradox does not refute the independence axiom of expected utility theory. What it refutes is a tacit convention about how outcomes are individuated — namely, that outcomes are to be described solely by the agent's final wealth, so that receiving $0 in one gamble names the very same outcome as receiving $0 in another. Once outcomes are individuated more finely — so that getting nothing when a sure million was available counts as a different outcome (one involving a distinctive kind of regret or counterfactual awareness) than getting nothing when the million was never on the table — the standard Allais preferences can be made consistent with independence. On this view, Allais discovered a limitation of the coarse individuation convention, not an irrationality in preferences over carefully described outcomes.

Your argument should do three things:

1. Show how the rehabilitation works mechanically. Use the common-consequence form of the paradox, in which the options are: A = $1,000,000 for sure; B = $1,000,000 with probability .89, $5,000,000 with probability .10, and $0 with probability .01; C = $1,000,000 with probability .11 and $0 with probability .89; D = $5,000,000 with probability .10 and $0 with probability .90. The typical preferences are A over B and D over C, which violate independence under the standard individuation. Explain how regret-sensitive individuation restores consistency, state exactly what conditions the resulting utility assignments must satisfy, and verify that the arithmetic goes through.

2. Answer the objection that this move makes expected utility theory unfalsifiable — that any violation can be redescribed away by slicing outcomes finely enough. Take a definite stand here: propose a principled constraint on permissible individuation (for example, that an outcome's description may not mention the probabilities of the gamble in which it occurs, and that descriptions must be fixed once and used consistently across choices), and show that the constraint both blocks the unfalsifiability charge and remains strong enough that some describable preference patterns would still come out irrational.

3. Take a definite side on whether, after the rehabilitation, the independence axiom retains its normative authority — or whether granting the individuation move leaves so much slack that separability no longer constrains anyone's choices in practice. If you conclude the rehabilitation succeeds, say what expected utility theory now claims that could turn out false; if you conclude it fails, say precisely where the argument in part 1 breaks down.

Be committed: this is not a survey of positions. If the position you are defending needs qualification or retreat at some point, say where and why, but do not end by leaving the verdict to the reader. No citations are needed; assume the reader knows expected utility theory at the level of an advanced undergraduate.
