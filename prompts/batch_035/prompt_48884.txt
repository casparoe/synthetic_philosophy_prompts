At the last meeting of my decision-theory reading group, two members, Dana and Marcus, spent the whole session on a pair of thought experiments and left more opposed than when they started. I'd like a careful verdict on their exchange.

They first discussed a version of counterfactual mugging: Omega, a perfect predictor of your decisions, flips a fair coin. If it comes up heads, Omega hands you $1,000 if and only if it predicted you would pay $100 if asked on tails; if it comes up tails, Omega asks you for the $100. In the case as presented, the coin has come up tails and Omega is asking.

They then discussed a version of XOR blackmail: you may or may not have a termite infestation (ex ante it's roughly even odds), which would cost you $1,000 to remedy. A blackmailer knows whether you have termites and also knows, as a perfect predictor of your own decision, whether you would pay a $100 extortion fee if sent a letter. She sends you a letter demanding $100 if and only if exactly one of the following holds: you have termites, or she predicts you will pay. You have just received the letter.

Their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Dana: You should pay the $100 in the mugging. The right object of evaluation is not the act but the policy; before the flip, the policy of paying nets $450 in expectation while refusing nets $0. If you refuse now, you are leaving $1,000 on the table in half of the objectively possible worlds — it is one-boxing with a coin toss in place of the sealed box.

(2) Marcus: The coin has landed, and it landed tails. You are not choosing a policy at the start of the experiment; you are choosing an act now, with the evidence in front of you. The heads branch is closed and the $1,000 exists nowhere. Ex ante I'd have wanted to be a payer, but my earlier self's preferences don't get to bind me here — conditional on everything I know, paying is $100 for nothing.

(3) Dana: This isn't about being bound by a past preference. Your decision now and Omega's treatment of the heads branch are two outputs of one and the same decision procedure, and you should want to be the procedure that wins across the objective chances. Refusing is like taking both boxes after being told the transparent box is empty — you are valuing the act in isolation from the procedure that produced it.

(4) Marcus: Newcomb is disanalogous precisely here: in Newcomb my act is evidence about what is already in the sealed box in my world, so conditioning on my situation favors one-boxing. In the mugging, once I've seen tails, my paying is evidence of nothing that pays. You keep importing value from a branch that never happened.

(5) Marcus (continuing): And notice that in the blackmail I take the opposite side of you. I received the letter. If I am a payer, then the letter's arrival means I'm termite-free; if I am a refuser, it means I have termites. My decision now is what determines which kind of sign the letter is, so I pay: not because paying removes termites, but because deciding to pay constitutes me as the agent for whom this letter is good news. So I refuse your mugging and pay this blackmail, consistently.

(6) Dana: That is the same evidential mistake run in reverse. You cannot purchase good news — the termites are fixed, and your $100 goes to the blackmailer and nowhere else. Evaluate the policy: a payer is sent the letter exactly when termite-free, and pays $100 for the privilege; a refuser is sent it exactly when infested, in which case it functions as a useful warning. The payer policy loses to the refusing policy, so I refuse the blackmail.

(7) Marcus: Then your ledger is bizarre. You count the closed heads branch of the mugging as containing $1,000 that your current act can somehow reach, but in the blackmail, where the dependence of the letter on my decision is equally real and runs through my present act, you refuse to let it count. That looks like one-boxing enthusiasm applied selectively.

(8) Dana: The distinction is not selective. In the mugging, every branch in which my policy's output matters is one in which paying is rewarded — the $1,000 is contingent on my policy, and my paying now is that policy's output. In the blackmail there is no branch in which paying gains me anything: the dependence on my policy exists only to bill me. Dependence on my policy that transfers value to me counts; dependence that only extracts payment doesn't.

They asked me to referee and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate move by move: assess each of the eight moves, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. Then address the two structural questions underneath the exchange: whether Dana's distinction in (8) between the two kinds of policy-dependence is principled or ad hoc, and whether Marcus's pairing (refuse the mugging, pay the blackmail) is internally consistent given what he says in (2) and (5), or whether his two commitments pull apart. In assessing the pairings, please also take seriously the two remaining combinations — paying in both cases, refusing in both — rather than assuming the dispute is exhausted by Dana's and Marcus's positions. Finally, say who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing.
