I'm a senior in high school and the most active member of our school's philosophy club, which our English teacher kindly supervises. For the past month we've been working through a case she brought in: Newcomb's problem. Two boxes, one transparent and visibly holding a thousand dollars, one opaque; a predictor who, weeks ago, put a million dollars in the opaque box only if it predicted you would take just that box, and left it empty otherwise. In our version the predictor has been right about previous club members roughly ninety percent of the time. My best friend and I have taken opposite sides and argued every lunch period, and in the process I've written down what I actually believe, partly to make sure I'm defending my real view rather than simply whatever he isn't. Since I'm scheduled to present my position at Thursday's meeting, I would like to know whether my set of beliefs even hangs together before I stand up in front of everyone.

Here is the set. (1) Facing the standard case, with both boxes closed in front of me, I would take only the opaque box, and I think anyone who cares about leaving wealthy should do the same. (2) My stated justification is that taking one box is, at the moment of choice, strong evidence that the million is sitting in it, and a rational person acts on the best evidence available. (3) I also firmly believe that backward causation is impossible — nothing I decide today can alter any part of what happened yesterday, including what the predictor recorded. (4) I believe in libertarian free will: at the moment I reach toward the boxes, with the entire history of the universe fixed, it remains genuinely open which hand I extend. (5) I would refuse to sign a binding contract a week in advance that locked in my choice, on the grounds that my later self will have everything my earlier self had plus a week of additional information, and that constraining a better-informed self is irrational. (6) In a variant our teacher mentioned, where a genetic condition independently causes both a fondness for smoking and cancer, and where smoking itself is completely harmless, I would smoke, since the smoke is the only thing actually under my control.

Some of these feel perfectly compatible and some don't, but I can't reliably tell which is which, and I would rather discover the weaknesses in my own premises now than have my friend discover them in front of the whole club on Thursday. So please audit the set rather than lecture me on the case in general: identify which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension and how badly, which ones only appear to conflict until examined closely, and, for each genuine tension, what the cheapest fix would be and what that fix would cost me elsewhere on the list. Please rank the tensions by severity instead of treating them as equally damaging, and tell me which single revision, if any, would clean up the most. I should add that I won't have time to look anything up before the meeting, so I'm hoping for reasoning I can follow, reconstruct, and defend on my own rather than references to track down.
