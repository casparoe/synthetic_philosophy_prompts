I've spent two years in a graduate seminar on philosophical theology that has become a running joke for its inability to move anyone: Molinism, or the doctrine of middle knowledge, and I am beginning to suspect the impasse is not about what the participants think it is about. I would like a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on Molinism.

Here is where things stand, as concretely as I can put it. Molina's idea, laid out in the 1580s, is that God has three kinds of knowledge: natural knowledge of necessary truths, including all possible worlds; free knowledge of the actual world, which depends on God's creative will; and, in between, middle knowledge of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom — truths about what any free creature would do in any fully specified set of circumstances, truths that are supposed to be prior to and independent of God's will. With middle knowledge in hand, God can survey, for every candidate creation, what each creature would freely do, and choose accordingly; and this underwrites the standard Molinist story about providence and, in Plantinga's free will defense in the 1970s, about why there is evil despite an omnipotent, benevolent God. The debate over this is old enough that the Church gave up on adjudicating it in 1607, when Pope Paul V declared both the Molinist and the Thomist positions permissible and forbade further publication on the matter; four centuries later, the arguments in circulation are, as far as I can tell, exactly the ones the participants had by the mid-1990s.

The objections have settled into a familiar cluster. The grounding objection, pressed most forcefully by Robert Adams and William Hasker, holds that nothing makes the counterfactuals of freedom true: the creature's decision is ex hypothesi not a truthmaker, since in the non-actual circumstances the agent never gets to decide; God's will cannot be the truthmaker, since the whole point is that these truths are prior to it; and there is no other candidate, so there is no middle knowledge because there is nothing there to know. The bootstrapping objection observes a circularity in the machinery: God's choice of which world to actualize is supposed to be guided by the counterfactuals, but which counterfactuals are relevant is fixed by that very choice, since circumstances include what God does. There is an epistemological complaint that even if the counterfactuals have truthmakers, it is obscure how God's knowledge of them avoids making the creature's act in some way dependent on that knowledge, which reintroduces the determinist pressure middle knowledge was invented to remove. And the defenders reply in ways that strike the objectors as accommodation rather than answer: that God's creative act is a "soft fact" relative to the counterfactuals; that the counterfactuals are true independently and God simply finds them out; that we already accept truths without obvious truthmakers, such as mathematical and negative existential truths, so the demand is being applied selectively. Both sides agree on the data — that free creatures sometimes do otherwise; that providence and predestination need some account; that the free will defense works only if the relevant counterfactuals have some determinate content — and neither side has moved in living memory.

I have two hypotheses about what kind of disagreement this really is, and I want them evaluated, not endorsed. First: that it is covertly a disagreement in the semantics of counterfactuals, not in philosophical theology at all. On the standard Lewis–Stalnaker treatment, a counterfactual's truth is a matter of similarity between worlds, and the Molinist counterfactuals resist that treatment in a distinctive way — the antecedent fixes everything up to the moment of choice, and the consequent is supposed to be settled by nothing, since the choice is free. The grounding objection then looks like the demand for a truthmaker where the semantics guarantees there is only a fact about which nearest worlds are like, and the Molinist reply looks like the claim that some counterfactuals are governed by a different semantic rule than all the others. If that is right, the theological vocabulary is mostly packaging, and progress should be expected from formal semantics rather than from more theology. Second: that the two camps are answering different questions under one heading — the objectors are asking a metaphysical question about truthmaking, while the defenders are trying to solve an engineering problem, namely how to get a God who is not responsible for sin and who can still guarantee providence out of indeterministic creatures, and would accept almost any metaphysics that serviced the engineering. On this diagnosis, the grounding objection, even if granted, does nothing to show the engineering fails, and the defenders' accommodations show they never took the metaphysical question to be the point. There is also a revision on the table in our seminar, which I mention because it tests both diagnoses: drop the claim that God knows what creatures would do and hold instead that God knows only what they might do — the counterfactuals of freedom are indeterminate in the way some medieval theorists held future contingents to be, and God, like anyone else, cannot know a truth where there is none. That saves the grounding objection's force but arguably forfeits everything middle knowledge was for, and I cannot tell whether that trade-off is a substantive discovery or a restatement of the open-theist position under a different name.

So: is this a substantive metaphysical dispute, a verbal one about 'true' or 'know', a disagreement about which cases count as data, or two research programs with incommensurable success criteria? Is some hidden question — about bivalence, about divine aseity, about whether the counterfactuals of freedom form a semantic class of their own — doing the real work? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a worked-out formal semantics for these counterfactuals, an agreed impossible test case, a decision to separate the providence question from the truthmaking question, or nothing — an argument that the positions are held fixed by prior, and never defended, commitments about grace and predestination, in which case the stall is the predicted result and four more centuries of it are what we should expect. I want the diagnosis supported by what each side actually claims, and I want you to be as willing to say the disagreement is ill-posed as to say it is deep.
