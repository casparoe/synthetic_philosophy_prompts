I work on AI policy, and over the past year I've settled on a position I'd call contractarian neutrality as the right answer to the question of whose values an aligned AI should serve. The system shouldn't aggregate citizens' full value sets into a weighted average, and it shouldn't be trained to promote any particular conception of the good life. It should instead operate with a thin, political set of values, and it should treat any contested action as permissible only if it could be accepted by each reasonable person affected, given that thin set alone — a per-person acceptability test, not a headcount and not a utility sum. The governing question is "could every reasonable person accept this?" rather than "what does the majority want?" or "which option maximizes aggregate preference?"

I want to know what would count against this position. Not just the familiar objection that neutrality is itself a substantive stance which happens to favor tolerant, privatized conceptions of the good over comprehensive ones that demand public recognition — though I want that objection taken seriously, including the precise point at which it bites. I'm especially interested in three other kinds of exposure. First, cases the acceptability test cannot decide, or can decide only by smuggling in thick values: a drafting request, for instance, where both granting and refusing are thick choices, so the test appears to return "blocked" only because blocking was covertly privileged. Second, structural results suggesting no rule can be both neutral in my sense and usable at all: if every action is vetoable by someone, the test yields paralysis, and every escape hatch — restricting the test to "reasonable" persons, a fallback baseline, a tie-breaking prior — seems to reintroduce exactly the substantive commitments neutrality was meant to exclude. Third, observations that the test collapses into something it was designed to oppose: majority rule under a new name, or a bias toward whatever status quo the fallback encodes.
