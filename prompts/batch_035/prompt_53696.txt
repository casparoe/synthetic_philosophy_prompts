My philosophy of mind seminar — fifteen students, one 90-minute session before break — has just worked through the Dennett chapters of Consciousness Explained that introduce heterophenomenology: the method of taking subjects' reports seriously as texts to be interpreted, without granting those reports privileged access to the mental facts they purport to describe, and without writing them off as mere behavior. The students grasp it on paper. What they don't yet feel is how difficult the neutral middle is in practice — how strong the pull is, once you hold a transcript, to slide into the Cartesian move (the report is a window onto experience) or the behaviorist move (the report is just more data about the organism). Design an in-class experiment that produces and then exploits that pull.

What I can supply: a seminar room, a TA willing to play a role, and simple props. My rough thought is a staged incident — a brief interruption by a supposed visitor, with some planted details accurate and others deliberately inconsistent — followed first by free written reports from the subjects and then by structured interviews conducted by experimenters who know the ground truth. Somewhere in between I want misinformation introduced, so that some subjects end up confidently reporting things that never happened. Treat this as a sketch: redesign the mechanics as you judge best, but the subjects must not know the script, and the experimenters must.

Give me roles and how to split fifteen students across them, a timeline, materials, written instructions for the experimenters — including what they are forbidden to do in the interviews and why — and a debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the exercise onto parts of the method: the subject's authority over what was said versus over what is true of the subject's mind; the heterophenomenological world in which the false detail is real-as-reported; and a three-way comparison of what a Cartesian, a behaviorist, and a heterophenomenologist each do with the identical transcript. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — subjects comparing notes, experimenters asking leading questions, a student who already knows the misinformation literature — and how to handle each. Finally, be honest about what the exercise cannot reproduce: the real method runs on converging objective measures we won't have, and nothing can hand the experimenters the subject's experience from the inside. Say plainly what the facilitator must supply in the debrief to close that gap, and close with whether an honestly run exercise of this kind vindicates Dennett's neutrality or exposes it as evasion — that's the question I want them arguing about in the hallway afterwards.
