I teach a philosophy of science course, and for the unit on classification we've been working through Ian Hacking's ideas about looping effects: the thought that when psychiatry classifies people, the classified can come to understand themselves through the category, change their behavior in response, and thereby alter the very kind the category was meant to describe. The students grasped the basic idea quickly, but when I asked them where they actually stand, their answers collapsed into vague gestures at "it's partly biological and partly social," which tells me they're choosing labels rather than commitments. So I'd like you to build me a decision tree that sorts them by cases instead.

The tree should begin from an entry node everyone in the class already accepts — something like the removal of homosexuality from the DSM in 1973, or a contemporary teenager who reads about a diagnosis online, recognizes herself in it, and starts organizing her life around it — and then branch on questions a student can answer without knowing which position it leads to. I'd like the branch points to track the genuinely contested choices, which I take to be at least these, though you should restructure them if you see a better logical space: (a) whether classifying people ever changes the classified in ways that feed back into the kind itself, as opposed to merely discovering a kind that was there all along; (b) when such a change occurs, whether it must flow through the classified person's own self-understanding, or whether the loop can run entirely through third parties — schools, courts, insurers — without the person ever reflecting on the label; (c) whether a kind's being subject to such looping makes it less real, or just makes it a different sort of kind, one that is made and remade rather than found; and (d) whether a category could in principle loop itself out of existence, so to speak — a diagnosis that fades precisely because it was named and reacted to, as some have claimed about certain historically transient conditions. Please put the leaves in terms of positions rather than just names: the biomedical realist who treats disorders as natural kinds; the Hacking-style view that interactive kinds are quite real but are not natural kinds; the strong social constructionist for whom looping shows the kind was never really there; an institutionalist who grants the loop but routes it entirely through third-party practices rather than self-understanding; and a deflationist who thinks classification changes people only in the way any description changes people, with nothing metaphysical following.

A few requirements on the execution. Aim for roughly four to six internal nodes and five or six leaves, phrased so that every question turns on a concrete case or a plain claim a student could assess without knowing the technical literature. Please flag rather than paper over any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate — I suspect the Hacking-style leaf and the deflationist leaf may be hard to pull apart, but test that rather than assuming it. And for any combination of branch answers that lands nowhere, note it briefly and speculate whether nobody holds that view because it's incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. The audience is sharp undergraduates with no prior philosophy of psychiatry, so keep the vocabulary at the level I've used here. Don't quote Hacking directly; paraphrase any of his formulations you want to invoke.
