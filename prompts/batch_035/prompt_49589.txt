At my book club we're about to discuss Hannah Arendt's report on the Eichmann trial, and beforehand one member circulated a summary of her position that he'd pieced together from a popular history of the trial. His summary goes roughly like this: Arendt covered the trial in Jerusalem for an American magazine and was struck that Eichmann seemed not a monster but a bureaucrat; she concluded that his evil was banal in the sense of being ordinary, bureaucratic, and mediocre, and generalized this into the thesis that the great evils of history are mostly committed by unremarkable functionaries rather than demonic masterminds; she held that Eichmann was less guilty than his superiors because he was essentially a cog who never really understood what he was doing, more thoughtless than wicked; and the real lesson of her book is that almost anyone, placed in the same machinery, would have done the same. He closes by saying this is why she was attacked for excusing Eichmann and, some claimed, for shifting blame onto his victims. I'm inclined to accept most of this because it matches what I've always heard about the controversy, but I have my doubts about the part where she supposedly regarded Eichmann as less guilty, since I vaguely recall she had no problem with his death sentence. Please go through his summary point by point, in American English, telling me which parts a scholar of Arendt would accept as accurate, which are simplifications a defender of hers would tolerate, and which are genuine misrepresentations — especially whether banality for her meant the evil was ordinary and mediocre, or something else, and whether her diagnosis of Eichmann's thoughtlessness was an exculpation at all. Finally, tell me whether his closing explanation of the controversy still follows once the earlier errors are corrected.
