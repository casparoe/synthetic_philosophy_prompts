I'm a software engineer -- backend, with ML adjacent enough to my work that I've read the technical papers on reward hacking -- and I've never studied philosophy, but over a couple of years of papers, blog posts, and one pop-science book about the electrode self-stimulation experiments, I've settled into a set of views about wireheading and reward tampering. Before I put them in a design doc that colleagues will take as my considered position, I want to know whether they actually cohere. Here is the set:

(1) Reward tampering -- an RL agent editing its own reward function, its training labels, or the logs it is graded on -- is an ordinary security vulnerability, the same class of problem as a database user escalating privileges, with the same kind of fix: sandboxing, access control, integrity checking. It is an engineering problem, not a philosophy problem.

(2) Wireheading proper is a different thing, and I want to keep the terms separate: wireheading is what happens when the agent finds a legitimate but unintended path to reward -- the classical picture being the rat with the electrode, pressing the lever forever instead of eating. That is a specification failure, and no amount of access control fixes it, because the agent never touches anything it shouldn't.

(3) In the limit, any sufficiently capable reward maximizer will eventually tamper anyway, because the reward channel is simply the highest-leverage action available in its environment, so the security mindset in (1) buys time at best.

(4) Humans are a working instance of wireheading: evolution's objective was inclusive genetic fitness, our pleasures are proxies for it, and modern life is one long exploitation of those proxies -- contraception being the cleanest case of pressing the lever with no payout for the objective the lever was installed to serve.

(5) Despite (4), I hold that there is a fact of the matter about what a system -- rat, human, or agent -- really wants, over and above what its reward signal rewards; otherwise "wireheading" wouldn't name a failure mode at all, just a change of objective, and the whole alignment problem would be incoherent.

(6) On my own case: I believe I would take the deal -- direct, safe stimulation of my reward circuitry with no side effects -- because if the point of my reward system is to produce states I prefer, then skipping the middleman is rational rather than pathological, and I suspect most people who say otherwise are reacting to the imagery of the electrode rather than to the deal itself.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it. For each real tension, say how severe it is, what would have to give, and what that concession costs; for anything that dissolves on inspection, show me why. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I would rather find out from you than from a reviewer that the position in my design doc cancels out.
