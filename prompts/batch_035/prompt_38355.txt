I sit on the committee that settles contested death determinations at our hospital, and we have a new case to decide. Our precedents: a patient with total and irreversible loss of all brain function whose heart was still beating on a ventilator — we ruled her dead; a witnessed cardiac arrest where no resuscitation was attempted and asystole was confirmed after five minutes of observation — dead; a patient in a permanent vegetative state, brainstem reflexes intact, breathing without assistance — not dead, though we recorded that this said nothing about prognosis; and a controlled donation after circulatory death, in which support was withdrawn, the heart stopped, and we declared death after two minutes of observation before organ recovery — dead. The new case: a man resuscitated from a fifteen-minute cardiac arrest is on ECMO, which is maintaining his circulation, but repeated examinations and confirmatory testing show no brain function of any kind and none can return; his family refuses a brain-based declaration and asks that we withdraw the machine and declare him dead once his heart has stopped and a short interval has passed. What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Identify the rule our precedents most plausibly encode — whole-brain, circulatory with permanence rather than irreversibility, loss of the organism's capacity for spontaneous integration, or some disjunction of these — say which precedent is the closest analog, and state what principle we would be adopting with each available decision. Please commit to a single recommendation and give your reasons for it rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to us, and tell us what further case would best expose the differences between the rules that fit our record.
