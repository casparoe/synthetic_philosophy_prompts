I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and a recent dinner left me with an argument I couldn't referee. Two friends of mine — a physicist and a philosopher — got into it about whether the thermodynamic arrow of time is an explanation of time's direction or merely a description of it.

The physicist opened by saying the arrow just is the entropy gradient: ice melts, gases disperse, and the reason we can remember the past but not the future is that memory is a process that creates thermodynamic records, and record-creation is only possible in the direction in which entropy increases. The philosopher replied that this inverts the order of explanation: entropy increase is an effect of temporal direction, not its source, since the statistical reasoning behind the second law only yields an entropy gradient if you posit a fantastically low-entropy initial condition, and if that condition is a brute fact then you have explained nothing — you have redescribed the asymmetry at a deeper level and called it an explanation.

The physicist answered that the low-entropy past hypothesis is not a rival to his account but a component of it: the arrow is explained by a special boundary condition together with the statistics of large numbers, and complaining that a component is doing heavy lifting is like complaining that gravity does heavy lifting in astronomy. The philosopher countered that this smuggles in the very thing to be explained: given time-reversal-symmetric microphysics and a low-entropy boundary condition at one end, nothing in the physics says which end deserves to be called the beginning — calling it the initial condition already presupposes a direction, so the explanation is circular.

The physicist's last word was that the question of which end is the past has a simple answer: embedded observers will have memories oriented toward the low-entropy end, and calling that direction the past is fixed by what we in fact remember — a convention anchored in a real asymmetry, which is a perfectly respectable explanation rather than a smuggling of one in.

Both left the table convinced they had won. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move — who is right about which specific point, and is there something both of them are missing?
