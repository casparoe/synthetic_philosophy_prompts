I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a hospice trust, and our next agenda item concerns a proposed trial of an immersive virtual-reality programme for patients in their final weeks. Because of pain and immobility, these patients' real-world options have narrowed dramatically; the programme would let them spend several hours a day in simulation as their healthy former selves — walking a coastal path, cooking in their old kitchen, playing with grandchildren who in reality visit rarely and only at the bedside. Patients would enter the programme knowing full well that it is a simulation; the question is whether that matters. Our clinical lead argues that we should maximise the quality of whatever life remains, and that the programme does so far better than anything else on offer. Two committee members respond that this is simply a bedside experience machine: whatever patients get from it is worthless, or worth far less, because it is not real, and the trust would be substituting a comfortable fiction for their actual final weeks. A complicating factor is that there is a genuine but thinner alternative: more frequent video calls and family visits, real but exhausting and often emotionally strained.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — adopt the trial, reject it, or adopt it with specified conditions — and it should engage the underlying philosophical dispute rather than assert it. In particular, it should address what follows about the programme if hedonism about well-being is true; what follows on desire-satisfaction and objective-list theories; whether the patients' relevant desires (to walk that path, to be with their grandchildren) are satisfied by simulated walking and simulated grandchildren, given that the desire seems to be about the world and not merely about experience; and how the committee should decide given that the correct theory of well-being is contested. It should also treat the strongest point on the losing side honestly, including the concern that the burdens of a simulated final month may fall partly on the families, who must decide how to remember it.
