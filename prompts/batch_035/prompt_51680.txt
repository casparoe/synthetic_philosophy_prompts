I serve on the governing board of a regional heritage museum, and I have been asked to circulate a written recommendation ahead of next month's meeting, at which we must finally settle the terminology for the permanent gallery on the famine that followed the colonial pacification campaigns of the 1890s in the territory our museum covers — locally the episode is still called 'the Great Clearing'. The underlying facts we would display are largely agreed among our advisory historians: troops confiscated cattle and burned grain stores, restrictions on movement and trade were maintained while official rations were knowingly inadequate, and mortality among the affected people plausibly reached a quarter of their population. The surviving administrative correspondence shows that some officials anticipated mass deaths and pressed on regardless, while others protested and were overruled; no court or international body has ever adjudicated the episode. Our descendant-community liaison insists the central panel must be headed 'genocide'; the government heritage body that co-funds the museum responds that this is a legal finding we have no authority to make, and prefers 'man-made famine' or 'mass atrocity'. The board is deadlocked, and several members suspect we have been treating this as a search for the historically correct label when it is really a decision about what kind of act of naming a museum performs.

It seems to me that Lemkin's own history bears directly on this, and I would like the memo to take that seriously. He coined the word in his 1944 study of occupation policies, and his original conception was considerably broader than what survived into the 1948 Convention: it encompassed the destruction of political groups and the erosion of a group's cultural, religious, and economic life — categories the final treaty text narrowed away. Some of our trustees argue that a label on a museum wall is a historical and moral judgement rather than a verdict of criminal law, so the Convention's intent requirement and its protected-group list should not govern our choice; others reply that if we deploy the term where courts and tribunals have not, we hand our government partner a pretext to withdraw funding and give revisionists an easy target. Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete heading and treatment for the central panel, including whether the controversy over naming should itself be exhibited as part of the gallery, provide the board with reasons it can act on, and engage honestly with the strongest consideration on whichever side the recommendation rejects.
