Over the past few months I've been part of a reading group on philosophy of mind, and I've come out of it with a scatter of gut verdicts about the causal status of conscious experience that I cannot make cohere. My working sympathies are with epiphenomenalism: I find the causal closure of the physical domain persuasive, I find zombies at least conceivable, and my roommate — a neuroscience graduate student — is always delighted to supply the neural mechanism behind anything I do. But when I go case by case, my verdicts wobble. When I yank my hand off a hot pan, I'm comfortable saying the qualia do nothing: the spinal reflex handles it and the sting just rides along. When I get up mid-conversation to fetch ibuprofen for a headache, I think I'm still comfortable saying the qualia do nothing — the unpleasantness itself contributes no force to my walking to the medicine cabinet. But when I sincerely say "this headache is killing me," I find myself resisting the thought that the awfulness of the experience does no work; the utterance seems to be about the pain and to track it. Similarly, when I reflect on the knowledge argument and come away convinced that Mary learns something new when she first sees red, it feels as though the qualia have just caused a belief in me — that my conviction is downstream of the experience itself. And in one more register: I readily explain my leaving a concert early by saying the sound was shrill and grating, and I do not want that explanation replaced by a story about my auditory cortex, even though I'd also insist the qualia caused nothing.

What I'd like is a single principle about when phenomenal properties earn causal credit, when they earn merely explanatory credit, and when neither, that captures all of these verdicts — or a demonstration that the set is unstable and some verdicts have to go. My tentative repair is something like: qualia get genuine causal credit only when a subject is forming a judgment or report about experience itself, and only explanatory credit elsewhere. But I can already see trouble: it seems to classify my deliberate ibuprofen-fetching alongside a spinal reflex in a way I'm not sure I accept, and I don't know what it predicts for the cases I haven't thought through — a pain-asymbolia patient who calmly says the pain is there while taking no analgesic and showing no distress; a marathon runner who insists she finished the race "because the pain was bearable"; or a color scientist with an inverted spectrum who writes papers about what it is like to see red. Please state your principle (or your small set of candidates) precisely, motivate each clause with the case that forces it, tell me what it predicts on the unconsidered cases, and be explicit about which of my verdicts you would overturn and why. I'd also like you to say how confident you are in each part of the answer and to single out the point you are least sure of — my own suspicion is that everything turns on whether the paradox of phenomenal judgment, the tension between experience being causally idle and our judgments about it seeming true because of it, is a genuine problem for epiphenomenalism or an artifact of how I've framed these cases.
