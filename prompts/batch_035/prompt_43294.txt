I am a philosopher of religion who has taught the historical Jesus to undergraduates and graduates for twenty years, and I hold the position I defend every year in that seminar: that the criteria of authenticity — embarrassment, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, coherence, and the traces-of-Aramaic test — while individually weak, function as a cumulative case, the way independent strands of testimony corroborate one another in a courtroom, so that where several criteria converge on a saying or an act we have something close to historical bedrock, and a portrait of Jesus — an eschatological prophet of the Galilean countryside, healer and exorcist, executed after a disturbance in the Temple — can be built up logion by logion with reasonable confidence. Over the years I have added two qualifications that I believe matter. First, I treat the criteria probabilistically: each raises or lowers a likelihood ratio rather than stamping items authentic or inauthentic, and convergence multiplies the ratios rather than merely counting votes. Second, I concede that coherence is not an independent test at all — it is a name for the explanatory fit of the emerging portrait — but I claim this is harmless, since a portrait whose parts explain one another is precisely what a cumulative case aims at.

Tell me what would count against this position. I do not want the pulpit's objection that faith needs no historians, and I do not want blanket skepticism that concludes nothing can be known; I want the specific pressure points, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Consider, for instance, the circularity worry: if the portrait certifies the material that in turn certifies the portrait, what exactly is the cumulative case accumulating? Consider the possibility that early Christian invention mimics authenticity in precisely the cases my criteria handle best — I have heard it said that social memory theory leaves the criteria without work to do, since communal memory neither preserves nor invents in the way the criteria presuppose; is that right, or only partly right, and if only partly, which criteria survive? Consider the charge that dissimilarity, honestly applied, yields a Jesus defined by what he does not share with either his Jewish context or his later church, so that the "bedrock" is a cabinet of curiosities rather than a portrait of a first-century Jew. Distinguish what I as a defender of the position would myself concede counts against it from what critics press but I would bat away; say what the view predicts that its rivals do not — presumably that trained investigators, applying the criteria independently, will converge in their judgments, though I want you to examine whether convergence of judgment is evidence here or merely an artifact of shared training; and tell me honestly whether my two qualifications have absorbed the standard objections or only relocated them. Finally, say where the analogy to converging courtroom testimony is doing real work and where it flatters the method — in a courtroom, after all, the witnesses are interrogated and can be caught lying, and I want to know whether the sources behind the Synoptic Gospels can be made to play that role or whether the analogy quietly assumes they can.
