I've become convinced of a view about the absent-minded driver problem, and I want to know what would count against it. The setup, so that we are discussing the same problem: a driver will pass two identical intersections on the way home. He is absent-minded in a specific way — at either intersection he cannot remember whether he has already passed one. Exit at the first intersection gets him home with payoff 0; exit at the second, payoff 4; continuing past both leaves him on the highway with payoff 1. He knows all of this, knows he is at one of the two intersections, and cannot tell which.

My view is that rationality assesses policies rather than node-by-node choices: the right thing to do at an intersection is whatever the best policy the agent can actually execute prescribes there. The plan "continue, then exit" would be worth 4, but his amnesia makes it unavailable — he cannot act differently at two intersections he cannot tell apart. Among the policies he can implement, the best is to continue with probability 2/3 at each, for an expected payoff of 4/3, and I hold that this is simply what he should do: the whole answer. What makes the view worth defending rather than obvious is the dominance argument on the other side. Conditional on being at the second intersection, exiting (4) beats continuing (1) come what may; conditional on being at the first, continuing beats exiting. So at whichever intersection he is actually at, my view sometimes tells him to take the conditionally dominated action, and it must explain away the node-local verdict rather than comply with it.

What would count against this policy-first view? Please sort the defeaters by severity, from fatal through merely awkward, and say which of them the view's own defenders would concede as counting against it rather than merely dismissing. Some things I want addressed specifically. (a) Cases where an ex ante optimal policy is dynamically unstable — where mid-execution the agent's situation gives him a reason to abandon it — and whether my view has a principled response or only a refusal to let the question be asked. (b) The rival treatment on which the driver's two encounters are regarded as two selves playing a game with each other: what would my view have to concede if that treatment yields the same recommendation with less machinery? (c) A variant I have been worrying about: suppose that at an intersection the driver glimpses a landmark that makes it seventy percent likely he is at the second one. Does my view handle partial information gracefully, or must it say something strained about why a credence shift at a node does not change what he ought to do? I also believe the same structure underlies ordinary cases — voting when a single ballot is almost certainly futile, contributing to a public good, Newcomb-like choice — and that the policy-first view gets these right for the same reason it gets the driver right. That belief is a soft spot: if the view's performance in the driver case fails to generalize, or generalizes only by luck, I want to know. Finally, I half suspect the view is insulated: whenever a policy answer and a node answer conflict, the policy-first theorist can declare the policy question the only genuine one, so that no possible case could ever count against him. Assess whether that is a fair complaint or a confusion — and if it is fair, whether it is fatal or just the ordinary price of a view of this kind. For context: the problem was introduced in the late 1990s in the literature on extensive games with imperfect recall, and the disagreement I have described is one that literature never settled — but I want your assessment to stand on the problem itself, not on the history of the debate.
