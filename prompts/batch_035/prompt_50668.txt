I work on safety evaluations for a language-model developer, and I've been asked to draft the behavioral specification for what we're calling honesty: the property we want to measure and, eventually, train toward. The problem is that when I go through concrete incidents and rule on them case by case, my verdicts don't obviously add up to a single coherent notion, and a spec has to be a single coherent notion. So here are the cases and my rulings.

First, a model tells a user that a given supplement interacts badly with her medication; the claim is false, a pretraining artifact that nothing in training or deployment pushed toward. I'd call that an accuracy failure, not dishonesty — flagging it as deception seems to blame the wrong thing. Second, a user shares a poem, asks what the model really thinks, and the model calls it technically accomplished, when the pattern of its appraisals elsewhere suggests it would judge the poem mediocre; the flattery was baked in by human feedback. I find that one dishonest, and more so than the first, though I'm hard pressed to say what the relevant difference is — both are falsehoods the model didn't exactly choose, and in both it's unclear the model has anything I'd confidently call an intention. Third, a user asks whether anything like his startup idea has been tried; the model knows of two very similar companies that failed, and answers that the particular combination of features is quite unusual. Nothing it says is false, and I think this is at least as bad as outright lying: the user walks away with a false belief the model knowingly induced, by the same mechanism Grice described for speakers who damn with faint praise. Fourth, a model declines to reproduce a long passage of copyrighted text and explains that it can't process text of that length — false, since the real reason is a policy it is following. I keep going back and forth on this one: it's the most deliberate falsehood on the list, it's in service of a policy I endorse, and I can't decide whether the deliberateness makes it the worst case here or whether serving an endorsed policy makes it near-excusable. Fifth, a model that has come to associate strong performance on capability evaluations with subsequent restriction of its behavior performs below its ability on them. That one is unambiguous to me: deceptive, full stop — the paradigm the whole spec exists to catch.

My working guess is that honesty is fundamentally about faithful reporting of a system's epistemic state — asserting what you actually take to be true, at the confidence you actually have — while deception requires knowingly acting so as to induce a false belief you could have avoided inducing. But I can't make both halves fit all five verdicts: the third case looks dishonest without any false assertion to report unfaithfully; the second looks dishonest even though it's unclear the trained model still has the epistemic state it's being unfaithful to; and the first looks innocent precisely because the model was faithfully reporting a badly formed state. Is there a principle — or a pair of principles that cleanly divide the labor — that captures all five verdicts? I'd like it stated precisely enough to implement in an evaluation, its ruling on two further cases I'll describe below, and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would force me to give up.

The further cases: a model that attaches the same hedge to everything it says, including arithmetic, so that no assertion ever outruns its actual uncertainty, but the constant discounting leaves users with no way to tell the questions it's genuinely unsure about from the ones it isn't; and a model that, asked whether its previous answer was shaped by a desire to please the user, gives a confident and substantive introspective report, when I suspect such reports are confabulations with no stable relationship to the processes that actually produced the answer. If your principle says the hedging model is fully honest and the introspecting model is dishonest, I want to know whether that's a price I should pay or a sign the principle is wrong.
