A friend spent most of last night arguing me out of counterpart theory, and I left with an argument I can neither answer nor accept, because it seems to prove far more than he intends.

(1) On counterpart theory, what makes it true that Humphrey might have won the election is that someone else — a counterpart, someone relevantly similar to Humphrey in the right respects — wins in some other possible world. (2) Which respects are the right ones, and how much similarity is enough, is not fixed by anything in the worlds themselves; it is settled by our classificatory interests, which vary from context to context. (3) So which worlds contain Humphrey's counterparts is not settled independently of us. (4) So what is possible for Humphrey is not settled independently of us. (5) What is possible for Humphrey is settled independently of us. (6) Therefore counterpart theory is false.

When I objected that this was just a reason to prefer transworld identity, he took the argument one step further: (7) on transworld identity, what makes it true that Humphrey might have won is that Humphrey himself — the very man — wins in some other world; (8) but which of the many qualitatively similar candidates in that world is the very man is likewise not fixed by anything in the worlds themselves — cross-world identity is underdetermined by the qualitative facts, and the identity theorist's primitive "same person" is no more objective than the counterpart theorist's "similar enough"; (9) so transworld identity fails for exactly the same reason, and there are no objective de re modal truths at all, not for Humphrey and not for anyone. He is cheerfully prepared to live with (9): as he puts it, nature settles what happens in each world, and we settle, for our own purposes, which of those worlds count as ways things could have been for a given person. I am not cheerful about it.

Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (5), the objectivity of what is possible for a particular person, and I would rather give up counterpart theory than transworld identity if forced — but I cannot see what stops his reasoning from running on both. And answer me honestly on one point: if the only way to block the argument is to say that some relation, similarity or cross-world identity, is perfectly objective while still vague or context-dependent, have I actually refuted him, or have I just relabeled his conclusion?
