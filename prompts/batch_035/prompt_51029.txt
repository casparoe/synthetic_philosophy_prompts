Just got out of a two-hour argument with my roommate about Buridan's ass — the donkey that dies between two identical haystacks because it has no reason to prefer one — and I want to see how someone else would carry it forward, because we hit a wall.

My position: when options are exactly tied, just picking one is perfectly rational. Demanding a reason for every choice is a confusion — "they're equally good and I need to eat" is all the reason there needs to be. His position is stranger and honestly kind of interesting: he thinks the paradox exposes a genuine hole in the idea that rational choice is reason-governed. If every choice must be determined by reasons, and the reasons run out in a tie, then something OTHER than reasons has to break the symmetry — and once you grant that, rational choice isn't fully reason-driven anymore, which he finds quietly devastating rather than a curiosity about donkeys.

He also insists that flipping a mental coin doesn't rescue anything, because the coin landing heads isn't a reason, it's just arbitrariness laundered through a procedure. I told him that's a sleight of hand: a policy of randomizing among tied options can itself be adopted for reasons (it guarantees you eat, in expectation), so the arbitrariness is contained inside a rationally justified frame. He says that just moves the bulge under the rug, since something still had to make THIS choice come out one way rather than the other, and the policy can't be it. Around and around we went.

Write a dialogue between the two of us continuing this argument — call me Ada and him Marcus. Put them in the middle of an actual tied-choice situation (two identical apartments they're viewing together, two equally-ranked items on a menu, something like that) so the argument happens while one of them genuinely has to decide. Both need to land real hits: Marcus shouldn't be a hand-wringer who just repeats "but reasons!", and Ada shouldn't win by default just because she's relaxed. Let Marcus argue at some point that exact ties never really occur — there's always some tiny asymmetry in the agent or the options — and let Ada press on whether that asymmetry is a reason or merely a cause of the choice, since that distinction is exactly where we got stuck last night. End it with one of them actually choosing, and make the ending feel earned, not like a dodge.
