I teach an upper-level seminar on ethics and emerging technology, and I've been grading seminar papers on responsibility gaps all weekend, which has forced me to notice that my own margin comments are inconsistent in ways I can't defend. So let me lay out my actual verdicts on a set of cases and ask you to find the principle behind them, if there is one, or tell me which verdicts have to go.

Case one: a military commander deploys an autonomous strike system in a mode where it selects its own targets within a designated zone, and it destroys a vehicle that turns out to be civilian. I want to say the commander is responsible — not for the particular strike, exactly, but for something, and I find I'm not sure what. Case two: a self-driving car encounters an unavoidable collision and executes an evasive pattern that kills the passenger of the other car rather than its own. Here my intuition is that no one is responsible at all, and that this is fine — bad luck, like a deer running into the road. Case three: interacting trading algorithms from different firms produce a flash crash that wipes out pension value. I feel the firms are collectively responsible even though no individual engineer wrote anything defective; each firm's code did exactly what it was specified to do. Case four: a hospital's triage algorithm deprioritizes an elderly patient who then dies, and the attending physician, uneasy but deferring to the system, does not intervene. Here I do blame a person — the physician — even though she neither wrote the algorithm nor had a better basis for the contrary judgment, and I'm aware this sits awkwardly with case one, where I absolved the operators who deferred to the machine. Case five: a household robot knocks an heirloom off a shelf. Trivial, but I notice I treat it exactly like a gust of wind — no responsibility anywhere, not even the manufacturer. And then the pair that's really bothering me: in one case, an industrial system produces a foreseeable-in-principle but unforeseen harm because its designers never modeled a rare interaction between components — I want to hold the designers responsible. In another, a surgical robot's motor fails in a way whose probability was genuinely unknown to the engineers — there I feel no one is responsible, and I can't articulate the difference between "unforeseen" and "unknowable" in a way that doesn't make responsibility for design hinge on luck.

So: is there a principle — perhaps something about control, or foreseeability, or the difference between the machine extending an agent's practical reasoning versus merely being an instrument with a gap in it — that captures these verdicts? I'd like you to state the best candidate precisely, show which verdict motivates each clause, tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't considered (say, whether the procurement officer who chose the cheaper triage system over a vetted one bears responsibility in case four), and be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and would have to overturn. I'd particularly like you to address whether case two and case five really should be treated alike, and whether my instinct that the trading firms in case three can be collectively responsible while none of their engineers are individually responsible is coherent or just mood. If your conclusion is that the only consistent option is to overturn several verdicts, say so directly — I'd rather repair my intuitions before Thursday's seminar than have a student do it for me in front of everyone.
