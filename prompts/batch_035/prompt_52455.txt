so I've been in this running argument about the absent-minded driver problem and before I keep defending my position I want to check that it even hangs together. quick setup so we're picturing the same thing: a driver is on the highway at night and needs to take the SECOND exit, but he's absent-minded in a specific sense — at an intersection he can't tell whether it's the first or the second, and he can't remember whether he's already been through one. at each intersection his only options are "continue" and "exit". payoffs: exiting at the first intersection gets him 0, exiting at the second gets him 4, continuing past both (missing his exit) gets him 1. since he can't tell the intersections apart, any plan he can actually carry out has to be the same at both of them — so the only pure plans are always-exit (worth 0, he'd exit at the first and never see the second) and always-continue (worth 1). a mixed plan that continues with probability p is worth p² + 4(1−p)p, maximized at p = 2/3 for a value of 4/3.

ok, here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) decisions are really choices of policy, and the right standard is how the whole policy does, evaluated from before you know which node you're at. in this problem that gives the 2/3 answer and I'm pretty confident in it.

(2) if there were a sign at the second intersection, or an angel just told him which intersection he's at, the answer is trivially continue-then-exit for 4. free information, you take it, no deliberation needed.

(3) when he's at an intersection and genuinely can't tell, his credence that it's the first one should be 1/2. the two intersections are perfectly symmetric and nothing he has access to favors one over the other.

(4) randomizing is only rational between options you're indifferent about. if you strictly prefer one act, flipping a coin about it is just irrational, full stop.

(5) now suppose instead he simply wakes up at an intersection with no memory of anything, not even of having planned a trip. I'd definately still tell him to do the 2/3 thing — the ex-ante vantage can always be reconstructed after the fact.

(6) there's one driver. I don't accept the framing where the first intersection and the second get treated as two agents playing a game against each other. thats a metaphor at best, not decision theory.
