A friend of mine who defends the modal argument against physicalism keeps leaning on an analogy I'd like audited. Conceivability, she says, is to possibility roughly as perceptual experience is to actuality: an episode of clear conceiving is defeasible evidence that something is possible, the way a clear look at a surface is defeasible evidence about how things are. Illusion in the source domain — the tilted coin that looks elliptical, the stick that looks bent in water — corresponds to modal error, cases where we conceive something impossible. And her notion of "ideal" conceivability, conceiving after full rational reflection with all relevant information in view, corresponds to looking under good conditions with functioning eyes. The analogy is doing real argumentative work: it is meant to answer the stock objection that since we can apparently conceive water not being H2O, or conceive Goldbach's conjecture turning out false, conceivability tells us nothing about possibility — her reply being that perceptual evidence also survives the existence of illusions, and for the same structural reason.

Please audit the analogy. First, lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the visual experience, the external world, the viewing conditions, the illusion, the ideal observer, and anything else that must be mapped for the comparison to be well-formed. Then locate where it breaks. I have two particular worries, but add your own. (i) In perception we have an independent error theory: we can explain, without consulting any particular disputed case, why the coin looks elliptical — perspective, projection, lighting. If there is modal error, is there an independently motivated account of why it arises that the analogy can be checked against, or does defending the analogy require invoking the very machinery the stock objection was relying on — for instance, a distinction between epistemic and metaphysical ways of grasping a proposition — so that the analogy no longer answers the objection but merely relocates the dispute? (ii) Idealization behaves differently in the two domains: "looking harder" is a process with known endpoints and known limits, whereas "conceiving under ideal conditions" may build the desired verdict into the method itself — though perhaps you disagree.

Finally, tell me whether her conclusion — that the conceivability of a zombie world is at least prima facie evidence of its possibility, enough to shift the burden onto the physicalist — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time. If the analogy is beyond repair, say what a better one for her position would look like, or explain why none is available. Please answer in American English.
