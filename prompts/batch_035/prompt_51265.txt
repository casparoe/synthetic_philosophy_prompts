My department has two people who've been at each other for a decade about Kripke and I got roped into writing up the aftermath. One is a hardcore Kripkean: proper names are rigid designators, identities like Hesperus/Phosphorus are necessary but known a posteriori, and this genuinely revolutionized how we think about modality and the analytic/synthetic distinction. The other isn't an old-school descriptivist exactly -- she's more of a two-dimensionalist type -- and her line is that the Kripkean "necessary a posteriori" mostly rests on running together two different ways of evaluating sentences (how things are given how we actually fixed reference, versus how things could have been, full stop), and once you keep those apart the revolution looks a lot smaller. They finally agreed to do a proper adversarial collaboration, with me as the notetaker, and now I have to produce the joint document. Can you draft it for me? Specifically I need:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. Not padding or platitudes -- real shared ground: about rigid designation as a property of how a term functions in modal contexts, about the fact that we learned 'water is H2O' empirically, about what the modal arguments (Aristotle might not have taught Alexander, etc.) actually did to the simplest version of descriptivism, and whatever else they genuinely share.

2. A disagreements table after that. Each row should state one disagreement in wording that the side it's attributed to would actually accept for their own view -- so the Kripkean's phrasing of "water is H2O is necessary" and the two-dimensionalist's phrasing of why that's only necessity-relative-to-actual-reference-fixing should both be phrased so their own side would put their name to it. One row should definately cover whether rigid designation is a fact about names themselves or an artifact of how we deploy names when doing modal reasoning.

3. The cruxes. I suspect one is whether the conceivability of Hesperus not being Phosphorus is genuine evidence of a possibility, or just a trick played by the descriptions we happen to entertain the objects under. And another is whether there are any genuinely new necessary a posteriori truths to be found, or whether once you see the mechanism you can generate them mechanically and the notion loses its mystique. Identify others if there are real ones.

4. For each crux, a case or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance (so it's falsifiable). They want at least one fresh test case in there, so use this one: suppose that due to a long-running measurement error, astronomers believe for fifty years that the bright morning star and the bright evening star are two distinct bodies, and coin two separate rigid names for them, M-star and E-star, complete with orbital calculations for each. Then the error gets discovered. What's the epistemic and modal status of "M-star is E-star"? Each side should commit to a prediction about whether the community ever counted as knowing something a priori, and what happens to the names after discovery.

5. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal -- e.g. they're both using "necessary" but meaning different restrictions by it, and would agree under an explicit stipulation -- say exactly which part and why. But don't cop out and say it's all verbal, they'd both find that insulting.

Tone can be relaxed since this is an internal working paper, but they're both picky about the word "semantic" being used as a hand-wave, so be precise with that one.
