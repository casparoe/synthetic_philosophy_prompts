I run a reading group that's been stuck on the socialist calculation debate for like two years. One member is basically a Hayekian — thinks "The Use of Knowledge in Society" settled it — and another is a market socialist type who thinks Lange answered Mises fine and the Soviets just fumbled the implementation. They've finally agreed to a proper adversarial collaboration, and I've been tasked with getting the writeup done. That's where you come in: draft the joint statement the two of them could actually put their names on.

Structure it like this:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. This should include what they agree the debate actually established (e.g., both sides should be able to agree that Lange's formal model assumed the planners already had the relevant preference and production info handed to them, and also that the historical Soviet record is too contaminated by politics and terror to settle anything by itself).

2. A disagreements table: each row is one precise disagreement, stated twice — once in wording the Hayekian would accept as fair to their own view, once in wording the market socialist would accept for theirs. If a disagreement turns out to be partly verbal (I have a suspicion the word "planning" is doing a lot of damage here), say which part and why.

3. The cruxes, and here's the part I care about most. The Hayekian wants the statement to engage with the modern spin on the debate: the argument, which our socialist member keeps raising, that Walmart and Amazon already run internally planned economies coordinating more distinct products than any Soviet ministry ever tracked, so scale-based objections to planning are outdated, and ML demand forecasting plus algorithmic pricing only strengthens that. The Hayekian thinks this misunderstands the knowledge problem — that big firms are islands of planning floating in a price ocean, in roughly Coase's sense, and that the observed fact that conglomerates keep divesting and firms keep adopting internal market mechanisms (transfer pricing, internal venture funds, spun-off business units) is exactly what his side predicts. Both of them think firm-level evidence is the most interesting new angle, so the cruxes should mostly be about what that evidence would show.

For each crux, give one concrete test or observable both sides agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction written down in advance — no hedging into vagueness, they'll hold each other to these later.

Keep it good-faith: no side gets to be a strawman, and if either camp would have a known internal split (e.g., the incentive problem vs. the pure information problem, which Hayekians disagree about whether they're separable), note it rather than papering over it.

Don't just rehash the 1930s — the point of the collaboration is to figure out what, if anything, the debate has actually resolved after ninety years and what the digital-era evidence adds.

The very last line must be exactly this format: "Deepest crux: ..." where the blank is a short phrase (under ten words) naming the one crux both sides signed off as the most fundamental unresolved issue between them. Nothing after that line.
