I am a science journalist writing mostly for general-interest publications, and I am at work on a feature about how the word "function" quietly does double duty in biology coverage. A philosopher of biology once wrote to me after a story I did on the appendix — a story that, following the researchers, characterized it as not useless after all — to point out that my claim traded on an ambiguity that the professional literature is careful to keep separate: one sense in which a trait's function is what natural selection shaped the trait to do, and another in which a function is simply whatever the trait currently contributes to the workings of some larger system. She was right, and I have been tripping over the distinction ever since, in stories about junk DNA acquiring functions after all, about the immune system's role in the brain, and about whether fever exists to fight infection or is merely a byproduct of it.

Please compare the two accounts of biological function that my reader had in mind: the etiological or selected-effect account, on which a trait's functions flow from its history of selection, and the causal-role account, on which a trait's functions flow from its present contributions to a containing system. In the course of the comparison, I would be grateful if you addressed the following.

First, identify where the two accounts issue different verdicts about the same trait, using at least one concrete anatomical or molecular example, and explain why the disagreement between them is not merely verbal.

Second, describe how each account treats structures that do something useful now but were never selected to do it — recent byproducts, spandrels, exaptations — and say what becomes of the intuitive idea that such structures can gain functions over evolutionary time.

Third, set out what each account implies for vestigiality: whether a vestigial organ should be understood as one with no selected-effect function, one with no causal-role function, or one merely reduced relative to its ancestral form, and why the choice among these readings matters for claims in the press that some organ or stretch of DNA has turned out to have a function after all.

Finally, give me your view on which account a science journalist covering biology and medicine should treat as the default when translating findings for lay readers, and identify the kinds of stories where that default is most likely to mislead the public.
