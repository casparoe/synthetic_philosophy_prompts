Quick question about the extended mind thing. In our phil of mind class we got the famous Otto and Inga case: Otto writes everything in his notebook, Inga just remembers. The parity claim is basically that Otto's notebook plays the same functional role for him that Inga's biological memory plays for her — so if her memory counts as part of her mind, his notebook should too. My professor said this analogy is doing basically ALL the argumentative work for the whole thesis, not just illustrating it. Meanwhile my roommate says it's obviously a bad analogy, that the notebook is more like a prosthetic leg — a tool you've learned to use really well, sure, but nobody says the prosthetic is part of your body just because you trust it and reach for it without thinking. Can you audit the Otto/Inga comparison? Like, actually map what's supposed to correspond to what (availability, trust, the way Otto consults it without deliberation, the fact it was endorsed by past-Otto, whatever else), then show where the mapping breaks down, and tell me which of the breakdowns actually matter for the conclusion. I'm especially confused about the asymmetry cases: Inga's memory can't be borrowed or stolen or read by others, and she can't consult it before deciding whether to consult it — do those differences sink the parity claim or are they just disanalogies that don't do any real work? And if the notebook analogy fails, does the extended mind thesis go with it, or does the prosthetic-leg counter-analogy have its own problems (people with prosthetics do talk about them as parts of themselves, don't they?). Also if there's a better analogy somewhere in the neighborhood I'd like to hear it.
