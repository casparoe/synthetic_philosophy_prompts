I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a broadsheet, and I'm at work on a long-read about a quiet schism in the theory of causation — one that, oddly, the two camps barely acknowledge in each other's presence. On one side stand the philosophers, following David Lewis, who analyse causation as counterfactual dependence between particular events: roughly, the throwing caused the shattering when, had the throw not occurred, the glass would not have shattered — where that last claim is settled by a similarity ordering over possible worlds, one of whose criteria prefers worlds that deviate from ours only through a small, local departure from the laws rather than through vast rearrangements of history. On the other side stand the statisticians and computer scientists, following Judea Pearl, who analyse causation through surgical intervention: the throw is a cause of the shattering when doing the throw, in a causal model held fixed except for the throw's downstream effects, changes what happens to the glass.

Please write a comparison of these two approaches, roughly 900–1,000 words, pitched at an educated general readership who have never seen a causal graph or a possible world. Cover, in whatever order serves you:

First, what each theory takes causation to be, in plain terms, and the analytical machinery each brings to bear: possible worlds and a similarity ordering on one side; causal graphs, structural equations and the do-operator on the other.

Second, where they diverge in their verdicts. I'm told there are cases where one framework finds causation and the other doesn't, or finds it only awkwardly. Preemption and overdetermination are the classic embarrassments for pure dependence accounts, and I gather there are equally awkward cases on the interventionist side — for instance, when the intervention is not a free, exogenous act but is entangled with the very system under study, as when a doctor's decision to prescribe is itself evidence about her diagnosis, or when a patient's changed diet signals something about her prognosis. Set these out honestly; don't pretend either framework sweeps all before it.

Third, a claim I keep hearing from both camps, and want to adjudicate in print: that Pearl's graph surgery is really Lewis's nearest-world analysis in disguise, an intervention being a kind of stipulated minor miracle. Is the resemblance deep or superficial? In particular, does Lewis's orientation towards particular events leave him without the resources to express population-level facts of the Simpson's-reversal kind — where a treatment appears to help in every subpopulation and yet to harm in the aggregate — which the interventionist can state almost trivially as the difference between conditioning and doing? Can the counterfactual theorist repair this gap, or is the mismatch principled?

Finally, a question from my editor: which framework should a newspaper adopt when it reports, as we regularly do, that a study has established a cause — and does the honest answer depend on whether the study was an experiment or merely observational?

Some ground rules. British English throughout. No footnotes, no bibliography and no citations of papers; if you name a thinker at all, let it be at most Lewis and Pearl, and sparingly. Do not invent quotations from anyone. Introduce any notation you use, and keep the mathematics to what an arts graduate could follow with patience.

A rough draft is fine; I'm more interested in whether you get the shape of the disagreement right than in polish.
