I mostly cover science for a general-audience magazine, but they've given me a slot in the back-of-book satire section this month and I'd rather not do another "scientists discover thing everyone already knew" piece. Write an Onion-style news article — headline plus 400-500 words — about a local woman who, after a 3 a.m. Wikipedia spiral into Bertrand's paradox, has become unable to assign probabilities to anything new. The jokes have to come from getting the epistemolgy right, not from lampshading it: the chord problem where the probability of beating the inscribed equilateral triangle is 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 depending on which of three equally natural randomization procedures you describe (random endpoints, random midpoint on a radius, random midpoint in the disk), Keynes's point that the principle of indifference silently demands a privileged partition of the outcomes that nothing in the world actually supplies, and van Fraassen's cube factory — where assuming uniformity over side lengths versus over surface areas gives contradictory answers for the same factory. Important: she's still perfectly fine with dice and coin flips, the genuinely symmetric cases, which makes her condition worse and funnier — it's only when the choice of partition is up to her that she freezes. Include one quote from her neighbor, a supremely confident maximum-entropist who keeps insisting Jaynes settled all this with transformation groups and invariance arguments, and one deadpan quote from her about how "equally likely" is doing all the work and nobody's paying it. Keep it deadpan throughout — no winking at the reader — and don't let the piece imply the principle of indifference is simply dumb; the target of the satire is overconfidence about which description of a problem is the natural one, not the math itself.
