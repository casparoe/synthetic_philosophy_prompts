The question set was:

"Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel are credited with showing that moral luck appears both real and impossible. Explain why the problem arises, using Williams's lorry driver and Gauguin cases and Nagel's taxonomy of luck, and assess how a defender of the Control Principle might respond."

The student's answer reads, in full:

"Moral luck arises when factors beyond an agent's control affect how morally assessable the agent is. The Control Principle says that we are morally assessable only to the extent that what we are assessed for depends on factors under our control, and its corollary says that two people ought not to be assessed differently if the only differences between them are due to factors beyond their control. Williams and Nagel both accept this principle, and that is precisely why they conclude that moral luck is real: since luck demonstrably does make a difference to our assessments, the principle must be given up.

Williams illustrates the tension with a lorry driver whose lorry, through no fault of his, veers onto the pavement and kills a child. The driver was not negligent; the outcome was pure resultant luck, since a second driver who drove identically on an empty street would have harmed no one. Williams argues that the first driver's feelings are not mere agent-regret but a form of moral guilt, since guilt is the appropriate response to having caused a death, even though the driver did nothing the second driver did not also do. This shows that our actual practice violates the Control Principle.

The Gauguin case shows the same thing on the side of resultant luck. Gauguin abandons his family to paint, and Williams says the decision is retrospectively justified if he turns out to be a great painter, but not otherwise. Since whether he becomes a great painter is largely beyond his control, his moral standing is hostage to luck.

Williams also gives the now-standard taxonomy of four types of moral luck: resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, and causal. Causal luck is essentially the problem of free will, since our actions are consequences of antecedent events outside our control. Circumstantial luck is illustrated by the German who would have led a decent life had he not happened to live through the Nazi period; constitutive luck by the fact that temperament is shaped by upbringing and genes.

A defender of the Control Principle could reply that the differences in our reactions reflect differences in what agents are responsible for, not in how blameworthy they are: the unlucky driver is responsible for a death in a way the lucky one is not, but both are equally blameworthy. Still, the practice of the courts is inconsistent with the Control Principle, since the harmful driver receives a heavier sentence than the one who ran the same light harmlessly; if the principle is true, the courts should impose identical penalties, so the law as it stands is morally in error."
