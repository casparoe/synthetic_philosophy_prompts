I cover science and ideas for a magazine whose editorial board I also sit on, and I need a written opinion before our next planning meeting. We are putting together a special issue to mark our centenary, provisionally titled Turning Points, built around long-form essays on moments in the history of science and public life that could plausibly have gone the other way: a vaccine trial that nearly lost its funding, a datacentre fire in the 1960s, a treaty negotiation that came down to a single phone call. The board is split three ways. One faction argues that publishing counterfactuals of this kind betrays our evidence-based mandate — that such essays are dressed-up fiction, unfalsifiable by construction, and that any claim of the form "had the funding been cut, the vaccine would have been delayed by a decade" can never be checked against anything. A second faction counters that our own retrospective coverage is already saturated with implicit counterfactuals — every claim that a discovery was "made possible by" a particular institution, or that a catastrophe was "waiting to happen," quietly asserts knowledge of what would otherwise have occurred, so the choice is between counterfactuals we acknowledge and counterfactuals we smuggle in. A third group is happy with the concept but wants each essay constrained to the "near miss" framing — small, well-documented contingencies — on the theory that this keeps the speculation honest, though some of them admit privately that near misses make for the least interesting reading.

The dispute has become oddly heated, and I suspect it is not really about editorial standards but about something underneath: whether history is a set of contingent accidents held together by narrative convenience, or whether the outcomes we observe were broadly overdetermined by structural forces that would have reasserted themselves anyway. I have to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday, and I want it to do three things. It should recommend a concrete course of action — kill the issue, proceed with the near-miss constraint, proceed without it, or something better you can devise — with reasoning that the losing factions will recognise as having taken their strongest point seriously rather than dismissing it. It should say something principled about when, if ever, an untestable claim about the past can legitimately appear in a publication that markets itself as rigorous — our fact-checkers cannot verify a world that did not happen, but perhaps rigour does not always reduce to verification. And it should give our contributors practical guidance, in the form of criteria an editor could actually apply to a draft, rather than an abstract lecture on the philosophy of history. Please write the memo in British English, in my voice, at a length I could circulate without trimming.
