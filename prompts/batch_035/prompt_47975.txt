ok so heres something my brother in law and i keep going around on. we're trying to settle whether "life" is a real category out there in the world or just a family-resemblance mess that only feels unified, and our plan was to grade the standard candidate definitions against a fixed set of hard cases and see if any definition actually wins. I want you to role-play as a philosophy of biology person who's seen all these fights before and has actual opinions, not someone who hedges everything into mush. Please brainstorm before committing to anything — like, actually sketch in a sentence or two what each definition says about each of the hard cases, THEN do the scoring. think it through carefully.

the candidate accounts of life:

1. the NASA-flavored one: a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution (roughly what the NASA working definition says, iirc often credited to Gerald Joyce, though he's apparently pushed back on that attribution)
2. autopoiesis, the Maturana and Varela thing: an entity is living iff it's an organized network of processes that produces its own components and maintains the very boundary that makes the network possible
3. the thermodynamic account, Schrödinger-descended: living systems are far-from-equilibrium systems that hold their internal entropy low by dissipating energy, like a whirlpool that keeps itself going
4. the junior-high checklist: metabolism, growth, response to stimuli, reproduction, made of cells
5. the historical/genetic one: x is alive iff x is descended from the last universal common ancestor — so hypothetical aliens, or even a second, independent genesis of life on earth, would not count as alive, only as life-like

and our test battery, stuff we picked precisely because its annoying:

- a mule (sterile, otherwise a perfectly ordinary horselike organism)
- a worker honeybee (sterile, but part of a reproducing colony)
- a HeLa cell in a dish, dividing indefinitely
- a tardigrade that's been freeze-dried and dormant for twelve years
- a virus
- a prion
- the self-replicating clay crystals from Cairns-Smith's origin-of-life hypothesis
- a self-reproducing machine, von Neumann probe style, built on the moon out of lunar ore
- a mitochondrion inside one of your cells right now, considered on its own rather than as part of the cell
- a minimal synthetic cell with a fully synthesized genome, J. Craig Venter style

give each of the five accounts a score from 0 to 10 over the whole battery, with a couple sentences per account on where it took its biggest hit and where it surprisingly did fine. then give me the final ranking, top to bottom.

then the part i actually care about: after the ranking, tell me honestly whether this exercise measures anything at all. my worry is that the battery just contains the cases the winner was reverse-engineered around — the Darwinian definition was built to say yes to cells and no to viruses, so of course it aces my list, that's not a discovery, that's circular. is there any way to run a definition-vs-cases comparison that isn't rigged by whoever picked the cases, or is it doomed by construction? and if it's doomed, does that count as real evidence for the family-resemblance view, or is that also unfalsifiable in the same sneaky way? don't go easy on my battery either — if you'd throw some of my cases out or add others, say which and why.
