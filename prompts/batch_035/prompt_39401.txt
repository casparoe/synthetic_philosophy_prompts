I teach a graduate seminar in political philosophy, and this term I've had a thoroughgoing libertarian student (who holds that taxing labor income is morally on a par with forced taking) and a social-democratic student (who holds that the taxation regimes of decent states are not merely permissible but sometimes required) arguing past each other all semester. You are a professional political philosopher with experience facilitating adversarial collaborations. Draft the joint statement these two could both sign after a year of good-faith engagement on the question of whether taxation is theft.

Before drafting, brainstorm carefully about where the positions genuinely converge — e.g., that forcible taking by a criminal gang is wrong, that states differ from protection rackets in morally relevant respects, that some uses of state power really are robbery in all but name — and only then about where they diverge. The document should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims, including substantive ones about the moral difference between states and protection rackets, the significance (or insignificance) of consent and exit, and the role that public goods and the legal infrastructure of markets play in making income possible in the first place.

2. A disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is phrased so that the side holding the view would accept the wording. Pay special attention to two suspected fault lines: whether the pre-tax distribution is a morally privileged baseline or is itself a legal artifact (so that calling taxation "taking" already smuggles in a controversial view of property), and whether the parties are using "theft" as a thick moral term or merely as a term of art for any non-consensual transfer.

3. The cruxes — sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party — each paired with an argument, thought experiment, or empirical case that both parties agree bears on it, with each party's prediction recorded in advance. Consider, for instance, a minimal state funded entirely by lotteries and user fees, or a society in which emigration is genuinely costless.

If you conclude that part of the dispute is merely verbal — about the word "theft" rather than about the moral status of taxation — say which part and why, while keeping the substantive remainder as exact as possible. The finished statement should be one both students would recognize as faithful, neither watered down nor slanted toward either camp.
