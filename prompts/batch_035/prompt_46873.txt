Six years ago I co-founded a small climate-analytics company, and I am now staring at the decision of whether to wind it down, so the question of sunk costs has stopped being a seminar topic for me and become the thing I think about at 3 a.m. My sister Dana, who trained in decision science and has appointed herself the family's referee on all matters of alleged fallacious reasoning, has a track record of rulings on these questions that I've started writing down, because I can't figure out what rule she's actually running. She has ruled it irrational to keep reading a dull 900-page novel with only 150 pages left, irrational for friends to stay in a mediocre nine-year relationship purely because of the years invested, irrational for a city council to finish a half-built stadium because the first two hundred million is already spent (she calls this the Concorde fallacy), and irrational to attend a play you dread rather than let non-refundable tickets go to waste. But she ruled it perfectly rational when she herself finished the last two exams of a professional certification that no longer had any bearing on her career, on the grounds that walking away at ninety percent would make the previous eight years a joke and she would have to know that about herself; and she refuses to call it a fallacy that her uncle keeps the family's failing cannoli shop open past all commercial sense, because his late father's entire working life is in that shop and closing it would mean something she won't reduce to a spreadsheet.

Here is my problem. Three new cases are converging on our family WhatsApp group, and I want your prediction of her verdict on each before Thanksgiving, plus a reconstruction of the rule she's implicitly using — and, just as importantly, of the other rules that would fit her six earlier rulings just as well, since I can already see at least two (something backward-looking about not rendering past sacrifice meaningless, versus something about identity and what quitting would say about who you are) that fit everything she's said so far but would come apart on some of the new cases. Case one is mine: continue the startup for another eighteen months of runway, even though I no longer believe the central hypothesis, because six years and my team's faith are in it. Case two is her neighbor Ruth, a sixty-eight-year-old retired actuary who has spent nineteen years writing a four-thousand-page fantasy saga that literally no one knows exists; she has lost all pleasure in it, expects no reader ever, and reports that she stopped thinking of herself as a writer two years ago, but could finish the final volume with two more joyless years. Case three is her friend Marcus, who publicly promised the concluding volume of a trilogy and now finds finishing it pure drudgery, with fans and a publisher waiting. For each case, tell me which of Dana's earlier rulings is the closest analog, which candidate rule predicts what, and your best guess with reasons. Then tell me what single further case I could pose to her over turkey that would best discriminate between the backward-looking rule and the identity rule — the one engineered so that honoring sunk costs makes sense on exactly one of them.

Please give the reasoned analysis first, in prose, with no citations or reading lists — I only care about the logic. Then end with a single machine-readable line of the form "Verdict: startup=irrational; saga=irrational; trilogy=rational" (each label being simply Dana's ruling on *continuing*, i.e., whether she would call it a sunk-cost fallacy or not), so I can log your predictions and compare them against hers.
