I convene a second-year module on Presocratic philosophy, and the opening seminar of the term — a single two-hour session — is devoted to Zeno of Elea. I want your help planning it, though not in the way you might expect: do not explain Zeno to me; I have taught the paradoxes of motion before. What I want is a plan for the explanation, because I have decided to change the syllabus this year in a way that creates problems I have not worked through.

The change is this: rather than leading with the paradoxes of motion, I want the seminar to focus on the arguments for the thesis that there cannot be many things — the plurality arguments as reconstructed from the fragments and doxography, and in particular the dilemma that if there were many, each of them would have to be both so small as to have no magnitude (since otherwise any one of them, being divisible, would itself be many) and so large as to be unlimited in extent (since, having magnitude, it has parts between which there are always further parts). The paradoxes of motion — Achilles, the dichotomy, the arrow — will appear only at the end, if at all, as an appendix, and the stadium and the moving-rows will not appear at all.

My audience is the obstacle. The cohort is mostly joint mathematics-and-philosophy students who have completed a first course in real analysis; they arrive with a set of cached, half-learned responses. They believe that Zeno was a confused man whom Cauchy refuted in the nineteenth century; they believe that a convergent series, once displayed, ends the discussion; and they have a positive allergy to anything that smells of denying that the sum of an infinite series can be finite. My worry is that if I teach the plurality arguments in the standard way, they will hear them as a botched anticipation of measure theory, file them accordingly, and learn nothing — but I also suspect that their analysis training, deployed properly rather than fought, is the best instrument I have for making the arguments bite. I have a view that the real payoff of the session is getting them to see that the horn of the dilemma about unlimited magnitude cannot be dissolved by the tools they already possess, and I would like the plan to either vindicate that view or tell me honestly that it is wrong.

Please produce, in British English, a teaching plan with the following components. First, the order in which the pieces must be introduced, with the dependency structure made explicit: what has to be established about the target of the arguments (a point my cohort will not know — that these are, on most readings, arguments on behalf of Parmenides against plurality, not puzzles stumbled into) before the dilemma itself is even stated; and within the dilemma, which horn must go first and why. Second, the single carrying example that will do the work of the whole session. I am inclined to use the seminar room itself — the cohort as a collection of many things — so that the first horn can be run on the students themselves: each of you has magnitude, so each of you has parts, so you are many, so we have not yet reached the many that the hypothesis promised us. Assess whether that example can carry the second horn as well, or whether it necessarily breaks there, and propose an alternative if it does. Explain also why I should not build the session around Achilles even as a foil: I suspect the motion paradoxes have been so pre-digested in their prior education that mentioning them early would activate exactly the cached responses I am trying to dislodge, and I want this suspicion confirmed or corrected.

Third, the misconception map: the misconceptions this specific audience will predictably form, the order in which they will surface as the sequence unfolds, and the exact point at which each should be headed off. I can already name three candidates — the belief that the standard theory of infinite sums constitutes a refutation, the belief that the arguments trade on an equivocation between 'many' as a predicate of collections and 'many' as a predicate of parts, and the belief that Zeno's conclusion was meant as anything other than a reductio — but I want you to extend the list, and in particular to tell me what misconception the second horn will generate in students who can prove, and will want to prove, that no infinite bounded set has an unbounded sum of pairwise distances between adjacent elements, and where that belongs in the sequence. Fourth, one question I can put to the room in the final ten minutes whose answers will tell me whether the dilemma has actually landed, as opposed to whether the students can merely recite its structure back to me.

Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so to the students — I want the omissions named as omissions, with a one-sentence promise about where each is picked up later in the module, rather than silently skipped. My own list includes the full Parmenidean setting of the one-many problem and any engagement with supertask machinery such as lamps that switch after infinitely many operations, but I am unsure whether the grainless argument or an appeal to the distinction between 'as many as they are' and 'infinitely many' belongs on that list of exclusions or belongs in the seminar's core. Decide that for me, and defend the decision.
