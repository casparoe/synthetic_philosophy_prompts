I'd like your help stress-testing the package of views I use to guide my charitable giving and, more broadly, my sense of what to work on. I suspect at least two of them are quietly at war, but I can't see where. Here is the set:

1. I accept the importance–tractability–neglectedness framework for cause selection: the expected value of additional resources devoted to a cause is, roughly, the product of how big the problem is, how much of it a given amount of resources can solve, and how few people are working on it — and the rational move is to back the cause that scores highest on that product.
2. I am a hedonistic totalist: wellbeing is what matters, creating happy people is as good as making existing people happy, and the moral weight of the far future therefore dwarfs everything else.
3. I believe we are deeply clueless about the long run: for almost any action available today, we cannot reliably determine even the sign of its effects on the world three centuries out. The only partial exception is very coarse indicators, such as the probability that humanity avoids extinction this century.
4. I nonetheless direct the bulk of my giving to far-future-oriented causes, on the reasoning that importance swamps the other factors.
5. I think neglectedness should be measured by the impact of the next effectiveness-oriented dollar, not by total resources flowing to a cause — so something like climate change can absorb trillions in mainstream spending and still count as neglected by our lights.
6. I favor a diversified portfolio, spreading resources roughly evenly across the handful of causes that come out near the top, rather than going all-in on the single highest-ranked cause.

Please take the role of a philosopher trained in ethics and decision theory, with a serious working knowledge of the effective-altruism literature. Before writing your final answer, brainstorm what each of these commitments entails beyond what it says on its face — especially what each one presupposes about what we can know. Then give me a consistency audit: identify which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension and which only appear to be, rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating all as equally damaging, and for each one say exactly what would have to give and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. If the cheapest fix is a reinterpretation rather than abandoning a commitment, say so. Close by telling me which single revision would clean up the most.
