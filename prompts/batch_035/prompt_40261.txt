I'm a marine veterinarian who has just been hired as the first welfare officer at a mid-sized Norwegian salmon farm that also rears lumpfish as cleaner fish for sea-lice control. My first task is a two-hour session with the site crews — practical people, allergic to anything that smells of ideology, and operating on the working assumption that fish are production units with water-quality requirements. Don't explain fish welfare to me; I know the science. Plan how I should explain it to them.

The conceptual core I want to land is the move from welfare-as-health to welfare-as-experience. They already monitor oxygen, lice counts, and gill damage — but as production parameters, the fish functioning as an indicator of the water rather than a patient in it. Before planning the sequence, ask yourself which idea has to be in place before that move can even make sense to them, and decide the order of the pieces: sentience, the distinction between mere nociception and pain, or the three classic conceptions of welfare (biological function, natural living, affective state). I would expect a good plan to say which of these can be skipped entirely with this audience.

For the carrying example, I have candidates, and I want you to pick one and defend the choice against the others: (a) the lumpfish themselves — everyone on site half-knows that the fish brought in to delouse the salmon often end up in worse condition than the salmon, and the crews already suspect this is wrong; (b) pre-slaughter feed withdrawal and the crowding during delousing operations — events the crews run themselves and could act on tomorrow; (c) the difference between a pen that is clean and a pen that is good to be a fish in, i.e., barren versus structured environments. Whatever you choose must be observable with their own eyes on this site within a week, and it must not presuppose that fish feel pain — that is precisely the thing the example is supposed to make thinkable.

Then the misconception map, in the order the crews will actually raise them: the folk belief that fish have three-second memories; the charge that welfare science is unfalsifiable sentiment dressed up as data; the conflation of welfare with naturalness, which on a farm nothing is; and the suspicion that welfare is a cost imposed by people who have never hauled a net. For each, tell me where in the sequence it should be absorbed and whether it should be met with evidence or simply conceded.

Two further constraints. First, the sentience question is scientifically live — there is a genuine published debate over whether pain experience in fish is established or whether we only have nociception — and if I oversell the certainty and one of the crew later reads the skeptical side, I lose all of them permanently; build the honesty about this into the plan at a specific point rather than appending it. Second, cite nothing and recommend no reading: I need the order of ideas, the example, and the misconception map, nothing I would have to look up.

Finally, the check. What should I be able to hear a crew member say, unprompted, during the session or a week later, that would show the move from production-parameter to experience has genuinely happened — and what would only show they have learned to flatter me?

One request in my own interest: if you think my framing is off — if, say, the health-to-experience bridge is the wrong entry point for this audience and the sentience question has to come first even at the cost of provoking the skeptics, or if one of my candidate examples cannot do the work I've assigned it — say so directly and argue for the alternative, rather than delivering a tidy plan built on a premise you think is wrong.
