Mostly we talk about tense, flashbacks, prolepsis, that kind of thing, but I like to drag in a bit of actual metaphysics each year so they don't think I invented it all. This year I put a question about presentism and eternalism on the take-home, and now I have to grade it, and honestly the philosophy is where I'm weakest — I can tell when prose is confident, I can't always tell when it's *right*, and I don't want to reward smoothness over correctness. Give me a grade (letter grade is fine, or out of 100 if thats easier) and the specific comments that justify it — like, point by point, what the student got right, what's subtly off, what I should say so they actually learn something rather than just feeling told off.

The question I set:

"A novelist writes a book where the narrator describes the protagonist's death in chapter 1 and her childhood in chapter 30, both in the plain past tense, and neither is ever revised or retconned — the death stays fixed. Does this technique commit the fictional world to eternalism (the 'block universe' view, on which past, present and future events are all equally real)? Or is the metaphysics of a story just underdetermined by narrative technique? First explain clearly what presentists and eternalists actually disagree about, then answer."

And here's the student's answer, copied verbatim (they type fast and didn't proofread, ignore the typos):

"I would argue the novel is committed to eternalism, and in fact most proleptic narrators are. Eternalism is the view that all events are equally real — past ones like the extinction of the dinosaurs, future ones like my great-great-grandchildren's weddings — and none is metaphysically privileged. Presentism says only the present exists. So on presentism, at the moment the narrator tells us about the death, that death doesn't exist yet, and you cannot describe what does not exist, any more than you can photograph a unicorn. Therefore the narrator must be speaking from a standpoint outside time, which is basically the eternalist's 'view from nowhere' (I think that's Nagel's phrase), and the world it describes has to be a block. McTaggart is relevant here: he showed the A-series is self-contradictory, because if only the present exists, then nothing can have the properties of being past or being future, so the presentist cannot even say that present things will become past, which proves the A-series is incoherent and only the B-series is real. Since past-tense narration traffics in B-series relations (before and after) rather than A-series ones (past, present, future), the novel's grammar itself fits the block universe. The growing block view, which is basically a version of presentism where the past also exists, would allow the childhood chapter (the past exists on that view) but not the death chapter, so if the narrator is reliable it cannot be a growing block world. Finally, underdetermination would only apply if novels were like scientific theories, but they aren't — they have narrators, and a narrator is always located somewhere in time, so every narrative voice carries a metaphysics with it whether the author intends one or not."
