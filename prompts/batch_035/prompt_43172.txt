In my Bayesian epistemology seminar we've been on the problem of old evidence, and a friend has constructed an argument that she claims shows not merely that Bayesianism mishandles one awkward case, but that it cannot account for any scientific knowledge whose evidence is more than a few days old. She sets it out as follows. (1) Confirmation is probability raising: evidence E confirms hypothesis H for an agent just in case conditionalizing on E raises that agent's credence in H — formally, P(H|E) > P(H). (2) Once an agent has learned E and updated on it, she assigns E probability 1, and conditionalizing on a proposition you already give probability 1 changes nothing. (3) So for any agent who has already absorbed E, E cannot now confirm any H for her; the most it can have done is confirmed it once, in the instant of updating. (4) If confirmatory support expired at the moment it was absorbed, a theory would be confirmed only on the days its evidence first arrived. (5) Hence for every established theory whose supporting evidence is old — general relativity at any time since the eclipse observations, atomic theory, Newtonian mechanics at any point after the eighteenth century — nobody now holds it on confirmatory grounds; the evidence that earned it acceptance no longer does any work. (6) That is absurd: scientific knowledge does not decay as its evidence ages. (7) So some premise is false, and she argues that the only remotely respectable option is denying (1), which she takes to gut Bayesian confirmation theory and, with it, the entire probabilist reconstruction of scientific reasoning.

For reference, her showcase is the textbook one: the advance of Mercury's perihelion, which astronomers had measured to high precision decades before 1915 and which was nonetheless taken to confirm general relativity precisely because it was old and puzzling — though she thinks the point is completely general. My own suspicion is that something illicit happens between (3) and (5): the word now seems to slide between confirmation as an event in an agent's history and confirmation as a standing relation to her current credences, and I can almost articulate it but not quite. I'd also rather keep (1) and (6) than anything else in the chain, and I'd like to know what that preference costs me. So: which step should be rejected, why did it look so innocent, and what does rejecting it cost — including the price of each of the usual escape routes (the hypothetical-prior strategy, treating support as something banked in past updates, and simply denying (1)) and whether her conclusion that only (1) can go is really forced. Please answer in American English.
