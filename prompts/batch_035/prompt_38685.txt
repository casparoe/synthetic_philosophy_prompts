I've ended up down a rabbit hole on how strikes are actually treated by the law in most rich democracies, and there's a pattern I can't quite make sense of, so I want someone to dig out the theory the pattern is quietly committed to.

Here's the patchwork as I understand it (not a lawyer, correct me if I've got something wrong). Nearly every country that protects union organising as a basic right — free association and all that — also goes out of its way to blunt the strike itself. You normally need a formal ballot of the members before walking out, with the state supervising or at least scrutinising the vote, and in some places minimum turnout thresholds too. Sympathy or "secondary" action, where workers put down tools in support of somebody else's dispute, is banned almost everywhere, even though its the most natural expression of solidarity. "Essential" workers — rail, hospitals, the police in some countries — face outright bans or special compulsory-arbitration machinery instead. And in the US you can't fire anyone for striking, but you can permanently replace them while they're out, which is a strange half-protection: you keep your job in name while someone else sits at your desk doing it.

So the puzzle. The law seems to want to say the strike is a protected exercise of a right — otherwise why bother with the half-protections at all — and at same time it writes rules that are most restrictive exactly where a strike would bite hardest: solidarity strikes, effective workers, moments of real leverage. What view of what a strike *is* makes that coherent? Is it being treated as a breach of contract which the state merely tolerates, as a bargaining chip inside a market negotiation (in which case limiting its most coercive uses is just a kind of antitrust policing), or as political speech — in which case half of these restrictions look like limits on speech we'd never accept elsewhere? Reconstruct what the pattern actually presupposes, and be honest about which parts are underdetermined, i.e. where several different theories of the strike would license exactly the same rules, so the practice doesn't really tell between them. Then tell me this: if a country rebuilt its strike law on one of the rival views — say the political-speech one — what concretely would have to change? I'm not asking you to argue for or against unions generally, just to tell me what theory is buried in the practice.

Also two practical things: please write the answer in British English, and go easy on statute citations — I won't be able to check them, so stick to the ideas.
