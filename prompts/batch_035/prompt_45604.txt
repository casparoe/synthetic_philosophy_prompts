I spent my career as an experimental physical chemist, the last twenty years of it fretting over the resolution limits of spectrometers, and since retiring I have become the de facto program chair of the monthly "Science and Society" seminar at our local lifelong-learning institute. The audience is roughly a dozen retired scientists and engineers plus a retired physician and a former mathematics teacher: sharp, courteous in a combative way, insistent on operational definitions, and quick to dismiss anything that smells of jargon for jargon's sake. Each session is ninety minutes with a whiteboard. Next month I want to present Timothy Williamson's argument that no non-trivial condition is "luminous" — that is, no condition is such that, whenever it obtains, one is in a position to know it obtains — together with its connection to the principle that knowing implies being in a position to know that one knows.

My first attempt, last month, was a failure, and I want to plan properly this time. I handed out Williamson's scenario of someone lying in bed at dawn, freezing, and warming by imperceptible degrees over milliseconds, and it collapsed in two different directions at once: half the room concluded that the whole point was the banal one that words like "cold" are vague, and the other half concluded that Williamson is a global skeptic who thinks we never know anything. I now see I had asked them to run the sorites before they could walk. I also have a private hypothesis I'd like your advice on: it seems to me that the "margin for error" reasoning is just the epistemology of an analog gauge — we never trusted a single reading taken at the limit of an instrument's resolution, and any measurement regime coarse enough to be reliable from one moment to the next cannot certify a sharp threshold. I suspect a drift or hysteresis example from the lab could carry the whole session for this audience, but I cannot tell whether the analogy would illuminate the argument or quietly replace it with the thought that knowledge is literally a meter reading, which seems to me to miss something about the case.

So I am not asking you to explain the argument to me — I have read it enough times for that. I want the teaching plan. Specifically: in what order the pieces must be introduced so that the sorites does its work instead of being mistaken for a point about language, and what exactly has to be on the board before I even show the warming-room scenario; whether the laboratory example or Williamson's own case should be the one that carries the session, and what each has going for it; the misconceptions this particular audience will form, in the order they will form them, and the point in the sequence where each must be headed off — I already know "it's just vagueness," "the principle about knowing that you know is a claim about introspective infallibility," "anti-luminosity means skepticism about everything," and "the room's temperature is a fact regardless of what anyone knows, so what does our knowing have to do with it?" — plus exactly where my gauge analogy is most likely to betray me; one question I could ask at the end of the ninety minutes that would let me distinguish the members who have understood the argument from those who merely enjoyed the analogy; and finally what I should openly decline to cover at this level — I am thinking of the modal machinery, but you tell me what else belongs on that list — and how to say so without making the group feel shortchanged.
