A popular psychology book I read summarizes the philosophical problem of self-deception like this: 'Self-deception is impossible, because to deceive yourself you would have to be both the deceiver and the deceived at once — knowingly holding the truth while believing its opposite, which no single mind can do. So what passes for self-deception is really just motivated reasoning: the person never actually believes the comfortable conclusion, they merely want it to be true, and wanting is not believing. Philosophers have largely accepted this, so the debate is essentially settled.' I'm inclined to think something in there is unfair — I've heard that the classic paradox is treated as a puzzle that motivates competing accounts rather than a proof of impossibility, and I doubt the field is as united as the book suggests. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate, where it conflates distinct claims (I suspect the "both roles at once" point runs together two different puzzles, one about the state of self-deception and one about getting into it), and whether the "settled debate" claim survives — in particular, what a defender of the reality of self-deception would accept in this summary and what they would reject.
