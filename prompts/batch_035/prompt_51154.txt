I'm a novelist, and I've been rereading Thornton Wilder's Our Town while drafting a book of my own about a small town — the people who stay, the people who leave, and the ones who come back to look at it from the outside. The play's treatment of cosmic scale keeps snagging me, and I've recently found what seems to me a remarkable foil for it in Frank Ramsey, the Cambridge philosopher and mathematician who died at twenty-six. In a note written in 1925, reprinted at the close of The Foundations of Mathematics and Other Logical Essays, Ramsey remarked: "Where I seem to differ from some of my friends is in attaching little importance to physical size. I don't feel the least humble before the vastness of the heavens." And: "My picture of the world is drawn in perspective, and not like a model to scale. The foreground is occupied by human beings and the stars are all as small as threepenny bits." He adds that he applies his perspective to time as well as to space: in time the world will cool and everything will die, "but that is a long time off still, and its present value at compound discount is almost nothing." He even professes a kind of cheerful anti-realism about the heavens themselves — he doesn't really believe in astronomy, he says, except as a complicated description of part of the course of human and possibly animal sensation.

Read Our Town through Ramsey's note. The fit seems to me full of friction, and I want that friction taken seriously. The Stage Manager opens the play by situating Grover's Corners in geological and historical deep time — precisely the scale-model view of the world Ramsey refuses to feel small before. The third act moves to the cemetery, where the dead have traded engagement with the living for a serene, or in Simon Stimson's case embittered, detachment from the foreground of life. Emily's disastrous return to her twelfth birthday is staged as the discovery that the living never notice being alive, and her farewell to the world — coffee in the morning, new-ironed dresses, hot baths — is exactly the foreground material Ramsey insists on keeping in perspective while the stars shrink to threepenny bits.

So: is Wilder's injunction to realize life while you live it a Ramseyite claim — attention to the foreground particulars is all the meaning there is, and the cosmic zoom-out is a kind of optical flattery — or its rival, on which the dead occupy a genuinely superior viewpoint that the living should submit to? Does Simon Stimson's sour verdict on the living represent the failure of Ramsey's cheerfulness, or its vindication by contrast? Does the Stage Manager function as Ramsey's perspective artist, a narrator who keeps humans in the foreground while invoking deep time only as scenery, or does he collapse the perspective whenever he invokes it? And does Ramsey's note survive being dramatized at all — his breezy compound-discounting of cosmic death is one thing in a philosopher who died at twenty-six without warning, and another thing when a play makes you sit in the graveyard with it.

I'd like a reading that is faithful in both directions: it shouldn't reduce the play to an illustration of the note, nor bend the note to fit the play, and it should say plainly where the honest critic has to admit the two can't be reconciled. And since this is meant to feed my own book, I'd like to come away with something practical: which scenes you would point a novelist toward as the ones that stage this argument most sharply, and what a novel — which has no Stage Manager unless I give it one — can do with the conflict between the perspective picture and the scale model that the theater does differently, or better, or not as well.
