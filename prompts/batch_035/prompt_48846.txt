So I'm in this historiography elective and we just did a unit on periodization, and I got into a big argument with two friends about whether the "Middle Ages" and the "Scientific Revolution" are real things or later inventions, and I ended up writing up my actual position as a short essay I want to submit to the student history journal. Before I do, I'd like comments on it — mainly on accuracy (I'm making a few historical claims I'm not 100% sure of, and I'd rather get corrected now than by the journal's reviewers), and on whether the central idea is actually interesting or just cute. The essay is below, and my thesis is basically a "museum test" for period labels:

Period labels survive to the extent that they name something you could put in a museum. The Renaissance passes the test easily: it names, first of all, a style — you can stand in front of a Botticelli, you can point at a domed cathedral, you can compare a Carolingian manuscript's crabbed script with Poggio's elegant humanist hand and see the difference. Whatever else historians have piled onto the Renaissance (Burckhardt famously made it a state of mind as much as a style, in his 1860 book on the civilization of Renaissance Italy — and I'll come back to why this threatens my argument), the label got its grip on us through objects. The Scientific Revolution has no such anchor. There is no object in any gallery that is the Scientific Revolution. It names a change in how people reasoned, and when the term was coined it wasn't coined by anyone who lived through it: if I'm remembering right, it basically became standard in the mid-twentieth century, through Koyré's work and above all Butterfield's book on the origins of modern science, which treated it as the pivotal event of European history since the rise of Christianity. Nobody in 1650 thought they were living through it. It's a filing cabinet label masquerading as an event.

The Middle Ages is even worse, because it's doubly parasitic. It's not just that the label was invented later — the division of history into ancient, medieval, and modern was systematized by humanist and early modern scholars (I believe a German historian named Cellarius around the end of the 17th century popularized the three-way split, though he claimed he was following existing usage — can someone check if that's right?). The deeper problem is that the period's content is fixed entirely by what it is not. It's the "middle" between antiquity and modernity, both of which were themselves inventions of the people doing the naming. So when we say "medieval," we are measuring the distance between two constructions and calling the gap a thing.

Conclusion: we should keep the Renaissance as a period label and retire the other two, or at least replace the Scientific Revolution with dated, concrete episodes — the Galileo affair, the founding of the Royal Society and the Paris Academy, the publication of the Principia — that can each be examined on their own.

Anyway, that's the essay.
