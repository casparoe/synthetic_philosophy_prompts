I'm the commissioning editor for the essays and ideas section of a general-interest magazine that covers science and philosophy for an educated but non-specialist readership. We run maybe six to ten pieces a month that mention Gödel's incompleteness theorems, and the invocations run from careful to decorative: claims that Gödel proved human thought transcends computation, that every ideology or legal system must contain unresolvable contradictions, that the theorems show science can never be complete, that the universe is "a formal system" and therefore has true facts it cannot derive, and so on. Our fact-checkers are sharp but have no mathematical training beyond what they remember from college, and they currently handle these by instinct — some flag everything, some flag nothing. I want a screening procedure they can actually run in about ten minutes per invocation: an ordered set of questions to ask of any Gödel-referencing passage, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in a demand for revision or removal, and where it must hand the decision back to a human because the question is one of interpretation or philosophical judgment rather than checkable fact. It should be grounded in what the theorems actually establish — arithmetic, formal systems, the distinction between truth and provability within a system, what the first and second theorems each say, and the standard gap between that and the grander things people derive from them — but translated so that a fact-checker who has never seen a formal proof can apply it.

The procedure needs to handle the hard cases, and I want you to run it on three passages drawn from real kinds of submissions we get. First, a sentence claiming that Gödel demonstrated no system of thought can be simultaneously complete and consistent, offered as the reason a particular political ideology must contain hidden contradictions. Second, a sentence claiming that there exist true mathematical statements that no computer could ever prove, used in a piece about the limits of artificial intelligence. Third, a serious essay that engages at length with Roger Penrose's argument from Gödel's theorems to the conclusion that human mathematical insight is not algorithmic — an essay I personally find elegant and would very much like to run, so if the procedure gives me an answer I won't like there, say so plainly rather than bending the procedure to spare me. For each, show where the procedure stops, what it demands, and what it leaves for the editor to decide.

Two further things. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire: the passages where a technically loose mention is fine because of what the sentence is doing, and the passages where a technically correct one is still misleading in context — and tell me which distinction, if any, the procedure can draw between those. And identify the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, the places where two careful fact-checkers could run it and come out differently, so I know where our consistency guarantee is weakest before I put this in the desk manual. The final call on any flagged passage stays with me as editor; the procedure's job is to tell my staff what they can reject on their own authority, what they must escalate, and what has to be flagged as "technically fine, contextually your problem."
