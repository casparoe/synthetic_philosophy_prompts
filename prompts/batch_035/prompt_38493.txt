I quit my desk job about a year ago to run a small vegetable farm, and somewhere along the way I became a full believer in the agriculture-was-a-mistake story -- Marshall Sahlins's original affluent society, foragers supposedly working a few hours a day, the skeleton evidence that early farmers got shorter and sicker, inequality taking off once you can store grain and defend it, the whole thing. I've been telling anyone who'll listen that my customers and I are all descended from people who voluntarily walked into a cage. My partner says I've read too much pop anthropology and I'm romanticizing, and honestly she might have a point, which is why I'm writing this.

I want you to take the role of an anthropologist with a strong background in economic history and philosophy of science, think carefully before you answer (maybe brainstorm the candidate defeaters first and then organize them), and tell me what would actually count against my view. Not the standard but-they-died-at-thirty pushback I can argue with in my sleep -- I want the stuff that should genuinely worry a serious defender of the position. Some things I've half-thought of myself, so you can build on these but don't stop at them:

- The skeletal evidence (stature, tooth wear, stress markers) has a selective mortality problem: the people whose bones we measure are the ones who survived to adulthood at all, so we may be comparing the frailest surviving farmers to the heartiest surviving foragers, or vice versa.
- The famous leisure estimates seem to depend enormously on what you count as work -- if grinding grain and cooking count for farmers but gathering and processing mongongo nuts doesn't count for foragers, the comparison is cooked.
- The Zen road to affluence thing: maybe foragers weren't rich, they just wanted little, and that's a claim about preferences, not material conditions -- which changes what kind of "mistake" we're even talking about.
- The inequality story might partly be an artifact of what leaves traces: farmers leave storehouses, granaries, and rich burials we can find, while forager inequality (in skill, social standing, mates) is nearly invisible in the record, so maybe we're just measuring the visibility of inequality rather than its amount.
- And the claim that farming was forced on people by climate stress rather than chosen -- if it turns out people adopted agriculture in places and periods where they plausibly had other options, that starts to look like revealed preference that farming was actually better hour-for-hour.

So: rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Say which ones a committed defender of hunter-gatherer affluence would concede really count against the view versus which they'd dismiss, and be honest about whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability over the years (I've noticed that every bad thing about forager life tends to get blamed on being pushed into marginal habitats or contact with farmers -- if that move is always available, what's left of the claim?). Also tell me whether the fact that this story is so appealing to people like me -- burnt-out professionals who want a story about how modern working life is a scam -- has any bearing on its evidential standing, and if the answer is basically no, explain why not rather than just saying it.

No reading lists or citations please, just the reasoning -- I'll chase sources later if I need to.
