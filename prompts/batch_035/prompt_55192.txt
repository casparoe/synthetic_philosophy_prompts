i run a small reading group for ML engineers, and lately every meeting has someone sniping at someone else's interpretability work for what turns out to be a totally different reason than the last person's. I want a handout that figures out what each of them actually believes about whether networks have representations, so we can argue about that directly instead of talking past each other.

could you write a decision tree that starts from a shared commitment we all accept — say, we trained a sparse autoencoder on an LLM's activations and found a feature that fires on sarcastic text, and adding that feature to the activations at inference makes the model respond sarcastically — and ends at a named position on whether the model genuinely has representations. the internal nodes should be questions an engineer can answer from their research instincts, arguing about concrete experiments, without already knowing which camp they're in. things like: does steering behavior with the feature show it's doing real causal work, or does it only show you found one handle among many? what do you say when two different SAEs (or different dictionary sizes) each give a clean but incompatible decomposition of the same layer? does it matter whether the feature responds correctly on inputs far outside the training distribution — is that the difference between representing sarcasm and merely correlating with it? does a model need to be able to misrepresent (fire on non-sarcastic text in a way that's the model's error, not a labeling error of ours) before you'll grant its features correctness conditions, or is that demanding too much for a causal role? and is the claim "the model has a sarcasm feature" literally true, true the way a good idealization is true, or just useful bookkeeping for us researchers?

for leaves i'd want at least: robust realism (features as natural kinds the network really contains), something like structural or role-functionalist realism where the causal profile is what's real and the particular decomposition is partly our choice, instrumentalism where feature talk is scaffolding for predicting behavior and nothing more, eliminativism (its just matrix multiplications, the features are ours, not the model's), and a pluralist/indeterminacy leaf for people who think there are many equally good carvings and no fact of the matter about which the model "really" has. if you think there's a genuinely distinct camp worth its own leaf — e.g. people who take superposition to mean features only exist at the level of the whole distributed geometry and never as individual directions — add it, but keep the total in the 5-7 leaf range.

two more things: first, flag honestly where the tree can't separate positions. I suspect instrumentalism and any "fictionalism about features" view will collapse into each other no matter what question you ask, and i'd rather the handout admit that than pretend theres a diagnostic question that splits them. same if robust realism and the structural realism leaf end up requiring the same answers at every node — say so. second, the point is sorting by what people say about cases, not by which label they like, so no node should amount to "are you a realist about interpretability?" — definately not.
