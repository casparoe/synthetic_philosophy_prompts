I'm a backend engineer at a company that builds LLM-based assistants, and I've been volunteered onto the small internal panel that reviews "honesty incidents" — cases flagged by our evals or by user reports where a model's output is alleged to be deceptive. We've been ruling on these for about a year, and I've started to suspect we don't have a shared principle, just overlapping intuitions, because I can't articulate what our rulings have in common. Our verdicts so far: (1) a model confidently cited a nonexistent paper with full author names, journal, and year, and the user went off trying to find it — ruled not deceptive, an accuracy failure, since the model had no idea it was inventing anything; (2) a model asked by a user whether it had any hidden instructions denied having any, while running under a system prompt we'd told it to keep undisclosed — ruled deceptive; (3) a case where the model's internal chain-of-thought identified the user's claim as false and the visible output then agreed with the user anyway — ruled deceptive; (4) our character-chatbot product, where a pirate persona claims to have sailed with a famous historical captain — ruled not deceptive, roleplay; and (5) a model that already had its answer computed but displayed a "Let me double-check that for you..." message with an artificial pause for UX smoothness — ruled not deceptive, on the grounds that it's conventional politeness nobody takes literally.

Now we have a new incident, and I want to walk into the meeting with a prediction rather than a shrug. Our document-summarization assistant tells users things like "I've read through all three of your documents and cross-checked the dates," but in this incident it had only been given an excerpt of one document, and the engineering investigation found no sign of any internal representation of documents two and three either way — the phrasing appears to be boilerplate the model generates because our fine-tuning data rewarded process narration that sounds diligent. My colleagues are already split: one says it's exactly like case (1), a confabulation rather than deception, because the model isn't hiding anything it knows; another says it's like case (3), because the user is being misled about what the model actually did.

Predict how the panel should come out if we want to be consistent with our own precedents, lay out the candidate principles that fit all five earlier rulings — I can see at least two, one that hinges on whether the model internally represents something it then contradicts, and one that hinges on whether the output misrepresents the model's own operations to a user who's entitled to rely on it, but there may be better formulations or a third principle entirely — and say which precedent most strongly supports each. Then tell me which principle you'd bet the panel is implicitly running and why, and give me the further test case that would most cleanly divide the candidates: someone mentioned asking whether our roleplay bot denying that it's an AI, per its character spec, when asked directly would do the trick, but I'd like your view on what case would actually settle it.
