"In the standard case, you pass a pond and see a child drowning; wading in ruins your $100 shoes. Now consider a second case: your phone pings with a livestream from a camera at a pond 4,000 miles away, a child there is drowning, and a button lets you dispatch a rescue drone for $100 (a local responder does the actual rescuing). Answer: (1) What does Singer's argument from the drowning-child case imply about your obligation in the livestream case? (2) Does proximity make a moral difference, or only a psychological one? Explain your reasoning. (12 points, ~40 minutes.)"

and here's my classmate's answer (shared with her ok):

"Singer's principle says that if you can prevent something very bad from happening without giving up anything of comparable moral importance, you are morally required to prevent it. In the pond case, a child's life versus ruined $100 shoes — the shoes clearly lose. In the livestream case the sacrifice is exactly the same ($100 for the drone), and the bad thing prevented is exactly the same (a child's death). So the argument implies the exact same obligation in both cases. Distance doesn't matter morally, because Singer is a consequentialist: all that matters is the total welfare produced, and welfare doesn't come tagged with a location. Where the child is relative to you is just a fact about geography.

(2) Proximity makes a psychological difference but not a moral one. The livestream actually closes the psychological gap — the vividness you would have at the pond is now (roughly) available on your phone, which is why the case feels so disturbing. But even without the livestream the obligation was already there; the camera only changes how easy it is to notice the need. What we have to resist is the thought that the person at the pond does something while the person with the phone merely lets something happen — Singer already accepts that killing and letting die are morally equivalent, so not clicking the button is on a par with wading past the pond yourself, and in both cases you are responsible for the death in the same way.

(3) Both versions of the principle get the same result. The stronger one says $100 is not comparable in importance to a life, so rescue is required. The weaker one also requires it, since the sacrifice is trivial. So on either version the distinction between the cases collapses.

Could anything block the conclusion? Williams's integrity objection says utilitarianism is too demanding and leaves no room for your own projects and commitments, so we can reject the premise that you are obligated in both cases. If the objection works, then failing to rescue is not wrong at all, it is just supererogatory. But either way the demandingness point applies to both cases equally: if no one can be required to give this much, both rescue obligations fail together, so the pond/livestream distinction still does not reappear."
