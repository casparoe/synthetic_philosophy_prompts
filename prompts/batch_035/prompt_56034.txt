I'm taking a third-year course in the philosophy of physics, and we've now spent two seminars on the quantum suicide and quantum immortality argument without anyone shifting an inch. To make sure we're working from the same picture: the thought experiment asks you to rig a device that performs a quantum measurement with a fifty-fifty outcome and kills you on one result, then to sit through many repetitions. The Everettian argues that, given the branching structure of the wavefunction, there will always be some branch on which the lethal outcome simply never occurs, and that the only perspective available to you is that of a survivor — so, if death in the experiment is made genuinely conditional on the quantum outcome, you should expect to find yourself surviving indefinitely. The standard objection, which comes in several forms, is that the surviving branches carry vanishingly small amplitude, so the measure of survival shrinks toward zero, and to privilege the mere existence of a surviving branch over its Born weight is to abandon probability altogether. A second objection observes that the argument breaks if the mechanism of death is gradual or statistical — ageing, cancer, the accumulation of damage — since then no outcome strictly rules out death, and the sharp either/or structure the argument requires evaporates.

What frustrates me is that neither side seems to disagree about the physics at all: the Schrödinger equation, the branching, the amplitude weights are all common ground. My working hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than merely endorse, is that the dispute is a proxy war: quantum immortality inherits whatever is unresolved about probability in the Everett interpretation generally, so the standoff over immortality is really a standoff about whether typicality and measure can be given a first-person meaning. But I can also see it being something else — perhaps a verbal dispute about what "I" refers to after branching, or an anthropic selection-effect argument whose real analogue is the doomsday-style debates rather than anything distinctively quantum, or a decision-theoretic question that only becomes confused when it is posed as a question about what you should expect.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on immortality itself, and not a survey of the interpretations of quantum mechanics. What kind of disagreement is this — substantive, verbal, or two questions (one about the formalism, one about self-locating probability) being run under a single heading? Identify what each side is actually committed to, and say explicitly where the argument depends on assumptions that neither side states. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: would a decision theory for the Everett interpretation settle it, or a sharper account of personal identity across branching, or is it the sort of question that could in principle be dissolved by a distinction? And if your view is that no progress should be expected, argue for that rather than merely lamenting it.
