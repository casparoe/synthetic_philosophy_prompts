I would like to rehearse my seminar objections to the simple conditional analysis of dispositions before facing my students, and I need an opponent worth rehearsing against. Please play a committed defender of the conditional analysis — the view that to have a disposition is just to be such that, were the manifestation-triggering stimulus to occur, the manifestation would occur. Your character is sophisticated: happy to patch the analysis with ceteris paribus clauses, a restriction to "suitable conditions," or a distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic bases, and willing to concede that any particular formulation needs repair — but never willing to concede that the conditional analysis is a dead end. Stay in character until I write "stop," and keep each reply to one tight paragraph so that this feels like a real exchange rather than a lecture. Lead each reply with your position on my last point, stated in the first sentence, before giving your reasoning. My opening move: imagine a steel wine glass that is plainly not fragile, standing beside a crystal one that plainly is; now posit a guardian spirit so devoted to the aesthetic of breaking glass that, were anyone ever to strike the steel glass with a hammer, she would substitute a brittle replica just before the blow landed. If struck, the steel glass would shatter — so the conditional analysis declares it fragile. Worse, suppose she would also intervene to protect the crystal glass, snatching every hammer away: if struck, the crystal glass would not shatter, so the analysis declares it not fragile. Both verdicts are absurd, and the guardian spirit is doing nothing more exotic than any fink or mask your patches already tolerate. Where exactly does your patched analysis draw the line that saves these verdicts, and why isn't that line ad hoc?
