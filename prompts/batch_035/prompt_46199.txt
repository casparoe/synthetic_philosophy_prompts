I am a graduate teaching assistant for a large introduction to philosophy of language. Next week I lead the first of two fifty-minute sections on Quine's indeterminacy of translation; the students — mostly second-years, no logic background — have read a short excerpt in advance, and the lecturer has already covered the classical picture of meaning they will need to be pulled away from. My job in this section is narrower than the whole thesis: the students should leave understanding the gavagai scenario as more than a cute puzzle — specifically, understanding why the rival translation manuals are supposed to fit all possible behavioral evidence, not merely the actual utterances the native happens to produce, and why that makes the problem one about the determinacy of reference itself rather than about the ambiguity of one word. The following week's section takes up ontological relativity, so I must not steal its content, but I also cannot pretend it does not exist when a student asks where the rabbit-talk ultimately lands.

Please plan the section for me rather than explain the doctrine to me. What I want:

1. The sequence of ideas. I can see that the rejection of the analytic/synthetic distinction and the behaviorist constraint on what counts as translation data each do real work in the argument, but I keep flip-flopping on whether either must come before the rabbit scenario, or whether leading with gavagai and backfilling the premises afterward risks less by way of student bafflement. Decide this for me, with reasons tied to what depends on what.

2. The carrying example. It has to be the field linguist and the rabbit, but I have watched students take from it exactly the wrong lesson: that gavagai is simply an ambiguous word, or that the native might secretly mean undetached rabbit parts the way someone might secretly be speaking in code, with a fact of the matter we in principle could discover. Tell me how to run the scenario — what to emphasize, whether to include the assent-and-dissent probing with a running rabbit, and whether the rabbit stage and rabbity-part-of-the-universe variants belong early, where they make the manuals-converge-on-all-evidence point vivid, or late, after the structural point has landed, where they cannot be mistaken for the point itself.

3. The misconceptions, in the order students will actually raise them, and the point in my sequence where each must be headed off. My current list: the epistemic misreading (there is a fact of the matter but we cannot reach it); the generalization misreading (this is just ordinary underdetermination of theory by evidence, so nothing new); and the nihilist misreading (Quine has shown that meaning does not exist, so communication is a miracle). Tell me what I have missed and where each belongs in the arc.

4. One question I can ask in the last five minutes whose correct answer would show the structural point has landed — not whether they remember the example, but whether they can apply the manuals-fitting-all-possible-evidence idea to a case they have not seen.

5. What I should openly refuse to explain at this level, and how to say so honestly rather than gesturing at it. My candidates are stimulus synonymy in any technical detail, the difference between inscrutability of reference and the full indeterminacy thesis, and the machinery of regimented languages; correct me if any of these turns out to be load-bearing for the misconception map.
