You are an expert consultant on aquatic animal welfare and on the design of third-party certification standards for food production. Please think carefully before drafting, and begin your work with a short internal brainstorm that considers at least three distinct framings of the decision described below before you commit to one; you need not reproduce the brainstorm in your final answer, but your memo should reflect that you weighed alternatives.

I sit on the standards committee of a mid-sized international aquaculture certification scheme — call it AquaCert — which certifies farms in about a dozen countries, mostly in Southeast Asia and Latin America. Our current standards for farmed whiteleg shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) cover water quality, feed sourcing, antibiotic use, and labor conditions, but say nothing about how the animals are treated while alive or how they are slaughtered. In practice, harvest on most certified farms involves gathering shrimp into dense masses out of water for extended periods, and killing them by immersion in an ice-and-water slurry or by drying them in the sun. Two other practices are also at issue: eyestalk ablation performed on breeding females to accelerate ovarian maturation, and the disposal of uneconomic larvae and juveniles.

At our last meeting the committee split three ways:

1. One faction wants to add a full humane-treatment module to the standard: mandatory electrical stunning before slaughter (or another method demonstrated to render the animals insensible before death), a phase-out of eyestalk ablation in certified broodstock operations within five years, and density and handling requirements. They note the growing scientific consensus that decapod crustaceans are sentient — a view that has begun to appear in legislation, such as the UK's recognition of decapods as sentient beings in 2022 — and argue that a scheme branding itself on sustainability will look cynical if it keeps ignoring welfare.
2. A second faction argues that the evidence on shrimp sentience is real but that welfare-relevant details are unsettled — including whether ice slurry is genuinely inhumane, whether electrical stunning at scale actually works on animals of this size, and what the subjective badness of eyestalk ablation is compared to its production benefits — and that mandating capital equipment like stunners would effectively decertify smallholders in our producing countries, handing the market to uncertified competitors with worse practices overall.
3. A third faction wants to declare the whole question out of scope: our scheme was created for environmental and social sustainability, they say, and welfare creep risks mission drift and audit fatigue on farms that already struggle with compliance costs.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before our next quarterly meeting. It should:

- Recommend one concrete course of action to the committee — whether a full module, a staged or conditional pathway (e.g., pilot requirements, an optional welfare add-on label, or a phased timetable with technical assistance and fee waivers for smallholders), or keeping welfare out of scope — with a defensible timeline;
- Engage seriously with the strongest version of each of the three factions' arguments, including the second faction's concern that a rigid mandate could perversely reduce total animal welfare by shifting production out of the certified segment;
- Address the epistemic problem of acting under unsettled science: what standard of evidence should a private certification body demand before writing sentience-based requirements into an auditable standard, and how should the scheme update if the science moves;
- Say something concrete about the two specific practices — slaughter method and eyestalk ablation — rather than treating welfare as an undifferentiated blob;
- Be honest about the commercial and reputational stakes, including pressure from buyers in North America and Europe, without letting reputational management masquerade as ethical argument.

The memo should run roughly 900 to 1,200 words, use the conventional structure of a committee decision memo (recommendation up front, background, analysis, dissent considerations, implementation timeline), and be written in polished American English. Address it to "Members of the AquaCert Standards Committee" and sign it as "A Consultant to the Committee." Avoid citations or references to specific named studies, since you cannot verify them; where you rely on the general state of the science, say so in those terms.
