At a planning meeting last week, our deputy director shut down a proposal to tie part of our agency's grant funding to a composite performance index by invoking Goodhart's law: 'When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.' I could not tell at the time whether he was making a strong and possibly correct point about incentive design or a hand-waving one that would equally rule out any metric anyone has ever used, and I want to be better prepared next time. Please disentangle the precise theses that sentence could be expressing and present them as a list. I would particularly like you to keep a few distinctions in view, since I suspect the readings differ along these lines: whether the claim is that the measure was never a good measure in the first place (so the targeting merely reveals this) or that it was good and the targeting itself destroys it; whether the mechanism is direct gaming, selection effects at the top of a noisy proxy, or a more general instability of statistical relationships once someone intervenes on them; and whether 'becomes a target' means publicly announced, tied to rewards, or merely attended to. For each reading, say whether it is a triviality, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false, and then tell me which readings, if any, actually support the move my deputy director made — refusing the index outright rather than using it with safeguards — and whether the same readings would also condemn, say, the exam grades by which he was himself hired.
