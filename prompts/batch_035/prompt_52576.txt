I'm in a small reading group working thru Leibniz's Discourse on Metaphysics (1686), and I drew the short straw for presenting section 9 this week. The section is mostly him arguing that every substance expresses the entire universe in its own way — every substance is like a little world, a mirror of God and of everything else that exists, each taking in the whole of reality from its own point of view, so that what makes each substance what it is includes its perspective on all the others. And then out of this he concludes: 'It is not true that two substances resemble each other entirely and differ solo numero.' No two substances can be perfectly alike, differing only in being two rather than one. (His immediate payoff is about how we can always tell any two things apart if we know them well enough, but the claim itself is what we now call the identity of indiscernibles.)

Here's what I can't figure out and would love help untangling: what exactly is the inference in this section? The mirroring claim sounds like it should be the engine — if each thing is individuated by its perspective on everything else, two things can't share all their features, since each one's view of the universe has to include the other one from a different angle. But my professor insists the real argument is theological: God does nothing without a sufficent reason, and if two candidate substances were exactly alike, God would have no reason to create both rather than just one, so he would create one. These feel like two completely different arguments to me — one metaphysical, one that leans entirely on God's choice-making — and I honestly can't tell from the text whether Leibniz thinks he's running both at once, or whether one of them is just decoration for the other. Which reading makes the section come out as an actual argument rather than an assertion?

Related thing I keep chewing on: when people bring up Black's spheres — the two identical iron spheres sitting alone in an otherwise empty universe — as the standard counterexample to this principle, are they answering the Leibniz of this section, or only the modern stripped-down version of it? In Black's universe there's no God and nothing mirrors anything in a Leibnizian sense, so it feels like the counterexample only bites once you've already deleted the theological premises my professor says are load-bearing. Is that right, or is there a way to read the mirroring claim that doesn't need God and still gets you to the conclusion? And if the theological reading is correct, does Leibniz even have a non-question-begging response to the spheres, or does the whole thing bottom out in "there couldn't be such a universe because God wouldn't make one"?
