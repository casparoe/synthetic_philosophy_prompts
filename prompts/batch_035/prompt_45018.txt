So I help run a small newsletter for a reading group — lefty-ish, mixed backgrounds, maybe a third of us have philosophy training — and we just spent two months on the whole "equality of what?" debate (the Sen vs. Dworkin stuff, expensive tastes, capabilities, relational equality). Problem: now everyone uses the word "egalitarian" to mean like four incompatible things and we keep talking past each other. I want a "which egalitarian are you, actually?" quiz to settle it.

Make it 9 questions. Each one is a concrete scenario with 2 or 3 answer options (options can be a sentence or two of plain English, not just labels). The reader has to be able to answer every question without knowing any of the theory — no question may just ask "do you believe in equality of resources?"

Before writing the questions, figure out what the load-bearing cuts in the logical space actually are — welfare vs. resources vs. capabilities vs. primary goods, brute luck vs. option luck, cheap vs. expensive tastes, adaptive preferences, whether equality is the right relation at all vs. sufficiency or priority, distributive vs. relational — and then build each question so it hammers on exactly one of them. Say a sentence or two up front about which cut each question is targeting, so I can check you didn't stack three distinctions into one item.

Cases that have to show up, because our group fought about all of them:
- Dworkin's champagne person — someone who's always preferred expensive things and it costs a lot to satisfy her, no question of her being unable to help it or wishing she were different. Did her situation get worse, morally speaking? Also include a variant where the person would love to be rid of the taste and can't be.
- Adaptive preferences: someone raised inside a hierarchy who genuinely, sincerely reports being content with very little, and whose contentment tracks her actual life.
- Conversion: two people with identical bundles of goods where one gets much less out of them because of some natural fact about her (metabolism, or a wheelchair user facing stairs).
- The gambler: two equally paid people, one bets her pay on a coin flip and loses everything.
- The surfer: someone who could earn a lot but chooses to work two days a week and surf, and ends up with much less than everyone else.

Plus one question built around a case you invent that is NOT in the literature as far as I know: someone who voluntarily, eyes open, and with no pathology, trades away a chunk of her own functioning for something she cares about more — say she takes a pill that permanently dulls her senses somewhat but removes a jealousy that was wrecking her life — and the question is what, if anything, a just society owes her afterward. If you can come up with a better invented case that pries two of the positions apart, use it instead, but tell me why you chose it.

Then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto named positions: at minimum welfare egalitarianism (hedonic vs. preference-satisfaction versions, if your questions can actually seperate them), Rawlsian primary goods, Dworkinian resources with the envy test, the capabilities view, luck egalitarianism, relational egalitarianism, and at least one non-egalitarian leaf (sufficientarian or prioritarian). Be honest about which pairs your questions can NOT pull apart — I suspect primary goods and resources mostly collapse into each other, and I suspect relational egalitarianism doesn't get sorted by distributive cases at all (our gambler question probably comes closest, but say so if it doesn't cut it, and tell me what question WOULD be needed even if it's too weird for a quiz). Also note any combination of answers that no real position occupies, with one sentence on whether that's because the view is incoherent or just because nobody's bothered to defend it.

Tone: readable by non-philosophers but don't dumb it down. Short intro for the quiz, then the questions, then the key.
