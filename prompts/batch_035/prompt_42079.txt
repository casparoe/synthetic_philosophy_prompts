I'm a senior at a large public high school, and I've noticed that our administration treats inherited rules in three different ways, and I can't figure out whether the pattern rests on any consistent principle. There is a rule that students may not use the front staircase during passing periods; it predates every current staff member, no written rationale exists, and when the student council asked to remove it, the principal refused on the ground that one should not abolish an old rule until one understands why it was adopted — the reformer who doesn't know a rule's purpose is like someone tearing down a fence before learning why it was put up. Yet when a single parent complained about the dress code, the same principal abolished it within a week, though no one ever investigated why it had existed either; and the rule that club budgets must be approved a full term in advance survives purely on the ground that re-doing the paperwork would be a nuisance, its origins never being mentioned at all. So we have three policies: keep the rule and never inquire into it, abolish the rule without inquiring into it, and keep the rule for reasons of cost, ignoring its origins entirely. What theory of inherited rules, if any, do these practices jointly presuppose? Does the staircase decision presuppose something Burkean — that rules which survive for generations embody accumulated knowledge no present official is likely to possess — or only the weaker claims that change is risky and that the burden of proof falls on whoever wants the change? Is the school genuinely applying Chesterton's fence, which instructs the reformer to go and investigate before abolishing, or has it quietly converted that into a standing presumption against reform, since it never actually conducts the investigation? Could the three policies be reconciled by some principle about who bears the costs of each rule — the students, in the staircase case; the staff, in the dress code case — or are they simply inconsistent? Please reconstruct the implicit view of tradition and institutional memory behind these practices, tell me which parts of the school's behavior strictly require that view and which would fit several rival views just as well, and say what would have to change if the school instead adopted the position that an unexplained rule has no claim on us at all — for instance, if every rule had to be re-authorized with a stated rationale every five years or lapse automatically.
