In the introduction to our historiography seminar, the course handbook tries to get us up to speed on the debate over historical objectivity before we read any primary texts. I've copied out the relevant passage, and something about it makes me uneasy — it's very confident, and I don't yet know enough to check it properly. The handbook says, roughly, the following.

It begins with Ranke. In the preface to his Histories of the Latin and Teutonic Peoples (1824), Ranke issued his famous injunction that the historian should show things "wie es eigentlich gewesen" — how they actually were — and, according to the handbook, this meant that the historian's job was to banish interpretation from his work entirely: to go to the archives, assemble the facts, and let them speak for themselves, exactly as a natural scientist assembles observations. On this reading, Ranke believed that a properly disciplined history could eventually yield general laws of historical development, on the model of the natural sciences.

It then turns to E. H. Carr's What Is History? (1961). On the handbook's telling, Carr demolished the Rankean position: the facts never speak for themselves but are selected and interpreted by the historian, so that every work of history is really a piece of autobiography, and Carr concluded from this that objectivity is impossible and that historians should instead openly write history in the service of present political purposes. Finally, the handbook presents Hayden White's Metahistory (1973) as completing the demolition: since historians inevitably impose narrative forms on their material — romance, tragedy, comedy, and satire — which are not themselves in the evidence, historical writing is a literary artefact, and White's conclusion was that there is no real difference between a historical narrative and a novel, and no fact of the matter about the past beyond the texts we have. The passage ends by declaring that the debate is now over and that Ranke lost.

I'm especially unsure about the Ranke paragraph: my supervisor mentioned in passing that the standard English rendering of Ranke's German involves a contested translation, and I'd like to understand whether that matters here. Second, does the handbook's closing verdict — that the debate is over and Ranke lost — actually follow from the corrected version of the summary, or does it depend on the misrepresentations? If you could also tell me what a defensible version of each side's position would look like, so that I can recognise fair and unfair characterisations of them in future reading, I would be grateful.
