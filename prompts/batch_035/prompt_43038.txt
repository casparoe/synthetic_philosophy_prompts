I'm a physician who joined the clinical ethics consultation service at a large teaching hospital about three months ago, and the abortion cases are the ones I feel least equipped to handle. Last month we saw a termination at 31 weeks for a fetus with bilateral renal agenesis — no possibility of sustained life outside the womb — and the following week an early first-trimester request that some of my colleagues wanted to refuse as a matter of "convenience." I found I had no principled way to explain why one case felt easy and the other hard, and I've decided that before I read another argument in the literature, I want to know what I should be demanding of a theory in the first place.

You are a professional moral philosopher working at the boundary of normative ethics and bioethics. Think carefully, and brainstorm before committing to your final list — I would rather see requirements you considered and discarded or merged than a list you didn't have to fight over.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of the ethics of abortion has to deliver. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or instead reflects a substantive commitment that a reasonable person could reject. At minimum, consider (though you need not accept):

- which cases the theory must get determinately right, or at least not get wrong: pregnancy resulting from rape, contraception failure, a lethal fetal anomaly discovered late, a serious but not lethal threat to maternal health, and the request that even the clinicians involved describe as purely social;
- which distinctions it must respect rather than collapse — killing versus letting die, the difference (if any) between a fetus, a newborn, and a patient in a permanent vegetative state, the difference between declining to sustain another person's body and actively ending a life that would otherwise continue;
- the consistency constraints: a theory that entails that infanticide is permissible, or that contraception and even abstinence are seriously wrong because they deprive a future person of a future, must either own the implication or explain it away — I want to know which requirements, once adopted, force those costs;
- what I'll call the line problem: the relevant biology is gradual — implantation, the emergence of sentience, viability that shifts with each generation of neonatal technology, birth itself — so any theory that concentrates enormous moral weight at a sharp line owes an account of why the line's location matters morally and not merely administratively;
- what I'll call the responsibility problem: whether, and how much, it matters that a pregnancy resulted from voluntary intercourse, and whether a theory can weigh that at all without treating gestation as a penalty for assumed risk.

Then flag which pairs of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want to see — and identify the single requirement that, once adopted, excludes the most otherwise-live theories. If you have room at the end, briefly say how Thomson's bodily-autonomy approach, the future-like-ours argument, and a gradualist view that ties moral weight to developing neural capacities each fare against your list, but keep the weight on the criteria: I'll do the scoring with my committee.
