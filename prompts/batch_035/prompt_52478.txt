My grandmother Astrid died last month at 94, and I'm giving the eulogy at the service in a little Lutheran church outside Stoughton, Wisconsin — mostly Norwegian-American relatives, a handful coming over from Oslo for it. She was the most stubborn small-d democrat I ever met (union steward, poll worker for something like forty years, argued politics with everyone her whole life) AND a devoted monarchist, which drove my cousin Erik up the wall. Erik has been a card-carrying republican since he was maybe fifteen; he thinks hereditary office is an insult to everything democracy stands for, and he and grandma had the same fight at every family dinner for thirty years. Her position was never "the king is above politics" — it was more that the crown was the thing that kept politics from swallowing everything. She'd talk about King Haakon saying no to Quisling in April 1940, when she was nine, and how to her that no was basically the founding fact of Norwegian democracy, and about how Norwegians could love their king precisely because he couldn't actually do anything to them.

Here's my problem: Erik will be in the second row, and so will grandma's oldest freinds, who are every bit as monarchist as she was. If I eulogize her by defending the monarchy, it turns into a political speech and Erik has to sit through his own grandmother's funeral being used as an argument against him. If I soft-pedal it to "she loved the royals the way she loved the Packers," that's a lie about what she believed — the monarchy was one of the two or three things she was most serious about, and everyone in that church knows it.

So write me a eulogy that threads the needle. I think the key has to be her actual idea: that separating the person a country loves from the person who actually holds power is a feature of a democracy and not a bug, that a purely symbolic head of state gives people's loyalty and affection somewhere to go that isn't a politician, and that this is something a republican could almost respect even while still wanting the institution abolished. Comfort a church full of grieving non-philosophers, honor her without turning it into a throne-and-altar occasion, and give Erik something he can genuinely nod at. Around five minutes, plain language — she hated pretension more than she hated anything except Quisling.
