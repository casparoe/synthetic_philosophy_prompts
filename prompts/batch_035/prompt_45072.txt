For my upper-level seminar on theories of truth I posed the following question on the final exam:

"Peirce, in his 1878 paper 'How to Make Our Ideas Clear,' identifies truth with the opinion that inquiry, pursued indefinitely and by the community of inquirers, is destined to settle on. James, in Pragmatism (1907), instead holds that a belief's truth consists in its working for the believer — in its being verifiable, profitable to our lives, and capable of being assimilated with the rest of our experience. Explain what separates these two accounts. Then evaluate the objection that Peirce's account makes truth hostage to the contingent course of inquiry: if inquiry were cut short, or if a consensus formed and later dissolved, would truth itself come and go? Does the objection succeed against Peirce as he intended the view?"

Student's answer:

"Peirce and James both reject the correspondence theory: truth is not a relation between beliefs and mind-independent facts but something tied to inquiry and practice. For Peirce, a belief is true if it is the belief the scientific community actually converges on. So truth is a social fact about what scientists agree on at a given time. James instead ties truth to consequences: an idea is true if believing it pays, if it leads to satisfactory relations with the rest of our experience.

The objection is that Peirce makes truth depend on the actual future of inquiry. If humanity goes extinct before convergence, the convergence never happens and nothing comes out true. I think this objection succeeds, and Peirce can simply accept it: on his view truth is time-indexed. Newtonian mechanics was true in the nineteenth century, when scientists agreed on it, and became false in the twentieth, when they stopped agreeing. This temporal character of truth is the distinctive feature of Peirce's pragmatism, and it is what James was reacting against: on James's account there is no such dependence on the distant future, since whether a belief works is settled by the believer's present situation.

James's account faces the familiar objection of subjectivism: what pays for me may not pay for you. But James can reply that a true belief must also fit with our other beliefs and with the course of future experience, so truth is not mere comfort. Finally, since both Peirce and James accept the pragmatic maxim — that a concept's meaning is exhausted by its practical consequences — they both hold a verificationist theory of meaning, and this shared core is what makes them pragmatists about truth rather than mere opportunists."
