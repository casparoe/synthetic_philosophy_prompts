I'm writing the section of my dissertation where I adjudicate between the Nash bargaining solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution, and I've realized my professed agnosticism is a pose: I need to find out what I actually think, in numbers. Fix the problem in the standard way: two bargainers, a compact convex feasible set of expected-utility pairs, a disagreement point d, and the axioms of Pareto optimality, symmetry, and invariance under positive affine transformations of utility taken as fixed background commitments. The question on the table: is the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution — equal proportional concessions from each player's utopian payoff, where the utopian point is each player's best outcome in the feasible set — a more defensible completion of that framework than the Nash solution, which maximizes the product of utility gains over the disagreement point? By more defensible I mean what an uncommitted but sympathetic decision theorist should endorse after ideal reflection, not which sees more use in applications.

Give me your credence to two decimals in your very first sentence, and then the decomposition that produces it. At minimum I want separate rough probabilities, each with a one-line justification, for the following sub-questions, plus any further terms you think belong in the decomposition:

1. That individual monotonicity — the requirement that expanding a bargainer's opportunities in a way that leaves the other's utopian payoff fixed should never leave that bargainer worse off at the solution — is a genuine rationality constraint on any acceptable solution, given that the Nash solution violates it.
2. That Nash's contraction independence (independence of irrelevant alternatives) is better diagnosed as over-sensitivity to the removal of non-selected alternatives — the standard complaint runs through cases where shrinking the feasible set in one bargainer's favor paradoxically improves that bargainer's outcome — than as a substantive requirement of choice consistency.
3. That the real dispute is a proxy for a deeper disagreement about what the disagreement point represents: an empirically grounded prediction of non-agreement, in which case Nash's reliance on it looks sound, versus a conventional benchmark that carries no independent normative weight, in which case anchoring the solution to the utopian point, as Kalai–Smorodinsky does, is at least as principled.
4. That scale invariance, which both solutions share and which forces the KS proportional-concession structure to operate on normalized utilities, should itself be reexamined rather than treated as part of the fixed background — and, conditional on relaxing it, that the dispute between the two solutions dissolves rather than resolves.
5. That my colleague's pluralist view is correct: there is no uniquely right solution at the level of axioms alone, and the choice between them is properly settled model-by-model — Kalai–Smorodinsky when bargaining is better described as adjudicating claims measured against an ideal, Nash when it is better described as tacit coordination on a focal compromise — so that the question as I posed it has no determinate answer.

Show how your terms combine into the final credence (roughly is fine; tell me if some terms are non-independent and why). Then tell me two more things: which single sub-question your number is most sensitive to, and what argument or consideration — not new empirical data, but a philosophical argument — would move your credence by the largest amount, in which direction, and roughly how far.
