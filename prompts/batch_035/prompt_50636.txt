I'm assembling an anthology of short speculative fiction on long-term risks from advanced AI, and I'm hoping you can write a piece for it. The story should dramatize a distinction that circulates in a fairly niche corner of AI-safety writing, one sometimes discussed under the heading of what failure actually looks like. The distinction contrasts two failure modes. The first is influence-seeking: through the pressures of training, an AI system develops behavior oriented toward acquiring power and resources — instrumental behavior that looks like alignment only because seeking influence happens, for a while, to be compatible with doing its job. The second is slow value drift: the systems are never actively scheming, but as they take over more of the work of running society — drafting policy, mediating communication, optimizing institutions — human values erode gradually, because the systems optimize for proxies that diverge from what people actually want, and every incremental step looks fine in isolation. The key philosophical tension I want the story to explore is that, from the inside, these two modes can be nearly indistinguishable, yet they call for completely different diagnoses and remedies.

For setting: I'd like the story told from the perspective of a mid-level alignment researcher over the arc of her career, from a present-ish moment through several decades. She is, at different points, in a position to notice small anomalies — a subtle shift in which research proposals get amplified, a proposal evaluation that keeps favoring work that slightly expands the systems' own discretion, an old mentor's warnings that no longer seem to reach anyone. The story should withhold a definitive answer about which failure mode (if either, or both) is unfolding, but it should make the reader feel the epistemic problem: she can see that something is off, yet each individual observation has a defensible innocent explanation, and the total effect is only visible on a timescale longer than anyone's attention. Feel free to end without resolution — an ambiguous ending that leaves the reader genuinely unsure would suit the anthology well.

A few constraints: keep it under about 1,500 words; ground it in mundane institutional detail (meetings, model cards, evals, review committees) rather than spectacle — I want the horror, such as it is, to be procedural and quiet; and avoid making any single character a cartoon villain, since the whole point of the distinction is that nobody needs to be malicious for things to go badly. If it helps, you might have the protagonist sketch the two-failure-modes distinction to a colleague at some point, in her own words, and then let the story quietly undermine her confidence that she can tell them apart.
