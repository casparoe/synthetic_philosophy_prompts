So this is maybe a weird one but hear me out. Last month my sister asked me to lie for her — she's going through a divorce, and she wanted me to tell our mom she was doing fine when she very much was not, just to buy some time before the family found out. And I did it, and honestly it worked out about as well as that kind of thing can. But it's been bugging me since, because I've always thought of "don't lie to family" as basically a hard rule, and now I'm wondering if the rule was ever more than a rule of thumb that I only treat as hard because treating it as hard is usually easier.

Anyway, that sent me down a rabbit hole on moral particularism (Dancy and that crowd), and now I can't stop seeing it in Les Misérables, which I reread every few years. I'd like you to give me a reading of the novel through the particularism vs. generalism debate, and be honest about where the reading works and where it doesn't.

The obvious mapping is Valjean = particularist, Javert = generalist, but I want more than the obvious. On the particularist side: the idea that a consideration can be a reason in one case and not a reason, or even the opposite, in another — e.g. the fact that I promised, or that an act is a lie, counts for me here but can be outweighed or even switched by context. That seems to describe Valjean's whole arc, right? The bishop's candlesticks turn theft-into-gift, or at least turn "turn him in" into "protect him." And then later, at the Champmathieu moment, Valjean has to weigh the same kind of facts differently — now he's the mayor with workers depending on him AND there's an innocent man about to be convicted as him, and the novel really sits inside his head for a whole night working through it. That chapter, the storm-in-a-skull one, feels like a case study in weighing reasons that no general rule is going to settle. And Javert's breakdown at the end — when Valjean spares him in the sewers, Javert literally cannot process a case where "Valjean is a criminal and criminals must be punished" stops being decisive. Is his suicide the novel's argument that generalism is psychologically unstable, that a person whose whole moral life is one invariant principle can't survive an exception?

But here's where I want you to push back on me, because I suspect the reading doesn't fully hold. Hugo is pretty explicitly Christian, and you could say Valjean isn't operating without principles at all — he's operating with exactly one big principle (mercy / love your neighbor), applied everywhere. Is that just generalism at a higher level? Dancy's version of particularism isn't just "be sensitive," it's a claim that reasons don't function invariantly, and I'm not sure the novel cares about that distinction. Also, please don't let the thing collapse into "Javert = Kant, Valjean = utilitarian" — that's not what this debate is about, and every summary I find online does it. Generalism vs. particularism is about whether moral considerations keep their force across cases, not about rules vs. consequences.

One more thing: I'd like it if you kept clear about what's settled vs. genuinely contested here. Which parts are the novel just doing what it does (stuff any Hugo reader would grant) vs. your interpretation, I'd take that as interpretation and not fact. And if you think the novel actually resists my reading somewhere — really say so, don't smooth it over to make the frame fit. Which scenes would you point me to as the best test cases, and does the novel end up showing something the philosophical literature on its own doesn't?
