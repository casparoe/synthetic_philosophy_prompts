In that group we just finished Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles and Max Black's 1952 counterexample to it: a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres of the same size and composition, symmetrically placed some fixed distance apart, with no observers and nothing else in existence — and Black's claim is that the two spheres share all their qualitative properties while nonetheless being two rather than one.

The rendering that occurred to me immediately is this: the configuration space of the two-sphere universe carries an involutive swap symmetry (a Z2 action), and the philosophical dispute looks like the question of whether that swap should be treated as a gauge redundancy — in which case we quotient the configuration space by it, the putative two-sphere state collapses to a single point in the physical state space, and Black's universe is secretly a one-object universe misdescribed — or as a physical, global symmetry that maps one genuine state to a distinct but indiscernible one, in which case Black wins and the quotient is the wrong thing to take.
