I'm in a history of economic thought course, we just did Hume's Political Discourses (1752), and I've talked myself into an argument that seems to prove every country should have been running a mild perpetual money-printing scheme since the 1700s. I refuse to accept that conclusion, but I can't find the broken step.

Here's what I take Hume to be saying (paraphrasing, partly from memory): in the essay "Of Money" he says money isn't itself one of the goods being traded — it's an instrument that makes exchange run smoothly, not one of the wheels — so within a single country the total quantity is basically irrelevant: more money just means higher prices. In "Of the Balance of Trade" he runs the famous thought experiments: if four-fifths of Britain's money vanished in one night, prices would sink, exports would boom, and the money would flow right back in until the country was level with its neighbors; if the money were multiplied fivefold in a night, everything would get so dear that money would drain right back out the same way. But — and this is the part that gets me — in "Of Money" he also says that a growing stock of gold and silver helps industry only during the gap between the new money arriving and prices catching up, since people don't raise wages and prices instantly, and he more or less credits the influx from the American mines for Europe becoming so industrious. So: the level of money is neutral, but increases of money do real work while prices lag.

Now the argument:

(1) A one-shot multiplication or annihilation of the money stock has no lasting real effect; the quantity of money in a nation is neutral in the long run.
(2) An increase in money stimulates industry during the interval before prices adjust, and that stimulation is real output, not illusion.
(3) Nothing fixes the length of that interval; it depends on how the increase arrives and how quickly people update. A slow, steady drip of new money keeps landing inside the interval.
(4) So a government issuing paper currency at a slow, steady rate can stay permanently inside the favorable interval — every new tranche restarts the lag before prices have fully absorbed the last one.
(5) Then a nation gets a permanent real stimulus from money creation, which flatly contradicts (1).
(6) And it's basically free: paper costs nothing to produce, unlike silver that has to be dug out of a mine in Mexico, so every sensible nation should have done this continuously since 1752 and grown faster than it did.
(7) Except Hume himself, in the very same essays, treats artificially expanding paper credit as a mistake — fake money that foreigners won't accept in payment, which just pushes prices up and chases the silver abroad. So he clearly didn't think his own premises licensed a printing press.

Conclusion: Hume's two claims, neutrality plus the lag, jointly entail a policy he'd have hated and that history keeps failing to vindicate, so one of them has to go. My TA says the argument equivocates somewhere but won't say where, and my roommate mumbled something about how the thought experiments work because they're surprises and a permanent announced policy can't be a surprise forever — which feels like it's pointing at something but also feels too easy, since Hume's merchants and laborers obviously weren't computing rational expectations either. So which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If you think more than one step is doing something fishy, say so — I'd rather get the full damage report than a tidy single diagnosis.
