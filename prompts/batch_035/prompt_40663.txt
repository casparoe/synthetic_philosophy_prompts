so I'm writing up my position sheet for a seminar on Quine's Two Dogmas and I'd like an audit before the others tear into it. Here's roughly where I stand:

(1) Quine was right about the web: no claim meets experience on its own, and even logic and math are immune to revision only because we choose to hold them fixed, at the cost of drastic adjustments elsewhere.

(2) "no bachelors are married" is true purely in virtue of meaning, and I wouldnt call it an empirical claim — though I admit that if the surrounding practices changed enough we'd just stop using "bachelor" that way, which I gather is the Quinean sense in which even it stays revisable.

(3) I'm not a global conventionalist: there's a real difference between changing our theory and changing our language, and there are facts about which of the two is happening in a given case.

(4) Classical logic could in principle be abandoned for empirical reasons — say a pile of inconsistent datasets and stubborn paradoxes kept pushing us toward something paraconsistent. But when that happens, I think the honest description is that "not" and "or" acquired new meanings, and the old classical laws were still true in the old language.

(5) "2+2=4" isn't an empirical generalization, but it isn't knowable a priori in any strong sense either — it's just very deep in the web.

Tell me which of these are actually in tension and which only look like it, and rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad. For each real one, say what would have to give and what it costs. I suspect at least one of the five has to go but I genuinely can't tell which, so don't soft-pedal it.
