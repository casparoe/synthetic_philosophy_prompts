I'm a first-year assistant professor, and last week I sat through my first faculty search committee meeting — and then through a two-hour argument about it afterwards that I couldn't follow to the end. Our bylaws say a candidate is to be hired only if the committee judges both that their research record is excellent and that their teaching fits the department's needs, and we are required to record separate votes on each of those judgments plus a final up-or-down vote. We are a three-person committee this year. On the research judgment, two of us voted yes and one voted no; on the teaching judgment, again two yes and one no — but it was a different pair each time, so every individual member's three votes were perfectly consistent with the stated rule. On the final question, however, the two members who had each been the lone "no" on one premise voted against the hire, and it failed two to one. The chair shrugged and said only the final vote is binding.

Over coffee afterwards, two of my senior colleagues fought about what had actually happened. Dana said the committee had been collectively irrational: a majority of us accepted the research premise, a majority accepted the teaching premise, the rule says those jointly entail hiring, and then a majority rejected the hire — so the committee, taken as a whole, believes all the premises of a valid argument and denies its conclusion. If we had voted only on the premises and let the rule mechanically determine the outcome, we would have been obliged to make an offer; the fact that we didn't shows we switched from premise-based to conclusion-based voting midstream, whenever it suited the result. Marcus answered that nothing irrational happened, because there is no "the group" that believes anything — there are three individuals, each with a perfectly consistent set of judgments, and the contradiction Dana is pointing at only appears if you illegitimately manufacture a fourth mind out of our majority votes. Dana replied that if there's no collective agent, then the committee's vote isn't a decision of anything and the bylaws are pointless: the entire institution of committee voting presupposes that judgments can be pooled into a group outcome, and any pooling method that lets "the group" contradict itself while every member is consistent has failed at its one job. Marcus said the real failure would be Dana's preferred procedure, which would have forced a hire that two-thirds of the committee opposed — including the very people whose judgments constituted the majorities on each premise — on the strength of an inference that only one of us actually endorses; majority rule is supposed to track members' all-things-considered judgments, not a syllogism the committee never voted on.

There were further rounds in this vein (someone brought up a candidate we rejected two years ago in circumstances that were the mirror image, and Marcus argued that premise votes are a crude instrument anyway because they can't register how strongly a member holds each belief). A passing graduate student muttered something about Condorcet, but when I looked that up it seemed to be about circular rankings, which we didn't have. What I would like is a careful adjudication of the argument as it actually unfolded: go through the main moves on each side and say who is right about what, whether our outcome was a failure of rationality, a failure of procedure, or not a failure at all, and whether Dana's premise-based procedure is a genuine improvement or merely relocates the problem. I'd also like to know whether there is any principled way to design a voting procedure that avoids whatever went wrong here — or some reason to think nothing could — and whether this exact configuration has a name of its own, since the Condorcet material doesn't seem to fit.
