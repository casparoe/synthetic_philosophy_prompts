I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been invited to give a forty-five-minute public lecture at a science festival on the question of when we are entitled to say that an AI system wants or believes something. I want to build the whole thing around Dennett's three stances and his account of real patterns, but not at the level of slogans: the audience will arrive split between thinking chatbots literally have minds and thinking any talk of beliefs here is sloppy anthropomorphism, and my aim is to make the position in between feel like the natural one. Plan the session for me rather than explaining the ideas yourself: the order in which the physical, design, and intentional stances must be introduced, and whether it is a mistake to start with the physical stance because it primes the audience to hear the other two as concessions; the single carrying example that can be scaled from a simple artifact up to something language-like, and what it must have and lack in order to work at every scale; the misconceptions the audience will predictably form — I anticipate real patterns being heard as real entities, and the whole framework being heard as an argument that minds don't exist — and exactly where in the sequence each one should be headed off. I'd also like to know what I should openly decline to explain at this level, and one question I can put to the audience at the end that would reveal whether they have grasped the central idea: that pattern-reality is relative to the stance we take, not a runner-up prize handed out when the physical description runs out.
