For some years I have kept a private list of cases in which I record my gut verdicts on whether something is corruption, and I have come to suspect that the list is less a moral position than a map of my own society's tolerances. I would like to know whether my verdicts add up to a principle, and I would like your help settling this.

The cases and my verdicts:

1. A procurement officer awards a road contract to the firm of an old schoolfriend. No money changes hands, and the friend's bid is anyway the lowest. My verdict: this is corruption, though nothing was bought.
2. The same officer accepts a case of wine at Christmas from a firm that regularly bids for his contracts. My verdict: not corruption — an ordinary courtesy, though I would want it declared.
3. A nurse expects patients' families to leave an envelope of cash in addition to flowers, as custom in her country more or less requires. My verdict: corruption.
4. The same nurse accepts only the flowers, which cost much the same as the envelope. My verdict: not corruption, and the difference between this and case 3 seems real to me even though economically it is nearly none.
5. A backbencher tells a village, openly and on the record: re-elect me and I will bring the water project here. My verdict: not corruption — constituency representation, arguably democracy working as intended.
6. A local councillor quietly gets parking tickets cancelled for the households that delivered her votes. My verdict: corruption, and I feel the gap between this and case 5 even though both trade targeted benefits for electoral support.
7. A clerk moves his cousin's licence application to the top of the queue as a family favour. My verdict: not corruption, just ordinary life.
8. The same clerk moves a stranger's application to the top for a cash payment. My verdict: corruption — and yet I cannot say what the cousin is entitled to that the stranger is not.

So my question. Is there one principle — breach of a duty of impartiality, betrayal of a role's defining obligation, exploitation of monopoly over a scarce good, or something else — that captures these verdicts, or close? Or is the best explanation simply that I have absorbed the institutional taboos of one particular corner of the West and mistaken them for universals? Please state the principle, or a short list of candidates, as precisely as you can; say which verdicts it captures and which it forces me to give up, distinguishing verdicts that are merely uncomfortable from those that are genuinely unacceptable to hold; and apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on, which I have deliberately not listed: a party machine that, in the week before an election, hands out sheets of roofing tin to householders in districts whose votes it needs.

Please answer in British English, in at most 500 words.
