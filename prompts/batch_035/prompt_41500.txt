I've spent the past year reading in philosophy of psychiatry, mostly around the DSM debates, and I've noticed a pattern I'd like diagnosed rather than adjudicated: the argument over whether mental disorders are natural kinds has been running in essentially the same shape since at least the 1980s and nobody budges. On one side, the realists (following the line of thought that runs through people like Jerome Wakefield) hold that there is a fact of the matter about what is and isn't a disorder — Wakefield's "harmful dysfunction" analysis, proposed in the early 1990s, being the best-known attempt at a hybrid: a condition is a disorder when some internal mechanism fails to perform a function it was shaped for by natural selection, AND that failure is harmful by the standards of the person's social world, so the definition needs both a factual component and a value component. On the other side sit the anti-essentialists and social constructionists, who point out that the evolutionary-function component is nearly unfalsifiable in practice (what was low mood selected for, exactly?), that the value component smuggles in an entire culture's standards, and that DSM categories look much more like historically contingent tools for billing, communication, and research coordination than like carved joints of nature. The two camps have exchanged the same objections and the same replies for three decades. It's beginning to look like my old LessWrong-shaped model of a disagreement that is generating heat without producing belief updates.

Here is my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: Ian Hacking's work on looping effects explains why the debate can't terminate, and in a way that suggests the question itself is ill-posed for psychiatric kinds. Hacking argued that classifications of people are "interactive kinds": when you classify someone as multiple personality, or as autistic, or as suffering from a given disorder, the classified people and the institutions around them respond to the classification, changing the very population the kind is supposed to name — so the object of classification is a moving target that partly constitutes itself in response to being classified. If that's right, then asking whether "major depressive disorder" names a natural kind may be like asking whether a shadow names its object: the kind's extension shifts under the causal influence of the kind's own label, and neither side's evidence could ever settle the matter. But I can also tell the opposite story, on which this is really two groups answering different questions under one heading: the realists are doing metaphysics (what could make a disorder attribution true), the anti-essentialists are doing sociology of the DSM (how the categories actually function institutionally), and the apparent contradiction between them is an artifact of both using the word "real." A third story is that it's a genuinely substantive empirical question that's just very hard, waiting on mechanisms and biomarkers that don't exist yet.

Please take the role of a philosopher of science and psychiatry, and think carefully before committing — I'd like you to briefly brainstorm the live diagnoses (verbal dispute, different questions under one heading, ill-posed because of looping effects, substantive-but-hard, something I haven't listed) and then argue for one with actual engagement in what the participants say, not a survey. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a research program, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. And please push back where my framing deserves it — if my Hacking-based hypothesis rests on a confused reading of looping effects, or if the debate is actually less stalled than I'm portraying, say so plainly rather than working within my frame. I'd rather have a committed diagnosis that flags its own weak points than a hedge that ranks all five possibilities as equally plausible.
