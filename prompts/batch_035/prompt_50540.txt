Analyze the subprime short trades of 2005–2008 — the credit default swap positions built by Michael Burry and the other investors later chronicled in The Big Short — as a case study for the no-trade theorems in financial economics, and then tell me precisely where the theorems break down as descriptions of what happened.

I want a disciplined mapping, not a loose analogy. Start with what the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem actually claims: with common priors, common knowledge of rationality, and a Pareto-optimal initial allocation of risk, newly arriving information cannot by itself motivate trade — so sustained speculative volume is a standing puzzle. Identify who the players in this episode map onto: the hedge funds buying CDS protection, the bank desks selling it, the CDO managers sitting in between, and the ultimate holders of the mortgage risk. Classify the CDS purchases carefully: to what extent were they insurance against pre-existing exposure versus pure speculation on disagreement, and note that the theorem only rules out the latter. Then confront the crux: the protection sellers were sophisticated desks that presumably knew their counterparties had studied the underlying loans more carefully than they had. So which assumption failed? Consider each candidate honestly — a non-common prior (the sellers simply assigned negligible probability to a nationwide housing decline, the buyers assigned it substantial probability), a failure of rationality on one side, or an agency-based breakdown of the rationality assumption (the desk's traders, the rating agencies, and the CDO managers were each optimizing for fee income and short-term marks rather than terminal wealth, so the entity trading was not the entity bearing the risk). Say which reading you find most defensible and why.

Then bring in the Grossman–Stiglitz impossibility result: if prices fully reveal informed demand, no one has an incentive to gather information in the first place, so equilibrium requires noise. Explain why CDS premia and CDO spreads failed to transmit the skeptics' information to the wider market for roughly two years — was this noise trading of unusual magnitude, or a failure of the price-revelation mechanism itself?

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode show about the theory? Note that the skeptics were nearly ruined before they were vindicated — the negative carry of the swaps, investor redemption threats, and margin dynamics meant that being right in 2006 could easily have meant being insolvent in 2007. The no-trade framework presupposes a fixed endowment of risk being reallocated; it is silent about liquidity, timing, and forced unwinding. And it is silent about the strangest feature of the episode: rather than merely reallocating existing mortgage risk, the demand for the short side induced banks to manufacture new synthetic exposure to the same underlying loans, so that the trade created part of the risk it traded on. Does that phenomenon fit any version of the no-trade framework at all, or does it show the theorem presupposes a conception of markets as reallocating given risks that this episode straightforwardly falsifies? Conclude with a verdict: does this episode confirm the rationalist epistemology of markets (the pessimists were eventually paid, and only a market with persistent disagreement could have paid them so richly), refute it (the price system was structurally blocked from aggregating the most available information), or reveal that the theorems were never intended as descriptions of markets with heterogeneous priors and agency frictions in the first place? Where the theory is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
