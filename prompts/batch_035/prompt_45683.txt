I am a novelist drafting a book in which a minor character is a metaphysician of persistence, and I want to get the material into my head by way of parody rather than by more textbooks. Please write a deadpan craft-of-fiction advice column, the sort that would appear in a magazine for writers, from the perspective of a hardline perdurantist instructing novelists on how to describe their characters. The advice must insist that characters strictly have temporal parts rather than enduring through the narrative: it should ban locutions that smuggle endurance back in (a character may not simply "be" brave across two chapters when only the chapter-one part is brave), treat the classical problem of change as a continuity error to be caught at copy-edit, and recast the counting problem as confusion about how many protagonists a novel with an unreliable narrator really has. Include a sidebar recommending stage theory as a rival "close third-person" aesthetic, in which each scene contains only one temporal part and first-person pronouns refer to whoever is currently on the page. The standard objections — the statue and the clay, the fission of the self — should surface as grumbles from the letters page rather than as stated arguments. Around 600 words, polished and deadpan throughout: the joke is that the metaphysics is being taken seriously and only the editorial absolutism is mad. It should be funny to someone who knows the Lewis–Sider literature yet perfectly readable to someone who does not.
