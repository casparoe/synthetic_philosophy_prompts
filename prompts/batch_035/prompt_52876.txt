I'm setting the take-home final for my graduate seminar on vagueness, and I want one essay question built around a case I've invented for the exam, so that nobody can coast on the standard Williamson-versus-supervaluationism summary. Here is the case:

The town of Fennwick has spent decades in litigation over its vague housing-law term "affordable." Finally the town votes to stipulate a sharp boundary: a dwelling counts as affordable if and only if its rent is at most thirty percent of the regional median income. Immediately after, two things happen. A local judge remarks that now the town has finally discovered what "affordable" meant all along — the cutoff was always there, and the vote simply located it. A councilwoman retorts that this is nonsense: before the vote there was nothing to locate, and the town didn't discover a boundary, it created one. Meanwhile ordinary speakers keep talking as they always did — calling a unit at 30.0 percent "affordable, barely" and one at 30.5 percent "not really affordable, though it's borderline-ish" — and a new dispute erupts over whether the median income figure itself has been measured precisely enough for the boundary to be applied.

The essay question should ask which of the two leading theories of vagueness — epistemicism or supervaluationism — gives the better account of Fennwick's situation, and in particular of the three stubborn facts in the case: that the stipulation seemed possible at all, that it seemed oddly inconclusive (the "-ish" talk survives it), and that borderline cases immediately re-emerge at the new boundary. Students must engage at least the following: whether the epistemicist can say the vote merely revealed a pre-existing cutoff, or must instead concede that stipulation changes meaning and therefore that the judge is wrong; whether the supervaluationist's picture of stipulation as selecting among admissible precisifications fits the councilwoman's remark better, and what it costs her to say the sentence "there is a sharp cutoff in Fennwick now" is true while no particular cutoff statement is; and whether the surviving "-ish" talk and the boundary regress are best diagnosed as fresh first-order vagueness, higher-order vagueness, or something else, and what follows for each theory.

Please deliver three things. First, the question exactly as it would appear on the exam, roughly 250 words, written so that a student who has only memorized the labels ("semantic indecision," "margin for error," "supertruth") cannot fake an answer. Second, a model answer of about 700 words. The model answer must commit: it should argue for one side outright, not survey both and abstain, and it should say plainly where the losing theory's reply goes wrong. It should stand on argument alone, with no citations, bibliographies, or appeals to what particular authors have claimed elsewhere — I want the reasoning self-contained. Third, a marking scheme out of 20 points, organized as five must-hit moves worth 4 points each, with for each move a one-line note on the error weaker answers typically make, plus a short closing paragraph naming the one additional thing that separates an A answer from a B answer. Please write everything in American English.
