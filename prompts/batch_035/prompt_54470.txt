For the final session of my graduate seminar "Darwin's Afterlives," I am staging a counterfactual: Darwin, given the whole of the last century and a half of science and philosophy to review, is asked to pass judgment on three developments that postdate him. My students will write on the first; I would like a model answer on the other two.

The first is the fate of his own account of the moral sense. In the Descent of Man, Darwin explains conscience naturalistically — sympathy and the social instincts, extended by reason, habit, and language, and reinforced by the praise and blame of the community — and he even allows that in the struggle for existence, groups whose members were more courageous, sympathetic, and faithful would tend to prevail. He therefore had at least a hand in the group-selection idea, and I want the students to say where he would stand in the twentieth-century controversy it provoked: Wynne-Edwards' excesses, Williams's critique, kin selection and the gene's-eye view, and the later multilevel-selection revival associated with Sober and E. O. Wilson.

The second, which I want you to address, concerns a remark Darwin made in one of his last letters. Writing to William Graham on 3 July 1881, after noting that his inward conviction is that the Universe is not the result of chance, he adds: "with me the horrid doubt always arises whether the convictions of man's mind, which has been developed from the mind of the lower animals, are of any value or at all trustworthy. Would any one trust in the convictions of a monkey's mind, if there are any convictions in such a mind?" Present-day metaethicists — Sharon Street is perhaps the best-known — have pressed essentially this thought into evolutionary debunking arguments against moral realism: if natural selection shaped our evaluative judgments for fitness rather than for truth, the realist owes us an account of why those judgments should be expected to track independent moral facts. Here is what I want from you: given everything Darwin actually wrote — the naturalistic story about conscience in the Descent, the trajectory from tentative theism to the agnosticism he describes in his Autobiography, and this late confession of doubt — would he welcome the debunking conclusion, resist it, or split the difference? And a related question about religion rather than morality: how do you think this same man, who was scrupulously reluctant to attack religious belief in public and who gently distanced himself from both atheism and dogmatic theism, would react to the combative New Atheism of the 2000s — to Dawkins, whom he would otherwise have reason to admire as a fellow champion of the gene's-eye view? Please ground your reconstruction in his actual commitments, distinguish clearly between what he demonstrably held and what you are extrapolating, and say candidly where you think the evidence runs out.
