I sit on the bioethics advisory panel of a national health ministry, and roughly twice a year we are asked to advise on policies that affect not only how well-off future people will be but, in some cases, which people will exist at all: rules for embryo screening, birth subsidies, warnings about maternal age, what fertility treatments the state will cover. The panel is permanently split on population ethics, so we have settled on a working compromise: we accept the procreation asymmetry — there is a moral reason not to create a life not worth living that has no counterpart requiring us to create a happy life — while staying officially neutral on whether there is positive value in creating additional lives that would go well. I would like that compromise turned into a written procedure the panel can run on any proposal that reaches us, in prose an official with no philosophical training can follow. It should state the questions in the order they are to be asked, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, mark the points where the procedure must stop and hand the question back to the panel because it turns on a contested reading rather than a settled one, and say where it can close on its own. Three distinctions must survive the translation into procedure: between policies affecting people who will exist either way and policies that change who exists; between the claim that a miserable life should not be created and the stronger claim that creating it wrongs the person created, on which the literature has no consensus; and between what the asymmetry forbids and what it merely declines to recommend. Then run the procedure on three actual proposals of the kind we see: state-funded preimplantation screening for a condition that guarantees death in early childhood but a life until then better than most children's; a cash subsidy for third children, defended with polling evidence that the children born because of it will be wanted and loved; and mandatory folic-acid fortification of flour, which improves the welfare of children who would have existed either way. At least one of the three should yield a conclusion I will find unwelcome, and you should say which one it is and why it stings. Throughout, keep separate what is settled among people who know population ethics and what is genuinely contested — the asymmetry itself is contested, as is any person-affecting reading of it — and never present a contested view as the consensus. Write the whole thing as continuous prose only, no headings, bullets, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a long letter to a colleague.
