Quick background so we're on the same page, then what I actually want from you.

So you probably know the setup: Williamson argues no condition is luminous, where luminous means something like: a condition C such that, whenever C obtains, you're in a position to know it obtains. Even your own sensations don't qualify. The showcase case is feeling cold. Put someone in a room and lower the tempurature a tiny bit each minute — each step too small to notice. If at minute 47 they can know that they feel cold, then by the safety idea (to know, you'd have to get it right in nearby cases where you'd form the same belief), they should count as knowing at minute 46 too, when they don't quite feel cold yet — or so the argument runs, walking the chain down until it hits an absurdity. So there's a margin for error even for how you feel right now, and the KK principle takes a hit, since knowing that you know p requires p to be luminous-ish.

The usual pushback is stuff like denying safety, or restricting which worlds count as nearby, or fiddling with what in a position to know means.

Here's what I want you to argue instead. First, argue that the anti-luminosity argument doesn't just threaten sensations — it generalizes to basically everything, including paradigm external-world facts like having hands. There are nearby worlds where your hands look and feel exactly the same to you but you're a handless illusion victim; if the safety principle is strong enough to create margins for feeling cold, it should create margins for having hands too. Second, argue that this isn't a reason to panic into skepticism but a reason to say the argument smuggled in a fixed, context-independent similarity metric between worlds, when really what counts as nearby tracks what's salient and at stake in the situation, not some raw similarity ordering. On that view the argument keeps its real lesson (there's always some margin for error if you insist on a global metric) but loses its bite against KK: whenever you know p and the question of whether you know p is actually live, the relevant alternatives to your knowing are all settled, so you can know you know. Restricted, stakes-flavored KK survives.

So the thesis, one line: the anti-luminosity argument is valid but unsound, and the false premise is the context-independence of closeness, not the safety principle itself.
