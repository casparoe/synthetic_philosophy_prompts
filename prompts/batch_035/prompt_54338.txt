My flatmate and I have spent two years arguing about whether utilitarian aggregation rides roughshod over the separateness of persons, and she has finally adopted a policy of refusing to discuss theory at all: she will only issue verdicts on cases. Her track record so far:

(a) A surgeon cuts up one healthy traveler to distribute his organs to five dying patients: impermissible.
(b) A utility monster who converts resources into vastly more welfare than anyone else would get, so that giving it everything maximizes total welfare: impermissible.
(c) The runaway trolley diverted from five onto one: permissible.
(d) Redistributive taxation of the well-off to lift the badly-off, in circumstances where incentive effects mean total welfare ends up slightly lower than it would have been without the redistribution: permissible.
(e) A compulsory national vaccination program that will predictably kill two people out of a hundred million while preventing thousands of deaths: permissible.
(f) Forcing one specific person to surrender a kidney to save two others, where the donor survives and is otherwise healthy: impermissible.

This week her city is voting on two proposals, and she has agreed to rule on them, sight unseen, after I describe them to you.

The first is a National Service Act: a fair lottery conscripts one in two hundred twenty-year-olds for a year of hazardous flood-defense and disaster-relief work. Conscription is compulsory, with prison as the alternative. Each conscript faces roughly a 1-in-500 chance of death and near-certain hardship; the program is projected to save several hundred lives spread across the whole population and to prevent catastrophic losses that would otherwise fall on identifiable coastal towns.

The second is a Voluntary Renal Compensation Scheme: the state offers a fixed, substantial payment for kidney donation to strangers on the transplant list. Uptake is expected to come almost entirely from the destitute, who would be volunteering in a robust but not literally coercive sense.

Here is what I want from you. First, lay out at least two genuinely different rules or principles that fit all six of her earlier verdicts — I can see she is running something, but I cannot tell whether it is a constraint against using identifiable people as mere means that yields in cases of equal ex ante risk, or an ex ante/ex post distinction about certain versus probabilistic burdens, or a prioritarian floor that licenses redistribution and risk-spreading but not sacrifice of the concentrated few, or something about consent and the voluntariness of the burden instead. Say what each candidate rule predicts about the National Service Act and about the Renal Compensation Scheme, which so far as I can see do not get answered in the same way by all of them. Second, give me your best single guess at the verdict she will issue on each, with your reasons for preferring that rule over its rivals as the account of her. Third, describe the one further case — you design it — that would most cleanly discriminate between your top two candidate rules. Finally, and this matters to me: keep straight what is settled from what is contested among people who actually work on this. I do not want you presenting, say, one particular way of formalizing the separateness objection, or one story about why she accepts the vaccination program, as though it were the consensus, when the real literature is divided on whether the objection even succeeds against utilitarianism and on what exactly the objection amounts to. Flag which parts of your reconstruction of her would be accepted by nearly everyone and which parts involve taking sides.
