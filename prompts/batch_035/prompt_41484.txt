Science journalist here, and apologies in advance for the half-baked question. Ages ago I read Godel, Escher, Bach and the dialogue with the ant colony that talks (Aunt Hillary, if I remember right) has basically haunted my reporting ever since, because I keep running into the same shape of thing everywhere. Here's the shape: a system whose parts are individually dumb as bricks — ants following pheormone gradients, neurons that sum inputs and fire, whatever — but where the whole reliably does things you'd call believing, wanting, remembering, planning, and where you cannot get the same predictive grip from the lower-level description, at least not in practice, only in principle. And whenever I raise this with scientists I interview I get one of two shrugs: it's "just emergence," or ascribing wants to the colony is "just a metaphor." Neither seems to touch the question I actually care about, which is something like: when is it literally true that the whole system wants food, versus merely a convenient shorthand that compresses our own predictions? Is there an established concept for this move of locating the beliefs at the level of the colony rather than the ants, and for the question of when such ascriptions track something real in the system rather than just our bookkeeping? And if the nearest concept really is emergence, why doesn't emergence by itself settle it? I don't just want a term — I want to know what the thing is and isn't, where the edges are, e.g. whether a thermostat is inside the boundary or outside it, and whether it matters that the parts of the colony would also happily describe *us* the same way if they could.
