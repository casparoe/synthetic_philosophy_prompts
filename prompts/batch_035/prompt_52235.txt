I teach the bargaining-theory unit of a final-year undergraduate course in cooperative game theory and normative economics, and I am setting this year's take-home exam. Please write one question, worth 20 marks in total, on why the Nash bargaining solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution give different answers, constructed so that it cannot be answered by reciting the two axiom lists.

The exam question should require students to do the following, in order: (1) construct a specific, numerically explicit bargaining problem — a feasible set given by inequalities or vertices in the plane, together with a disagreement point — on which the two solutions disagree; (2) compute both solutions for that problem, showing their work; and (3) argue, with reference to that same problem, which retained axiom they find more defensible: Nash's independence of irrelevant alternatives, or Kalai–Smorodinsky's individual monotonicity, where monotonicity is understood relative to the ideal point (the vector whose i-th coordinate is player i's maximum feasible payoff, subject to the other player receiving at least their disagreement payoff). To make the clash concrete rather than abstract, the question should embed the construction in a stylized application — for example, wage bargaining in which an outside option improves for one side only — so that the student must say which axiom the improvement ought to register through, and why.

The question must be self-contained: state any definitions and notation a student needs, since our course covers bargaining solutions only in the final two weeks and I cannot assume memorized axioms.

Then provide (a) a model answer of about 600 words that actually carries out the computation for a concrete bargaining problem — the numbers should work out cleanly, and the answer should display both solutions' coordinates; and (b) a marking scheme that allocates the 20 marks across the construction, the two computations, and the evaluative discussion. The scheme should separate an A answer from a B answer by a single named criterion: an A answer must locate the precise point at which the two axiomatizations part ways — that Kalai–Smorodinsky conditions on the ideal point, which tracks the shape of the feasible set's upper boundary, while Nash conditions only on the set of alternatives itself — whereas a B answer merely observes that the two solutions can differ. The scheme should also specify deductions for the confusions students actually have: treating independence of irrelevant alternatives as if it were a Pareto requirement, conflating the disagreement point with the ideal point, and concluding that one solution is simply "fairer" without connecting the claim to an axiom.
