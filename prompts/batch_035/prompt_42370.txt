so im taking a seminar on well-being this semester and the final assignment is basically to invent an adversarial collaboration between two positions that have been arguing all term, and write the joint statement they'd both sign. my two TAs have basically been cast as the two camps and i get to write the document lol. the setup: one is a committed hedonist about well-being (pleasure and the avoidance of suffering is all there is to how well your life goes for you), the other is a desire-satisfaction theorist (your life goes well insofar that what you actually want -- including wanting things about the actual world, not just about how you feel -- is really the case).

you can guess the flashpoint all semester: the experience machine. but they've also fought about all the satellite cases, like the guy who wants to actually climb the mountain vs the guy who just wants the experience of climbing it, the "deception is fine if you never find out" question, and the sour-grapes thing where someone's desires reshape themselves to fit a bad life.

heres what i need the document to contain:

1. a numbered section of claims both camps can sign. these have to be real claims, not diplomatic filler -- like stuff about what the experience machine is actually testing, about which versions of the case produce which intuitions and in whom, and about what any theory of well-being owes us. no "both agree philosophy is hard" garbage.

2. a disagreements table. each disagreement has to be phrased so the camp it describes would accept the wording -- so the hedonist would sign the hedonist row and the desire theorist would sign the desire-theorist row, without either feeling strawmanned. one row should be about the person plugged in for fifty years: is their life going well? another should be about whether the moment you're unplugged and debriefed matters for how the life as a whole went. if any of their disputes turn out to be partly verbal -- like maybe they mean different things by "good for you" -- the document should say which part and why, and put the genuinely substantive residue in its own rows.

3. cruxes. for each, a concrete case or argument BOTH would agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction written down in advance. im pretty sure one crux is whether global desires (like wanting your kids to actually be ok) can fail in a way that's bad for YOU even when all your experiences are perfect. another might be whether a hybrid theory -- splitting well-being between felt experience and actual satisfaction -- is a stable position or just a way of hiding from the disagreement. feel free to add cruxes i havent thought of, and invent the test cases: my professor loved the idea of a "gradual slide" machine where your real life gets replaced with simulation so slowly nobody could ever notice the transition, and i think something like that could genuinely stress both camps differently than the original machine does.

the whole thing should read like something two people who spent a semester in good faith would actually produce, not a textbook summary. the value is in finding formulations neither side would flinch at, and in making the residual disagreement exact enough that you could tell what evidence would move it.
