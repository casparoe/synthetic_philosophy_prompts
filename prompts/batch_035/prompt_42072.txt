I teach an intro ethics elective at a community college -- about 18 adult students, mixed ages, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class activity that gets them to *feel* the difference between guilt and shame as moral emotions, not just take notes on it. My idea: some kind of multi-round game with stakes that matter a little, where small transgressions get flagged two different ways -- one frame pointed at the act ("you broke rule 4"), the other at the player ("that shows what kind of person you are at this table") -- and we watch what each framing does to behavior in later rounds: repair and coming clean vs. hiding, withdrawing, or blaming the rules. Then a last segment where the class collectively decides which "culture" to run the final round under, and a debrief that ties that vote back to the old anthropological shame-culture/guilt-culture contrast, including whether a culture can really *choose* its moral emotion like that or just drift into one. Give me rules, a scoring system that doesn't need real money, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the distinction the game can't reproduce on its own (e.g. shame working through an internalized witness when nobody's actually watching) and I'll have to supply in the debrief. Keep the names of the philosophers out of it until the end -- I want them to meet the ideas before they meet the labels.
