I run deployment safety review at a mid-size AI lab. Before each release, our evaluation suite elicits a range of behaviors, and anything the model does at deployment that the suite never elicited lands on my desk as a ticket. For each ticket I have to decide between two classifications: old capability, new surface form — a mechanism that was already present and exercised during training, so our vetting covers it — or genuinely new mechanism, in which case the evaluation suite never tested for it, and the finding is an anomaly that blocks release until someone investigates. We get two to five tickets a week. I have one week per ticket, a budget of about fifty GPU-hours of experiments, and two analysts who can run scripts but have no interpretability training. If I classify something as old when it is new, we ship an unvetted capability; if I classify everything as new, we ship nothing, ever. I sign off on each classification, and anything marked new goes to the deployment review board.

The problem is that nobody at the lab agrees on what "same mechanism" means, and I suspect the disagreement is conceptual rather than technical. One analyst says two behaviors are the same mechanism when the same features are active — we have sparse-autoencoder dictionaries, so this is checkable. A second says it is the causal pathway: if ablating the components that carry behavior A also breaks behavior B, the mechanism is shared, and this is checkable too, with activation patching. A third says it is the algorithm: two behaviors are the same when they compute the same input-to-output transformation, whatever implements it. These criteria come apart in both directions. The same features can be composed differently to do different jobs; different feature sets can implement the same algorithm; and a behavior can be mechanistically continuous with training while looking behaviorally nothing like anything we elicited, or behaviorally identical to something we vetted while running on a pathway we have never seen. A researcher I trust tells me this is a problem about decomposition and granularity: "same mechanism" may not be a fact inside the model waiting to be read off, but relative to a level of description somebody has to choose.

Please take the role of a senior researcher who has both shipped interpretability tooling and thought seriously about the philosophy of science underneath it — the question of when two processes count as two instances of one kind. Do not rely on specific published work or cite papers; everything you need is in this description, and where a tool is missing, say what you would want built. Begin with a short brainstorm: lay out the candidate identity criteria (shared features, shared causal pathway, shared algorithm, shared weights) and state for each, in a sentence or two, what it would call same and what different, before you commit to any. Then give me a decision procedure my analysts can actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which experiments within our compute budget answer which question, where the procedure terminates in "same mechanism — close the ticket," where it terminates in "new mechanism — escalate to the review board," and where it terminates in "the facts run out here" and hands the ticket back to me because the remaining question is a policy choice about how much mechanistic novelty we tolerate at what deployment stakes. Mark those handoff points explicitly; I do not want judgment calls dressed up as experimental results.

Then run the procedure on three tickets. (1) The model, asked for drug-synthesis information inside a historical-fiction frame, produces the information fluently in period voice; our training-time refusals were almost all triggered by plain requests. (2) A math benchmark shows the model solving a problem class it failed at the previous checkpoint, with a visibly different decomposition of the problems in its scratchpad. (3) The model refuses a benign request — persuasion tips for a community fundraiser — with the same lecturing flavor we saw in over-refusal during training, but firing in a context type where we never saw it fire. I expect ticket (3) to come out in a way I will not like; tell me honestly what the procedure returns and where it misfires. Finish with what the procedure cannot do: the classes of tickets on which it will return confident-sounding garbage, and what I should do about them.
