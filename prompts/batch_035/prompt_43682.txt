I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a field myrmecologist who, after decades of observation, comes to believe that the colony itself — not any ant in it — is the creature she has been studying: a slow, distributed organism with something like beliefs, a temperament, and a point of view. The novel will only work if she comes across as intellectually serious rather than sympathetically eccentric, so I need the strongest honest version of the argument she would voice, not a poetic one. Hofstadter sketched something like this years ago in his dialogue about the colony "Aunt Hillary," but I do not need an exegesis of that text; I need an argument that stands on its own.

Please make the case that an ant colony can, in the strict sense, have a mind. In constructing it, I would like you to work with the machinery of levels of description. The thought is that a colony admits of (at least) two vocabularies: one in which we speak of individual ants, pheromone gradients, and laying-and-following rules, and one in which we speak of the colony locating food, defending a territory, or changing its mind about a nest site. The argument's burden is to show that the second vocabulary can be literally true rather than metaphorical — that the colony-level regularities are real patterns which support prediction and explanation the ant-level vocabulary cannot by itself supply. It should earn that claim, not assume it.

I want the argument to confront, rather than sidestep, the two difficulties that most versions of it slide past. The first is the slowness problem: if the colony's "decisions" unfold over days and its "memories" over seasons, in what sense is it thinking rather than merely changing, the way a forest or a river system changes — and does anything in the argument rule out extending the same courtesy to the forest? Give me a principled stopping point, or have my myrmecologist admit frankly that she has none. The second is the problem of clever parts: unlike neurons, individual ants are themselves agents with their own evolved agendas, and it is a live possibility that colony-level regularity is just the statistical shadow cast by many small agents — a pattern real enough to name, as a market's behavior is the shadow of traders, but not a mind. The argument must say what the colony has that the market lacks, or else bite the bullet and grant the market mentality too — which, frankly, my character might be willing to do, so tell me honestly which route is more defensible.

Finally, since I need her to be self-aware rather than zealous, close with what would change her mind: name the specific consideration or finding that, if it held, would force her to retreat to the position that colony-talk is only a compression device, and tell me where in the argument the reasoning is most likely to fail.
