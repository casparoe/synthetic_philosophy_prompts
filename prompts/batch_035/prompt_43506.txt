I teach political philosophy, and over the past term a single sentence has been put to me three times, twice as a conversation-stopper: that the adoption of agriculture was "the worst mistake in the history of the human race." An economist colleague offered it at a faculty dinner to dismiss my passing remark that we tend to romanticize the transition from foraging to farming in exactly the wrong direction — his point being that the transition was, if anything, a step down, so there is nothing to be romantic about in either direction. A student in my seminar on theories of progress then produced the same line, attributed to a popular science writer whose name I now forget, to argue something stronger: that humanity would have been better off had farming never been adopted at all. Most recently a member of my book club used it to explain why we are stuck — trapped by our own early choices into hierarchy, famine, and drudgery, with no way back. Same sentence, three quite different jobs, and I suspect that at most one of the jobs can be done by anything true in the sentence.

Before I respond to any of them, I want a menu of the distinct precise theses this one-liner could be expressing. Some candidates I can vaguely see: a comparative-welfare claim about the typical individual (worse diet, more disease, harder work under farming than under foraging); a claim about aggregate welfare, which the first does not settle, since farming supported vastly more people at a lower standard of living; a claim about the origins of inequality and hierarchy; a counterfactual claim about what would have happened had farming not been adopted, which "mistake" arguably presupposes; and a lock-in claim, on which the transition was less a decision than a ratchet — population growth under farming making a return to foraging impossible, so that "mistake" is a strange category for a process nobody chose. There is also, I take it, a much weaker reading on which the sentence is just an injunction against assuming that history's large transitions were improvements, which may be true but is close to a platitude. For each reading, I would like to know whether it is trivial, defensible but contested, or likely false; how it stands relative to the evidence usually cited on both sides, from skeletal markers of health and workload to the old anthropological thesis that foragers were in a certain sense affluent precisely because they wanted little; and, crucially, which of the readings, if any, would actually support the use my colleague, my student, or my book club member was making of it. My colleague's conclusion, for instance, seems to need much less than my student's, and I cannot yet say whether my student's conclusion is supported by anything at all.

One more request. If you think the sentence is so loose that none of its precisifications does the work any of these people wanted done, please say that plainly rather than rescuing the line by charitable construction — I would rather reply to each of them with "you are right for weaker reasons than you think" or "your conclusion does not follow from anything defensible in what you said" than with a polished defense of the slogan. And if my framing is off — for instance, if the readings I have listed are not genuinely independent, or if I have missed a reading that philosophers or anthropologists take more seriously than any of mine — I would want to hear that too. Please keep the empirical and the conceptual layers visibly separate, since my dinner-table opponents tend to run them together, and flag any point where you are summarizing a live scholarly dispute rather than reporting a settled result.
