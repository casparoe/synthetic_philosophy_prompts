I've been keeping a list of consent cases where my gut has a firm verdict, and I want to know if there's a principle underneath them, because right now it feels like I'm just reacting to vibes. The list, with my verdicts:

1. A surgeon tells a patient the serious-complication rate for a procedure is "around one in a hundred." Patient consents. Actually the surgeon's own audited rate is one in twenty-five — he's not lying, he sincerely believes the one-in-a-hundred figure, it's just out of date and he never checks. Gut says: consent invalid. If she'd known the real number she'd have refused.

2. Same surgery, but this time the surgeon knows his real rate and volunteers nothing either way. The patient asks no questions, signs, consents. Gut says: valid. Doctors don't have to volunteer everything, asking is on the patient. (I'm least sure about this one.)

3. A man on a dating app says he's 44 when he's 49. They meet, sleep together, both happy about it after. Gut: valid, obviously. Five years is nothing, nobody's marrying the number.

4. Same man, same five-year shave, but he's married and says he isn't, and the other person has said up front they would never knowingly sleep with a married person. Gut: what happened is wrong, but I wobble on whether the consent was *invalid* — it feels like they consented to him, and the lie just made him available under false pretenses? Or maybe they consented to a person who doesn't exist? I can't get this one straigt.

5. A broke student signs up as a paid research subject. The pay is $900 for two days of testing, about six weeks of her rent. Nobody threatened her with anything, she genuinely wants the money, and she says afterwards she'd do it again. Gut: valid. A big inducement isn't a gun.

6. Same study, but the recruiter tells her that if she quits after day one she gets nothing — so she's already done a day unpaid and now feels locked in. Gut: her consent to day two is compromised? Or is it fine, since she can walk away, just poorer? I go back and forth on this one a lot.

7. A 16-year-old refuses a blood transfusion, calmly, repeatedly, explains her reasons in detail, and her parents back her. Gut: override the refusal. But a 16-year-old usually knows her own mind as well as a 25-year-old does, so this verdict can't be riding on competence — and I can't say what it IS riding on instead.

So: is there one account of what makes consent valid or invalid here — something like "the consent has to be from the person, informed about what a reasonable person in her position would want to know, and given without being trapped" — that covers all seven? If there is, tell me which of my verdicts it has to overturn, because I can feel that #2 and #4 pull against each other and maybe #7 doesn't belong with the rest at all. And then tell me what the principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: a patient who consents to a trial after her doctor says "most patients in your situation choose this," which is true, but the doctor knows this particular patient is unusually swayed by what most people do.

I'd rather lose a verdict or two than keep them all with a principle so full of epicycles it can't ever be wrong. But tell me which verdicts you're dropping and why that one and not another — don't just quietly keep the ones that fit.
