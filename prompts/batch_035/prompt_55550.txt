I've been reading LessWrong and adjacent blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, so the vocabulary I'm actually fluent in is that of Bayesian epistemology: priors, likelihood ratios, log-odds updates, calibration, Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity, minimum description length, and the various flavors of "rationality is systematized winning." I want you to work within that vocabulary, and I want you to be strict about it.

Take Wigner's puzzle about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences, together with the standard gloss of his argument: that mathematics developed for internal, aesthetic, or seemingly arbitrary reasons keeps turning out to be indispensable for physics, and that this is treated as a striking fact demanding explanation. Restate that puzzle in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Sketch how it looks if you say: the universe is compressible, simple hypotheses are preferred by the prior, physics is the ongoing search for short programs that reproduce our observations, and the fact that internally-motivated mathematics later fits the world is just what you'd expect when mathematicians are sampling from the same space of simple structures the universe itself runs on. Be careful to keep the translation honest: don't quietly assume the Bayesian framework answers the question and present that as translation. In particular, notice where you have to fudge — e.g., whether Solomonoff induction has anything to say about why mathematicians' aesthetic preferences track simplicity in the compressor's sense, or whether "simple" for the prior and "elegant" for the mathematician are the same predicate at all.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of Wigner's puzzle translate cleanly into this vocabulary? Which survive only with distortion? And — the part I actually care about — is anything left over that the Bayesian frame cannot express at all? Consider specifically whether the felt "unreasonableness" is a substantive observation about the world or an artifact of intuitions formed in environments where compressibility is rare; whether the residue points to something the Bayesian framework is missing, or to something Wigner's framing overstated. Don't assume the answer in either direction; the residue is the deliverable.
