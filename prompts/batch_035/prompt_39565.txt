My oldest philosophy-of-language correspondent, Wren, and I have traded thought experiments for years, and I've now written up enough of her verdicts — reading each summary back to her for confirmation — that I want to try predicting her ruling on a case she hasn't seen. Here is her track record:

1. On classic Twin Earth — the planet just like ours except the clear, drinkable liquid in its rivers, lakes, and taps is XYZ rather than H₂O — the word "water" as used by its inhabitants refers to XYZ, not to water; and this was already so in 1750, before anyone on either planet knew the first thing about chemical composition.

2. In the arthritis case, Bert, whose community uses "arthritis" only for joint inflammations, sincerely reports "the arthritis in my thigh is acting up." She holds that Bert means arthritis by "arthritis" despite his wrong belief, while his internal duplicate, embedded in a community whose word standardly covers thigh and joint ailments alike, means that wider class of ailments by it.

3. She cannot tell an elm from a beech and never will, yet her word "elm" refers to elms and not to beeches, because the botanists she silently defers to can tell the difference, and what their competence fixes is elms.

4. "Phlogiston," as used by eighteenth-century chemists who causally interacted daily with combustion, smoke, and calcination, referred to nothing whatsoever — a meaningful term with an empty extension.

5. "Madagascar" today names the island, even though the name originally belonged to a stretch of the East African mainland and was transferred to the island through a medieval traveler's mix-up; once the new use dominated, the old referent was simply lost.

6. In the Gödel-style case — suppose every proof published under the name "Gödel" was actually the work of an unacknowledged man named Schmidt — she holds that "Gödel" still refers to the man the name circulated about, not to Schmidt.

Now the case I want to put to her next, and I'd like to walk in with a prediction. In 1780, an English-speaking expedition settled an isolated valley and had no further contact with the outside world. Unbeknownst to everyone, the liquid filling the valley's lakes is not H₂O but a microphysically different substance, V, indistinguishable from water in every ordinary respect; no H₂O exists anywhere in the valley. From the first day, the settlers called the lake liquid "water," and their descendants still do. The books they brought speak of water too. Last year, a valley chemist discovered that the lakes contain V rather than "the real water of the old books," by which she means the substance H₂O — a substance she has never encountered and knows only through those books. I intend to ask Wren: (a) before the discovery, did the valley's word "water" refer to V, to H₂O, to both, or to neither? (b) after the discovery, when the valley newspaper prints "our lakes contain no water, only V," what does that sentence say? and (c) does the chemist's talk of "the real water of the old books" succeed in referring to H₂O at all, given that no valley inhabitant has ever causally encountered it? I care most about (a).

So: predict her verdict, at least on (a) but ideally on all three. First set out the candidate rules that fit her record — I suspect more than one does — and say specifically where those rules agree and where they come apart on the valley case, since that divergence is the point of it. Then give me your single best guess for her, with your reasons for preferring it over the rivals, and name the one further case I could put to Wren that would most cleanly separate the rules that currently run together.

One demand: don't hedge into a tie. If you think her verdicts as I've listed them can't all be held by any single rule, or that I've misdescribed one of the classic cases, or that the valley case quietly conflates two different questions, tell me that plainly rather than smoothing it over — I would rather learn my record is incoherent than get a forced prediction. But if a prediction can responsibly be made, commit to it and tell me how confident you are.
