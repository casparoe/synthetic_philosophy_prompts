My first novel came out last year, and the reviews keep crediting me with an allegory I never planned and honestly could not see until the critics pointed to it. That sent me into the theory of interpretation, where I found that the argument over whether a text means what its author meant has been running in essentially the same form since Wimsatt and Beardsley attacked the intentional fallacy, and that in all that time hardly anyone has changed sides. The intentionalists, following E. D. Hirsch, hold that interpretation aims at the author's meaning and can succeed or fail at getting it; the anti-intentionalists, running from the New Critics through Barthes, hold that once the text is public the author's private purposes are neither reliably available nor authoritative, and that readings the author never dreamed of can be the best ones. My working hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than agreed with, is that this is not one disagreement but two crossed ones: a semantic question about what fixes a text's meaning, and a normative question about what critics may legitimately do with a text, with each side winning its home question and mistaking that for winning both. But there is a wrinkle I cannot fit into that story: with wills, contracts, and statutes, nearly everyone—including committed anti-intentionalists—reads like an intentionalist, while nobody thinks a misreading of a poem is actionable in the same way. If the dispute were purely semantic, genre should not matter, yet it plainly does. So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the stall verbal, substantive, or a matter of two questions being argued under one heading? What is each side actually committed to, once their slogans are unpacked? Does the genre wrinkle fit my hypothesis, break it, or show that both sides are asking a third question—about the point a particular text serves—that neither has noticed? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here: a distinction, a test case, a change in the question, or nothing at all? As a working author, I would finally like to know what your diagnosis implies about whether I am entitled to tell my reviewers they are wrong.
