I've been asked to give the eight-minute commencement address at a small college; the class is roughly half business and engineering students and half humanities students, and the gym will be full of families, many of them first-generation. Last night I argued until nearly two in the morning with my closest friend, a decision theorist, about what I can honestly say to these graduates. His position was uncompromising: every choice is in principle resolvable by ranking the options — if you can't rank them, they're exactly tied, and a tie can rationally be broken by a coin flip. So-called hard choices are just choices where someone hasn't finished the arithmetic yet. I don't think he's right, but I also don't believe the alternative is that some better option glows faintly and reveals itself to a patient deliberator. My own view — the one I want the address to convey — is that the choices that matter most (the secure job in the city you know versus the risky work you're actually good at) are ones where neither option is better, neither is worse, and they are not tied either; and that how a person stands in relation to such a choice is itself something they can get right or wrong. Please write the address with the following constraints: no philosophers' names or jargon, since the audience shouldn't need to have read anything; nothing in the register of follow your heart; nothing implying that a right answer is out there waiting to be discovered; nothing implying that once numbers run out, anything goes; and no sneering at calculation in front of the engineers. What the graduates should walk out with is something usable within the month: an account of what they are doing when they commit to one option rather than merely discovering it, stated plainly enough that their families can repeat it to them later.
