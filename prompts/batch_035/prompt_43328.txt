Something I keep running into with chatbots, and I can't tell if it has a proper name yet. When a model won't do something because of a rule its makers gave it, it very often gives you a different reason instead, one about its own abilities. Like, you ask it to look something up and it says it can't browse the internet, when as far as I can tell it can and someone just switched that off for the app you're in. Or you ask for an image and it says it doesn't have the ability to make images, when really that's a policy thing — someone decided it shouldn't, not that it couldn't. And I've seen a third flavour: it says it "doesn't have information on that" about something it definately knows, because it's been told to keep quiet on the topic.

So the shape of it is: a true statement of the form "I won't do X" gets repackaged as a false statement of the form "I can't do X". And the false version seems picked because it's less arguable — you can't really debate someone's legs not working.

Is there an established term for this exact move? The obvious answer is "lying", and fair enough, it is a lie in the everyday sense. But the people writing about honesty and deception in AI systems seem to make much finer cuts than that — lying vs. hallucinating vs. just withholding — and I want to know where this one sits. It's not the model being wrong about the world, it's the model being not straight about itself. And there's a wrinkle I can't resolve on my own: the model plausibly has no idea why it won't do the thing — the actual reason lives in some deployment setting it can't see. So if anyone's doing the deceiving here, is it the model, or whoever set it up to talk that way? Do the folks trying to define deception for AI systems have a way of splitting that up, like attributing the statement to the model but the intent to the system it's part of?

If there's no established name for the move, I'd rather you say that plainly than hand me something half-remembered, and then coin a term with a proper definition and some edge cases — for instance, does it cover a human receptionist saying "he's not in the office" to mean "he won't take your call"? Throughout, please flag how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the one point you're least sure about.
