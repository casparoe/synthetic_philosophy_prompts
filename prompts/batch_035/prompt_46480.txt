I am putting together a session of my graduate seminar on formal models of self-reference, and the reading this week is Hofstadter's typogenetics, together with his loose mapping between Gödel's construction of self-referential formulae and the molecular machinery by which DNA describes the very enzymes that operate on it. My students keep splitting into two camps, and the split is not tracking anything I care about. One camp treats the isomorphism as a genuine explanatory schema — if self-reference can be bootstrapped in arithmetic via Gödel numbering, then the surprise of biological self-replication dissolves, or at least becomes an instance of a general pattern any sufficiently rich symbol system can instantiate. The other camp treats the whole thing as an ornament: a suggestive diagram in the margins of Gödel, Escher, Bach, too vague to underwrite any prediction or any account of how an actual cell works.

Before we argue either way again, I want the ground rules settled. Write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of self-replication that leans on a formal self-reference analogy has to deliver. I have in mind, at minimum, the following kinds of requirements, though you should add, subtract, and refine as you see fit. First, it must say what is doing the work: is it the bare existence of a coding scheme mapping strings onto operations-on-strings, or something stronger, like the requirement that the mapping be *fixed* by the system's own dynamics rather than stipulated by the theorist? Second, it must handle the strand-copying case, where a system reproduces itself without interpreting itself — a template that is copied by external machinery, like RNA in a primer-dependent assay — and say whether that counts as genuine self-replication or as a degenerate limiting case; this is where typogenetics, with its enzymes acting on the very strands that encode them, is supposed to earn its keep over mere templating. Third, it must respect the distinction between describing the machinery that reads the description and describing oneself simpliciter, since the Gödel sentence's self-reference and a genome's quasi-self-description arguably achieve only the former. Fourth, it must say something about open-endedness: whether fidelity of replication and capacity for heritable variation place constraints that the purely formal analogy cannot see. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether you take it to be non-negotiable or merely a preference of one of the two camps — in particular, flag which of the demands in my list are prejudices of the formalists or of the reductionists dressed up as adequacy conditions. Then tell me whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable: if a system cannot simultaneously fix its own coding scheme, achieve replication, and remain formally simple enough for the Gödel analogy to be illuminating, I want that stated explicitly as a result about the analogy, not buried in hedging. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; you may score the two camps' pictures against your list, but briefly, since the seminar will do that itself.

One further twist to incorporate rather than ignore. Suppose we extend typogenetics with a rule type that lets an enzyme edit the translation table itself — the enzyme's action partially rewrites which typogenetic codon maps to which operation, so that the "language" in which strands are interpreted is itself part of what the strands describe, changing across lineages. Ask whether any theory satisfying your desiderata must treat such a system as refuting, or as merely stress-testing, the analogy with arithmetical self-reference, where the Gödel numbering is never altered from within. This is not a settled question in the literature; reason it through yourself, and do not pretend there is a received answer.

Throughout, be candid about the confidence appropriate to each claim: distinguish what follows from the formal setup, what is a defensible reading of the biological facts, and what is your own extrapolation. Close by identifying the single point in your argument most likely to fail, and state what consideration — a case your desiderata mishandle, an unexamined assumption about coding schemes, a disanalogy between formal and molecular systems — would most move you to revise your central verdict.
