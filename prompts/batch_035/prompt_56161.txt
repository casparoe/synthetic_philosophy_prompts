I teach a big intro course, and every spring we do the thought-experiment unit: Thomson's violinist, the experience machine, the Chinese Room, Twin Earth, footbridge vs. switch. Over the years I've developed private verdicts about which of these little machines earn their keep and which ones cheat, and my TAs keep telling me my sorting is incoherent, so I want to lay it out and see if there's actually a principle underneath it, or whether I'm just liking the ones whose conclusions I already hold. Here's the ledger. Thomson's violinist earns its keep -- the case isolates the question of what you owe a person who depends on you, and I find that students who change their minds on it really do change their minds about the underlying thing. The people-seeds variant cheats: the ridiculous precaution (screens, seeds drifting in the wind) does so much emotional work, and the pregnancy-relevant disanalogy is buried under whimsy, that I don't trust anyone's intuitions about it, including mine. The switch trolley feels legitimate, footbridge feels like a cheat -- but my TA swears that "cheat" is just my name for an intuition I refuse to take seriously, namely that up-close physical pushing matters morally, and maybe that intuition is tracking something real rather than being an artifact of the setup. The Chinese Room cheats, I think, by handing you a little man inside the room to pity, when nothing in the argument says there's a little man in there. Twin Earth earns its keep. Nozick's experience machine mostly earns its keep, though I've noticed the pump only lands if you describe the machine's world in some detail, which makes me suspicious of it too -- why should vividness of the description do any evidential work if the pump is supposed to reveal a fact about what we value?

Two more complications. My colleague runs what she calls a "memory swap" pump: you can trade one true but boring memory for a false but blissful one, everything downstream stays consistent, no one is harmed, and everyone in the seminar intuits the swap is a harm. She thinks it's a great pump; I think it's a cheat, though I can't say why any better than "the false memory is described in warm detail and the true one is described as boring," which is exactly the kind of move I forgave in the violinist case (the violinist being a famous violinist is doing work, and I never complained). And when I try out my current best guess at a principle -- something like "a pump is legitimate when the intuitive pull survives the same case told flatly, in the most boring possible prose" -- it convicts the violinist along with the memory swap, and it seems to let footbridge off the hook only if I describe the pushing in truly boring terms, which feels like it's rigging the test in advance.

So: is there a principle that captures my ledger, or close, and tells me which of my verdicts have to go? I want it stated precisely enough to make a prediction about two cases I haven't sorted: Mary's room, and the utility monster (the one who converts resources into happiness so efficiently that utilitarianism says give him everything). And please be upfront about how confident you are in each major claim and flag the single point you're least sure of -- I'm going to build a lecture on this, so I'd rather know where the ice is thin. After the prose answer, give me a compact JSON summary with these fields: "chosen_principle" (one sentence), "supporting_clauses" (array, each clause paired with the verdict that motivates it), "verdicts_overturned" (array of my verdicts your principle rejects, with a one-line reason each), "predictions" (object mapping "marys_room" and "utility_monster" to "earns_its_keep" or "cheats" plus a one-line rationale), "confidence" (one sentence), and "least_sure_point" (one sentence).
