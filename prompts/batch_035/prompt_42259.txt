I'm a novelist, some decades into the trade, and I've spent the last two years writing a serialized book — installments published as they're finished, which means no going back. Along the way, despite my outlines, I have contradicted myself. In an early chapter a character has a scar from a ferry accident; forty installments later, a flashback gives her the scar from a different accident entirely, and I only noticed afterward. What startled me was the readers. No one infers from this that the book is broken; no one writes in asking whether the character also has three scars, or no scars, or whether anything at all is now true of her. Instead the correspondence divides cleanly: readers who read the ferry chapters treat the ferry accident as what happened, readers of the flashback arc treat the second accident as what happened, and when the two camps meet they say, cheerfully, that both are true "in their parts of the book." The same thing is everywhere once you look: Doyle moved Watson's war wound from shoulder to leg across the stories and generations of readers have simply recorded both without complaint; Greek myth hands you three mutually exclusive parentages for the same goddess and no one concludes that she therefore had nine; comic readers speak of contradictions in a run as both "counting" without either one collapsing the fiction. In each case the contradiction is real, visible to everyone, and somehow inert — it infects a small neighborhood of claims and leaves the rest of the story-world exactly as true as it was before.

I have tried out names on my editor and none of them fit. It is not an unreliable narrator, because there is no hidden true version beneath — both versions stand, and the audience knows it. It is not an alternate timeline or a retcon, because those are framed as replacements, one account officially expelling the other, whereas here the two accounts sit side by side, each fully in force wherever it is being read. It is not simple vagueness, because the two claims are each perfectly sharp; it is only their combination that is impossible. And it is not that readers don't notice the contradiction — they will point it out to you with delight. What they decline to do is treat it the way a contradiction behaves elsewhere, as a hole through which everything pours. So I want to know whether this configuration — an explicit contradiction that a whole audience holds true-in-both-versions without triviality, with truth seemingly assessed locally, passage by passage or scene by scene rather than over the fiction as one whole — has an established name and literature behind it, and whether it has been studied by logicians or philosophers of fiction or whoever the right people would be. And if the answer is that the nearest existing concepts only partially fit, I don't want a menu of near misses; I want you to take a position on which one fits best and where precisely it fails, and then coin a term for the residue, with a definition, and with at least one boundary case — say, whether a contradiction inside a single scene behaves the same as one strung across two installments, which my editor and I genuinely disagree about. I would rather have a definite carving of this thing, with reasons I can argue with, than a cautious survey.
