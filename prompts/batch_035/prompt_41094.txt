My uncle is a retired psychiatrist with fierce views about what counts as a real illness versus what he calls "problems in living." He once gave me a Szasz book, though I'm not sure hes a straight Szaszian about it. His track record of verdicts over the years: epilepsy — real illness; Alzheimer's — real illness; Tourette's — real illness, he's had patients who genuinly could not suppress their tics no matter what they tried; grief after losing a child, even when it drags on for years — not an illness, just suffering; alcoholism — not an illness, a habit, since he's watched too many people quit cold turkey to call it a disease; PTSD in a veteran — not an illness, a reaction to what happened to him; insomnia brought on by work stress — not an illness. Next week he's staying with us and my cousin's recent OCD diagnosis is bound to come up — intrusive violent thoughts, four hours a day of checking and washing, no trauma and no trigger anyone can identify, and she hates every second of it. What's he going to say? I can see at least two different rules that fit his past verdicts — like "if it originates in the brain rather than in your life, it's illness" versus "if you genuinely can't stop it, it's illness, and if you could with effort, it isn't" — but I can't tell which way either of those lands on the OCD, since her compulsions are technically resistible but only at horrible cost to her. What's your best guess, what other rules fit his track record just as well, and is there some case — or some question I could casually ask him over dinner — where the rules actually come apart, so we could figure out which one he's really running?
