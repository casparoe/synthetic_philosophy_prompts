At a recent reading group on whole brain emulation, a senior researcher dismissed the skeptics with the line that the brain is just a computer made of meat: scan the wiring, port the program, and the mind runs wherever you choose to run it. A grad student pushed back with a list of disanalogies — no separation of hardware and software, no central clock, continuous chemical dynamics rather than discrete state transitions, no clean boundary between input, processing, and output, and a machine whose learning rewires its own substrate while it runs. The senior researcher's reply was the interesting part: he conceded every one of those points and said the analogy never needed any of them, since a computer is just anything that implements a computation, and on that reading even an analog, wet, self-modifying system is a computer — indeed a better subject for emulation than a von Neumann machine, because implementation is substrate-independent. Someone muttered that by that definition the wall behind him was implementing every computation, and the meeting broke up unresolved.

Audit the analogy under both readings. First, take computer in the ordinary, desk-machine sense: lay out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the processor, the memory, the program, the data, the input/output channels — then separate the mappings that are doing real work for the emulation conclusion from the ones that are incidental, and say specifically where the grad student's disanalogies bite and where they are red herrings. Second, take the abstract implements-a-computation reading: does it rescue the argument by weakening the claim into something close to a platitude? If a wide enough range of physical systems implements some computation or other, state precisely what content remains in the claim that the brain is a computer, and identify the extra premises the emulation conclusion would need — for instance, that the computation the brain implements can be individuated at a level a connectome-plus-concentrations scan could capture, or that a system that simulates an implementation is thereby one of them rather than merely a model of it. For each such premise, say whether it is plausible, question-begging, or probably false.

Finally, tell me which single disanalogy or hidden premise is actually load-bearing on each side, and give a verdict on the two moves the senior researcher made: when you concede all the concrete disanalogies and retreat to implementation-talk, have you preserved an argument for the feasibility of emulation, or merely restated a prior commitment to substrate independence in vocabulary that sounds like one?
