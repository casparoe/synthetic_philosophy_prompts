For the past three years my group — we do interpretability and evaluation work — has been stuck on a disagreement that has stopped moving, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. One half of the group holds that alignment research should aim at worst-case-style guarantees: treat the system under evaluation as an adversary that will exploit any gap between what we test and what we deploy, and hold that safety arguments which hold only on average are, in their phrase, marketing rather than engineering. The other half holds that all safety engineering is expected-value reasoning under uncertainty — fault trees, sampled red-teaming, deployment-distribution risk budgets — and that worst-case demands are unfalsifiable, have yet to yield a single deployable technique, and would have forbidden every useful technology ever fielded. Everyone knows everyone else's arguments; position papers get written; nobody budges. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement isn't about values or probabilities at all but about whether the uncertainty over AI behavior is ordinary risk, where you can sample from a distribution, or something closer to Knightian uncertainty, where drawing samples changes the distribution you are sampling from — so the two sides never actually share a probability space to disagree within. But I may be wrong: it could be verbal, a conflation of two questions (what a research program should target versus what justifies deploying a given system), or a bedrock difference in how novel each side thinks the technology is. Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually says, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis. One consideration I'd like you to take up explicitly: in cryptography, worst-case and average-case hardness can sometimes be connected by formal reductions, and it has struck me that nobody in our debates ever says whether any such reduction is even in principle available for alignment — whether that thought helps your diagnosis or is a red herring is itself something I'd like you to judge.
