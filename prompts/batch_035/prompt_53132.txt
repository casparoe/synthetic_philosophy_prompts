I am a professor of philosophy who works on formal epistemology and the philosophy of probability, and for the past two years I have sat on the methodology advisory panel of a regional transportation authority. The authority's analysts do what they call reference-class forecasting for capital projects: to estimate the cost and schedule of a proposed light-rail extension, they assemble a set of comparable completed projects, look at the distribution of cost overruns and delays in that set, and use it to adjust the plan's numbers. Every fight on the panel is about the set. For a mid-sized city's eleven-mile rail extension, is the right class all urban rail projects in developed economies, rail projects of similar length, the authority's own completed capital projects regardless of type, or every megaproject in the jurisdiction over the last thirty years? Analysts holding identical data reach estimates that differ by factors, and each side suspects the other of choosing the class that produces the answer they already favored. The panel has asked me to write something for their next methodology retreat, and I would rather not hand them a seminar paper on Reichenbach.

What I want from you is a decision procedure a working analyst can actually run in an afternoon, without reading any philosophy: an ordered list of questions to ask about a proposed project, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure should stop and say explicitly that the choice between two defensible classes is a judgment call that must be escalated or documented rather than hidden inside a number. The procedure must handle the considerations the philosophical literature actually turns on — the tradeoff between narrow classes that match on causally relevant features but contain few members, and wide classes with many members that lump together superficially similar but structurally different projects; the fact that the target project is described differently under different descriptions and no description is the "real" one; and the genuinely novel case, where nothing in the jurisdiction's history or the sector's looks like a close match. It should say something about whether to pick one class at all or instead blend estimates across nested classes, and about when the analyst should stop treating the reference class as the problem and instead ask whether the plan's own cost estimate is biased, which is arguably the diagnosis the whole method presupposes. Then run the procedure on two cases: the light-rail extension above, where comparables are plentiful but the definition fights are bitter, and a proposed replacement of an aging river crossing with an immersed tunnel, where the authority has never built one and any reference class will be thin, heterogeneous, or both. At least one of the worked cases should come out with an answer the panel will find unwelcome, and the procedure should flag in advance which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Please take the role of a philosopher of probability who has spent enough time around practitioners to know that a procedure nobody runs is worthless, and think carefully before writing — in particular, brainstorm the candidate ways one might tame the reference class problem (exogeneity of the class, weighting by similarity, the Berkeley-style move of conditioning on all the specific evidence you have, the pessimist's move of always widening the class until the estimate gets worse) and be explicit about which of these the procedure adopts, which it rejects, and why. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — state the core rule of the procedure in one or two lines — and then give the reasoning and the procedure itself, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
