I've been going back and forth on the demandingness objection and want your considered credence on a precisely stated version of it. Define the objection as follows: an ethical theory is defeated if it implies, of actual-world agents like us, that each is morally required to devote the large majority of her discretionary resources to preventing distant, preventable suffering. My credence that this objection succeeds against act consequentialism is around 0.65, but I suspect my number is doing something shifty, so I want yours decomposed rather than asserted. Assign rough probabilities to the sub-questions it rests on: that our intuitions of excessive demand are evidence about what duties there actually are rather than about the self-serving limits of the people having the intuitions; that partiality to one's own projects and relationships has moral weight not reducible to the instrumental value of the projects themselves; that a moral requirement can be genuine even when no one is appropriately blameworthy for violating it; plus any other terms you think belong, with one line of justification for each, and then the combination. To make the middle term concrete, consider a nurse who each year gives just enough to prevent one distant death while keeping the income she needs to stay in nursing — at what marginal sacrifice, if any, does further giving stop being required, and does the answer depend on whether the sacrifice destroys her projects or merely makes them harder? Finally, tell me which term of your decomposition you are least confident in, and what argument or reflective shift would move it by at least 0.2 — I want to know where my disagreement with myself most likely lives, not just a number.
