I sit on the ethics subcommittee of my university's catering board, and for the past term I have been pressing for a rule requiring that all crabs and lobsters served on campus be stunned or otherwise killed humanely before cooking, rather than boiled alive or dismembered live in the kitchen. My case rested on an argument I now find I cannot repair, because every repair seems to prove far too much.

The argument, as I presented it:

(1) A creature is owed moral consideration for its own sake only if there is credible evidence that it can experience pain — genuine suffering, not mere detection of and reflex withdrawal from noxious stimuli.
(2) That evidence cannot be neuroanatomical, at least not as the demand is usually framed: decapod crustaceans lack anything answering to a mammalian cortex, so requiring a particular brain architecture would decide the question by fiat rather than by evidence. The evidence must therefore be behavioral and motivational: surrendering something of value to avoid a noxious stimulus, making trade-offs between avoiding harm and obtaining a reward, learned avoidance that persists, and protective tending of an injured body part.
(3) Decapod crustaceans exhibit all of these. In the published experiments, hermit crabs abandon shells that have delivered a shock, and evacuate more readily the stronger the shock; shore crabs forgo a shelter that has shocked them and discount that shelter's value thereafter; crabs with injured claws guard and rub the wound. It was on evidence of roughly this kind, alongside a parallel case for cephalopods, that the UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 came to cover lobsters and crabs alongside vertebrates.
(4) So decapod crustaceans can plausibly suffer, and are owed moral consideration for their own sake.
(5) Therefore the campus should require stunning before cooking, and should stop sourcing from suppliers who boil or dismember the animals live.

At the last meeting, the subcommittee's resident skeptic granted me every premise and then observed that the argument proves far too much. The behaviors I appealed to in (3), he said, are the same ones now being reported for insects — flies and bees trading off feeding against heat, learning to avoid noxious conditions, tending injuries. If the argument licenses a stunning requirement for lobsters, it licenses at least as much about flypaper, about the swatters in campus kitchens, and about every pest-control contract the university holds. And since the university will not abandon pest control — and I would not ask it to — the argument must be unsound somewhere.

I can see three exits, and I dislike all of them. I could reject (1), holding that moral consideration should not wait on evidence at all: perhaps where sentience is uncertain we should give the benefit of the doubt. But that version proves even more too much, since the precaution then extends wherever anyone can raise a doubt, and I cannot see what bounds it. I could reject (2) and require something beyond behavior — some integrative neural capacity — but every such criterion I can name either excludes animals we already protect or looks reverse-engineered to exclude exactly the inconvenient cases. Or I could say the skeptic's reductio smuggles something in: that behavioral evidence comes in degrees, so the case for decapods may be stronger than the case for insects even where both satisfy the same checklist. But I cannot yet say in what that difference of strength consists, and until I can, this sounds like an insistence rather than an answer.

Tell me which step to give up and what rejecting it costs — including, if the flaw lies in the skeptic's move rather than in mine, precisely where his inference from lobsters to flypaper goes wrong, whether it turns on evidence, on the difference between owed consideration and owed identical treatment, or on something else. For what it is worth: I would sooner accept startling conclusions about insects than give up (5), and I would like to know what that preference commits me to, not merely that I hold it.
