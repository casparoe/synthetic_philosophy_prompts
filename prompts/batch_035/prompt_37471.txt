So I spent thirty-odd years as an immunologist and now that I'm retired I've fallen into philosophy of science, and I've hit a wall with the scientific realism thing. As far as I can tell the whole debate has been stationary since before I was born: realists say the success of science would be a miracle unless successful theories were at least approximately true, anti-realists point at phlogiston and the luminiferous ether -- theories that were spectacularly successful for ages and are now flat wrong -- and then everybody digs in. Every argument on both sides is on the table, has been for decades, and nobody has moved an inch. Worrall's structural realism move (we got the equations right even when we got the substances wrong) just seemed to spawn another layer of the same fight.

My working hypothesis, and tell me if it's daft: this isn't really a disagreement about science at all. It's a disagreement about what counts as evidence, and each side is reasoning the way its training tells it to. The realists reason like me and my old colleagues -- extraordinary predictive success is normally evidence of getting something right, why would science be the exception? The anti-realists reason more like philosophers -- one dramatic counterexample to an inductive pattern is meant to sink it, and "approximately true" is vague enough that neither side can pin the other down. So I can't tell whether it's substantive, verbal (nobody has told me what "approximately true" is supposed to mean for a theory that's wrong about the fundamental entities but right about the maths), or two groups answering different questions under one heading.

Diagnose it for me using what the sides actually say -- don't just survey the positions, I've read the surveys. And be honest: given your diagnosis, is there anything that would count as progress here, or should I conclude the question is ill-posed and go back to reading about causation?
