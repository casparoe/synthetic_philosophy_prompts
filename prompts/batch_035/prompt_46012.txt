My friend from my political philosophy seminar keeps handing out these little distributive justice verdicts, and I want to test whether she's actually consistent. The seminar is literally called "equality of what," and her track record so far:

Someone born blind, needs expensive equipment and support to live a normal life -- she says society owes her the resources for that, full stop. A guy who gets paralyzed climbing a mountain he was explicitly warned was dangerous, having chosen the climb for a thrill -- she says he's owed nothing beyond what everyone gets, his gamble was his. A woman who cultivated a taste for expensive wine over many years and now feels her life is impoverished without it -- owed nothing, the taste is hers. A surfer who deliberately works part time at low pay because he'd rather surf, and is perfectly content -- also owed nothing, and notably she doesn't think he owes anyone else anything either. And a woman with chronic depression from no fault of her own -- she says treatment and support are owed, same as the woman born blind.

Next week there's a new batch of cases, and I want to walk in knowing what she'll say. Case one: a guy who, from early childhood and without ever choosing it, can only get deep satisfaction out of one activity, flying small planes, which is expensive -- otherwise he's healthy, functional, and doesn't complain. Case two: two neighbors with identical incomes and health; one is delighted with her life for no apparent reason, the other is persistently dissatisfied and gloomy purely by temperament, no diagnosable condition. Case three: a woman who lost everything to gambling, but she was raised by parents who taught her nothing about money except that the lottery was the family's retirement plan.

My take is her verdicts so far are compatible with more than one underlying principle -- something like "compensate for bad brute luck in resources, ignore everything that traces to choice" versus something like "make sure everyone has a fair shot at welfare, but hold people responsible for preferences they identify with" versus maybe a third one about what people are actually able to do and be. So: what does each of those principles predict on the three new cases, which of the cases best pulls the principles apart, and if you had to bet on her verdicts, which principle do you back and why? And what's one further case I could raise in seminar that would settle which principle she's really running? I care more about the principled breakdown than the predictions tbh.
