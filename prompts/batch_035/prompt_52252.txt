I'm a third-year PhD student in cognitive psych, and I've been attending a reading group with some evo psych grad students where we work through classic and current EP papers. Something about the genre conventions of these papers keeps bugging me and I want to figure out what the field's standard operating procedure actually commits it to, philosophically speaking.

Here's the template I keep seeing. You take some contemporary behavior -- men reporting more distress over sexual than emotional infidelity, people preferring landscapes with water in them, mate preferences supposedly shifting across the ovulatory cycle, whatever. You propose it's the output of a mechanism that solved a specific adaptive problem in the Pleistocene, usually with a plausible-but-unverifiable story about ancestral conditions attached. Then you gather evidence that's one or two steps removed from the hypothesis itself: questionnaires, cross-cultural replications, sometimes a cross-species comparison. Confirmation of a prediction is treated as support for the whole adaptive story. My question: what view of evidence and explanation does this practice presuppose? The obvious reading is that it's abductive design-detection -- the same kind of reasoning a biologist uses when she infers what the vertebrate kidney is for, and nobody calls that a fable. So does the practice strictly need the strong stuff the field is famous for (massive modularity, a roughly uniform environment of evolutionary adaptedness, selection as the default explanation of complex functional design), or can the weaker Popper-flavored material carry the weight on its own? And is it even internally consistent, given that the same journals and reviewers will browbeat you about falsifiability while accepting adaptive hypotheses that can accomodate almost any pattern of results by reaching for the byproduct or exaptation escape hatch?

Relatedly, and this is the part I actually got into an argument about at the pub: the just-so story accusation itself. When critics say an adaptive explanation is a just-so story because you can always invent a rival one after the fact, what philosophy of science is that complaint presupposing? It seems to assume a Popperian standard -- one deduced risky prediction or it isn't science -- that we don't apply to historical sciences generally; nobody says paleontology or historical linguistics isn't science because you can't rerun the Cretaceous. So is the criticism smuggling in a view of confirmation that the critics themselves would reject anywhere else? What I want is a proper reconstruction of the implicit commitments: what the EP research program strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits well with, where it's underdetermined such that several epistemologies would license the exact same practice, and what would have to change in how these papers get written and reviewed if the field dropped one of the big presuppositions -- say, stopped treating the Pleistocene as the relevant selective environment, or stopped treating selection as the default. I don't need citations or a reading list, I have plenty, I need the actual reconstruction and a sense of where the pieces can and can't be pried apart.
