I'm teaching a law-and-philosophy elective and we just finished the Hart–Devlin debate, then next week we're doing A Clockwork Orange (the book, though I'll mention the Kubrick film). The obvious take is that Burgess wrote the anti-Devlin novel: the state uses the Ludovico technique to make Alex literally incapable of evil, and the prison chaplain makes the point that goodness thats chosen counts for something while conditioned goodness doesn't (paraphrase, don't quote — I don't have the text to hand). So there's the dramatization of the idea that enforced virtue destroys moral agency, and Hart wins.

But the more I sit with it the less clean that feels. Devlin's position was about society enforcing its shared public morality to hold the fabric together — majoritarian, tradition-rooted, about acts that offend the common conscience. The Ludovico technique isn't quite that: it's more like a therapeutic state treating immorality as pathology and rewriting the offender. That's arguably closer to something nobody in the debate actually defended. Meanwhile the interior minister stages everything for the press and wants Alex softened up or hardened up depending on the polls — which raises a question I'd actually like your help with: is the novel a refutation of legal moralism at all, or a refutation of state crime-control by conditioning that we misread as being about Devlin?

Two more things. First, I want to press a distinction we never got to in the debate: moralism about acts (society may punish wrongdoing even absent harm) vs. moralism about character (society may ensure its members *are* virtuous people). Devlin is usually read as the first; the Ludovico technique literalizes the second. Does keeping those seperate change who the novel is arguing against, and is there a defensible version of Devlin the book leaves untouched? Second, the ending: the US edition originally cut Burgess's final chapter, the one where Alex is deconditioned, gets bored of violence on his own, and starts imagining a son who might turn out better. Which edition does more work for which side — does the cut American ending (Alex cured of the cure, gleefully himself) actually support the moralist more than the British one, where virtue arrives by maturation and not by choice in any robust sense?

I'd like a reading that's fair to Devlin rather than a strawman, one or two scenes the class can argue about (the demonstration in front of the doctors, the chaplain's objection, the minister's press op), and an honest verdict on whether Burgess is dramatizing something the Hart–Devlin exchange missed or just dramatizing Hart. If the answer is that the novel needs a third position the debate lacked, say so and sketch it.
