I write novels, mostly speculative stuff, and I've got a plot problem I think is actually a philosophy problem in disguise, so bear with me. The book I'm drafting is set maybe fifteen years out: a private lab has grown a wholly new organism for deep-sea salvage work — soft-bodied, eight-ish limbs, a big distributed nervous system, and rather more going on upstairs than anyone planned on. The plot turns on whether the thing ends up as equipment or as somebody. But I realised while outlining that I don't actually know what our world's practices would do with it, because I can't articulate the theory those practices are running. So that's what I'm asking you for: not book notes, but the implicit philosophy.

Here's the practice as I understand it, laid out concretely, because I want us looking at the same thing. British animal law counts all vertebrates as sentient — fish included, which took its own fight. It also, since 2022, counts octopuses, cuttlefish, crabs, lobsters and shrimp as sentient, after a government-commissioned review by scientists with at least one philosopher in the mix concluded there was strong evidence for it in cephalopods and decent evidence for decapods. That review also led to official advice against boiling crustaceans alive without stunning them first. Meanwhile a bee, a spider, an earthworm: nothing. Lab rules are similar — experimenting on an octopus or a mouse needs licences and justification, experimenting on insects barely registers as a category.

So my first instinct was: fine, the line is brain size, that's intuitively appealing and it would matter enormously for my creature. But then I checked some numbers and it falls apart. Something like a million neurons in a honeybee, something like a hundred thousand in a lobster — the lobster is inside the line and the bee is outside it, with an order of magnitude going the wrong way. And we obviously don't grade humans by cranial capacity either, or people with microcephaly would lose standing while elephants gained it over us. So the size thing can't be the actual criterion, though it might be the lazy heuristic everyone uses.

What I want from you, then, is this. Reconstruct the theory of moral status that this whole pattern of law and practice presupposes — is it something like evidence of sentience assessed creature by creature (the review apparently cared about pain behaviour, trade-offs between pain and reward, self-medication with anaesthetics, that sort of thing), and brain size is just a correlate that got the public's imagination? Say which rival views the practice quietly rules out — a pure encephalisation view, a species-membership view, a view where only total neural count matters. And be honest about where the practice is underdetermined, i.e. where several quite different moral theories would all license drawing the line exactly where it's drawn, so the law doesn't really commit us to any of them. Then tell me, given all that, what features I'd have to give my fictional creature for its status to be (a) clearly settled by the current implicit rule and (b) deliciously contested — I can pick whichever serves the plot better, but I want to know I'm doing it on purpose and not by accident.

Two more things. Please answer in British English — I know that's an odd request, but the manuscript is UK-set and I want to steal your phrasing without having to de-Americanise it. And don't give me a confident lecture: tell me flat out where your reconstruction is most likely to go wrong, and what would change your mind about it — say, what evidence about insect pain, or what a differently-designed review process, would force a different verdict on the whole edifice. If my creature's fate is going to hinge on your analysis, I want to know exactly where the analysis is load-bearing and thin. Also if I've got any of the law wrong above, say so, because I'm working off news coverage and I definately want the practice described right before we do philosophy about it.
