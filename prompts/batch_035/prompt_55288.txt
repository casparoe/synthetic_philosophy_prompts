I teach moral philosophy, and for several years now my graduate seminar on beneficence has kept circling a configuration of commitments that I find myself unable to name, which is starting to irritate me. The configuration shows up in arguments about charity allocation: someone points out that if there is even a one-in-a-hundred-billion chance that donating to pandemic preparedness or asteroid deflection averts the extinction of humanity, then, since the losses from extinction include all future generations, the arithmetic product of probability and stakes dwarfs the product for any conventional intervention, and so every dollar ought to go to the extinction work. My students who resist this are told they are being irrational; the students who accept it are told they have been mugged. What I want to identify is the underlying position: the conjunction of three commitments — that welfare aggregates additively over arbitrarily many people and arbitrarily long stretches of time; that probability enters the ranking of outcomes purely as a linear multiplier; and that no probability is so small, and no stake so large, that the multiplication ceases to be decisive. Together these entail that a lottery with a minuscule chance of an astronomically valuable outcome always outranks a modest certain benefit, once the stakes are scaled up far enough, and that this ordering never flattens out.

Colleagues have offered me labels for pieces of this, but none of them quite carves the thing. The St. Petersburg paradox is often invoked, but that case turns on infinite or undefined expectations, whereas the arguments in my seminar involve enormous but perfectly finite products. Pascal's mugging is also invoked, but that seems to me to be about our evidential position — we are being asked to trust a self-interested teller's astronomically precise claims — whereas the configuration I have in mind survives even when the probability estimate is made in good faith. And risk aversion, in the economist's sense, is curvature in a utility-of-money function, which doesn't apply when the units are already lives or welfare and the dispute is over how tiny probabilities combine with huge stakes rather than over diminishing marginal returns. There is also a companion phenomenon on the other side that I equally cannot name: colleagues who happily accept the moderate versions — a one-in-a-trillion chance of saving ten-to-the-fifteenth lives does beat a certain ten thousand — but dig in their heels when the exponents grow further, in a way that reminds me of threshold deontology's treatment of absolute constraints. Is that a coherent stopping point, or just an inconsistent one, and does it have a name?

So my question is this: does the configuration I have described — the combination of unbounded additive aggregation and unbounded linear probability-weighting that generates the minuscule-chance, astronomical-stakes preference — have an established name in the decision-theory or population-ethics literature? If it does, I want to know precisely what it covers and what its recognized escape routes are (bounding the value function, discounting tiny probabilities as epistemically meaningless, non-linear probability weighting, and so forth), and how each escape route is itself classified. If there is no established term, then please coin one, define it so that it is distinguished from the three nearest neighbors I mentioned, and tell me where its edges are — in particular, where the boundary falls between rejecting the configuration outright and merely professing less than complete confidence in the probability estimates that feed it. Please open your response with the bottom line in its first sentence — whether a name exists and what it is — and give the reasoning afterward, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
