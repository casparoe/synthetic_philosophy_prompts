I am a novelist, and my most recent book has just gone through translation into both Dutch and Japanese. The two processes were startlingly different, and the difference has left me unsure what the practice of translation actually presupposes about what a line of dialogue is.

My book's narrator is a blunt woman who says things like "that's wrong," "I don't like it," and "no" without softening. The Dutch translator kept nearly all of this, and in a few places actually sharpened it — she cut my narrator's "I'm afraid that won't work" down to something closer to "that won't work," on the grounds that the hedged English sounded evasive, almost insincere, in Dutch. The Japanese translator did roughly the opposite: direct refusals became hedged and apologetic, "you're wrong" became a form of words that invites the other person to reconsider, and one character's blunt rejection of a dinner invitation was rendered as an apology and a vague expression of difficulty. When I objected that my character would never say that, both translators and their editors gave me versions of the same answer: that a literal rendering would make the character read as rude, aggressive, or simply foreign to the reader, and that a good translation should make the character sound the way she is meant to sound — decent, abrupt but not cruel — within the reader's language.

Here is what puzzles me. In every other respect, fidelity was treated as sacred. When a translator misstated which day a scene occurred on, or got a plot fact wrong, it was corrected without discussion. The propositional content of each sentence was understood to belong to the book and to be preserved. But the way characters speak — the directness, the hedges, the white lies and refusals — was treated as cultural packaging, to be swapped out for the local equivalent so that the same social meaning could be delivered. My narrator's blunt refusal and the Japanese character's apologetic non-refusal apparently count, in this practice, as the same utterance.

So my question is: what theory of what is being communicated does this practice presuppose? It seems to presuppose that a line of dialogue has two separable layers — a propositional core that is invariant, and a politeness layer that is merely the local costume the core wears to get itself accepted socially. Is that a coherent view, or does it smuggle in something more controversial: that the indirect or softened version means the same thing because white lies and hedges carry no assertive content of their own, that they are not really assertions about the world at all? Because as a novelist I resist that. To me, whether my narrator says "no" plainly or wraps the refusal in an apology she does not feel is part of who she is; a character who is direct and one who spares feelings are different people, and a reader of the Dutch and Japanese editions is, I suspect, meeting two slightly different women.

I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires — which views of meaning, communication, or sincerity it cannot do without — from what it merely sits comfortably alongside. In particular, tell me whether the practice is neutral between, on one side, views on which polite indirection and white lies are genuine acts of deception (and so in some tension with what the translators are doing, since they are inserting deceptions on the author's behalf that I never wrote), and, on the other, views on which such indirection is not deception at all but a different and equally honest way of communicating, since what is being conveyed is something about the relationship between the speakers rather than about the world. Does the practice presuppose one of these, or would either license it? And if the underlying theory were replaced — say, if a publisher accepted the idea that a character's directness is part of the work's content and must be preserved even at the cost of making the character seem rude — what would the resulting translation practice have to look like? I'm told such approaches exist, and are generally uncommercial; but I want to know whether they are philosophically motivated or just stubborn.
