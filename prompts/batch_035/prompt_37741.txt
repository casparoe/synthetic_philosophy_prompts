In three weeks I present my paper to a philosophy department's colloquium, and the talk survives or dies in the Q&A, so I need a prep sheet built for the room I will actually be facing.

The thesis I am defending: the problem of old evidence — raised in its modern form by Glymour using the case of the precession of Mercury's perihelion confirming general relativity — is not one problem but two wearing a single coat, and each has a different treatment. First, there is a diachronic problem about actual agents: how can a scientist's confidence in a hypothesis legitimately rise upon learning that the hypothesis entails something she already knew? Second, there is a synchronic problem about the evidential relation itself: in what sense is a long-known fact evidence for a newly proposed hypothesis at all? My move is to deny that the second problem belongs to probability theory. I claim that evidential relevance in the retro-confirmation case is fixed by the history of the hypothesis's construction: roughly, which of the agent's prior epistemic states is the right one to measure confirmation against is not settled by anything in the probability calculus but by facts about how the hypothesis was built and what its author was and was not attending to. For the diachronic problem I accept a version of the counterfactual-credence strategy in the tradition of Howson and Urbach: ask what credence in E the agent would have had prior to the hypothesis, then run the standard comparison. For the synchronic problem I argue that the calculus is silent and that a defensible confirmation relation must be indexed to a partition of the agent's pre-theory possibilities that the historian's and the theoretician's details, not the formalism, select. I further conjecture — and this is the part I think is original and the part I most expect to be attacked — that the notorious difficulties with choosing the right counterfactual or the right partition all trace back to trying to make a fact about the origin of a hypothesis do work inside a formalism that has no vocabulary for origins.

The room: a department with no decision theorists. Two formal epistemologists — one a committed subjective Bayesian who regards diachronic Dutch book arguments as settling most of what I am trying to reopen, the other working on imprecise credences and likely to object that my talk of the credence the agent would have had presupposes a determinate point value where there is none. A senior philosopher of science steeped in the Glymour tradition, who thinks the whole old-evidence discussion, including the counterfactual fixes, is a symptom of Bayesianism's inability to say anything about hypothesis construction and that my patch concedes his point. A historian of early twentieth-century physics, bluntly hostile to rational reconstruction, who will want to know whose credences in 1915 I take myself to be talking about and on what archival basis. A statistician who will say practicing scientists solved this decades ago and call it out-of-sample testing, so my problem is an artifact of philosophy's toy cases. The rest are graduate students, one of whom has just written a seminar paper on the garbling problem for counterfactual solutions.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet: roughly eight objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they would do to my talk, each phrased the way that particular member of the audience would put it, each with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is soft. Include what I should concede up front rather than fight about. And include the one question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that would hurt most coming from that room specifically, given what my conjecture commits me to. Please write the whole response in American English.
