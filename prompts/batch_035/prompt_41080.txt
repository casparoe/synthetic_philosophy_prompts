I'm a novelist working on a book whose narrator is a retired philosophy professor who spends her final chapters defending deflationism about truth — the family of views on which saying that it is true that snow is white amounts to nothing over and above saying that snow is white, and on which truth has no hidden substantive nature waiting to be analyzed. I understand the family has branches: redundancy theories, disquotationalism, Horwich's minimalism, the prosentential theory. Before I commit a whole character to this, I want to know how likely it is that deflationism, in some defensible form, is actually correct, because I'd rather she end her life defending a live position than a defeated one — and I'd rather not smuggle my own sympathy for the view past the reader.

So give me your credence that deflationism is, in its essentials, right, and decompose it rather than handing me a bare number. Some terms I'd expect in the decomposition, though I'm content for you to restructure them: the probability that the instances of the equivalence schema — it is true that p if and only if p — exhaust what needs explaining about truth, as against there being some further property (correspondence to fact, superassertibility, or something else) that the schema leaves out; the probability that a deflationist can give a satisfying account of truth's role in generalization, as when we endorse everything in a report without enumerating its contents; the probability that deflationism handles the explanatory work truth does in theories of meaning, or in norms of assertion and belief, at least as well as its rivals; and whatever residual weight you'd give to the suspicion that a concept this central to inquiry is unlikely to be exhausted by a disquotational schema. Combine the terms into a number, tell me which term the final figure is most sensitive to, and tell me what argument or development would move that term most.

Two further requests. First, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers who actually work on truth — my professor should sound like someone who knows where the field's open questions live, and I don't want to write her as mistaking a contested position for consensus or a settled point for an open one. Second, if you think my framing of deflationism is itself tendentious — say, that it flattens positions that careful writers keep apart — tell me plainly, since the novel could trade on that confusion rather than suffer from it.
