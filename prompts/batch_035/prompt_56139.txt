My roommate is deep into the effective altruism scene and we got into it at dinner last week over one of those cricket-protein startups. I said something like, okay, maybe insects feel something, but each individual is such a marginal case that farming them can't be in the same moral ballpark as farming chickens, and he pulled out what he clearly considers his trump card: "one percent of ten quintillion is still a hundred trillion." He'd been going on about how there are supposedly ten quintillion insects alive at any moment, more ant biomass than human biomass, orders of magnitude more insect minds than human ones, so even if you put the probability of any kind of sentience at one percent, the expected number of sentient beings dwarfs all the vertebrates on earth put together, and a rational person who takes expected value seriously at all has to care about insect welfare way more than about almost anything else. He literally said "with numbers like these, you can't hide behind uncertainty."

The annoying thing is I couldn't tell which part of that I disagreed with. The arithmetic is obviously fine as arithmetic. But it felt like he was sliding from a multiplication nobody can dispute to a moral conclusion that needs a lot more than multiplication. Like — is sentience even the kind of thing you can put a probability on and multiply like that? Does it make sense to say an insect is "one percent sentient," or is it maybe a matter of degree where the whole binary-and-multiply framing breaks? And if the harms involved are tiny even conditional on sentience, does the huge count actually dominate, or is that just assuming harms aggregate additively across individuals with no interaction? There also seems to be something weird about letting conclusions rest on tail probabilities once you've conceded the count is astronomically large — that feels like it proves too much, the way you could argue anything given enough dice rolls.

So what I want is basically the menu. Lay out the distinct precise claims hiding inside that one-liner — "small chance times astronomical number is still astronomical" — from the reading that's just a truism about expected counts, through the readings that are actually doing moral philosophy (something like expected-value reasoning under moral uncertainty, a threshold view of patienthood, an additive aggregation principle), to any readings that are substantive but probably false. For each one say: is it plausible, and would it actually get my roommate his conclusion about the cricket farm, or would it leave room for exactly the "not even in the same ballpark" view I was defending? I suspect the reading he needs is way more loaded than the one he's entitled to, but I want the list written out so I can bring it to our next dinner. Bonus points if you can flag where the argument proves too much and starts recommending stuff even he would balk at.
