I'm a graduate student writing a term paper on how to act when you can't settle which moral theory is correct, and I keep running into the same move. My supervisor — and, at greater length, the literature descending from MacAskill, Bykvist, and Ord's *Moral Uncertainty* — treats moral uncertainty as a straightforward analogue of ordinary empirical uncertainty. In the empirical case, you have credences over states of the world, you assign each outcome a value, you multiply and add, and you choose the option with the highest expectation. So, in the moral case, you take credences over moral theories, ask each theory how choiceworthy each option is, weight by your credence, and pick the option with the highest expected choiceworthiness. The analogy is doing real argumentative work in my circles: it's meant to show that deep moral disagreement is no excuse for paralysis, and that a principled person can commit to a career or a donation budget without first settling whether consequentialism is true.

I want you to audit the analogy rather than take it on faith. Please take the role of a professional philosopher specializing in decision theory and metaethics, someone who has published on decision-making under moral uncertainty and is skeptical of both lazy endorsements and lazy dismissals. Before writing, think carefully and brainstorm the mapping on your own — I'd like you to explicitly work through the correspondence table: what plays the role of the states of the world, the probability distribution, the utility function, the act, the expectation. Then turn to the disanalogies, and here I want you to be selective rather than exhaustive. The candidates I can see include: (i) the problem of intertheoretic value comparisons — utilities within a single decision-maker's empirical problem are on a common scale, but the choiceworthiness scores that different moral theories assign to the same option may have no common currency (the suffering-focused theorist and the Kantian are not disagreeing about the magnitude of one shared quantity); (ii) the fact that many theories are agent-relative or options-indexed in a way that resists collapsing each option to a single number per theory; (iii) a difference in the epistemology — empirical uncertainty is about which world is actual, while moral uncertainty may be about which way of evaluating worlds is correct, and it's not obvious these are species of the same genus. For each candidate disanalogy, tell me whether it merely complicates the expected-choiceworthiness machinery or whether it breaks the practical conclusion the analogy was invoked to support. Concretely: does the anti-paralysis conclusion survive even where the maximize-expectation rule fails, and if so, on what footing — mere decision-theoretic regret minimization, "moral hedging" in the sense of choosing options acceptable under most of the theories I find plausible, or something else?

Please use one running example throughout, and make it a live one for my paper: a recent graduate deciding between taking a high-earning job in order to donate a large share of income to global health charities, and taking a modestly paid direct-work job at a local homeless shelter, while being genuinely torn between a broadly consequentialist outlook and a more contractualist or partiality-friendly one that gives special weight to the people near you. Show me how the correspondence table plays out on that case, where the analogy strains in applying to it, and what the analogy quietly imports — for instance, whether treating moral theories as hypotheses over which you have credences already assumes they can all be evaluated as if from a standpoint outside all of them. If you think a better source domain exists for the comparison (uncertainty about which of several experts to defer to, unresolved disagreement within a single agent, measurement under multiple incommensurable scales), say so and sketch what it would change. I care most about a clear final verdict: is the expected-choiceworthiness framework the right practical default for people like my graduate, a useful heuristic with known failure modes, or a false comfort that only looks rigorous? Please reason step by step and be willing to give an answer that would annoy both the framework's boosters and its dismissers.
