My oldest friend is a lifelong birder and amateur taxonomist with decided opinions about where species boundaries fall, and over many years of hikes she has built up a track record of verdicts that I have never quite been able to trace back to a principle. Here is the record. She counts the carrion crow and the hooded crow as two separate species, despite the hybrid belt across Europe where the two regularly interbreed; she counts two mosquito lineages in our region that are indistinguishable to the naked eye and differ only in the fine structure of their chromosomes as two species; she counts the lineages of bdelloid rotifers, animals that have managed entirely without sex for many millions of years, as perfectly good species, on the grounds that for them breeding is beside the point; and she is adamant that the domestic dog and the gray wolf form a single species — she writes Canis lupus familiaris without apology and insists that a Chihuahua and an arctic wolf are conspecific whatever their anatomy. The one time I pressed her for a criterion, she said something vague about gene pools having fuzzy edges and then changed the subject. So I honestly cannot tell whether she is running one concept, two concepts for two kinds of organism, or none at all.

Her birthday is next month, and I want to test her over dinner with two cases I have been saving up. The first is the famous ring of salamander populations in the mountains around California's Central Valley: working around the ring, each population interbreeds with its neighbors, but where the two arms of the ring meet again in the south, those two forms live side by side without interbreeding at all — one species or several? The second is polar bears and brown bears, which interbreed in the wild and produce fertile offspring, yet are about as different in build and habits as two bears can be — one species or two? Please predict her verdict on each case, but what I really want from you is the analysis underneath: which single principle would best reproduce her track record as a whole; which rival principles fit that record about as well, and where those rivals agree and disagree on my two cases; and, if her record leaves the candidates genuinely tied, what further case — something I could realistically pose to her at the table — would split them apart. If you conclude that no single principle can cover all four of her earlier verdicts, I would rather hear that, along with what she would need to add or give up for her line to become consistent.
