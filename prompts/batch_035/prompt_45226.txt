I have been invited to give the opening address at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop on voluntary action. The audience will be roughly two dozen experimental psychologists and cognitive neuroscientists who know Benjamin Libet's readiness-potential experiments and the surrounding literature intimately, together with a handful of philosophers: two hard-line incompatibilists of the manipulation-argument persuasion, two Dennettian naturalists, and one quiet deflationist about mental causation.

My thesis is deliberately provocative, and I want help defending it. The two most familiar lines of resistance to free-will scepticism in recent decades are, I shall argue, a single manoeuvre. On one side there is Daniel Dennett's programme, announced in Elbow Room (1984) and developed in Freedom Evolves (2003): trade the metaphysical question — whether choices are uncaused causes — for an engineering question about which varieties of control are worth wanting in creatures like us. On the other side there is the reply, familiar from later modelling of the readiness potential as a stochastic accumulator rather than a decision signal: Libet's finding that conscious intention arrives late shows not that the brain decides before the mind does, but that neither decides in the way the question presupposed. Both, I claim, decline the question as posed and answer a nearby question about the design of agents instead. The manoeuvre is sound, but it has a price its users rarely acknowledge: once conceded, the "worth wanting" test stops looking like a discovery about free will and starts looking like a stipulation about what we are permitted to call free will — and stipulations, unlike discoveries, can be contested simply by declining them and helping oneself to the old word.

Please draft my question-period preparation sheet. I would like:

1. The eight or so objections I will actually receive from this audience — not the ones I would receive from a general audience — ordered by how much damage they would do, with an indication of which subgroup (the neuroscientists, the manipulation-argument incompatibilists, the Dennettians, or the deflationist) is most likely to raise each.
2. Each objection phrased in the vocabulary that subgroup would actually use, so that I recognise it when it comes.
3. A reply of roughly two minutes' length for each, plus a candid note on where the reply is soft.
4. A judgement about what I should concede up front in the talk itself in order to take the sting out of the objections that cannot be fully answered.
5. The single objection that has to land for my thesis to survive the workshop, and why.
6. The question I am privately hoping nobody asks — the one that, were it asked well, I could not honestly answer without conceding more than I have budgeted for.

Where an objection depends on a specific empirical wrinkle in the Libet paradigm — the timing of the awareness reports, the averaging artefacts, the veto window — assume the questioner knows the details better than I do, and tell me how to keep my reply from straying onto their terrain.
