Software engineer here, mostly backend stuff, zero philosophy training, so bear with me if this is a thing that got settled in 1757 and every undergrad knows the name for it.

I've noticed a pattern and I want to know if it has one. It goes like this: a fact about the *maker* of a thing changes how the thing itself comes across to me — not my decision about whether to consume it, but the actual experience, as if it were a property of the artifact.

A few instances:

1. I had a band I'd been into for like a decade. Then the singer turned out to be a genuinely terrible person (long story, it was in the news). Objectively the MP3s are identical, but the songs just sound worse now — thinner, kind of smug, like the production got cheaper. I'm not talking about skipping them out of principle, I mean the sensory experience itself degraded.

2. The reverse direction also happens. There was a janky indie game I bounced off of. Later I read a postmortem by the solo dev about how they rebuilt the whole thing three times over five years while their life basically fell apart, and when I went back to it, all the rough edges suddenly read as brave and honest instead of sloppy. Nothing in the build changed.

3. Closest to home: I once inherited a beautifully architected service from a senior engineer, genuinely elegant stuff, I learned a ton from it. Later it came out that this same guy had deliberately sabotaged a production database on his way out. Now when I read that code, the elegance reads as sinister — the clean abstractions feel like they're hiding something, like a too-tidy explanation from a liar. Same code, same tests, everything green, and it *feels* different.

So the pattern is roughly: moral facts about the creator function like a filter on the artifact's perceived qualities — good moral facts upgrade the craftsmanship, bad ones degrade it — and it happens automatically, before any reasoning about what I ought to do.

Things it's NOT, as far as I can tell:

- It's not the boycott question (should you keep streaming the songs). I don't care about the should here, I'm describing what the songs *sound like*.
- It's not death of the author / intentional fallacy stuff, at least I don't think so? Those are about whether the author's intentions should figure into the *interpretation* of the work. My interpretation hasn't changed — I still know exactly what the song is about and what the code does. It's more like the flavor changed. Though maybe I'm wrong about the boundary here.
- It's not plain ad hominem, because the maker's character genuinely is in question, I'm not using it to refute an argument — there's no argument, just an artifact.

Is there an established concept or literature for this specific thing — moral knowledge modifying perceived aesthetic/technical quality directly? If yes, what's it called and who do I look up? If it doesn't have a name, then honestly I'd like you to coin one, define it carefully (like, is my code case really the same phenomenon as the band case, or does code even have an aesthetic in the relevant sense?), and say where the edges are — e.g. at what point does this just collapse into ordinary interpretation of the work, since some art obviously wants you to know things about the artist. I won't be satisfied with just a label btw, I want to know what the thing is and isn't.
