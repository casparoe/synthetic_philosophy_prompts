Retired physicist here — forty years in radio astronomy, mostly hydrogen-line surveys, and now that the telescope time is over I've finally started reading philosophy properly and I keep tripping over Wigner's old point about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences. My old supervisor used to grumble that it only feels like a puzzle if you've never had to actually build an instrument, so I'm suspicious of my own reaction, which is that there IS something genuinely spooky there. I still remember when the SU(3) business predicted the Omega minus before anyone had found it, and that has never fully stopped spooking me.

So here's what I want: your actual credence that the effectiveness of maths in physics is genuinely 'unreasonable' — by which I mean NOT fully explicable by the boring explanations. Let me list the boring ones so we don't talk past each other:

(1) selection effects — we only remember the bits of maths that happened to fit and quietly forget the rest; nobody writes essays about the unreasonable ineffectiveness of number theory in solid-state chemistry, though I've sat through the seminars;
(2) mathematics being reverse-engineered from physical regularities in the first place — geometry grew out of land surverying, calculus out of mechanics, so of course it fits, it was cut from that cloth;
(3) humans being constitutionally incapable of even caring about concepts that don't map onto the middle-sized world our brains evolved in, so everything we've built is downstream of that world whether we notice or not.

Give me the number in your first sentence, then the decompsition: a rough probability that each of those three, taken alone, is enough to do the whole job; whether you think they're additive or the alternatives to 'unreasonable' need something more like a catch-all residual term, and if so what probability that residual gets cleared by anything we could actually articulate. One line of justification per term, no essays. Then tell me which term you're least confident in, and what evidence or argument would move it most — I want to know where the soft spot in my own position is before I start bending a philosopher's ear about it at the next departmental tea.
