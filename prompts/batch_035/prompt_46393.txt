I play Nomic, Peter Suber's 1982 game in which changing the rules is itself a move, and a friend at our weekly session has argued me into a corner I cannot find my way out of. His claim is not just that a rule cannot authorize its own change, but that, once you grant that, no legal rule can ever be validly changed at all. His argument, as I best understand it:

(1) A legal act is valid only if it is performed under a power conferred by a rule that is in force when the power is exercised.
(2) A rule is not in force after it has been changed: the old version, once replaced, confers nothing.
(3) Take a rule A that reads, in effect, "any rule, including this one, may be replaced by unanimous vote," and suppose the players vote unanimously to replace A with a different rule A′.
(4) The only possible source of authority for that vote is A itself.
(5) The vote does not merely precede the change of A; the act, properly individuated, is the changing of A. Its performance and its effect are the same event.
(6) So A would have to be in force at the very instant it ceases to be in force: the power and the destruction of its own source would have to coexist. Nothing can be in force and not in force at once.
(7) Therefore the vote was unauthorized, and self-amendment is impossible.
(8) But every legal system that can change its own higher rules faces the same circle: a constitution's amendment clause draws its authority from the very constitution it empowers people to change, so by the same reasoning no constitution could ever be validly amended.
(9) And since a constitution's original adoption was not authorized by any valid prior rule — no pedigree can go back forever — (1) implies that no constitution, and hence no law in any legal system, was ever valid.

To be fair to him, he concedes that Nomic games run perfectly well in practice: the votes are treated as binding and nobody objects. But he says this shows only that the game works sociologically, the way a club's customs do, not that its rule-changes are valid in the sense (1) describes. He makes the same point about actual constitutions: they are followed, and their amendments are obeyed, but obedience is not authority.

I cannot accept (9); it seems to prove far too much. My instinct is that something slips between (5) and (6): he has fused the vote with its effect, so that the authorizing rule is made to persist to the very moment of its own death, whereas nothing in (1) demands that an authorizing rule survive its exercise. When I point out that nobody thinks an ordinary repealing act is invalid just because the repealed rule is gone, he replies that I have picked the wrong casualty — in ordinary repeal the authorizing rule is untouched, and the whole puzzle is what happens when the authorizing rule is itself the thing destroyed. When I say that A's conferral of power at the moment of the vote settles the vote's validity once and for all, whatever happens to A afterwards, he says I am assuming precisely what is at issue: that a rule can confer an authority whose exercise annihilates the conferral itself. Is he right that this is circular?

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to keep (1) if possible — the alternatives look worse. Denying (1) seems to let valid acts float free of any authorizing rule, which is hard to distinguish from mere effectiveness. Adding an unamendable rule at the foundation would stop the regress, but Nomic refuses entrenchment by design, and no actual legal system seems to contain a rule that is unchangeable in principle rather than merely in practice. And I want to know whether the time-indexing move I keep gesturing at is a genuine distinction — between when a power is exercised and when its effects take hold — or just my conclusion restated in temporal vocabulary. Please name the step, explain why it looked fine, and price the alternatives.
