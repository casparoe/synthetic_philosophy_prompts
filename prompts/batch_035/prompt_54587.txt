When engineers try to infer what people want from what they do, they lean on a common skeleton. Inverse reinforcement learning treats an observed trajectory as a noisy sample of behavior that approximately optimizes the preference being inferred — the standard formalization makes the human Boltzmann-rational, choosing better options more often but sometimes worse ones. RLHF inherits the same skeleton at one remove: human raters' pairwise comparisons are modeled as noisy reports of a latent reward via a Bradley–Terry-style choice model, and the system is then optimized against that reward. Recommender systems do the same thing at industrial scale, treating clicks, watch time, and thumbs-up as samples of a latent user preference.

Yet the same organizations treat those signals as corrupt everywhere else. Designers A/B-test dark patterns that inflate click-through precisely because they know clicks do not track what users value. Trust-and-safety teams decline to treat raw engagement as a measure of a good outcome. Even the IRL literature itself concedes that humans deviate from rationality systematically — through fatigue, framing effects, habit — and proposes noise models to correct for it. But the correction is another layer of the same assumption: that there is a fact of the matter about the preference, and that behavior is evidence of it once you model the noise properly.
