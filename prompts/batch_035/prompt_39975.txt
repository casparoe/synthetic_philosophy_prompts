Take what happened in Hungary between 2010 and roughly 2014 — Orbán's Fidesz winning a two-thirds supermajority on about 53 percent of the vote, then using that supermajority to write an entirely new constitution (the Fundamental Law, in force from 2012) without any opposition votes, rewriting it several more times in the first eighteen months, packing and then expanding the Constitutional Court, lowering the judicial retirement age in a way that forced out a chunk of the senior bench, and setting up a media council stocked with ruling-party appointees, before moving on to redrawing the electoral map itself. No tanks, no decree, every step voted on by a freely elected parliament. Now run this episode through the lens of executive aggrandizement as it's usually characterized in the backsliding literature (the Nancy Bermeo-style typology where the alternatives are coups, promissory coups, and strategic election manipulation — legal, incremental weakening of checks on an elected executive).

What I want is the actual mapping, done carefully. Who counts as the "executive" here when the instrument was a legislative supermajority rather than presidential fiat — does the theory quietly assume a presidential system, and does Hungary break that assumption or just stretch it? What exactly were the "checks" being weakened, one by one, and were they all weakened at the same rate, or did the court-packing and the media capture work through different mechanisms? Is "gradual" even the right word for a country that swapped constitutions wholesale in eighteen months — is the theory of gradual aggrandizement forced to misdescribe the single fastest constitutional transformation in postwar Europe, and if so is that a fatal anomaly or just a boundary case?

Then run it the other direction, what the episode does to the theory. Bermeo's typology was partly built to distinguish backsliding from old-school coups so that outside actors (the EU, mostly) could respond appropriately — so does Hungary vindicate that or embarrass it? Hungary was an EU member the whole time, with treaty mechanisms on the books, and none of it stopped anything — does that show the concept of "legal, incremental" backsliding was doing real analytic work while the response side was empty, or does it show the typology is downstream of what Western governments already wanted to call a coup anyway (i.e. more of a partisan label than a category)? And on the "who decides" question — the Hungarian government's whole claim was that this was ordinary majority politics, that calling it backsliding is just refusing to accept you lost an election. Where, if anywhere, in the aggrandizement framework is there a non-question-begging answer to that?
