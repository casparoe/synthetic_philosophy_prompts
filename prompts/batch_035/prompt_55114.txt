I teach a third-year seminar in collective decision-making for philosophy and PPE students — around 14 people, one 75-minute session. Design a role-played exercise in which the class becomes a university promotions committee deciding a borderline case. The case should be built from three independently votable premises — say, research record, teaching performance, and service — with a rule that the candidate is promoted only if all three pass, and you should distribute individual views across the committee so that a majority accepts each premise while a majority rejects the promotion outright. Participants should first deliberate and vote on the case as a whole, then be made to vote premise by premise, and only then discover that the two procedures deliver opposite verdicts from the same set of opinions. In a second round, let the committee choose its own procedure knowing both outcomes, so they confront the fact that selecting the aggregation rule is itself a substantive decision. The debrief should map what happened onto the distinction between premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation, gesture at why no attractive aggregation method escapes the problem in general, and be honest about which features of the real theory the exercise cannot reproduce. Don't name the discursive dilemma or judgment aggregation until the debrief. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — someone spotting the trap and voting strategically, someone refusing to compartmentalise their deliberation — and how I should handle each. Please write your answer in British English.
