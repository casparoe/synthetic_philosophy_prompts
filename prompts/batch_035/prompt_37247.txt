I'm taking a second course in mathematical logic, and we've just covered the completeness theorem and the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems. In seminar this week a classmate laid out an argument running from Skolem's paradox to the conclusion that the cardinality of the continuum is not a determinate fact. I'm fairly sure it proves too much, but I cannot locate the damage. Here it is as she gave it.

(1) If ZFC is consistent, it has a model, and by the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem it then has a countable model M.
(2) ZFC proves that the reals are uncountable, so M satisfies "the reals are uncountable": no function in M is, according to M, a bijection between M's natural numbers and M's reals.
(3) M is countable, so there exists a bijection from the natural numbers onto M's reals; it simply fails to be one of M's sets.
(4) Countability is defined by the existence of such a bijection, and the definition nowhere requires that the bijection reside in any particular model. So M's reals are countable.
(5) M's reals are therefore both uncountable and countable. Since that is impossible, and since nothing in (1)–(4) misfires, "countable" cannot denote a model-independent property: countability is relative to a model.
(6) What holds of M holds of any structure, including the universe V we actually inhabit. The real reals are uncountable in V, but this is not a fact about them; it is a fact about V, namely that V happens to lack a bijection between the naturals and the reals, just as M happened to lack one. Had some larger structure contained such a bijection, the reals would be countable, and nothing about the reals themselves would have changed.
(7) So Cantor's theorem, though provable, states nothing absolute; whether the continuum is countable has no determinate answer, and the cardinality of the continuum is an artifact of where we happen to stand rather than a subject for mathematics.

She notes that this is not a new thought — Skolem himself drew a relativist moral from the paradox — so she takes herself to be stating the classical conclusion cleanly rather than overreaching.

What I cannot shake is a tension inside the argument. Step (3) is stated flatly, as though "M is countable" were an absolute fact about M; yet if (5) is right, (3) is at best true from where we stand, and the clash in (5) then looks like a collision of perspectives rather than a contradiction — in which case nothing forces (5) on us, and the slide to (6) and (7) loses its engine. On the other hand, if I insist that (3) is absolutely true, I seem to concede that some countability facts are absolute, and she will demand to know why the uncountability of the real reals cannot be absolute in exactly the same way. So either horn looks like it hands her something.

Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it is worth, I would like to keep the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem as stated, and I would like to keep the thought that the question whether the real continuum is countable has an answer, even if I must pay for that with some account of what makes V the relevant standpoint. I would also like to know whether her argument, if it survives, proves too much in further directions — whether, for instance, it makes finitude relative in the same way, or even the consistency of a theory, since those notions likewise look different from different models.
