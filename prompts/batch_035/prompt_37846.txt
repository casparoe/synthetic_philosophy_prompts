I do registration and collections data entry at a mid-sized regional museum, and something happened recently that I can't stop thinking about. We had a 17th-century landscape that had been on the wall for something like 40 years, attributed to a fairly big name, and after an authentication study (pigment analysis, dendrochronology, connoisseurship, the works) the attribution got downgraded to "follower of" that painter. Physically nothing changed — same canvas, same paint, and honestly the docents who gave tours in front of it for years couldn't tell you what the study even found. But it came off the main wall, got moved to a secondary gallery, and if it ever goes up for sale the price will be maybe a twentieth of what it would've been. Meanwhile, in the classical wing, a Roman marble that's a copy of a lost Greek bronze is one of the most beloved objects in the building, and the label is upfront that it's a copy. And I know a trained copyist who can make a near-indistinguishable duplicate of a Van Gogh in a month, and that duplicate is worth almost nothing, not even as a thing to hang on your wall and look at.

So here's what I want to figure out. What does this whole cluster of practices — the relabeling, the price collapse, the relegation to storage, the special case where copies of lost originals are treasured — actually presuppose about where the value of an artwork lives? My naive thought is that it presupposes aesthetic value can't be the whole story, since the aesthetic properties of the landscape didn't change one bit when the label did. But I can't tell whether the implicit theory is that value is some causal-historical relation to the artist (the painting as a relic or trace of a particular person's mind), or that we value the artist's achievement and the object is just a certificate of it, or something else entirely. And there are complications: we happily listen to cover versions of songs, we don't much care that famous quotes are misattributed, and even in our own museum the "follower of" painting is still considered worth displaying and insuring — just less.

Can you reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) behind how museums and the art market treat authenticity, and tell me which parts of the practice strictly require that theory versus merely fit well with it? For instance, maybe the price collapse is fully explained by scarcity and signaling without any claim about where value "really" lives, or maybe the storage decision isn't. I'd also like to know where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, i.e. several rival theories of artistic value would license exactly the same behavior. And finally: if the museum replaced the implicit theory with a straightforward aesthetic formalism — value is entirely in the observable properties of the object — what concretely would have to change? Would anything besides the labels and prices be different, or would the whole institution barely notice?
