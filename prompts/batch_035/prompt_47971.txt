My philosophy club has been stuck for two meetings on an argument about Twin Earth, and I've been assigned to write up where it goes wrong — but I can't find the faulty step. As I understand the thought experiment, on Twin Earth the clear, drinkable stuff in the lakes is not H2O but a different chemical, XYZ, and the internally identical locals use a word that sounds exactly like our word for that stuff — so their word refers to XYZ rather than to water. The argument runs: (1) What a word contributes to what its user says includes what it refers to. (2) So when a Twin Earthian sincerely utters the sentence water is wet, that person is saying something about XYZ. (3) Twin Earthians are internally identical to Earthlings, yet their word means something different from ours. (4) If two speakers can be internally identical while their words differ in meaning, then meaning is fixed partly by the environment. (5) Environments differ between any two regions of Earth as well: the trees, minerals, and animals surrounding one population are not those surrounding another. (6) So whenever environments differ, the two populations' words differ in meaning, and since a language is just a system of meanings, the populations speak different languages. (7) Therefore British and American speakers — whose words for chips, the ground floor, and even elk pick out different things — do not strictly speak the same language, and neither do I and my great-grandmother, since our word for water referred to H2O only once chemistry discovered what water is. The conclusion strikes all of us as proving too much, and yet every step sounded fine when we checked it against the others. Most of us want to keep the externalist steps, but I can't tell whether that is where the leak is. Which step should we reject, why did that step look so innocuous, and what do we give up by rejecting it?
