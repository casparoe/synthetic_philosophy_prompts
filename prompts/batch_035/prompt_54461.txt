I was away for two weeks and asked my neighbor to water my orchids; he forgot, and they died. At dinner a few nights ago, two friends of mine — Ben, who practices tort law, and Sofia, who is a graduate student in ecology — spent most of the evening arguing about whether my neighbor caused the orchids' death, and the argument kept sliding past itself. I'll try to reconstruct it fairly, because I honestly could not tell who was winning, and I'd like a considered verdict.

Ben opened with the simple line: the neighbor caused the death, because there is counterfactual dependence — had he watered the orchids as he'd agreed to, they would have lived, so his not watering is what made the difference. Sofia immediately objected that this test is profligate: by the same reasoning, her cousin in Lisbon also caused the death, since had her cousin flown over and watered them, they would have lived too; so would the professional gardener I never hired, had he existed and shown up. Every non-waterer in the universe passes the counterfactual test, she said, so the test cannot be what distinguishes the causes from the non-causes; at most it identifies a huge class of equally sufficient absences, and something other than causation must be picking out my neighbor from that class.

Ben conceded the point about profligacy and amended his position: the extra ingredient is the agreement. An omission counts as a cause only where there is a duty or reasonable expectation, and that is how the law treats omissions anyway, so his view is not an ad hoc patch but a description of settled practice. Sofia replied that this makes causation norm-relative rather than a natural fact, and she thought that was too high a price: on Ben's amended view, the identical physical situation — neighbor doesn't water, orchids die — contains a cause in a town where I had asked him and no cause in a town where I hadn't, which she found incredible; and she noted that norm-free causal talk about absences is everywhere in science, as when people say a lack of rainfall caused a crop failure, where no duty was breached by the sky. Ben's answer was that causal claims are tacitly contrastive and context-sensitive: a question like what caused the death always carries an implicit contrast (the death rather than the survival) and an implicit field of candidate answers, and the norms do not create the underlying dependency relations, which are simply there in the world; the norms only select which of the many real dependencies we bother to name, so nothing is made true by convention. Sofia's final move was to press on truth: when a judge says the defendant's failure caused the death, we can ask whether that sentence is true, not merely apt or salient, and she argued that Ben cannot have it both ways — if his causal facts are norm-independent then his view collapses back into profligacy, and if they are norm-dependent then their truth is hostage to whatever expectations the community happens to have, which is not how we ordinarily think causation works. At that point they appealed to me, and I declined, since the orchids were mine and my sympathies were suspect.

I would like you to adjudicate this dispute. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher specializing in the metaphysics of causation, and think through the exchange carefully before committing to any verdict. Go through the moves in order — Ben's opening counterfactual claim, Sofia's profligacy objection, Ben's duty amendment, her norm-relativity objection, his contrastivist reply, and her final challenge about truth — and for each, say whether the move is sound, mistaken, or right about something smaller than it claims, with a brief justification. Then tell me who is more right overall, and identify anything of substance that both of them missed. Please respond in continuous prose only, the way one would write a long email or letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
