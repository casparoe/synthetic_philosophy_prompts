I'm a graduate student in public policy, and our seminar spent last week on the choice of the social discount rate for climate cost-benefit analysis — the number that determines how much a ton of damage in 2100 counts against a dollar spent today. One of the other students, arguing for a high rate, kept falling back on the proverb 'a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush': present welfare simply counts for more than future welfare, he said, and moralizing about that is fighting human nature. When the rest of us pushed back, he stretched the proverb in two directions at once: to our great-grandchildren's welfare (they're in the bush, so their interests count for less) and to his own retirement saving (his seventy-year-old self is also in the bush, which is apparently why he sees nothing irrational in under-contributing to his pension).

I suspect the proverb is doing several different jobs at once and that some of them don't support him at all. It seems to slide between at least these possibilities: a bare psychological report that humans prefer nearer goods to farther ones; a prudential claim about how one person should trade off her own future against her present; a technical point about money, since a dollar today can earn interest, so present dollars and future dollars aren't comparable at par; a concession to uncertainty, since the bird in the bush might fly away — future goods are less certain, so discount them for that; and a full-blooded normative thesis of pure time preference, namely that the mere fact a benefit arrives later makes it count for less, whoever receives it. I've also heard it argued that even if some discounting is defensible within a single life, it becomes arbitrary when the bush contains other people rather than your own future self, since a person's claim on us shouldn't depend on when they happen to live — though I'm not confident I could defend that line myself, and I don't know whether it's supposed to rest on impartiality or on something about the metaphysics of personal identity.
