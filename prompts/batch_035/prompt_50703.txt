I'm a science writer doing a feature on 1816, the year without a summer — Tambora erupts, crops fail across the northern hemisphere, food riots, typhus outbreaks, migration. My editor is obsessed with one angle: the volcano as historical actor. He wants the piece structured like a biography of Tambora, with section headers where the volcano "chooses," "strikes," and "gets what it wants," plus a sidebar ranking the five most influential non-humans in history (the plague bacterium is on the list, obviously). It's vivid and I get why it sells, but something bugs me and I can't articulate it properly. It feels like the framing quietly says humans were just furniture responding to atmospheric chemistry, and that if you pushed it far enough you'd end up explaining all of history with germs and weather, which I'm pretty sure is a mistake people have made before. But when I try writing the "humans still mattered" version it comes out mush — everyone is a little bit responsible and nothing really caused anything. Is there a principled line between "the volcano was a cause of the famine" and "the volcano is the protagonist of the famine"? Or a way to keep the dramatic framing without commiting to the determinism underneath? Basically: can I give the volcano agency as a rhetorical device only, or does the metaphor do the arguing for me whether I like it or not?
