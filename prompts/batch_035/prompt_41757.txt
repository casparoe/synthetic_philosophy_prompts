I'm the campaign manager on a statewide race (attorney general, if it matters) and I keep running into the same thing: staff coming to me with stuff where the honest answer is "yeah we could do it, it'd work, but its a bit gross." A quote taken out of context in an attack ad, quietly telling a big donor something that's technically true but meant to mislead, feeding a reporter a half-wrong story to knock the opponent off message for a cycle, threatening to primary a legislator who votes with us 80% of the time just to show we'll do it. I'm tired of deciding these by gut, because my gut is mostly "will we get caught," which is not the standard I actually want to be using.

There's an old idea in political philosophy, dirty hands: that a politician can face situations where doing the right thing overall requires doing something that is still a wrong thing, and the mark of a decent politician isn't avoiding these but knowing afterwards that they did wrong, instead of letting it calcify into "that's just how politics works." I want this turned into something my senior staff and I can actually run in ten minutes, the night before a decision, with maybe one phone call to me and no one else. It has to be usable by a 26-year-old deputy comms director whose read zero philosophy.

I suspect the procedure should do roughly this, but rearrange if you think I'm wrong. First, a gate for whether we're even looking at a candidate: a lot of our "dirty hands" cases are ones we walked into earlier through choices that looked costless at the time, and I want the procedure to force a look at that, because a dilemma we built ourselves shouldn't get the same slack as one the world handed us. Then, whatever the philosophy actually supports as the difference between (a) an act that's fully justified — uncomfortable but not wrong — and (b) an act that's right all things considered but still wrong in the residue sense. That distinction has to survive into the procedure, because I think what we owe afterwards depends on it, and by afterwards I mean concrete things: whether the staffer who did it owes an apology they can't deliver to anyone, whether they have to say no to the next similar ask for a while, whether we disclose it to the candidate, whether the person rotates off that beat so it doesn't get easy. The procedure needs a rule for each aftermath — not "reflect on it."

Format I want: the questions in order, what a sufficient answer to each looks like, where it stops with a straight no, and where it stops and hands the decision back to me because it's a question of values, not facts. Mark those honestly — I don't want the procedure pretending to be neutral where it isn't. Then run it on the three cases I listed, include at least one where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, and tell me where it goes quiet: I'm guessing it can't tell me how much moral residue is worth how much message control, but I want to know what else it can't do.
