I'm a novelist, and for my fourth book I'm writing about a man in his fifties who has been blind from birth — congenital cataracts, both eyes, in a village in India, if that matters — and who, late in life, recieves sight through a charity surgical programme. While researching I kept bumping into what philosophers apparently call Molyneux's question: if a man born blind, now able to see, were shown a cube and a sphere, could he tell which was which by sight alone, before touching them? But what actually struck me wasn't the philosophy papers — it was the clinical side. After these operations nobody just hands the patient his vision and wishes him luck; there's a whole rehabilitation routine, weeks of it, where therapists guide him through learning to fixate, to reach for objects, to match what he now sees with what he already knew by touch. And in the case reports I've read the clinicians talk as if the visual world arrives more or less meaningless and has to be slowly attached to the old tactile one — though oddly, the patients themselves sometimes seem to make some connections almost immediately, which the therapists then tend to explain away as very fast learning rather than genuine recognition.

So here's what I want from you. Please write it in British English, and as continuous prose only — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, nothing list-like; just flowing paragraphs, the way one writes a long letter to a thoughtful friend. Take the practice of post-operative visual rehabilitation for people blind from birth as it is generally understood to work, describe it concretely, and tell me what it presupposes philosophically. Does the rehabilitation routine presuppose a no to Molyneux's question — that the newly sighted person cannot immediately recognise shapes by sight — and does it thereby quietly rule out the alternative, where some mapping between touch and sight is already in place and the therapy merely surfaces it? Is the clinicians' habit of describing early successes as learning rather than seeing a substantive commitment, or just therapeutic caution about claiming too much? And where is the practice underdetermined — could a rehab programme designed by someone who thought the answer to Molyneux was yes look almost identical, with the same exercises re-described as confirmation rather than construction? I ask because I want to know what my fictional surgeon and my fictional therapist would each believe they are doing, and whether either of them would realise they had taken sides on a three-hundred-year-old question at all.
