I run a monthly ethics discussion night at a pub — fifteen or so people, a mix of students and randoms with no philosophy background — and I want to make a which-position-do-you-actually-hold quiz on particularism vs. generalism in ethics. So: does moral thinking run on principles, or on judgment applied case by case?

The quiz itself: eight or so questions, each one a concrete case with two or three answer options, and then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the positions. The questions have to sort people by what they say about the cases, not by which label they like — so no question can just ask do you believe in moral principles, and nothing that needs jargon to parse.

A few cases I definitely want in there. One, the classic about lying to someone at the door who intends to kill the friend hiding in your house, asked point blank — that one should test whether any principle survives without exception. Two, a promise you ought to break to prevent serious harm, where the real question isn't whether you may break it but what's left over afterwards: does the broken promise still count against you, do you owe the person an apology, or did the promise simply stop applying? That should separate people who think principles get outweighed from people who think they get switched off by an exception clause. Three, at least one case where the same fact seems to flip its moral polarity depending on surroundings — suffering that's deserved, or a risk somebody knowingly signed up for — to test whether reasons depend on their context.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names: figure out the questions and their combinations first, then locate the actual positions in that space. The leaves must include exceptionless generalism; the outweighing kind (Ross-ish, prima facie duties that leave a residue when you break them); generalism with codified exception clauses; generalism where the exceptions are real but can't be written down in practice, so principles are just reliable rules of thumb; and straight particularism, where there are no true moral principles at all and reasons are holistic. If some combination of answers in the space is unoccupied, say so and take a guess at why — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Work the codifiability regress in somewhere too: once you write down the exceptions to a principle, do the exceptions have their own exceptions, and so on — and is a principle with a thousand clauses still a principle, or just a notebook? And keep the semantic question (are there true principles?) separate from the epistemic one (could a human ever apply them). Maybe a case where an angel hands you the complete, exception-free list of moral rules — would you then just look things up like a code, or would judgment still be doing the actual work?

Two things I want from you beyond the quiz. First, don't be wishy-washy on the hard calls: if two leaves can't really be separated by any question a reader could answer without already knowing the theory, say so plainly, and then say which side you'd personally shove the reader into and why. Second, tack a short where-I-stand section on the end — which leaf you actually think is correct, with your reasons. I'd rather recieve your committed view and argue with it over a pint than get a menu.
