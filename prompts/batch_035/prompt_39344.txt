I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm building an interactive feature for our website to accompany a piece on the units-of-selection debate — the old fight over whether natural selection acts on genes, organisms, groups, or something else. Readers will arrive either convinced the question was settled forever by the selfish-gene era or never having suspected genes and organisms could compete for the same explanatory job, so the feature has to sort them by what they actually believe about cases rather than by whose book they liked.

Please write me a decision tree that takes a reader from the starting commitment that natural selection happens and favors the fit, to a leaf naming their position: gene-level selectionism, organism-centered selection, multi-level or group selection, species selection, and the pluralist option that several of these are legitimate descriptions of the same process rather than rivals. The tree must be built from the logical space, not from the history of who argued with whom, so first work out the questions that genuinely separate the positions and only then place the named views in the resulting grid; note any combinations of answers nobody holds, and say whether that's because the view is incoherent or merely unattractive or just unexplored.

The branch nodes should each be a question about a concrete case that a reader with no background can answer without knowing which position it leads toward, and the tree must include at least these: an allele that cheats at meiosis, getting itself into more than half the gametes while harming the organism carrying it; sterile workers in a eusocial insect colony, where the individual's fitness is zero but the lineage thrives; a haystack-style setup, where organisms do better or worse depending on the composition of the group they happen to land in; a trait like a species' geographic range or generation time, which varies between species rather than between organisms; and a tumor lineage competing against other cells within one body's lifetime. There should also be at least one node that forces the reader to confront the difference between selection genuinely operating at a level and a lower level merely doing the accounting — the byproduct question — since I suspect most readers have never separated causation from bookkeeping and it's the hinge of the whole debate.

Phrase every node so the reader is choosing between descriptions of what happens in the case, not endorsing slogans. At each leaf give the position's name (or mark it unnamed if that's more honest), plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the case it has to swallow hardest. Finally, tell me which pairs of leaves the tree cannot actually separate, so the feature can say so up front rather than pretending the questions are sharper than they are; I'd rather concede in the interface that, say, two positions come apart only at a level of formal machinery we're not going to show readers than have a reader land somewhere by accident.
