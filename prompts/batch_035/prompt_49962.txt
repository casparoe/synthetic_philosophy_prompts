At the last meeting of my philosophy of mind reading group, which has been working through Dennett's writings on what he calls the strange inversions of reasoning due to Darwin and Turing, two members got into a sustained disagreement that no one else could referee, and I'm still not sure how it should come out. Amira began by arguing that the two inversions amount to the same move, and that the move is more radical than we usually let ourselves notice: Darwin explained the appearance of design in organisms without any designer who understood what it was building, and Turing showed that flawless computation can issue from a machine that grasps nothing about arithmetic, so taken together they suggest that comprehension does no causal work anywhere — that all the real work is done by accumulated, mindless competence, and "comprehension" is at best a name we give to certain well-organized bundles of it. Ben objected that this overshoots: the inversions show only that comprehension is not needed at the base of the explanation, not that it never exists, just as DNA needs to understand nothing even though whole organisms plausibly do; and he pointed out that the very slogan "competence without comprehension" presupposes the contrast class, so Amira's reading turns Dennett's phrase into a self-undermining slogan.

Amira answered that this is like insisting "life" survived mechanistic biology as a genuine extra ingredient: once every case of comprehension is constituted, without remainder, by comprehensionless competences, the term is left as a folk convenience rather than a real kind, and she noted that we keep moving the goalposts anyway — chess engines and now large language models have whatever competence we point to, and each time we say the missing thing is comprehension, we can't say what would detect it. Ben replied that there is in fact a detectable, functional difference, and it matters practically: a calculating savant who can multiply six-digit numbers but cannot say when an answer is obviously wrong or transfer the skill to a novel problem has competence without comprehension in a perfectly meaningful sense, and we trust such a person differently in unfamiliar situations than we trust someone who understands arithmetic; so comprehension should be read not as an extra ingredient but as an architectural property of a competence — recombinability, sensitivity to reasons for why it works, self-monitoring for failure — which is a structural difference, not a ghostly one. Amira's final move was that this concedes her point in the only way that matters: Turing's inversion guarantees that any such architecture can be built out of parts that understand nothing, so Ben's "structural property" is just more competence stacked on competence, and calling the top of the stack comprehension adds nothing that "well-organized competence" doesn't already say; Ben's closing reply was that "just competence stacked on competence" assumes the very thing in dispute — that levels of organization never have powers their parts lack — and that denying emergent powers is a metaphysical prejudice the inversion itself gives us no reason to accept, since Darwin never showed that there are no organisms, only that organisms are made by something that isn't one.

Could you adjudicate this exchange move by move — saying who is right about which specific point, and where each of them slides between claims (for instance, between what an explanation can leave out and what really exists, or between comprehension as an ingredient and comprehension as a structural organization)? And if there is a genuine crux the two of them never isolate, please say what it is and how a defender of each side ought to respond at that point. Finally, tell me who you think is more right overall, and why.
