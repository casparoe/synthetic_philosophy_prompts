Our decision-theory reading group spent last term on the classics and has now gotten badly sidetracked into a fight about what the coherence arguments actually buy. One member, an AI-safety-inclined graduate student, keeps pressing a strong reading: the Dutch book argument for probabilism, money pump arguments for acyclic preferences, and the von Neumann–Morgenstern and Savage representation theorems jointly show that any sufficiently rational agent must be an expected utility maximizer, and that this is a substantive fact about how such an agent behaves — not merely a consistency condition on whatever preferences it happens to have. I'm skeptical: as I understand them, the arguments at most establish that an agent's credences and preferences must be internally coherent, and a perfectly coherent agent can still be a satisficer, or have a utility function that ranks the space of outcomes in ways that don't amount to anything we'd recognize as a goal. What I want from you is your credence in the strong reading, decomposed rather than a bare number.

Please break the question into at least the following sub-claims, assigning each a rough probability with a line of justification, then show me how they combine into your final number: (a) that money pump arguments really establish full transitivity of preferences rather than something weaker, given the known dynamic-choice escape routes — an agent that commits resolutely to earlier plans, or reconsiders its preferences over time, seems hard to pump; (b) that the independence axiom is forced by coherence considerations rather than by an extra, substantive consequentialist assumption, with the Allais preferences as the standard test case; (c) that, granting the full representation theorem, being an expected utility maximizer then constrains anything beyond the internal consistency of the agent's ordering — my worry is that any agent with a complete transitive ordering, however arbitrary its goals or however halfheartedly it pursues them, comes out as maximizing expected utility under some assignment, which makes the theorem close to vacuous as a behavioral constraint; and (d) that the arguments transfer at all from idealized, unbounded agents to real or realistically resource-bounded ones. Feel free to add or restructure terms if you think I've carved the question badly.

For calibration: my credence in the strong reading is about 0.2, while my friend's is about 0.85. Tell me which of the decomposition's terms most of that gap probably lives in, which term your own final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or consideration would move that term the most. To preempt the question of what would count as resolution: I'd treat the strong reading as settled against my friend if no principled route can be found from the internal consistency of preferences to any substantive claim about what an agent will pursue or how persistently it will pursue it, and settled in his favor if there's a compelling argument that a coherent-but-gainless agent — say, one that deliberates and acts coherently but only ever between indifferently many options — is in some way defective.
