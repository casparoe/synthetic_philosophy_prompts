I teach fifth grade at a public elementary school, and our district recently adopted a schoolwide behavior system that I'm supposed to administer: students earn points for observable prosocial acts — helping a classmate pick up dropped books, defusing a conflict at recess, including a left-out kid at lunch — and the points are redeemable for privileges and small prizes from the school store, with point deductions for infractions. Homerooms post a weekly leaderboard. At the very same time, we hold assemblies where we tell the children that honesty and kindness matter for their own sake and not for what you can get out of them, and my colleagues and I get visibly uneasy when a student stops to ask whether she'll earn points before she helps anyone. I'm also taking an evening course in Chinese philosophy, and it has made me unable to stop seeing my own school through two ancient rivals.

Here is the background from the course, in case you need it. Mencius held that human beings come equipped with incipient moral tendencies — he compared them to sprouts that grow into benevolence, righteousness, ritual propriety, and wisdom if they are cultivated, and wither if they are neglected or damaged — and he took the spontaneous alarm anyone would feel on seeing a small child about to fall into a well to show that these tendencies are native, prior to any calculation of gain or reputation. Xunzi, his great opponent, thought this was exactly backwards: on his account human nature is self-centered and unruly, our desires run toward what benefits us, and goodness is the deliberate artifact of culture — ritual, instruction, and law working on us the way a craftsman steams and bends a straight piece of wood against a form to curve it.

What I want to know is what theory of human nature and moral development our point system actually presupposes, if any. On its face it looks Xunzian: goodness produced from the outside by structuring incentives, which suggests the school doesn't trust the sprouts to grow on their own. But maybe that's too quick — maybe points are just the watering and tending that a Mencian would endorse, and the two views can't be distinguished by anything the school actually does. And then there's the apparent inconsistency between the leaderboard and the assemblies: one practice treats virtue as externally produced, the other insists it must come from within, and I'd like to know whether these really conflict or whether they can be read as aiming at different phases of the same development. There's also a Mencian-sounding worry I can't quite shake: if you pay children to be kind, do you crowd out the sprout rather than cultivate it, so that the point system presupposes its own pessimism about human nature and thereby makes that pessimism true? Please reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, tell me which parts of the school's practice strictly presuppose one view rather than the other and which parts are compatible with several, and describe concretely what a school built on the rival view would do differently — what would a Mencian alternative to a token economy actually look like on a Tuesday morning in my classroom? Please answer in American English.
