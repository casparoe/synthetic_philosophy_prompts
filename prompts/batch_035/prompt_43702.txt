I teach a graduate seminar on consciousness this term, and Dennett's quining of qualia — the Chase and Sanborn coffee-taster intuition pump and the argument it feeds — has been sitting uneasily with me for reasons I finally managed to articulate in the form of an argument I can't refute to my own satisfaction. Perhaps your reader can. The argument is meant to show that the quining method proves too much: that if it disqualifies qualia, it disqualifies beliefs as well, and thereby dismantles the very intentional-stance framework Dennett meant it to serve. Here is the argument as I've written it up for the seminar:

(1) A mental posit deserves elimination when two conditions hold: every fact we could ever establish that seems to involve it can be reestablished, without predictive or explanatory loss, in other terms; and the posit lacks independent identity conditions of its own — every attempt to say what it is, as opposed to what it does, degenerates into confusion or contradiction. (2) Chase's and Sanborn's introspective reports — that the coffee now tastes less pleasant than it did when they were hired, or that they suspect their tastes have shifted rather than the beans — can each be recast in terms of discriminative capacities, reactive dispositions, and memory updates, with nothing left over. (3) No conceivable experiment could adjudicate between the qualia-inclusive and the qualia-free redescription, because any behavioral or neurological result would simply be absorbed into whichever redescription one favored. (4) Therefore, by (1), qualia are not merely dispensable but incoherent posits, to be quined. (5) But the same treatment is available for belief: first-person avowals of belief can be recast in terms of dispositions to assert, to draw inferences, and to act; any behavioral or neurological result would be absorbed equally smoothly; and the notion of a belief as something over and above its role in cognition is, if examined directly, no less a source of confusion than the notion of a quale. (6) Therefore beliefs are incoherent posits too. (7) But Dennett's intentional stance treats beliefs as real patterns that earn their keep, and he is emphatically not in the business of eliminating them. (8) So either the quining argument against qualia fails, or the real-pattern account of belief fails — the method has refuted the theory it was devised to defend.

Now, I am not willing to accept either horn of (8). I don't want to surrender (7) — the real-pattern treatment of belief strikes me as one of the more durable achievements of that whole research program — but I am also reluctant to weaken (1) so far that the quine loses its bite and qualia come streaming back in. My suspicion is that the trouble lies somewhere in the transition from (4) to (6), in the assumption that redescribability transfers from qualia to beliefs, but I cannot yet pin down precisely where the analogy breaks, or whether it breaks at all. So I would ask this: which premise or inference in the argument should be rejected, and why did that step seem acceptable in the first place? In particular, I would like to know whether the asymmetry I keep gesturing at — that beliefs are somehow defined by their role while qualia were supposed to be something over and above any role — is a genuine disanalogy that the argument ignores, or merely the qualia-friend's last refuge dressed up as a principled distinction. And if the disanalogy is genuine, tell me exactly what the quining argument needed to claim about qualia, and only about qualia, for the elimination to go through — and what it costs to concede that the argument's force was never quite as general as the premises made it sound.
