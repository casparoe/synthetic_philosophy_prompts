R went first: he said J was misusing the number from the start. heritability is about how much of the *variation* in some population tracks genetic differences, it's not a breakdown of any one person. saying "IQ is 80% heritable" does not mean 80% of J's intelligence comes from his genes and 20% from his life, that's just not what the statistic is.

J said fine, whatever, variation in a population — but if the variation tracks genes that strongly, then kids' outcomes are mostly locked in by their parents, and stuff like better schools is mostly a waste. isn't that the obvious practical upshot, why else would anyone care about the number?

R said no, and here's where he got kind of excited. heritability is a statement about one population in one set of enviroment conditions. he said his whole job is basically moving that number around: if he takes his corn lines and waters them unevenly on purpose, heritability for yield crashes, because the differences between plants get swamped by which patch got more water. and the flip side: if you give everyone a good, equal environment, whatever variation is left over is more genetic, so heritability goes up. so a high heritability can actually be evidence that you've already equalized the environment — the exact opposite of J's "its hopeless" reading.

J pushed back and honestly this was his best point I thought. he said R's corn example is the idealized case. in the real world the environments aren't equal — measured heritability in a country includes people with wildly different schools, neighborhoods, lead exposure, all of it. so the measured number is inflated by all that environmental variation, and if you somehow made environments perfectly equal, heritability might come out even higher. which is worse for R's side, not better.

R said ok, but then J has to swallow the mirror image: if the environments aren't equal, the measured number is *depressed* by environmental variation too, so J can't lean on the measured 80% to argue schools are pointless — the number can't carry that conclusion in either direction. his point wasn't "genes don't matter," it was that heritability is basically silent about what interventions would do, that's the actual lesson.
