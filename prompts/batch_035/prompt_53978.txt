So here's my situation. I sit on our country's animal sentience advisory panel (it's real work but let's treat it as hypothetical, doesn't matter) — the body that decides which species get counted as sentient for purposes of welfare law. The annoying thing is we never adopted a written criterion. We just issue verdicts species by species, and the track record now looks like this: all vertebrates grandfathered in, no questions asked, including a deep-sea fish that basically nobody has ever observed behaving in the wild; octopus, cuttlefish, and squid in, on the basis of big brains and famously flexible behavior; crabs and lobsters in — and in that ruling we leaned hard on experiments where shocked crabs trade off shelter against shock avoidance, and show anxiety-like guarding of wounds; honeybees out, and I want to flag that when we ruled on bees the evidence on the table was associative learning plus withdrawal reflexes, and the ruling explicitly said learning is not suffering; crickets out, jellyfish out, sea urchins out, roundworms out.

Now a new application has come in for bumblebees, backed by a study I'll summarize so you don't need to go find it: bees were offered sucrose behind a surface that could be heated. Unheated, all of them fed. As the heat went up, bees with dilute sucrose quit early, while bees with concentrated sucrose endured temperatures they otherwise avoided, and the tipping point tracked sugar concentration. Injured bees also showed wound-directed grooming and reluctance to return to where they'd been hurt. The applicants say this is motivational trade-off behavior of exactly the kind we cited for crabs.

I have to write up a recommendation. So: what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Work through what rule (or rules) our track record actually fits — I can see at least two that both explain everything we've done so far — and say what each one predicts here. Identify the closest precedent on our record and say whether it really controls. And if our record genuinely underdetermines this, tell me straight: what principle would we be adopting if we vote the bees in, and what would we be adopting if we vote them out? One thing nagging me that I'd like your read on: we let in a vertebrate nobody has ever seen do anything, on brain grounds alone, while a bee with better behavioral evidence than that fish will ever have is currently on the outside — is that tension something my recommendation has to resolve, or can it just live with it?

Also, since I suspect our precedents don't settle this: name the one further case (a real-ish species or a hypothetical animal) that would most cleanly distinguish the candidate rules you come up with, so I know what we'd be getting ourselves into at the next hearing.
