You are to respond as a professional philosopher whose specialty is population ethics and the ethics of procreation — the kind of colleague one consults before a workshop, not for a literature review but for careful analytic reconstruction. Please reason the way you would at the seminar table: before committing to any prediction, brainstorm the candidate principles that could fit the evidence, test each against it, and only then commit, flagging where your inference is shakiest. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; any view you invoke from the literature should be stated in your own words so the reconstruction stands on its own.

Here is the situation. A colleague of mine — I will call her Professor R — has spent years issuing crisp, unhedged verdicts on cases in the ethics of creating and ending lives. She defends each verdict when pressed, but she has never, to my knowledge, stated the principle behind them. Her record, as best I can reconstruct it:

1. It is wrong to conceive a child who predictably will suffer more than she flourishes — and this remains wrong even if the child, once grown, will be glad to have been born and will say so.
2. It is permissible, and a good thing for the child, to conceive a child who predictably will flourish far more than she suffers; yet a couple who could easily conceive such a child and choose not to do nothing wrong, not even a minor wrong.
3. Preventing the conception of a child who predictably would have had a miserable life is a genuine moral achievement, and its goodness does not consist in benefiting anyone who exists or ever will exist.
4. A person killed instantly and painlessly in her sleep, and thereby deprived of decades of happy life, suffers a harm — a harm consisting in the deprivation itself, not in any pain she experiences.

Next week Professor R will comment on three new scenarios at a workshop, and I would like to anticipate her answers.

Case A (the gamble): a couple can conceive a child whose life is a coin flip — with equal probability, either a life of severe, unrelievable torment or a life of unqualified bliss, so that the expected balance is strongly positive.

Case B (the endorsed hardship): a child is guaranteed a life containing a period of severe suffering, later redeemed and arguably outweighed by decades of deep fulfillment; from the inside, she will endorse the whole package and would not trade it away.

Case C (the comparison): two couples, each certain to conceive; the first child will have a life with ten units of suffering and fifty of pleasure, the second a life with ten units of suffering and twenty of pleasure. Asked whether creating the first child is better than creating the second, what should R say?

My request, in order. First, articulate at least three genuinely distinct principles that fit her record on cases 1 through 4 — distinct in their consequences, not merely in their wording — and verify each against all four verdicts, noting where the fit is loose. Second, say what each principle predicts for Cases A, B, and C; I expect them to diverge on at least two. Third, give me your single best prediction for each case, with a confidence level and your reasons, acknowledging where the candidates remain tied. Fourth, design the one further case that would most cleanly divide the surviving candidates — the hypothetical verdict that, whatever R actually gave, would settle the most. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which additional recorded verdict from R would most destabilize your reconstruction, which of her four recorded verdicts you would most suspect of being misremembered by me, and where in your own reasoning you think the failure is most likely to occur. I would rather have a candid account of fragility than a confident guess presented as inevitable.
