Quick context: I'm a phil grad student and my diss chapter is on the nonidentity problem, and I've hit what I suspect everyone hits -- the debate has been completely static for decades. You basically have two camps: the person-affecting people, who insist that wronging requires a victim and then tie themselves in knots trying to find one in Parfit's depletion cases or the recklessparents-type cases, and the impersonal people, who shrug and say a choice between two possible futures can be worse even when it's worse for nobody, and each side knows every argument the other has and nobody ever converts. My advisor's pet hypothesis is that it's bedrock intuition about whether existence itself can be a benefit, but I have a competing one: that the fight is actually a hidden semantic/metaphysical one about whether "made worse off" is even well-defined when the two alternatives contain different people, and both sides are silently assuming an answer about cross-world welfare comparisons that they never argue for. Which of us (if either) is right? I want a genuine diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is -- verbal, different questions under one name, clashing starting intuitions, an ill-posed question -- not a survey of the literature, I've read it. And then be honest about what would even count as progress here: a smarter case, a formal result about person-affecting restrictions, or just admitting the question is broken?
