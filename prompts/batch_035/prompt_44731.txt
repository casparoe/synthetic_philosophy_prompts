I spent thirty-odd years in pharmacology before I retired, and now that I read philosophy in the evenings I keep coming back to something I never thought to question at work. My whole career rested on animal models: we screened antidepressants in mice with the forced swim test, studied memory in sea slugs, mapped reward circuits in rats -- all on the assumption that what we learnt would tell us something about human depression, human memory, human motivation. Nobody ever spelled out why a rat pressing a lever for sucrose is supposed to model a person's anhedonia; we just asssumed the mental state was the thing under study and the brain implementing it was an implementation detail. Reading philosophy now, this looks like functionalism and multiple realizability in a lab coat: the same mental state realized in wildly different hardware, so the mouse's despair and the person's despair share some functional organization at some level of abstraction. But which level? Too abstract and the model tells you nothing specific, too specific and it doesn't transfer across species. So what exactly does the animal-model practice presuppose about the mind, does it quietly rule out type identity theory, and could an identity theorist still run those experiments with a straight face? And is the newer habit of treating deep neural networks as models of human cognition just the same assumption pushed further? Please answer in sections called "What the practice presupposes", "What it doesn't need", "Where it gets shaky", and "What would change" -- a paragraph each, with "Where it gets shaky" the longest.
