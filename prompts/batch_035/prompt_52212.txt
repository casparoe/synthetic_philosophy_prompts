I'm writing an essay for a graduate seminar on Bernard Williams and want to pair it with Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, which I've long suspected is a Williams novel in butler's livery. Read the novel through the integrity objection and the "one thought too many" point, and tell me whether the pairing actually holds up or whether I'm forcing it.

Here is the shape of the fit as I see it, which you should test rather than assume. Williams's integrity objection attacks the utilitarian picture in which an agent's own projects count only as one input among all sentient beings' interests, so that a person can be required to abandon what gives their life shape whenever the sum favors it — the agent becomes a conduit through which the world's utility is pushed, and the demand that one always be ready to view one's deepest commitments from the impartial standpoint is, on Williams's telling, an alienating demand. Stevens is a man who has thoroughly internalized something like this stance toward himself: he treats his life as material in the service of Lord Darlington's projects — the conferences, the appeasement policy, the hosting of the great and good — and repeatedly describes his own dignity in terms of the emotional restraint and professional poise that let him serve as an instrument of a great man's effectiveness. When his father is dying upstairs during the 1936 conference, Stevens stays at his post downstairs because the evening's success matters more; when Miss Kenton announces her engagement, he can respond only with talk about staffing arrangements and his failure to appreciate her banter. So the novel looks like a case study in exactly the kind of alienation Williams describes — except that Stevens is not a utilitarian, and no theory made him do it.

That is where I want you to press. Does the integrity objection survive translation from the philosophy of theory to the philosophy of a life? Stevens's dispositions are not imposed by commitment to the utilitarian calculus; they are role-internal, self-cultivated, and in his own eyes a matter of professional excellence. If Williams is right that integrity requires ground projects of one's own, Stevens seems to have them — dignity, service, the memory of serving a great man — and yet the novel presents these as precisely the mechanism of his self-effacement. Is this the integrity objection in a form Williams never considered, or does it show something Williams underplayed: that ground projects can themselves be the vehicle of alienation when they are structured by an institution and a class? Relatedly, take the one thought too many. Williams's point is that there are situations — saving one's drowning wife — where deliberation itself is the failure, where the man who reasons to his obligation has already lost something that a spontaneous act would have expressed. Stevens's missed moments with Miss Kenton look like textbook cases: where love called for unreflective response, he offered considerations. But there is a reading in which Stevens's problem is not one thought too many but one thought too few, or the wrong first thought entirely — and there is a question of whether Williams's own valuation of spontaneity over reflection has any grip on someone for whom restraint is not the suppression of feeling but its chosen form. Consider, too, the ending: the retired butler on the bench at Weymouth, his admission that he gave his best to Lord Darlington and it was a mistake, and his resolution to practice banter. Does the novel finally vindicate Williams, or does it offer a kind of dignity in defeat that Williams's framework can't classify?

I'd like the reading to be faithful in both directions: it should take the actual mechanics of Williams's arguments seriously — negative responsibility, the impartial standpoint, ground projects, the expressive character of spontaneous action — and it should honor what the novel knows about its protagonist rather than turning him into an illustration. Say plainly where the novel resists the Williamsian frame and where Williams would resist the novel. And end by telling me what, if anything, the novel gives the debate that the arguments by themselves don't — I suspect it is something about the temporality of self-deception, the way Stevens narrates his own life to himself, that neither the integrity objection nor the one-thought point was built to capture, but I want to know whether that suspicion can be made precise or whether it's just the fact that novels are long. No need to quote the text; I know it well, and paraphrase is fine. I would like you to be willing to conclude that the pairing half-works, and to say exactly which half and why.
