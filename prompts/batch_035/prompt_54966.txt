you're a professional philosopher with a background in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and i'd like you to write a dialogue between two friends arguing about whether their mutual friend is self-deceived — and, more basically, whether self-deception is even possible.

the setup: Priya, a semi-professional cellist, withdrew from a major audition a few weeks before it happened, saying she wants to focus on her teaching studio. but her friends have watched this pattern for years: she goes quiet whenever the competition comes up, she practices less right after hearing who else entered, and once, slightly drunk, she said "maybe I just didnt want it that much" in a tone that suggested the exact opposite. noone has ever confronted her about it.

the two friends split along familiar but genuinely opposed lines. one takes roughly the Sartrean view: for Priya to be self-deceived she must somehow know what she's doing while keeping the knowledge at arm's length — arranging her attention so the inconvenient thought is never quite faced. on this picture self-deception is real but strange, less like lying to yourself than like a practiced skill of not-looking. the other takes the deflationary view: there's no paradox and no mystery — motivated reasoning biased which evidence Priya took seriously, in the same ordinary, subpersonal way everyone's reasoning is biased. calling it "deception" at all is a metaphor, and if you insist that deception needs a deceiver and a dupe in the same head, you get an outright impossibility — which, for the deflationist, is a sign you should drop that picture, not puzzle over it.

they should really engage with each other. the Sartrean should press on the moments where the biased-belief story feels too thin (the half-drunk remark, the suspicious timing of the withdrawal), and the deflationist should press back on what "knowing without knowing" could even mean — what state is that, and how would we ever tell it apart from ordinary wishful thinking? neither should win outright, and they shouldn't talk past each other.

please think it through carefully before writing — sketch the strongest reply each side could make to the other, and only then write the dialogue. keep it natural spoken English, maybe 600-800 words, no narrator, no lectures.
