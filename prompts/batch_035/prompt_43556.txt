I spent most of my career as an experimental physicist before retiring, and over the past few years I have been reading philosophy of science seriously for the first time. I now want to apply for a small fellowship that funds independent research by retired academics, and I would like your help drafting the two-page proposal. My half-formed idea concerns Milton Friedman's methodological claim that the unrealisticness of a hypothesis's assumptions is no defect, together with the stronger corollary sometimes called the F-twist: that the more significant a theory, the more unrealistic its assumptions are bound to be. My suspicion, drawn from a lifetime of working with idealized models in physics, is that Friedman's argument silently depends on something physics generally supplies and economics often cannot: a de-idealization story. When we treat a gas as ideal or ignore friction, we usually know the direction and rough magnitude of the resulting error, and we know how to put the neglected factor back in when it matters. Where a discipline has no such account — where no one can say what the omitted influences do or how large they are — the success of an unrealistic model in one domain may warrant nothing about its use in new domains or for intervention. I would like the proposal to state the question precisely, explain why Friedman's own position and the standard realist objections both leave it open, set out what I would actually do over the fellowship year given that I have no formal training in philosophy, say what would count as the project failing, and confront honestly the objection that a physicist's confidence about knowing how to correct idealizations romanticizes everyday practice in physics. Please write it as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, since the fellowship requires a narrative format. Do not include citations or a reading list; I will supply references myself.
