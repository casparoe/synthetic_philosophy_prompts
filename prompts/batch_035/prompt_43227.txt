Two members of my ethics seminar have spent a year arguing past each other about whether a larger population is, other things equal, better. One is a totalist: a world containing more people with lives worth living is better, ceteris paribus, and public policy should weight that fact. The other holds a person-affecting view: an outcome can be better or worse only by being better or worse for particular people, so creating additional people with good lives — people who would not otherwise have existed — does not by itself make an outcome better; as she likes to put it, the point is making people happy, not making happy people. Please draft the joint statement they should have produced from a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Structure it as follows:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. These should cover: what Parfit's Repugnant Conclusion and his Nonidentity Problem each actually show (and what they do not); the empirical preconditions any defensible population policy needs, such as how added people affect per-capita welfare through crowding, innovation, and dilution of fixed resources; and any parts of their year-long dispute that turn out to be verbal — in particular, whether "better than," as each uses it in population comparisons, is an impersonal or a person-relative relation — with an explanation of why that part is verbal rather than substantive.

2. A disagreements table: each remaining disagreement stated so that its own side would accept the wording, together with the crux behind it — the subquestion that, if settled, would move at least one of them.

3. For each crux, a concrete case both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance — including what each predicts the other will say about the case. At least two cases should be newly constructed by the collaboration along these lines: (a) a government with a fixed budget choosing between pronatal subsidies expected to lead to roughly a million additional births of lives comfortably worth living, to parents who want more children but face financial obstacles, and a life-extension research program expected to give already-existing people perhaps fifteen additional healthy years; and (b) a variant in which the additional lives, though worth living, are only barely above neutrality. The two should also record where they expect the other's intuitions to flip, and what that would reveal about the crux.
