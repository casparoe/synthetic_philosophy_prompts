Write a satirical advice column of about 650 words for a fictional Copenhagen weekly of the mid-1840s, titled along the lines of "Ask the Public." Every letter is answered, in a deadpan editorial voice, by "the Public" itself — the signature being no name at all, since the Public is not a person but an abstraction, an aggregate of readers only in their moments of leisure.

The column should consist of four or five letters, each a few sentences, each followed by the Public's reply. The target of the satire is the phenomenon Kierkegaard diagnosed in Two Ages (1846): leveling, passionlessness, reflection offered as a substitute for passion, envy operating as a negative principle, and the press serving as the public's creature. The humor must come from getting this diagnosis right rather than inventing features of it, so observe the following constraints on the Public's voice throughout:

- The Public never addresses a correspondent as an individual, only as an instance of an interchangeable class; each reply begins by dissolving whatever is particular in the letter-writer's situation.
- The Public never commits itself to anything for which it could be held responsible. Its advice always takes the form of further reflection, prudent deferral, and the cultivation of sensible opinions held at a comfortable distance.
- Passion and commitment are treated as embarrassments — in poor taste, out of season. The era applauds instead the clever remark, the knowing irony, and the collective mockery that administers in print what stoning once administered in person.
- The Public refers to itself in the third person as if it were a great beast with no members, and speaks of the paper as its humble instrument.

Suggested correspondents, though you may invent others: a young man asking whether he should devote himself to a cause; a writer wondering whether to attach his name to an essay or publish it anonymously; a reader who feels he is being laughed at by a satirical paper and asks whether he ought to mind; and, as the final letter, a persistent lone critic — sign him with a single initial — who charges the column with consuming the very individuals who write to it. The Public's reply to him should be its most serene: it congratulates him on his individuality, observes that envy is the only passion the age still permits, and reflects that every age gets the martyrs it deserves, this one preferring to stone its prophets with laughter rather than rocks.

The irony must remain legible without winking: no authorial asides, no acknowledgment of the source of the critique, and never the label "leveling" or the name Kierkegaard — the piece should show the diagnosis, not cite it. A reader who knows The Present Age should recognize that the author understands it, and find the portrait fair. Begin your response with the column itself — title, letters, and replies in order — with no preface, introduction, or commentary of any kind.
