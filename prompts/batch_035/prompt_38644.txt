My brother is a military lawyer (JAG) and after a long argument about rules of engagement I came away confused about what the rules are actually assuming about morality. Here's the setup as he explained it — I might be getting details slightly wrong but I think I have the shape of it. In the law of war you can bomb an ammo depot even when you know some nearby civilians will die, as long as the expected civilian deaths aren't excessive relative to the military advantage. The deaths are foreseen but not intended, and that's supposedly what makes them permissible at all. Meanwhile you obviously can't just shoot those same civilians to get the same military advantage, even if shooting them directly would mean fewer deaths overall. And the weirdest part: you also can't use civilians as a means even when it saves lives — like making locals walk in front of a convoy to deter an ambush. Nobody dies, everyone is better off than under the alternative, and it's still one of the most serious violations there is. So the number of deaths clearly matters *inside* the foreseen-side-effect bucket (that's what proportionality is doing), but the intended/foreseen line seems to trump numbers entirely, and it trumps them in both directions.

So my question: what does this whole practice presuppose about ethics? Is it basically the doctrine of double effect written into law, and if so which version — the one where what matters is what you're aiming at, or the one where what matters is whether the harm is part of your plan versus a side effect you'd gladly prevent if you could? Or can something else explain it entirely, like a rights view that doesn't care about intentions at all, where civilians have a right not to be *used* that's somehow more stringent than their right not to be foreseeably killed? That seems bizarre to me but maybe it's defensible. Also, is the practice even internally consistent — I can't tell whether proportionality (which counts up expected deaths) and the intention distinction (which refuses to count across the line) are quietly fighting each other or actually fit together as one view. And finally, if a military scrapped the intended/foreseen distinction and just did a straight expected-consequences assessment of each proposed strike, what would concretely change in how targeting decisions get made?
