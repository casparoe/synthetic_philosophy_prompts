I'm on the steering committee of my 13-person housing co-op, and we finally have to settle on an actual voting method for our big quarterly decisions, which has forced me to figure out what I actually believe about voting instead of just gesturing at "democracy." So here are my gut verdicts on some cases from our meetings — tell me what principle, if any, captures them.

(1) If literally all 13 of us prefer A to B, the co-op has to prefer A to B — a method that gets that wrong isn't even a voting method.

(2) If A beats B seven-to-six in a straight head-to-head vote, I feel like the co-op prefers A to B, period — that's just what the group's preference is, not a weird artifact.

(3) But we had a three-way fight about the garden where A beat B 7-6, B beat C 8-5, and C beat A 9-4, and my gut says that's fine: the group just doesn't have a preference there, and picking by some tiebreak isn't unfair, it's necessery bookkeeping.

(4) Under plain plurality, our contractor vote went 5 for A, 4 for B, 3 for C — and it turned out the C people all liked B more than A. A won anyway, and I actually think that's fair, not a bug: everyone got one equal vote, nobody was cheated.

(5) But then someone suggested Borda (rank all three, points by rank), and I noticed that if just two people flip their C-vs-A rankings and nothing else changes, the winner between A and B can flip too. That strikes me as sneaky somehow — though I can't say why, since case 4 never bothered me.

So: is there a principle that gets all five, or close to it? If not, which verdict has to go — the plurality one or the head-to-head one? And whatever principle you land on, tell me what it says about instant-runoff, which the most persistent committee member keeps proposing. I have a feeling it's going to upset me.
