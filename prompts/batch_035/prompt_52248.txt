I'm TA-ing a mid-level well-being/value theory course and could use some help grading a stack of papers on Nozick's experience machine. The assignment question was: Nozick's experience machine argument is supposed to refute hedonism about well-being. Explain the argument, and then assess how much damage the psychological literature on status quo bias (plus related debunking arguments in that spirit) really does to it. Can the hedonist dismiss the intuition that we'd refuse the machine, or is something left over?

One student's paper is giving me pause because it's mostly quite good but I have a nagging feeling about a couple of moves, and grading at this hour I can't fully trust my own read. The paper gets the visible stuff right: it lays out the setup correctly (the machine gives you any experiences you want, indistinguishable from real ones), runs the modus tollens properly (roughly: if only pleasurable experience mattered, we'd have no reason not to plug in; we do have reason not to; so something besides pleasurable experience matters), and it correctly lists Nozick's own three reasons for refusing — wanting to actually do things rather than just have the experience of doing them, wanting to be a certain kind of person rather than an indeterminate blob, and wanting contact with a deeper reality rather than a man-made simulation. So far so good.

Then for the debunking part, the student first brings in Kagan's point (without naming it, but the idea is there): our refusal may just track how we feel about the unfamiliar option, not its value — and the de Brigard experiment is supposed to show this, since when people are told their real life outside the machine is a miserable one, most suddenly want to stay plugged in. The student cites the numbers more or less right (only about 13% would disconnect if their real life is a maximum-security prison, versus a bit over half when told nothing, and around half for the Monaco millionaire case). But here's where I start doubting myself. Two things in the paper's final section feel off to me. First, the student writes as if the debater's side is only the psychological one — as if once you show the refusal intuition is status-quo-biased, hedonism follows, i.e. the only live alternatives are hedonism and believing the machine intuition at face value. That seems too fast: showing the intuition is biased undercuts one route to anti-hedonism, but it doesn't by itself make the positive case for hedonism, and more importantly the student never considers whether the three Nozickian considerations could be defended independently of the raw intuition — e.g. as claims about what makes a life good that don't depend on surveying our gut reactions at all. Second, and this one I can't quite pin down: near the end the student asserts that the de Brigard result shows the machine intuitions are unreliable because people's answers track facts about their own life rather than facts about well-being, and on that basis concludes the intuition gets no evidential weight whatsoever. Something about the move from biased to zero-weight strikes me as a conflation, but I can't articulate cleanly why, and I don't want to dock points for it if it's actually fine.

So: please grade this paper. Give me a letter grade, and more importantly a point-by-point breakdown of what the student got right and exactly what went wrong in those last-section moves — whether my two worries are real errors, whether there are any other subtle ones I've missed, and what comments I should write to justify the grade. Please be concrete enough that I could lift your comments straight onto the paper.
