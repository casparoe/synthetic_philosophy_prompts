I've been reading LessWrong and adjacent blogs for over a decade, and one argument that never dies there — usually in cryonics threads, occasionally in aging-research threads where someone suggests, half-seriously, that fear of death is the real problem — is the ancient Epicurean one: death is nothing to us, because when death is present we are not, and the person you need to be harmed never coexists with the harm. The academic literature has been circling the same drain since Nagel and Silverstein: the deprivationalist says death is bad by depriving you of goods you would otherwise have had; the Epicurean replies that a deprivation with no subject is not a harm; the deprivationalist invokes the goods of the counterfactual continued life; the Epicurean comes back with Lucretius's mirror — we don't regret the eternity before our birth, so why the eternity after; the deprivationalist concedes the mirror isn't perfectly symmetric and then struggles to say why without looking ad hoc; and every few years someone like Silverstein or Feldman adds a twist — that the subject exists after death, or that bad things can happen to people without being bad *for* anyone — and the whole thing just resets. My sense, watching both the academic literature and the forum threads, is that nobody on either side has changed their mind since antiquity, and that the empirical facts were never in dispute.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis — is that it isn't about death at all: it's about whether 'bad for' requires a subject standing in some relation to the bad thing, so that what looks like a first-order fight about mortality is really a standoff between a relational logic of harm and a comparative or impersonal logic of welfare. But I can think of rival diagnoses: that it's verbal, a fight over the word 'harm' that would dissolve under sufficiently careful definitions; that it's a downstream symptom of unresolved questions about personal identity (once you drop a simple enduring self, it's less obvious whose goods the counterfactual life contains); or that each side is answering a different question under one heading, with the Epicurean answering something like 'will it hurt?' and the deprivationalist answering something like 'how well did the life go?'. Evaluate my hypothesis against the rivals, using what the participants actually say, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a formal account of welfare, a conceptual distinction, a reframing that makes the question decision-relevant (does anything here bear on whether signing up for cryonics is rational, or is it orthogonal even there?) — or argue that no progress should be expected and why. I know all the standard moves, so don't restate them at length; spend your words on the structure of the dispute. Hard limit: 600 words.
