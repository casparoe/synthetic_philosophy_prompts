Here's one that's been knocking around my head. I want the Lucas Aerospace workers' plan of the mid-70s analysed through Marx on alienation, and I want it done honestly in both directions — what alienation theory illuminates about the episode, and what the episode does back to the theory.

So we're on the same page, the facts as I understand them (don't hang anything on exact details, and tell me if I've got something wrong): Lucas Aerospace was a big UK defence contractor; by the early 70s defence contracts were shrinking and management was threatening mass redundancies. Instead of just fighting the cuts job-by-job, the shop stewards' combine — an cross-plant, multi-skill committee — put together what got called the Lucas Plan (published 1976): a proposal that the workforce, using skills and equipment already in the plants, switch to producing socially useful things. Their list of candidate products ran to well over a hundred items — heat pumps, kidney dialysis machines, a hybrid power pack for vehicles, that sort of thing. Management thanked them and rejected it, and the plan became a lasting touchstone for people who argue workers could plan production better than bosses.

Now the analysis I want. Map the plan carefully onto the components of alienation. Which of the four does the plan actually target — alienation from the product (they're proposing different, socially needed products), from the activity of labouring (unchanged, still wage labour inside Lucas), from species-being (workers planning production according to social need — arguably the heart of it), and from other workers (the combine itself, across trades and plants, as an antidote to competitive segmentation)? Be disciplined about the mapping: say which features of the plan go to which component, and where you're stretching.

Then the part I actually care about. Marx's account roots alienation in the sale of labour-power as a commodity — the wage relation itself, not the content of what gets made. The Lucas Plan leaves that relation fully intact: same employer, same wage packet, different products. So on a strict reading the plan can't touch alienation at all, and the workers were proposing a palliative. But the workers themselves clearly experienced the planning exercise — the research, the surveys of social need, the design work — as the opposite of alienated labour. So does the episode show that alienation from the product and alienation from the wage relation can come apart, and that Marx (or at least the standard reading) under-weights the first? Or does it show something else: that planning as an activity is doing the anti-alienation work, regardless of whether it's ever implemented? Notice the plan was never produced at scale — so if it was experienced as unalienated labour, the unalienation happened entirely within a capitalist firm's planning department, in effect. Sit with that.

Also bring in exploitation, and tell me whether the two theories come apart here in an instructive way. As far as surplus value goes, nothing changes: whatever Lucas sells, the workers produce more value than they're paid, and swapping missiles for heat pumps doesn't alter the rate of exploitation one bit. So exploitation theory is indifferent to the plan while alienation theory (on some readings) is moved by it. Is that a clean demonstration that they're independent claims, or does the structural reading of alienation predict the indifference and come out ahead?

And I want an honest section on where the whole exercise breaks. Obvious confound to address: the plan was devised under threat of the sack, by workers desperate to show their skills had use-values beyond defence work — is that self-realisation or a bargaining chip, and does the theory have the resources to tell the difference? Also the structural reading predicts management would reject it, and management did — but is that really a confirmation, or would almost any capitalist firm reject a plan that shrinks its market, whatever the theory says?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble and no restating my question back at me, and no reading lists or citations — argue it from the theory, not from the literature on the plan.
