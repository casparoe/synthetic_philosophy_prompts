Been reading a lot of pop-history about ancient collapses lately (Jared Diamond type stuff) and I've noticed my gut has opinions about which episodes "count" as collapses and which are just societies changing. I want to know if there's a principle behind my verdicts. Here they are:

- Roman Britain in the 400s: coins, villas, towns, pottery all gone within a couple generations. Collapse, obviously.
- The late Bronze Age around 1200 BC: half the eastern Mediterranean's cities burned or emptied, long-distance trade died, Greece forgot how to write. Collapse — though Egypt limped through it, so maybe only partial?
- The classic Maya around 800–900 AD: the big southern cities emptied and monument-building stopped. I say collapse, but it took a couple of centuries, the northern cities kept going, and there are millions of Maya alive today, so this one feels wobbly.
- Easter Island: collapse. No more big trees, no more statues, big population crash. Even though the society itself never actually disappeared.
- The USSR in 1991: not a collapse, to me. A superpower, an empire, and a whole official ideology evaporated within about two years, and I still want to call it a change.
- Handloom weavers after the industrial revolution: their entire way of life ended within a lifetime, but nobody would call that a collapse, it's just change. And the amount of stuff lost was atleast as big as in Roman Britain, so these two don't sit well together.

My rough attempt at a principle: a collapse is a big loss of specialized complexity (writing, trade, big buildings, division of labor) that happens fast compared to a human lifetime and that nobody chose. Trouble is the Maya case is way too slow and I still call it collapse, and the USSR looks fast and pretty unchosen and I don't call it one. Maybe speed is the wrong axis — maybe it's whether daily life for ordinary people keeps functioning (food, markets, safety)? That fits USSR vs. Roman Britain, but then Easter Island should be borderline too since people carried on after the crash, and I'm definately firm on that one.

Can you sort out what's going on in my head? Take the role of someone who thinks about the philosophy of history for a living, brainstorm a bit before answering, and then give me one or two candidate principles stated precisely, telling me which of my cases motivates each part of them. Be honest about which of my verdicts the best principle just can't save. And tell me what the principle predicts for the case I keep flip-flopping on: the Norse in Greenland — by roughly 1450 the whole settlement was simply gone, every last person dead or sailed away. Is a society "collapsing" when it outright ends, or does collapse require survivors whose life gets worse? One last thing: where you touch on the actual archaeology or history, please mark which parts are settled and which are still genuinely fought over among experts (I gather the Easter Island story is one of those), so I don't walk away thinking a contested take is the consensus.
