I serve as the senior adjudicator for a mid-sized professional body that licenses civil and structural engineers, and part of my job is writing reasoned decisions in misconduct cases that turn on whether a licensee deliberately or knowingly disregarded a risk to public safety. Those adverbs matter enormously in our scheme: a finding of 'deliberate disregard' allows suspension or striking-off, whilst mere negligence caps us at a reprimand and mandatory retraining. Adjudicators like me handle perhaps one such case a month, have a few weeks per case, and our decisions are appealed to a panel of lay members — accountants, retired teachers — who will not read anything that presupposes training in philosophy or psychology, so whatever guidance we adopt has to be runnable by a busy professional and explicable in plain language.

Last weekend I had a long and unresolved argument with an old friend who teaches cognitive science. He walked me through a finding from experimental philosophy that I have not been able to shake off since: when ordinary people are told about a chairman who adopts a profitable programme whilst openly saying he does not care that it will harm the environment, most of them say he knowingly harmed it; but when the identical story is told with the programme helping the environment, most say he did not knowingly help it — the very same mental state, opposite verdicts, purely because of the valence of the outcome. My friend's view is that this means our adjudicators' findings of deliberateness are systematically contaminated by whether things went badly, and that we should build in a symmetrising correction — say, requiring extra justification whenever harm occurred. My counter was that the asymmetry may be tracking something real rather than corrupting the judgement: perhaps calling something 'deliberate' partly registers that the agent violated a norm, so an agent who accepts a harm for profit really is more culpable than one who accepts a lucky benefit, and flattening the asymmetry would license the lucky recklessness of engineers whose gambles happened not to kill anyone.

I would like you to settle this in the form of something we can actually use: a written decision procedure that our adjudicators can follow when a case turns on 'deliberately' or 'knowingly', with the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a finding either way, and where it must explicitly hand the question to human judgement because it is a values question rather than a factual one. The procedure has to take a stance on our disagreement — whether to correct for outcome-asymmetry, treat it as signal, or split the difference — and defend that stance. Please then run it on three cases: a licensee who approved foundations he had reason to believe were under-tested but did not check, because the delay would have cost his firm a contract; a licensee who knowingly used a cheaper design that was adequate-but-marginal, was open about the trade-off at the time, and where nothing failed; and a licensee whose site agent falsified inspection records, with the licensee claiming he wanted compliance and simply never looked. One of these should come out in a way I will find uncomfortable, and I would rather know that now than on appeal. Please also say plainly what the procedure cannot do — where the psychology leaves off and our institutional values have to carry the decision.
