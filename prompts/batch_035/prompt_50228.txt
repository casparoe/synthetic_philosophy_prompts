Quick bit of context on me: I write about science and the environment for a regional magazine, and somehow that made me the "communications person" the county mosquito control district wanted for its citizen advisory board (long story, it's a stipend of $200 and a lot of Tuesday evening meetings). Next month we vote on whether to renew the district's adulticide program — the summertime truck fogging with permethrin — and I've been asked to circulate a written recommendation beforehand that the other board members can actually respond to. The situation: after two wet summers and a West Nile season that hospitalized a dozen people, current policy is to spray whole neighborhoods whenever trap counts cross a threshold and the virus shows up in the sentinel chickens. But a residents' group, backed by a retired entomologist, is petitioning to pause the program, and their argument is more interesting than the usual chemicals-are-scary stuff. Their letter says the fog doesn't discriminate — it kills moths, fireflies, and whatever pollinators are out at dusk — and that if there's even a modest probability insects can suffer, running a fog that kills billions of them across the county every summer amounts to a moral cost the district has literally never put on its balance sheet.

The alternatives on the table are (a) going all-in on larvicide — the Bti dunks dropped in standing water, which kill mosquito and midge larvae before their nervous systems are much of anything — plus aggressive source reduction, or (b) keeping the fogging but raising the trigger, so we spray only when actual human cases appear rather than at the first positive chicken. I'll admit I find the residents' expected-value-style argument harder to dismiss than my board colleagues do, but I also don't want to be the person whose memo gets someone's grandmother sick because the moths were more photogenic. And the precaution cuts both ways, right — waiting for human cases means waiting until people are already infected, which is exactly when spraying does the least good.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate. It needs a concrete recommendation with an actual decision rule (triggers, thresholds, timing — something the board could vote on and the district could implement), and it needs to engage the sentience argument seriously rather than just asserting that bugs are little automatons — but also without treating a contested empirical question about insect suffering as settled. Deal honestly with the strongest point on whichever side loses. Length-wise, something the board members will actually read before a 7pm meeting, so maybe 800-1000 words.
