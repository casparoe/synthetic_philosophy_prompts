A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about the Hart–Fuller debate, and the argument ended not in agreement but in a shared confusion I would like to have untangled. Here is where we got stuck.

My friend maintained that Hart's discussion of what any legal system must contain if it is to serve its purposes — the argument that rests on general facts like human vulnerability, limited resources, and limited altruism — shows that even a committed positivist cannot avoid invoking something resembling natural law, and that Hart's insistence on the separation of law and morality is therefore less stable than it looks. I replied that Fuller's complaint against Hart concerns something quite different: not the substantive content of legal rules but the manner in which law is made and administered, so that Fuller's desiderata for legality describe an internal morality of form rather than any constraint on what the law may substantively require. At that point we realized we had lost track of whether we were disagreeing about which of the two thinkers made the deeper concession to natural law theory, or about whether these two concessions are even the same kind of concession — the one substantive and teleological, the other procedural and formal.

Please explain the conceptual difference between these two ideas: first, the claim that law necessarily has a minimum content resembling natural law, given certain abiding facts about the human situation; and second, the claim that lawmaking is answerable to an internal morality of its own, consisting of formal and procedural conditions on how rules are generated, publicized, and applied. Specifically, I would like to know: (a) whether both claims should be counted as concessions to natural law theory, or only one, or neither; (b) whether a traditional natural law theorist of the Thomist stripe would recognize either as capturing what natural law theory actually asserts, or would instead regard both as changing the subject; and (c) whether it is mere accident of vocabulary that both ideas get described in natural-law terms, or whether there is a genuine unifying thought behind them — perhaps something about the idea of a purposive enterprise whose purposes constrain what counts as functioning within it, and about how far that analogy between law and other practices can be pushed before it breaks down. I am not looking for a summary of the debate as a whole or for pointers to the literature; what I want is the underlying conceptual anatomy of the two claims and of the relationship between them.
