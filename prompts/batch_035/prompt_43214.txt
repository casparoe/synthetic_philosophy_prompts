My flatmate and I have been arguing for months about whether "better than" is transitive, and I want to actually referee it properly instead of trading gut reactions. My starting point was that of course it's transitive: if A is better than B and B is better than C, then A is better than C — that seemed about as negotiable as logic. She keeps coming back with spectrum arguments. Her favorite is a chain of headaches starting with a barely-there headache lasting an hour and ending with years of severe migraine, where each step along the chain differs from the previous one only trivially (duration nudges up by a minute, or intensity rises by an amount you couldn't even notice while the rest stays fixed). If trivial differences can't flip better-than, and better-than is transitive, you get that the last item is no worse than the first. She'd rather drop transitivity than accept that, and honestly I don't know what to say back, especially once she brought up Quinn's self-torturer guy who ratchets up electric shocks in imperceptible increments for cash and ends up voluntarily in agony.

So before I go read Temkin or Chang or anyone else, I want the spec: write the desiderata a theory of the better-than relation has to meet to count as adequate. Include the cases it must get right (spectrum chains like hers, but also hard choices between incommensurable options, cross-dimensional comparisons like more years of mediocre life vs fewer of a great one), the principles it must either vindicate or explain away (transitivity itself, the no-small-difference intuition, the thought that a chain of worsenings can't land you no worse than the start, Chang's parity as a possible fourth relation alongside better/worse/equal, Temkin's idea that betterness might be "essentially comparative" — depending what you're comparing an option to), and the constraints it can't violate, especially whatever the connection to rational choice is supposed to be: if A is better than B, can you still rationally pick B? For each desideratum say what motivates it, and mark whether it's genuinely non-negotiable or just a camp prejudice that transitivity-defenders or spectrum-people tend to smuggle in as obvious.

The part I care about most: tell me which subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable if the spectrum cases work — i.e. if something has to give, what's the cheapest thing to give up, and what does each candidate sacrifice cost? Also, along the way, flag where you think her argument might be smuggling something in — e.g. whether "an imperceptible difference in betterness" is even coherent, or whether she's sliding between my not being able to *tell* the difference and there being no difference. But do that as part of the desiderata discussion, not as a separate verdict on who wins. I suspect the requirements list itself is where the real action is, so keep the weight there and don't spend more than a paragraph or two scoring the existing contenders against the list. a table is fine if it helps, just don't let it do the thinking for you.
