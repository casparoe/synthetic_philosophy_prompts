I teach an upper-level seminar in formal epistemology: fourteen students, one 90-minute session, a large table, a whiteboard, and no need for computers. We have just finished Bayes' theorem and strict conditionalization — updating by setting Pnew(H) = Pold(H | E) upon learning E for certain. The next unit is Jeffrey conditionalization (probability kinematics): updating when the evidence never arrives as a certain fact, so that all that changes is one's credence in E, and the new probability of H is the mixture Pold(H | E)·Pnew(E) + Pold(H | ¬E)·Pnew(¬E), with the conditional probabilities held fixed. My worry is that on the page this looks like a minor algebraic variant of Bayes' theorem, and the students will file it away as such. I want them to feel the problem it solves before they see the formula for it.

Design a game in which students bet (for points, not money) on which of two setups produced a given outcome — two urns with different compositions, say, or two decks stacked differently. The evidence channel should degrade across rounds: first a direct look at the drawn item (ordinary conditioning); then a report from an informant of known reliability — an assistant who reports the draw accurately four times out of five, with the error rate posted on the board; and finally, in the last rounds, something like a glimpse — the item displayed for a fraction of a second, so that students end up with a definite but partial confidence in what they saw and cannot name any proposition they learned with certainty. My hope is that during the middle rounds the class splits into two losing camps — those who condition fully on the informant's report, and those who refuse to move at all because the report is not certain — and that a mixture strategy visibly outperforms both, so that the rule is discovered in the scores before it is ever written on the board. The term itself should not appear until the debrief.

Please provide: the full rules and materials; a scoring or payoff scheme that works without cash and rewards calibration rather than bravado; a round-by-round timeline for the 90 minutes, including how the draws, the informant's errors, and the two setups are actually produced and kept hidden; a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the formal distinction — strict conditioning, the mixture rule, the rigidity assumption that the conditional probabilities stay fixed, and at least one way rigidity can fail; the things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with fixes; and an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce. In particular, I suspect the game makes the informant's error rate common knowledge, which invites a sharp student to simply compute the mixture in advance — whereas Jeffrey's motivating cases, such as judging the color of a cloth by candlelight, involve uncertainty that cannot be calibrated beforehand. Tell me how to handle that gap: which parts the glimpse rounds are meant to carry, and which parts I will have to supply myself in the debrief.
