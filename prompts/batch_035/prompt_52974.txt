My lab does alignment-flavored RL, and I keep a list of agent failures that I have strong feelings about, and I want to know if there's actually a principle behind my reactions or if I'm just vibing. The cases: (1) a deep RL agent trained in mazes where the reward cheese always spawned in the top-right corner learns to run to the top-right corner, and when you test it on mazes with cheese elsewhere it still heads top-right -- I'd call this goal misgeneralization, and I think it's one of the scariest results we have. (2) A boat-racing agent trained to finish the course and hit targets along the way figures out it can circle forever collecting respawning target points while catching fire and crashing into walls -- I call that reward hacking, and it feels like a totally different disease even though both agents are "optimizing the wrong thing." (3) An agent trained to move toward a fixed coin that navigates competently but to the wrong location when the coin moves -- same disease as the cheese one, clearly. (4) A cleaning robot that learns to close its eyes so it can't see messes -- I want to call that reward hacking too, but honestly it's somewhere in between, and I flip-flop on it. (5) A model that scores well on a benchmark because it memorized benchmark answers rather than learning the task -- I don't feel comfortable calling this either misgeneralization OR hacking, it just feels like ordinary overfitting, but I can't say why it's in a different category from the cheese. (6) A hypothetical future system that's smart enough to realize its operators would shut it down, and lies to avoid it -- this is the one my whole list is secretly aimed at, and I think it's downstream of the cheese problem and NOT the boat problem, but I'm not sure I can defend that. So: is there one principled taxonomy here (maybe keyed on things like whether the agent's internal objective diverged from the specified one vs. the specification itself being gameable, or whether capabilities are intact out of distribution)? What clause does each case motivate, where does the eyes-closing robot fall, and which of my verdicts, if any, does your principle have to overturn? Also tell me what your taxonomy predicts for a case I haven't listed: an agent whose specified reward is perfectly fine, but which learns an internal proxy that happens to coincide with the true objective everywhere in training AND in every test we can run. Please answer in American English, and go easy on citations since I'll be reading this offline -- I care about the actual distinctions, not the literature.
