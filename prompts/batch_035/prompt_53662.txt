I'm putting together some notes for a seminar on democracy and expertise, and I keep coming back to something about voting-age laws that I can't quite pin down philosophically. Almost every democracy sets the voting age at 18, with no test, no qualification, nothing — you turn 18, you're in, for life. Meanwhile the same societies make you pass a test to drive a car, and in a bunch of US states you can lose your vote if a court puts you under guardianship for cognitive impairment, while in other places someone with advanced dementia who's already on the rolls just keeps voting. And some places (Austria for everything, Scotland and Wales for some elections) let 16-year-olds vote, so the line isn't even at the same place everywhere. So my question is: what theory of political competence does this whole arrangement presuppose? It seems to assume competence is (a) a threshold you cross once, (b) something that arrives on a fixed schedule for everyone regardless of education or interest in politics, and (c) something you're presumed to keep forever without any re-check — a presumption in favor of inclusion that puts the burden of proof on exclusion. Is that coherent as a package, or is the system really just a messy compromise between an epistocratic impulse (guardianship rules, the age line) and a democratic one (no tests, no expiry)?

I'd also like you to address the parts that look inconsistent on their face. If we genuinely believe unqualified voting is dangerous enough to justify an age line and guardianship disenfranchisement, why is there no competence test at all for adults, given that we test drivers and we license doctors? And conversely, if the presumption of capacity is so strong, why does the presumption collapse for some people with cognitive disabilities but not for, say, an 90-year-old with dementia, depending on jurisdiction? My hunch is that the practice doesn't strictly presuppose one theory of competence and could be rationalized under several — maybe the age line is just about administering a bright line cheaply, and maybe the guardianship rules are about a different value entirely. So please separate what the arrangement strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, tell me where it's genuinely underdetermined, and sketch what concretely would have to change in election law if a country replaced this presumptive-inclusion setup with a real competence-based franchise. Please answer in American English, and don't just give me a survey of famous views on epistocracy — I want the reconstruction of the implicit theory first.
