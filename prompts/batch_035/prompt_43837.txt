I need some help refereeing an argument that broke out at my flat last weekend between two friends of mine, and I'm afraid I don't know enough about the underlying issues to call it myself. Background: we'd been watching a documentary about factory farming, and the question came up of where a modest monthly donation should go. Dan has been giving to chicken welfare charities for a while. Maya thinks the farmed-animal stuff matters but is sceptical of a lot of the movement's grander claims. Neither of them is a fanatic; both had genuinely decent points, which is why I couldn't pick a winner as it happened.

The argument went roughly like this. (1) Dan said that even if you put the probability of insect sentience at something tiny like one per cent, the arithmetic still comes out crushing everything else: insect farming for feed, mainly black soldier fly larvae, involves trillions of individuals a year, so at one per cent credence you still get tens of billions of "sentient-equivalents," which dwarfs what the same money could do for chickens, where he thinks the probability is near one but the numbers are thousands of times smaller. That's why he'd been quietly moving his donations over. (2) Maya said this is exactly the reasoning pattern that produces Pascal's mugging: take a number you're not really confident in, multiply by an astronomical number, and out comes a verdict that outruns all your evidence. A one per cent credence in sentience isn't a measurement like a one per cent chance of rain; it's a confession that you don't know what consciousness even is, and expected value arithmetic treats it as if it were a precise quantity. (3) Dan answered that refusing to multiply is just protecting the status quo: if the numbers are real then the multiplication is real, and she does the same move herself elsewhere — she gives to pandemic-prevention causes where the probability of the bad event in any given year is likewise a rough guess, and nobody calls that mugging. (4) Maya said the analogy fails, because there the uncertainty is about the world (will a pathogen escape?), whereas here the uncertainty is about which moral framework is true, and it isn't obvious that moral uncertainty behaves like empirical uncertainty at all — you don't "discover" that insects matter in the way you discover a comet. (5) Dan's last word was that if moral uncertainty can't guide action then she needs to explain how she ever acts under moral disagreement at all, and that her veganism is precaution over a small probability that animals' suffering counts, which is the same structure he's using, just with smaller numbers attached. Maya said being vegan is a cheap precaution you can take without doing any expected-value arithmetic, and that his version "proves too much" — run it consistently and it eventually tells you the best cause is always whichever one involves the most individual organisms, whatever they are, which she suspects is a reductio rather than a result.

They eventually agreed to drop it for the evening, and I honestly couldn't say who came out ahead. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — rate each of the five steps from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification, say who is more right overall, and tell me if there's something important both of them are missing. Please write your answer in British English.
