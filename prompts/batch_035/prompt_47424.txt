I've been reading rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and Hempel's raven paradox gets re-litigated there every couple of years. The standard community-approved resolution is stated in one breath: a white shoe does confirm that all ravens are black, just negligibly, because non-ravens vastly outnumber ravens — so the paradox is an artifact of ignoring effect sizes. I've come to suspect that this reflexive answer runs together two genuinely different resolutions, and I'd like a careful comparison of them.

Resolution A (the quantitative discount): Nicod's criterion and the equivalence condition are both perfectly correct; a randomly selected non-black non-raven genuinely confirms the hypothesis that all ravens are black. The air of paradox comes only from conflating qualitative confirmation with its degree: given the actual ratio of non-ravens to ravens, the evidential weight is minuscule, and intuition is wrongly reporting "negligible" as "none." The lesson is about watching your likelihood ratios, not about revising the logic of confirmation.

Resolution B (the screening-off dissolution): the intuition is instead pointing at something structurally wrong with Nicod's criterion when treated as a universal constraint. In any realistic setting, we already knew the object was a non-raven before examining its color — it came from the drawer, the shoe rack, a random object in the universe — and evidence we've already conditioned on screens off the observation. On this view, deriving that white shoes are confirmatory requires an assumption about the sampling scheme that is almost never satisfied, and the paradox is a genuine counterexample to the naive package of contraposition plus Nicod.

Please compare these two as answers to the question of what exactly goes wrong in the raven paradox. In particular:

1. Do they even disagree, or do they merely distribute the same correction differently between "the logic is fine, the psychology is off" and "the psychology is fine, the logic is off"?
2. Which better dissolves the paradox for a committed Bayesian, and why? Consider how each handles the two-step character of actual observations — you first notice what kind of thing you're looking at, then what property it has — and whether Resolution B's reliance on background knowledge makes confirmation hopelessly context-relative.
3. A twist I'd like your take on: imagine a toy world in which non-black objects are exactly as scarce as ravens, so that stumbling across a non-black thing is as informative as stumbling across a raven. In that world, does observing a white shoe confirm that all ravens are black to a non-negligible degree? If so, does this thought experiment vindicate Resolution A (the intuition just mis-measured the magnitudes in our world) or Resolution B (the intuition was tracking sampling structure all along)?
4. If you had to write the definitive LessWrong-style explainer post and could endorse only one resolution as the headline takeaway, which would it be, and what would the post's single-sentence conclusion be?
