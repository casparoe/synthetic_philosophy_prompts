I've been reading a pop-philosophy book about women philosophers whose work got sidelined, and the chapter on Émilie du Châtelet has been bugging me for a week now. It spends most of its pages on the science, which is fair enough — the Newton translation, the Institutions de physique, the vis viva fight — and then it dismisses her little treatise on happiness in one paragraph, roughly like this:

"Du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness is essentially a manual of pleasant self-deception. Her thesis is that we cannot be happy without illusions, and since illusions are just lies we tell ourselves, the art of living is choosing your lies well. She even ranks them: love and glory are 'dangerous illusions' that can wreck your life, while gambling is a 'voluntary illusion' for people who can't handle the dangerous ones. Her advice reduces to: figure out which pleasant falsehoods you can sustain, feed your passions, avoid boredom at all costs, and stop asking whether anything is true, since truth has nothing to do with happiness. In the end it's an enlightened hedonism — arrange to be pleasantly deceived — and it reads more as salon advice than as philosophy."

And then the book concludes from this that her real contribution was translating and popularizing other people's science, and that whatever she thought about happiness is minor.

Something about that summary smells off to me, but I honestly can't put my finger on where. It's not that I doubt she said we need illusions — I've seen that attributed to her elsewhere. It's more the tone, like she's been turned into a lifestyle influencer, and this from a woman who was rigorous enough to spend years on a physics dispute nobody else could settle. So I want an audit, not a defense: go through the book's paragraph and tell me which parts a scholar of the Discourse would accept, which are simplifications a defender would let pass for a popular audience, and which du Châtelet would flat out reject. In particular I'd want you to look hard at what she means by "illusions" (is it really lies we tell ourselves on purpose?), at whether she actually thinks we should stop caring about the truth, and at whether the wise person needs illusions at all or whether that's a distinction the book flattened. And then the part I care about most: does the book's conclusion — her philosophy is minor, her contribution was popularization — actually follow from the accurate parts of the summary, or only from the distorted ones? I'm inclined to say the arguement leans on the distortion, but I'd like to be shown, not just agreed with.
