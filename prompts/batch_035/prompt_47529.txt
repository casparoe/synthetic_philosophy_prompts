I write historical fiction — five novels over twenty years, most of them set in the industrial valleys of South Wales — and I have become aware that I operate by a credo I have never properly examined. Before I give a talk in the spring, I would like a consistency check on it. Here is the set of commitments, as honestly as I can reconstruct them:

1. Where a community's memory conflicts with the documentary record, the record does not automatically win. The archive is one witness among others, not the senior one; a town's remembered past has a standing claim that a historian's findings can override only for weighty reasons, not by default.

2. The novel is an organ of collective memory. A scene may be true to what an event meant while being false to what happened, and this is not a lapse but the form's whole point.

3. My primary fidelity is to the living — to survivors and descendants, and to what they carry — rather than to paper.

4. The author's note must never state a falsehood. However mythic the novel, the historical note at the back belongs to the register of fact, and I have corrected interviewers, and my own earlier notes in later reprints, when they got the record wrong.

5. There is no innocent archive. Every collection of documents is curated by somebody's interest, and the historian's claim to have established what actually happened is itself a genre with conventions, not the end of the story.

And my verdicts in three local disputes, since the principles alone will not expose the trouble:

Case 1. The town of my second novel holds that its colliery disaster was caused by the owners' criminal negligence. A historian has since shown that the owners acted within the safety norms of the day, and that the blame-myth grew out of a union pamphlet written decades after the event. I publicly sided with the town: the novel keeps the owners guilty, and the note records the historian's findings alongside a statement that the novel keeps faith with how the valley remembers it.

Case 2. A community a few miles away remembers a man as an informer during the war; his grandchildren still live there and are quietly shunned on his account. A careful reconstruction of the record exonerates him. Here I sided with the record: I argued in print that the community should relinquish its story, and in the novel I exonerated him.

Case 3. A council proposed to inscribe on a war memorial a casualty figure that the historians' count does not support — the community's figure is several times the larger. I objected, and supported the smaller number on the plaque.

I cannot locate the trouble myself, though I am confident it is there. Please audit the set rather than lecture on collective memory in general: tell me which of my commitments are genuinely in tension and how badly; which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection; and, for each genuine one, what would have to give and what that would cost. I would also like to know whether my three verdicts follow from the commitments as stated, or whether I have in practice been applying some further rule that I have never put into words.
