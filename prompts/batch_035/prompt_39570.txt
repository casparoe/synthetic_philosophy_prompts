I'm a program officer at a mid-sized regional foundation. We disburse roughly forty grants a year to nonprofits, and the terms of each grant specify a small set of numbers the grantee reports quarterly — meals served, job placements at six months, students reading at grade level, shelter nights provided. Reviewing those numbers is part of my job, and over the past few years I've watched several of them go strange. A job-training grantee's placement rate kept climbing while the employers who used to call us with feedback stopped calling; a literacy program's book count tripled in the two quarters after I mentioned book counts at a gathering of our grantees, while its waiting list stayed the same length. I know the slogan version of Goodhart's law — that when a measure becomes a target it stops measuring what it used to — but the slogan doesn't tell me what to do on a Tuesday afternoon with a renewal decision due Friday. And I suspect the slogan papers over several different situations: sometimes the number is being gamed, sometimes the number never measured what we assumed, sometimes our attention itself improved the work, and sometimes the grantee has simply gotten better at counting.

Please write a decision procedure I can actually run: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure ends in a no to renewal; where it flags the metric as broken rather than the grantee as dishonest; and where it hands the decision back to me or to the board because the underlying question is whether we still care about the thing the number proxies, which is a question of values rather than facts. Two constraints to respect: I generally have one afternoon per grantee, and any change to reporting requirements must be approved by the board and announced to all grantees at once, so the procedure should say explicitly when replacing a metric is worth that cost and when quietly living with a degraded one is the better call.

Then run the procedure on three cases: the job-training placement rate described above; a food bank whose meals-served number is flat and, as far as I can tell, honest, but whose client interviews suggest demand has shifted toward a population the metric does not capture; and a homelessness grantee whose shelter-nights figure sails past our target every quarter — where I want the procedure to be able to conclude that the grantee is fine and the real problem is that we set the target too low, a conclusion I am not eager to bring to the board, since we set the target. Finally, be explicit about where the procedure will misfire: the recurring situations it was not built to handle, and the ways a determined grantee could satisfy every step of it while still hollowing out the underlying work.
