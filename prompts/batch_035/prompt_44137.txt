I have been keeping a list of my own verdicts about when it is legitimate to form aesthetic beliefs on someone else's say-so, and I would like to know whether they add up to a coherent principle. My verdicts are these. If a friend whose taste has tracked mine for years tells me that a film is excellent, I feel entitled to believe it is excellent before I have seen it. If a stranger with unknown taste tells me the same, I do not feel entitled to this. If an art historian testifies that a painting is a forgery, my aesthetic opinion of it is rightly diminished, even though the testimony is about a non-aesthetic fact. If the same trusted friend tells me that a wine is a superb example of its region, I will happily take her word for planning purposes, yet I feel I do not properly know the wine is superb until I have tasted it. If she tells me my favourite poem is sentimental rubbish, I find I simply do not update, even though I would have deferred to her about a film we had both yet to see. And if I read a well-argued essay by a critic I respect explaining that a symphony I find dull is structurally ingenious, my opinion improves somewhat, but I feel I never fully grasp the work's beauty from the essay alone. Is there a single principle about the transmission of aesthetic justification that captures all of these, or nearly all? If several candidate principles are in play, please state each precisely, note which case motivates which clause, and say what the strongest principle predicts about the case I keep going back and forth on: whether someone who is congenitally deaf can come to know, purely through the testimony of trusted friends, that a particular piece of music is sublime. Finally, be explicit about which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, and why it is that verdict, rather than the principle, that should be abandoned. Please answer in British English.
