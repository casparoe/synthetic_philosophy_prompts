I am the student representative on the advisory board of our regional history museum, and I need a decision memo for next month's exhibits committee meeting that I can circulate beforehand. The committee is divided over how to label a group of about forty small clay figurines from the late Neolithic period that will anchor our reinstalled prehistory gallery. Our senior curator wants the labels to present the figurines as evidence of a prehistoric mother-goddess religion, on the grounds that many cultures made similar figures and several nearby burials contain what look like the same type of object placed deliberately near the dead. Two other board members argue the honest label is simply "function and meaning unknown," since we have no writing from the period and no way to ask the makers. The curator replies that such labels read as intellectual surrender and that visitors come for stories, not epistemological disclaimers.

I have been trying to work out what separates the inferences we are entitled to from the ones we merely want to be true, and I think there may be a middle position: a label system that sorts claims into three tiers — what the object physically is, what we can infer about its use, and what we can only guess about its meaning — and that openly presents the competing interpretations with the specific evidence each depends on. But I am not certain this would satisfy anyone, and there is a further complication: the goddess narrative is featured in our grant application to a foundation that funds "women's heritage" programming, so the decision is not purely scholarly.

Please draft the memo the committee could act on: a clear recommendation among the three options (the goddess interpretation, the agnostic label, or my tiered-competing-hypotheses approach), the reasoning for it, a serious treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side — including the funding worry — and a concrete proposal for what the label text should actually do, phrased so that a visitor with no background in archaeology understands it.
