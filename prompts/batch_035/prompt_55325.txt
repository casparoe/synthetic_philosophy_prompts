In the late 1980s an economic historian published a now-famous argument, built around the QWERTY keyboard, that small historical accidents, amplified by network effects and switching costs, can leave an entire market permanently stuck with an inferior technology. Critics replied in the 1990s on two fronts: that the celebrated cases don't survive scrutiny (the rival keyboard's advantages had been wildly overstated), and, more fundamentally, that if a switch away from the locked-in arrangement were genuinely efficient, there would be some margin along which it would happen — contracts, buyouts, gradual migration — so 'inefficient lock-in' is either false in every real case or true only in a sense that stops being inefficient once you count the costs of escaping. Both sides wrote their replies and counter-replies, the antitrust case that gave the argument much of its political charge went away, and by the early 2000s the debate had essentially frozen, even though the underlying idea is cited constantly to this day — about VHS versus Betamax, about light-water nuclear reactors, even about why decimalized clocks and reformed calendars never caught on despite their obvious conveniences.

I'm not asking you to adjudicate who won. I want a diagnosis of why this debate stalled and what kind of disagreement it really is. Specifically:

1. Which of the following best captures the structure of the dispute, judged by what the participants actually wrote? (a) An ordinary empirical dispute about the historical record that simply lost its funding and its urgency once the policy moment passed; (b) a conceptual dispute in disguise about what 'inefficient' means when switching costs are real — whether an arrangement that no individual or coalition can profitably escape counts as inefficient, or whether that category is only defined relative to a hypothetical coordinator nobody actually has; (c) a dispute about the admissibility of counterfactuals — the proponents need the claim that the rival technology would have won under different initial conditions, and the critics say that is an unfalsifiable rescue operation; (d) a proxy fight over antitrust and industrial policy that ended when the policy question it was attached to was decided. Some combination, weighted, is a perfectly good answer, but I want reasons tied to the actual positions, not a survey.

Is there a hidden formal question doing the work — something like whether a dynamic system with increasing returns and multiple equilibria can be said to 'select' among those equilibria in a way that carries any welfare significance at all — such that the debate is really waiting on a theorem or an identification condition rather than on more archival case studies?

3. Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? A cleaner case study, a sharper definition of remediable inefficiency, a formal model with testable conditions, or should we expect this dispute only to dissolve and re-form whenever the policy question it proxies for becomes live again?

Two requests about the epistemics of your answer. First, please separate what is genuinely settled among economists who know this literature — I take it that network effects can in principle produce multiple equilibria is not in dispute, while whether any of the famous cases is a genuine instance very much is; correct me if that's wrong — from what remains contested. Second, my impression is that both sides in this fight ended up claiming the other had tacitly conceded; please don't present either side's framing as the consensus when it isn't, and say plainly which parts of your diagnosis you'd hold with confidence and which are judgment calls.
