I'm a PhD student in cognitive science, writing a dissertation on visual category learning, and my lab's reading group recently worked through a popular book on creativity and machine intelligence. Its chapter on how we recognize letters summarizes Douglas Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project more or less like this:

"Hofstadter set out to find the essence of a letter — the thing that makes every 'a' an 'a' no matter how it's drawn. His answer came in the form of a computer program, Letter Spirit, which recognizes letters by comparing each new glyph to a stored mental average: a prototype built up from all the a's you have ever seen. Recognition is then just similarity — the closer a glyph sits to the average, the more confident the identification. The program confirmed the theory by generating complete new typefaces: shown a few letters of a partial alphabet, it filled in the rest by staying close to the average shape, which is why its invented letters all looked like members of a single font. Hofstadter concluded that the old philosophical idea of essences has been vindicated: each letter has one fixed inner form, and the average simply mirrors it. A corollary is that style is a surface effect with no real role in recognition — since the average abstracts away font-specific quirks, a letter's identity is wholly independent of the typeface it happens to appear in."

A couple of things struck me as off. My own area is the prototype-versus-exemplar debate, and the description of the recognition mechanism didn't sound like anything I'd heard attributed to Hofstadter — averaging over stored instances sounds like a fairly ordinary prototype model, and I'd understood his whole project to be aimed at something more radical.
