You are a senior metaphysician with a specialization in persistence and ontology, and I'd like you to reason carefully rather than survey the literature. Please begin with a brief brainstorm of possible diagnoses before committing to one.

The debate between endurantism and perdurantism appears to me to have been completely stationary for roughly four decades. The moves are all well known. The perdurantist holds that objects persist by having temporal parts, and gets a clean solution to Lewis's problem of temporary intrinsics: the poker that is straight on Monday and bent on Tuesday is straight at one temporal part and bent at another, so nothing has incompatible properties simpliciter. The endurantist replies that this dissolves the subject of change — the very thing that was supposed to persist — and that indexed or time-relative instantiation is a cheaper fix; and she presses the counting-style worry that if I meet you on Tuesday, on her picture there is strictly no single thing I meet, only a brief temporal part of you. The perdurantist has stock answers (parthood rather than identity does the work, or the relevant cross-temporal relation is what 'same object' tracks), the endurantist has stock answers to those, and I cannot find a single case since the 1980s of a major participant changing sides. No new data could arrive; there are no experiments to wait for.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a substantive disagreement about what exists, but a disagreement over what a parsimonious ontology is *for*: the perdurantist treats a uniform, quasi-spatiotemporal account of persistence as a theoretical virtue worth paying for in the commonsense manner in which ordinary objects persist, while the endurantist refuses to pay in ontology for something she thinks our ordinary tensed talk about things already secures. But I'm not confident — perhaps a hidden substantive question about modality, or about what 'same object' semantically picks out across times, is doing the real work, or perhaps the two camps are answering different questions under one heading.

Diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says, and tell me honestly: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a new puzzle, a formal semantics, an agreed cost-benefit framework for ontological commitment — or is the impasse structural, and if so, why?
