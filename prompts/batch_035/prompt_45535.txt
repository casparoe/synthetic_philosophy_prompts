Write a short story (roughly 800–1500 words) that engages with Douglas Hofstadter's tripartite account of the location of meaning in a message: the frame message (the signal that says, in effect, "I am a message, decode me"), the outer message (the information needed to build a decoding mechanism, which must itself be guessed by the receiver), and the inner message (the actual content).

The setting: humanity's first received alien transmission. The signal's frame message is unmistakable — its statistical structure screams artifact rather than noise. But the outer message proves treacherous. Every apparent decoding key that linguists and cryptographers extract yields the same inner message regardless of the key used: a short, flat, near-empty string that seems to carry no content at all. Eventually one researcher becomes convinced that this is deliberate. The senders constructed a message whose inner content is empty because the meaning they intended resides entirely in the outer layer — in what the long process of attempted decoding forces the receiving civilization to learn about itself, its assumptions, and its blind spots.

Make this philosophical position (meaning as partly constructed by the receiver rather than contained in the message) emerge through plot and character rather than through exposition, though a character may articulate it in dialogue near the end. The story should demonstrate a real grasp of the three-layer distinction: for example, show researchers explicitly puzzling over the difference between recognizing that a signal is decodable at all and knowing what it says, and have some of them drawn into the tempting but confused view that the empty string itself must be the profound message. You are free to decide whether the researcher's interpretation is ultimately vindicated, undercut, or left ambiguous, but the ending should turn on the conceptual issue rather than on external plot mechanics.
