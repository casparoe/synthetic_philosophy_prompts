I've been reading up, as an amateur, on how biologists decide what counts as a species, and I've gathered that there are a dozen or so rival definitions in circulation — the interbreeding one, ones based on shared ancestry and branching points, ones based on appearance and DNA — and that depending on which you use, the number of species in a given group can change quite a lot, yet no definition has won. Professional biologists, who otherwise largely agree on the evidence, seem to have been circling this for a century without settling it. What I want is a diagnosis rather than a survey: is this a genuinely substantive disagreement about where the boundaries of nature lie, or a verbal one about the word 'species', or are the rival camps actually answering different questions under one name — say, one about a practical naming convention and another about whether the tree of life really has joints to carve? If it is at bottom verbal or conventional, I'd like that explained carefully, because it doesn't feel verbal to the participants; and if it is substantive, I'd like to know what fact of the matter could decide it. Please say what, if anything, would count as progress here — a conceptual distinction, a rule for picking definitions by purpose, or the admission that the term should simply be abandoned. I'd prefer the answer in British English.
