I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of fifteen years — the old Sequences, the comment threads that grew up around them, the occasional workshop in that orbit — and somewhere along the way I picked up the habit of treating introspection as a tool: noticing an ugh field, asking what it's made of, checking whether a belief is load-bearing. Which is why it bothers me that I've never seen a clean name for a pattern that keeps showing up in how that tool behaves.

The pattern, concretely, in three instances:

1. Emotional debugging. There's a standard move in that culture: when you feel diffuse resistance to some task, stop and ask what, specifically, the feeling is. When this works, you get a crisp handle — "this is fear of being blamed" — and the handle does quiet the feeling. But notice what happened: the diffuse state didn't get reported, it got replaced. Before the inspection there was an unarticulated something; after it, there is an identifiable fear with a label attached. And the part I find genuinely strange: there is no way to check whether the labeled state is the same thing as what preceded it, because every subsequent act of introspection finds only the labeled version. The first observation overwrites the original, and all later observations are of the overwrite.

2. Asymmetric visibility. Some states are destroyed by introspection — try to catch yourself at the exact moment something becomes funny, or observe your own falling-asleep, and the observation annihilates the state. Other states seem to be created by it: sincerely asking myself "am I anxious?" tends to produce anxiety about whether I'm anxious, which then shows up on the introspective readout as evidence that I was. So the same instrument is destructive of some states and constitutive of others, and it doesn't come with a label saying which.

3. Self-fulfilling diagnoses. A few years ago I sorted a recurring afternoon aversion as probably low blood sugar, on not very good evidence. It now presents as hunger — distinctly, recognizably, the way hunger presents. The classification reshapes later instances of the state so that they conform to it. An introspective guess, possibly a confabulation at the time, becomes retroactively accurate.

So what I'm after is the configuration as a whole: introspection operating as a write rather than a read, where the write is invisible to subsequent reads because the write is what there is to read.

I know some nearby concepts, but each seems to miss the specific thing. Confabulation is about reports being wrong about causes; here the interesting part is that the report becomes right by editing the state reported. Reactivity in the psychological sense is about an observer altering the observed's behavior; here there's no separate observed — the instrument is overwriting the only copy of the data. And the general skepticism about introspective accuracy — the point that we overtrust our own self-reports — presupposes that there's a fact about accuracy to be wrong about, whereas in these cases the accuracy is settled by the act of asking.

Is there an established term for this, in philosophy of mind or psychology or anywhere? If there is, I want more than the label: I want to know exactly what it covers and how it relates to the three concepts above, since each of them overlaps part of the terrain. If there isn't, I'd like you to coin a term — and, more importantly, define the phenomenon precisely enough that its edges are visible. Boundary cases I'd want the definition to settle: when I notice that a recurring thought is intrusive, am I constituting it as intrusive or just attending to it? Does the phenomenon require the state to be affective, or does it extend to noticing one's own reasoning (where the noticing arguably improves it rather than distorting it)? Is bodily interoception — hunger, breath, heartbeat — immune, and if so why? And is there a principled difference between the destructive cases in my second instance and the constitutive ones, or is that a single mechanism showing two faces?
