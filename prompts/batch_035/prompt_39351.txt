I've been around the rationalist blogosphere since the Overcoming Bias days, through peak LessWrong and into the diaspora, and somewhere along the way I absorbed the local doctrine that the Ship of Theseus is a dissolvable pseudo-question: that sameness is a two-place predicate, that there's no fact of the matter, that the whole puzzle is an artifact of our pattern-matching algorithms. I'm also a software engineer, and I've noticed that when I think about the puzzle now, I instinctively reach for engineering vocabulary rather than philosophical vocabulary. I'd like to know whether that instinct is faithful or whether it's quietly distorting the puzzle. So here is the request: restate the Ship of Theseus debate in the vocabulary I actually use, and then give me the residue report.

The target vocabulary is the working toolkit of a version-control-era engineer: object identity versus structural equality, pointers and what they point at, content-addressing (a git blob just is the hash of its contents), history as a DAG of commits where identity is anchored in parent pointers rather than in the current working tree, forks that both keep the name, long-running refactors that keep the interface frozen while every line of implementation is replaced, bug-for-bug compatibility, in-place database migrations, cache invalidation. Assume I'm fluent in all of this and in the standard rationalist toolkit (levels of abstraction, dissolving questions, two-place predicates); don't re-explain what == means.

Restate, as faithfully as you can: the original gradual-replacement puzzle; the classic extension in which the discarded planks are gathered up and reassembled into a second ship; and, to the extent they translate, the main positions people actually defend — survival through functional continuity, identity through original matter, both, neither, and the deflationary "no fact of the matter." I'm genuinely unsure how the debate carves up once Aristotle's matter-and-form vocabulary is off the table. Do the rival positions correspond to different identity functions you could write down for the same data, or to different choices about which level of abstraction is even allowed to carry identity? If the endurantist/perdurantist distinction can be expressed in this vocabulary at all, tell me how — commit history seems like a natural fit for one side, and I'd like to know if that impression survives scrutiny.

Then the residue report, which is the part I care about most. First, what maps cleanly: I suspect gradual replacement is structurally an incremental rebuild, and I suspect content-addressing gives the reassembled ship a stronger claim than philosophers usually grant it, since in my world a blob reconstructed from its hash is not a copy but the very same blob. Say whether those suspicions hold up, and where they break. Second, what had to be distorted to fit: in software, identity conventions exist because something downstream depends on them — callers, caches, audits — whereas a ship's sameness seems to matter in a different way, for things like ownership, honors, and the plain fact that the rowers might care whether they rowed on the original. Is identity in code always chosen for downstream use, and if so, does that support the deflationary line or quietly beg the question against it? Third, what has no counterpart at all: git's identity rules are artifacts of a design that solved a particular engineering problem, so the fact that they work is no evidence that ships work the same way — tell me if anything in the philosophical debate genuinely can't be said in my vocabulary, rather than merely isn't usually said.

And then the verdict. Is the residue there because engineering vocabulary is missing something the philosophers were tracking, or because they were tracking something less substantive than it looked? Relatedly, my crowd's doctrine says the question dissolves once you notice you can pick your identity function per use case — but that's a claim about ships, not about predicates, and I've never seen anyone argue for it rather than assert it. Does your translation vindicate it, or does "choose your identity function per use case" turn out to be a substantive metaphysical thesis wearing pragmatist clothing? Keep the translation honest: don't smuggle in engineering pragmatism and call it translation, and don't grade the philosophy against my vocabulary's hidden assumptions. The residue is the interesting part; treat it that way.
