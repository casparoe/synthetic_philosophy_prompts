For roughly two decades now, the debate over causation by omission seems to me to have been running in place, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another round of it. The shape of the dispute: on counterfactual-dependence and interventionist theories, omissions are unproblematically causes — the plant died because the gardener didn't water it, since had he watered it, it would have lived; an omission is just a variable taking the value "did not happen." The objection is profligacy: on that account everyone on Earth who failed to water the plant caused its death, and since absences are everywhere, non-events end up doing more causal work than events ever do. The friends of omission-causation reply in two main ways — either contrastively (the gardener's omission, unlike the stranger's, is a failure to water rather than a failure not-to-water, against a background in which watering was the relevant alternative) or by letting norms, roles, and expectations pick out the causally salient omissions. Their critics answer that both replies concede the point: if norms or contrastive backgrounds decide what counts as a cause, then causation by omission is not a relation out there in the world, and Lewis's old line that absences are at best quasi-causes gets reasserted. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is really a disagreement about whether "cause" is univocal — perhaps the dependence theorists and the production theorists (in the "two concepts of causation" tradition) are each vindicated on their own causal relation, and the fight over whether omissions "really" cause is verbal — though I suspect that cannot be the whole story, because the norm-based replies seem to do work that no two-relation view explains: a two-relation story says which relation an omission can enter, but not why the gardener's failure rather than the stranger's counts. So: what kind of disagreement is this — verbal, a hidden metaphysical dispute about negative events, a clash between the standards governing causal explanation and those governing causal attribution, or two different questions run under one word? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a new distinction, empirical work on ordinary causal judgments, a formal result — or is this an impasse we should expect to persist, and why?
