Compose a sestina in the voice of the man inside the Chinese Room, written for the closing session of a philosophy of mind seminar that has spent the term on Searle's argument, and I want the form to carry the argument rather than merely accompany it.

The six end-words should be the materials of the room itself — I'd suggest something like characters, rulebook, basket, door, answers, and room, though substitute freely if you find a better set. The crucial constraint is that these words recur only by the sestina's fixed permutation and by nothing else: never re-chosen for sense, never swapped for a synonym to make a line land better. The vocabulary must be shuffled by rule, exactly as the man shuffles symbols by rule, so that the form enacts the central claim — six stanzas of pure syntax turning in circles, fluent on the surface, with the same six counters passing through the man's hands over and over without his ever grasping what any of them mean.

The envoi should give the systems reply its turn. Use all six end-words in the three lines, two per line, but let the middle line break the poem's voice: the man speaking plainly in his own words — insisting, as he has all along, that he understands nothing — while the first and third lines, read together across him, form a reply fluent enough to have passed under the door. Do not reconcile the two. The poem should end with the man's testimony and the room's fluency sitting side by side, unreconciled, because that adjacency is the whole argument.

A specialist should be able to map each formal feature to the dialectical move it enacts — the permutation as rule-following without understanding, the fixed and unchosen vocabulary as the absence of semantic access, the envoi's sudden fluency as the systems reply and the middle line as the intuition that reply has to defeat — so please append a short prose note stating that mapping explicitly, including one sentence on which formal choice you think a critic could fairly accuse of cheating.
