Two members of my philosophy of law reading group have spent the past year arguing past each other about the Hart–Fuller debate, and I would like to give them something constructive to work from instead of another round of talking past each other. One is an inclusive legal positivist in Hart's tradition: she holds that whether a norm counts as law in a given system is settled by social facts, while conceding that a system's rule of recognition can, at the discretion of that system's officials, build moral criteria into legality. The other is a natural lawyer in Fuller's tradition: he holds that law is partially constituted by procedural morality — a system that persistently fails the demands of generality, publicity, prospectivity, and congruence between announced rule and official action does not merely make bad law, it makes less law, and in the limit no law at all. Neither is a caricature: she does not think wicked law is automatically void, and he does not think every unjust statute evaporates.

Please draft the joint statement they should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, structured as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: statements about the debate itself, about the grudge-informer cases, and about Nazi-era law that both could put their names to, phrased in wording neither side would balk at — not vague enough to be signed vacuously, and not slanted toward either camp.

Second, a disagreements table. Each residual disagreement should be stated twice: once in wording the positivist would accept as a fair statement of her view, and once in wording the natural lawyer would accept as a fair statement of his. The work here is in the drafting; formulations that either side would refuse as question-begging are failures.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of the two to revise. I suspect one crux is whether legality admits of degrees or is an all-or-nothing matter, and another is which claim of the positivist's separation thesis is really at stake — a claim about validity, about existence, or about the content of law being independent of its merits — but identify whatever you take the genuine cruxes to be, and say which alleged disagreements between them are really verbal disputes about the word law, and whether anything survives once that part is set aside.

Fourth, for each crux, a concrete case both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Please construct a novel test case rather than recycling the grudge informer. In particular, consider these two systems and whether you can refine them into something sharper: (a) a regime that satisfies every one of Fuller's desiderata — general, public, prospective, stable, congruent — and uses that very machinery to entrench something morally monstrous; and (b) its mirror image, a regime whose formal lawmaking violates the desiderata routinely but in which citizens' expectations are nonetheless stably satisfied through other channels, such as honest extralegal announcements by officials and consistently honored custom. For each system, what would the judges, practicing lawyers, and ordinary citizens inside it actually say when asked whether the norms are law, and would they treat them as obligation-generating or merely as backed by threats? Record what each of my two colleagues would predict, and also what each would count as evidence against his or her own view — an honest adversarial collaboration writes down its own exposure, not just the other side's.

Keep the register of the document itself that of a joint statement: measured, first-person-plural where possible, and free of advocacy.
