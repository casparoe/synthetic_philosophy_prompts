Quick background so you know where this is coming from: I'm a retired biochemist, thirty-odd years in a protein folding lab, and since retiring I've gotten embarrassingly deep into philosophy of science. Something I've been chewing on lately is a fight between two old friends of mine, and I want you to help me with it in a specific way.

Here's the fight. One friend, a retired lab guy like me, thinks all the "genetic code" language is a metaphor we've just gotten numb to. His line is: the codon-to-amino-acid mapping is a chain of chemical reactions, full stop. Calling it a "code" is like calling a river a plan for the sea — useful shorthand for us, but not a description of what's actually going on in the cell. If we'd discovered molecular biology without computers existing, he claims, nobody would ever have reached for the word "code." My other friend, a retired physicist turned information-theory hobbyist, thinks this is just stubbornness. The codon assignment is arbitrary in the relevant sense — there's nothing about the chemistry of CCA that demands proline, and the code could have been otherwise (and partly was, in mitochondria and ciliates). There's a molecule-level decoder, the tRNAs and the ribosome, that implements a lookup table. If that's not a code, he says, then nothing is.

I've refereed their arguments for a year and mostly failed, so I want to try the adversarial-collaboration format I've been reading about: a document both parties could actually put their names to, with the leftover disagreement made exact instead of shouted about.

Please draft that joint statement. Specifically:

1. A numbered section of claims both would sign. Include claims about the chemistry itself (which parts of the transcription-translation story are chemically forced versus historically contingent), claims about what everyone should concede regarding information in the plain Shannon sense, and claims about which uses of "code" and "information" are harmless shorthand that neither side should get worked up about.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should be worded so that the side it describes would accept the wording — no strawmanning. Candidates they've fought about, which you can add to or reshape: whether arbitrariness of the codon assignment is sufficient for codehood or merely necessary; whether calling tRNAs a "decoder" is a real structural claim or the metaphor smuggling itself back in; whether the cell contains semantic information — information that is about something, for something — or whether "aboutness" only shows up when an interpreter like us shows up; and whether DNA computing and DNA data storage (where we really do put a code of our own choosing into the molecule) changes what it's fair to say about natural DNA.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them — and for each crux, one concrete comparison, case, or argument that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. A few things my friends have gestured at, which you can use or replace: the stereochemical question of whether amino acids have any intrinsic binding preferences for their codons or anticodons (if they do, one side says the mapping isn't arbitrary after all, and the other side has to say what follows from that); the slippery slope of other arbitrary-looking biomolecular correspondences, like restriction enzyme recognition sites or histone marks, and whether code-talk should extend to them; and the implementation point — Morse code and a CD track are "just physics" at the bottom too, so if being reducible to physics doesn't disqualify those, why does it disqualify DNA? One side of that last one needs to draw the line somewhere; make them say where, and make the other side say whether they accept that line.

4. And this is the part I personally suspect most: if some part of their dispute is purely verbal — if they'd agree on all the facts and are only fighting over the word "literal" or "code" — say which part and why. But only if you actually believe it; don't use "it's verbal" as an escape hatch from the hard rows in the table.

The finished statement should read like something two careful people signed, not like a lecture. Where you have to make a judgment call a real philosopher of biology might contest, or where you lean on one camp's framing to make a row signable, flag it in a short note rather than burying it. And since I'll be sending this back to both of them: tell me how confident you are in the document's central calls, and single out the claim or crux formulation you're least sure of. American English, please — the physicist is picky about that even though his arguments are worse. Sorry, that's the biochemist in me talking, leave it in. Actually no, take it out but keep a little of that energy.

One more thing: don't be too confident. These two have caught me overreaching more than once, and I'd rather the statement be careful than impressive. If a crux can't honestly be paired with a test or case both sides would accept, say so instead of inventing one.
