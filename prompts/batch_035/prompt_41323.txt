I'm writing an essay for an applied ethics seminar on what literal lifeboat cases do to "lifeboat ethics" as a philosophical position, and I'd like a careful two-directional analysis: the cases through the lens of the theory, and the theory as tested by the cases.

First, the facts I want treated as given. In November 1820 the Nantucket whaleship Essex was rammed and sunk by a sperm whale roughly two thousand miles off South America, and her crew of twenty took to three small whaleboats. After weeks of starvation the survivors resorted to cannibalism, and on Captain George Pollard's boat, according to the survivors' own accounts, the men drew lots to determine who would die so the rest could eat. The lot fell to sixteen-year-old Owen Coffin; Pollard is said to have offered to die in his place, and Coffin refused. In 1884 the yacht Mignonette sank in a storm some sixteen hundred miles from the Cape of Good Hope, leaving Captain Tom Dudley, Edwin Stephens, Edmund Brooks, and the seventeen-year-old cabin boy Richard Parker adrift in a thirteen-foot dinghy with almost no food or water. After more than three weeks, Parker fell gravely ill from drinking seawater. No lots were drawn: Dudley and Stephens decided Parker, near death and without family, would be killed, and Dudley cut his throat. The three survived partly on his body until rescue. On their return, Dudley and Stephens were tried for murder. The court rejected necessity as a defence, convicted them, and imposed the death sentence, commuted within months to a token imprisonment; Brooks was never charged.

The theory is Garrett Hardin's 1974 lifeboat ethics. Hardin asks us to picture a lifeboat carrying fifty people with room for ten more, surrounded in the ocean by a hundred swimmers, and uses the resulting dilemmas — whom, if anyone, to admit — to argue against foreign aid, open immigration, and food relief, on the ground that admitting swimmers swamps the boat and produces what he called complete justice and complete catastrophe. Notably, Hardin also turns the lens inward at one point, raising as open questions what the boat's occupants owe each other as supplies run out, including whether the dying may be killed so the rest can live.

Here is what I want analyzed, in roughly this order, though deviate if the argument demands it. First, the mapping and its central reversal: Hardin's dilemma is about admission from the water — whether to take swimmers aboard — while the real cases are about the consumption of those already aboard, since in each case the person who died was crew, not a swimmer. Is the metaphor's rhetorical force parasitic on blurring this, or is Hardin's real claim about triage under scarcity among insiders, with the swimmers a dispensable ornament? Second, the lottery. Essex and the Mignonette amount to a natural experiment: identical extremity, but in one case lots were drawn and in the other the weakest was selected. Does the drawing of lots genuinely transform the moral character of the act — from murder into something like a permissible killing — and if so, on what grounds: procedural fairness, the impossibility of any member claiming special desert, or manufactured consent? Note that in both cases consent was compromised: Coffin's acceptance of the lot arguably coexisted with a denial of Pollard's offer to substitute himself, and Parker was comatose. Third, the custom of the sea. Mariners' drawing of lots appears to have been a recognized convention among seafarers facing repeated crises of this kind, and I'm interested in treating it as an emergent norm — something like a contractarian solution stabilized by repetition. Does the existence and acceptance of that convention say anything about Hardin's picture, given that his lifeboat norms are norms of exclusion rather than norms of equal chance? Fourth, the verdict. R v Dudley and Stephens stands for the proposition that survival does not excuse murder — an absolute constraint on what necessity permits — while the public sympathy of the day ran to the castaways, and no one from the Essex was ever prosecuted at all. Hardin wants scarcity-based reasoning to license harsh exclusion; the legal tradition behind the Dudley verdict appears to say the opposite, that even the extremest necessity does not justify killing. So does the precedent reinforce Hardin's hard line about the limits of rescue, or does the metaphor's actual pedigree run directly against the use he puts it to? Finally, and honestly: tell me where the theory runs silent. What is it about these cases — Brooks's non-participation mattering to our judgment of him, the impossibility of evaluating acts chosen in desperation by standards formed in comfort — that lifeboat ethics simply has no apparatus for?

Some constraints on the response. Reason from what I've given you and general knowledge only; do not supply citations, references, or a bibliography, and do not lean on specific readings of the primary texts beyond what I've described. Where you must go beyond the record I've sketched, flag it as interpretation rather than fact.

One more thing, in my own words: if you think the comparison I'm asking for is confused at its root — if, for instance, you conclude that Hardin's metaphor is about the distribution of resources between nations and the Dudley and Essex cases are about what morality permits in extremis, so that "lifeboat ethics" is really two unrelated projects sharing a single word, and my question forces an artificial unity on them — say so plainly and analyze the confusion instead of producing the comparison I asked for. I'd rather be corrected than accommodated, and I'd rather hear that a sub-question can't be answered as posed than watch it be answered by stretching the theory past what it can carry.
