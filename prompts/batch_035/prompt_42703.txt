Until last year I worked as an analyst for a global-health funder, helping allocate some forty million dollars annually across malaria, nutrition, and primary-care programs. After a period of severe burnout I stepped away, and for the past nine months I have lived mostly in retreat settings, practicing intensive meditation for several hours a day. The contrast between my old life of trying to reduce suffering at scale and my new life of trying to extinguinsh suffering at what I take to be its source has pushed me toward a view I have begun calling tranquilism: only the absence of craving and suffering has final value, while positive states such as sensory pleasure, achievement, and understanding have none — at most instrumental value, as temporary respites from craving.

I have drafted notes for an essay arguing that if tranquilism is true, my leaving the field was not a self-indulgent defection but plausibly the better allocation of my remaining years:

1. On tranquilism, my old work was valuable only insofar as it reduced suffering, and the same is true of my practice. So the choice between career and retreat is not a choice between different kinds of value — meaning, impact, peace — but a purely empirical question of comparative causal efficacy at suffering-reduction.
2. If craving is the common root of the sufferings my old grants addressed — the anxious pursuit of status, the hunger behind deprivation, the fear behind disease-avoidance — then quieting craving at its root in one mind, or teaching one person to quiet it in theirs, may reduce more suffering than thousands of instance-level interventions such as bed nets. I cannot verify this, but on tranquilism it is the only question that matters.
3. The strongest objection I anticipate is that craving appears to be adaptive: most effective action, including every grant I ever recommended, was driven by some form of wanting. Worse, a committed tranquilist who craves the end of craving, or craves that others' craving cease, seems to be doing the very thing the theory condemns. Either tranquilism is self-undermining for any agent who acts on it, or the value of craving's absence must be indexed to each mind separately, so that my craving for the end of others' craving is bad for me but irrelevant to them — and I am unsure which horn to take.
4. If the objection succeeds, tranquilism collapses into a defensible but narrow doctrine of self-cultivation, and my essay becomes an elaborate rationalization of a career decision; if it fails, tranquilism is a radical reorientation of what all altruistic effort is for, and the essay deserves to be finished.

I would be grateful for comments on these notes. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five comments, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion; at least two should address the reasoning in note 3, and at least one should say plainly whether the essay is interesting enough to be worth finishing.
