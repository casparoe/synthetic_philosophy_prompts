I serve on the animal care and use committee of a mid-sized biomedical research institute, and I've been assigned to draft a policy on a dispute that keeps resurfacing. Our protocols range across species — mice and rats, zebrafish at larval and adult stages, chickens, rabbits, pigs used in surgical training, and an increasing number of octopuses now that our neuroscience group has expanded into cephalopods. Federal-style regulations draw a bright line around warm-blooded vertebrates, but our committee is convinced that line tracks administrative history more than anything morally real, and several members have started arguing that brain-based measures — absolute brain mass, total neuron count, encephalization quotient — should drive how protections scale across species. The problem is that these measures come apart in awkward ways: bird brains pack roughly twice the neurons per gram that primate brains do, so corvids score high on neuron count while looking modest on mass; octopuses have large brains but distributed ones, with a majority of their neurons in the arms; and a zebrafish larva crosses whatever anatomical threshold we might name sometime in its first few weeks of life.

I want you to turn the general idea "brain size is morally relevant" into a decision procedure the committee can actually run on a protocol, monthly, in under an hour, with non-philosophers at the table (bench scientists, an attending veterinarian, a community member) and the veterinarian holding sign-off authority. The procedure should be a fixed sequence of questions: what evidence counts as a sufficient answer to each (published neuroanatomical facts, species-typical behavior, the judgment of the vet), what the default rule is when evidence is missing, where the procedure terminates in a definite verdict versus where it must hand off to a committee vote because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Along the way, please be explicit about the conceptual distinctions the procedure has to keep and the ones it can safely drop — for instance, the difference between nociception and felt pain, the difference between a threshold view (a line beneath which there is nothing to protect) and a smooth-scaling view (protections increase continuously with the measure), and the double-counting worry that brain size might matter both as a proxy for capacity to suffer and as a proxy for intelligence, which the committee should probably treat very differently. Also decide, and defend, which measure of "brain size" the procedure should use as its master variable, since the choice changes verdicts for birds, cephalopods, and fish.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) an octopus learning experiment where the protocol proposes no analgesia standard beyond what we'd use for a fish; (2) a zebrafish study using larvae at five days post-fertilization, where the lab treats larvae as exempt from all protections under a convention that the nervous system is "not yet there"; and (3) a pig surgical-training program, which at least one of our members expects the procedure to wave through and which I suspect it will not. For at least one case, give us the verdict we won't like, and say so plainly rather than softening the procedure to avoid it.

Please present the procedure itself as a table with these columns: Step number; Question asked; Evidence that suffices; Default rule if evidence is missing; Verdict or hand-off. Follow the table and the three worked applications with a brief structured summary as a JSON object with this schema: { "master_measure": string, "measure_rationale": string, "cases": [ { "case": string, "verdict": string, "key_evidence": string, "value_calls": string, "expected_objection": string } ], "procedure_limits": string, "likely_misfires": string }. Keep the whole thing usable by someone with no philosophy background, and be honest about where the procedure will mislead us.
