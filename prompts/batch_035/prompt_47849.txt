I teach a 300-level epistemology seminar on testimony and expertise, fourteen students, one 75-minute session, and I have a specific target: I want them to feel the difference between answers being available and understanding being in place -- and, more pointedly, what it does to you when the availability of the answer trains you out of the generation of it. The topic has stopped being academic for me: over the past year I have quietly outsourced my own lecture derivations, problem-set answer keys, and even my reading questions to an AI assistant, and last month I discovered I could no longer reconstruct, unaided, an argument I had "produced" and taught twice. I don't want to lecture them about this; I want to run it on them.

Design a two-phase game. In phase one, students work in pairs on a set of moderately hard but learnable tasks -- I'm thinking short formal derivations or map-coloring-style constraint puzzles, where each step is small but the strategy must be internally generated. Each pair has access to a help mechanism I'll call the Oracle: a stack of sealed slips, one per puzzle step, that they may open at will. Here is the crucial design constraint: the slips must be written so that using them feels like learning rather than cheating. Each slip should explain its step genuinely and well, so that students who use them can pass an immediate comprehension check on what the slip said. Whatever the payoff scheme is (points toward a prize, some token currency), it should reward completed puzzles, and it should not directly penalize slip use -- I want the dependence to emerge from the incentive structure, not from a rule I imposed.

Phase two comes without warning: the same pairs, the Oracles removed, and a set of isomorphic-but-shifted problems that require transferring the strategy, not replaying the steps. Some pairs will be fine; the ones I'm after are the ones who were fastest in phase one entirely on slip power.

For the debrief, map specific moments in the game onto the conceptual distinctions: access versus ability, competence that survives only while the artifact is plugged in, the way each slip was individually a genuine learning event while the whole pattern of use was not, and the self-assessment problem -- the students who used the slips most will report having learned the most, and I want them to see their phase-two scores alongside those self-reports. Tell me what materials I need, a minute-by-minute timeline, how to write the slips so they hit the individually-helpful/globally corrosive balance, how to handle the pairs who ignore the Oracle entirely and thereby become a control group I didn't plan for, and what will predictably go wrong the first time. Finally, be frank about what the game cannot reproduce: the Oracle is honest, instantaneous, and always available, and none of those three things is true of my relationship with the tool I actually use, so tell me which of those gaps matters most and what I must supply in the debrief myself.
