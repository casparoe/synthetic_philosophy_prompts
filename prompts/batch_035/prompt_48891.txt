I help run a small decision theory reading group — mostly grad students and a couple of math-y hobbyists, everyone comfortable with expected value and basic game theory, but nobody has done any of the fancy stuff like updateless decision theory or anthropics. Next session I want to present the absent-minded driver problem, and I've realized that every time I've tried to explain it informally I've made a mess of it, so I want an actual plan this time instead of winging it.

To remind you of the setup since we'll be working with it the whole session: a driver leaves work late at night and has to pass two identical intersections on the way home. The roads all look the same and he's tired enough that once past the first intersection he genuinely can't tell whether he's at the first or the second one. If he exits at the first intersection he ends up in a bad neighborhood and gets payoff 0, exiting at the second gets him home quickly for payoff 4, and if he sails straight through both he has to take a long loop for payoff 1. At each intersection he just chooses "exit" or "continue," knowing he'll be in exactly the same mental state at both. That's the whole problem and the group will have 50 minutes with a whiteboard.

What I want from you is not the explanation itself but the teaching plan. What has to be established first before the puzzle can even feel like a puzzle (I suspect they need to first see why "just exit when you think you're at the second one" is not an available option, but I'm not sure how to stage that). Then there's the classic two-solutions business — the ex ante commit-to-a-randomized-policy answer versus the reasoning-on-the-spot answer — and I don't know whether to present them as a debate, or as a series, or whether showing one first poisons the other. Relatedly: I'm worried the payoff numbers will make them think this is an optimization exercise rather than a decision theory exercise — where do I head that off? Also: which misconceptions should I expect (my guesses are "this is just Sleeping Beauty" and "the driver is stupid, real agents keep a memory" — but there may be better ones), and where exactly in the sequence should each get defused? What should I openly refuse to cover in 50 minutes (FDT? the amnesiac cosmetics literature?) and just say so? And give me one check at the end — a question or micro-exercise — that would tell me whether they've understood why the problem is hard rather than just correctly computed the optimum. If it matters for sequencing, the group has previously done Newcomb and has at least heard of self-locating probability.
