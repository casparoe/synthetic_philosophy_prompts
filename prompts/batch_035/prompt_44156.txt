I sit on the editorial board of a peer-reviewed cognitive-science journal that publishes roughly forty empirical articles a year. At last month's meeting our editor-in-chief proposed that every issue carry a standing notice: that each article has passed the same review process, that the process is sound, and that we nonetheless expect some of our published findings not to replicate, so readers should treat individual conclusions accordingly. Half the board thinks the notice is overdue honesty; the other half thinks it is self-undermining — that a journal cannot coherently stand behind each of its articles while announcing that it does not stand behind what they jointly amount to. Two board members have been circulating handouts on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and the vote has become a proxy fight over whether those puzzles actually describe what a journal does. Our bylaws give the board authority over publication policy by majority vote; we vote in three weeks.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate beforehand. It should recommend one of three outcomes — adopt the notice as proposed, reject it, or adopt it with a concrete modification (for instance, per-article statements of confidence instead of, or alongside, the blanket notice) — and defend the recommendation by engaging the actual structure of the problem: the acceptance rule each reviewer is applying at the article level, the aggregate claim the notice makes at the issue level, and whether asserting both is a genuine incoherence or something an institution can rationally live with. It should present the strongest argument on the losing side at full strength — if the losing side is right that the notice changes what reviewers are doing when they accept a paper, the memo should say exactly how. And if you think the board's framing is confused — that the vote as posed misses the real issue, or that the notice is neither honest nor self-undermining but merely empty — say so plainly rather than picking one of our three options politely.
