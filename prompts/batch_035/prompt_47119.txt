I'm a PhD student who has just taken over, this term, our university's consent workshops for incoming students — the sessions the student union runs during freshers' week. My co-facilitator is a final-year law student with unusually settled views: whenever students raise a hypothetical, she hands down a verdict on the spot, and over our first few sessions a track record has accumulated. She has ruled that a man who exaggerated his feelings — insisting he was in love and wanted a shared future when he wanted neither — nevertheless obtained valid consent, however shabby his conduct; that the self-inventing dater who claims a glamorous career he doesn't have obtains valid consent; but that a man who silently removed a condom during sex, to which his partner had agreed only with protection, does not. On the pressure side, she has ruled that blackmail — threatening to expose an affair unless a woman has sex with him — vitiates consent; that a partner who threatens to end the relationship unless sex happens does not vitiate it ("she can show him the door"); and that a manager who tells an employee she will be sacked unless she goes on a date with him does vitiate it.

Next week's session has three cases waiting, which students have already emailed in, and I want to predict her rulings before she delivers them, because the students keep asking where the line actually falls and I would rather reconstruct her line than improvise my own in front of a room. Case one: a much older, extremely wealthy man offers to clear a postgraduate's crushing debts if she spends a night with him; she accepts, and later says she would never have consented but for the offer, and saw no realistic way out of her debts — he threatens nothing and takes nothing away from her. Case two: a woman makes clear she will sleep with a man only if there is no risk of pregnancy; he assures her he has had a vasectomy; he has not, though the encounter is otherwise exactly as she envisaged it. Case three: a landlord with a contractual right to terminate on a technicality he has always overlooked says he will begin eviction proceedings unless his tenant has sex with him.

The three answers matter less to me than the machinery behind them. What rules fit her track record equally well — something like "threats and deceptions about the act itself vitiate, while offers and inducement-lies do not"; or "consent fails whenever no reasonable alternative remains or control over the bodily act is defeated"; or something about the wrongfulness of the leverage exerted — and on which of the three cases do those rules genuinely come apart? Give me your best guess at her verdicts, with your reasons for preferring it, and then tell me which further case, if a student raised it, would best drive the rival rules apart.
