I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun reading serious philosophy, and I have gotten stuck on the debate over vagueness. In my working life, an uncertain quantity was never an indeterminate one: a length measured as 2.3 plus or minus 0.1 millimeters was still some exact length, and the error bar described our ignorance, not the world. This background makes epistemicism about vagueness feel almost obvious to me: of course there is some exact number of grains that first makes a pile a heap, and of course we cannot know which number it is. What I cannot yet get my head around is the main alternative, supervaluationism, and in particular a persistent objection to it that I keep encountering in summaries but never see worked through.

Please explain, at a conceptual level, the following. First, how the supervaluationist defines truth for borderline sentences: the idea that a sentence is supertrue when it comes out true under every admissible precisification of the vague term, so that a sorites premise like a clear case minus one grain still remains a clear case is true, while each individual step sentence about the borderline region comes out neither supertrue nor superfalsch. Second, the objection that this machinery does not eliminate sharp boundaries but only relocates them: every complete precisification draws an exact cutoff in the sorites series, so the supervaluationist seems to have traded one suspicious sharp line for infinitely many sharp lines plus a further vague question about which precisifications are admissible. Third, how the notion of penumbral truth and higher-order vagueness is supposed to respond to this, and whether the response ultimately succeeds or merely iterates the original problem one level up.

Finally, since I would like to test my physicist's instincts against yours: I am tempted to model supertruth as something like a truth-value averaged over an ensemble of admissible sharpenings, the way one averages a quantity over an ensemble of measurement models in my old field, and I am equally tempted to say that if an epistemic gloss on imprecision is completely standard for physical quantities, it should be no more mysterious for predicates. Please assess whether either analogy is apt or misleading, and say where the disanalogy lies if there is one. Answer in American English, and please do not rely on citations or references; I am after the conceptual geography, not a bibliography.
