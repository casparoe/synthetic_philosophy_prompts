For some years I have been recording my private verdicts on when an unrealistic assumption in a scientific model counts as a legitimate idealization and when it counts as intellectual cheating, and I would like to know whether a single principle governs them, because I have begun to suspect that it does not. Here are the verdicts.

The ideal gas model's noninteracting point particles: legitimate, and the model is not even discredited by its failure near condensation, provided we know where it fails. The infinite-population assumption of classical population genetics: legitimate, even though every real population is finite and drift is real, because the finite models converge to the infinite one wherever it matters. Friedman's billiard player, the expert who plays as though he had solved the relevant optimization problem, and Friedman's leaves, which behave as though they had deliberately positioned themselves to maximize exposure to sunlight: legitimate in both cases, even though a psychologically realistic model of the player would predict missed shots, and a realistic model of the tree would predict shaded branches, that the as-if model cannot. Calvo pricing in macroeconomics — the assumption that firms may reset their prices only at randomly arriving opportunities: I accept it, flagrantly false as it is, purely for tractability. But the mortgage-security pricing models built on the assumption that a simultaneous nationwide fall in housing prices was effectively impossible: those were also chosen for tractability, and I refuse them — they seem to have assumed away the very phenomenon they existed to price. Likewise a model of bank runs that grants every depositor rational expectations and common knowledge of solvency: cheating, since the panic is the explanandum. The case I cannot settle is the homogeneous-mixing assumption in the compartmental epidemic models governments actually used in 2020: false in every particular, since contacts are structured by households, workplaces, and schools, and yet those models were useful enough to steer policy. And I confess a standing asymmetry: I accept as-if explanation in biology without blinking, but I distrust the same move when economists claim that firms behave as if they were literal profit maximizers, and I cannot tell whether that reflects a principled difference or merely my sense of which discipline has earned its idealizations.

My tentative principle is this: an assumption is legitimate just in case substituting the more realistic assumption would leave the model's predictions about its target roughly unchanged. I can see that it is in trouble, since the billiard player and the leaves look legitimate to me while a realistic replacement would change the predictions considerably. Repair my principle or replace it so that it captures my verdicts; if nothing can capture them all, tell me which verdicts I should surrender, and why those rather than the others. State the resulting principle precisely enough to decide new cases, and tell me what it says about two cases I have not yet classified: the Coase theorem's zero-transaction-cost assumption, and the rational-expectations assumptions in the integrated assessment models used to price long-run climate damage. Finally, tell me whether the biology-versus-economics asymmetry can be given a principled defense, or whether I should discard it as prejudice.
