I've come to hold a deflationary view of the Death in Damascus family of cases in decision theory, and I want to know what would count against it. The case, as it has come down from the Gibbard-Harper literature: you meet Death in Damascus, and whichever you choose — staying put or fleeing to Aleppo — your choosing it is evidence that a nearly infallible predictor anticipated the choice and arranged your death in that city, so each act confirms that it is the fatal one. My view has three parts. First, cases of this shape are adversarial by construction: the world is stipulated to track the agent's choice with near-perfect accuracy, so what the case measures is the predictor's power over you, not the quality of your decision procedure. Second, such cases cannot discriminate between decision theories, because for any decision theory one can build an analogous case in which a predictor punishes exactly the choices that theory recommends — so the fact that causal decision theory is refuted by one of them, evidential decision theory by another, and any future rival by some further variant shows only that every theory has a rigged case built against it. Third, it follows that eliciting verdicts on these cases is methodologically confused: intuitions about rigged problems should carry no weight in theory choice, and the right answer in Death in Damascus itself is simply that no deterministic choice is better than any other, with randomization at best securing a coin flip's worth of survival. What would count against this view? In particular, I can see three possible lines of attack, and I'd like them assessed along with any others: the extension where a bookie offers a side bet on your survival, which the standard causal reasoner takes even though the bet is good only if his own picture of the case is false — does that show the case discriminates between theories after all, rather than merely exposing them to a rigged predictor? The self-ratifying mixed-strategy resolutions of the instability — if there is a principled right answer lurking at the fixed point, does that suggest there is a fact of the matter that a correct decision theory could get right, and that the case is not purely adversarial? And the continuum from fallible predictors to perfect ones — if the adversarial character fades smoothly as prediction quality drops, and theories do give genuinely different recommendations against fallible predictors, is my category of rigged cases well-defined, or have I drawn a line around only the endpoint of a spectrum? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which of them a committed deflationist should concede as counting against the view and which he would dismiss, and tell me honestly whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability — whether any refuting case could always be redescribed as one more rigged case — and, if so, whether that is a defect in a methodological view of this kind, or just what it means to be a claim about how evidence about decision theories should be gathered rather than about which decision theory is true.
