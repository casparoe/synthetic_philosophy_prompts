Dana thought this was way too generous. Marco's answer was that metabolism is special because it's what holds the organism's structure together against decay -- the tardigrade, even dried out, is doing the staying-intact work, whereas the virus just sits there and waits for something else to do the work. Dana pushed back that "doing the staying-together work" is a spectrum with no obvious cutoff: bacterial spores, freeze-dried yogurt cultures, even a crystal that sits unchanged on a shelf for a century is suspiciously good at not decaying, and at some point "resists decay" collapses into "is very stable," which nobody thinks is a definition of life.

Dana said the mule kills that one: a mule is sterile, it does no Darwinian evolving of its own, and it's obviously alive -- so that definition is really about lineages, not individual organisms, which means it can't answer the actual question we started with, namely whether THIS tardigrade in THIS vial is alive right now. Marco conceded the mule point but tried to combine the two definitions: alive means self-sustaining now, plus descended from an evolving lineage. Dana said now "lineage" was doing all the work and he'd made life partly a historical property, so if a lab someday assembles a working cell from scratch, with no ancestor at all, it's not alive by his definition, which she found absurd. Marco said maybe that's not absurd, maybe it really would lack something, and honestly at that point they started arguing about synthetically assembled cells and I lost the thread.
