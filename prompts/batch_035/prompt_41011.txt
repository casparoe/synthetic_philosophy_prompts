My tutor keeps pushing a distinction on me: does a given monster objection attack the aggregation itself (the summing of welfare across beings), or does it only work because the monster's welfare is empirically exotic — beings that just don't convert resources into wellbeing like that? Meanwhile my flatmate reckons all the monster variants are secretly the same objection wearing different hats, which annoys me but I cant quite prove him wrong.

So here's the job. Take the role of a professional moral philosopher who specialises in aggregation and population ethics. Start with a short brainstorm before any ranking — just reasoning, no lists yet: for each of the five monsters below, work out which utilitarian commitment it really exploits. Is it the additivity of welfare, the maximisation of the total, the claim that only experienced welfare counts, or merely an empirical assumption about welfare conversion? Then sort the five into tiers by how much of a problem survives once the utilitarian is allowed to reply "beings like that are biologically impossible". In other words, tier them by philosophical force modulo empirical contingency, and give a sentence or two per monster explaining its tier. If any two turn out to be the same objection in a different coat, call that out explicitly — I'd love to settle the flatmate argument with something better than "it feels different".

The five variants:

1. The classic: one being whose welfare increases enormously for each unit of resource given to it, so the total is maximised by funnelling everything to it and leaving everyone else near zero (Nozick's version).
2. The negative mirror: a being whose suffering is wildly out of proportion to small deprivations, so the theory says everyone else must be drained to keep it marginally comfortable — not a super-pleased monster, a super-harmed one.
3. The swarm: not one super-receiver but a vast population each barely above neutral, whose sum edges out a small, genuinely flourishing population.
4. The null receiver: a being that converts resources into no welfare whatsoever — a perfect utility sink, so the theory counts giving to it as pure waste.
5. The threshold case: a being that gains nothing below an enormous resource threshold and everything above it, so aggregation says starve everyone else until the threshold can be met.

Finish with two things: (a) which single monster you'd hang the essay's punchline on, and (b) whether any of the five forces the utilitarian to give up additivity itself, or whether they can always get away with denying an empirical premise or biting a bullet. Think carefully before you rank — I'd rather have a defensible ordering then a tidy one.
