I've been reading rationalist forums and econ blogs for over a decade now — long enough to have internalized the standard line that utility is a bookkeeping device for preferences rather than a substance in your head, and to have heard a hundred times that "people maximize utility" is either trivially true or an empirical claim about consistency. A friend of mine who did graduate work in economics ran an argument past me last week that seems to prove far too much, and although I'm confident there's a seam in it, I can't locate it precisely.

His argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) In revealed-preference theory, a preference is nothing over and above what choice reveals: you prefer X to Y when you choose X over Y with both available.
(2) So whatever a person ever chooses, they preferred to the alternatives available at the time.
(3) Utility is just a representation of preference: whatever a person prefers more, they get more utility from.
(4) Therefore, whatever a person chooses maximizes their utility — by construction, not by luck.
(5) This covers every choice there is: relapsing, procrastinating, staying in a bad relationship, overpaying for a bad coffee — each is chosen, so each maximizes utility, so each is rational in the only sense the theory recognizes.
(6) By parity of reasoning, it covers anything that "selects" outcomes: a rock rolls downhill, so it "prefers" the bottom of the hill; natural selection culls the unfit, so the gene pool "prefers" fitness; my spam filter maximizes its utility every day. So either utility-maximization does no discriminating work anywhere, or it does no discriminating work in humans.
(7) A concept satisfied by everything explains nothing; so "people maximize utility" is a restatement of the choice data, not a theory, and the whole utility apparatus should be retired in favor of just describing what people pick.

He concludes that economists are running an unfalsifiable doctrine dressed in math.

Here's what's bugging me. My instinct is that something equivocates between (2) and (4) — maybe between preference as revealed in one particular choice and preference as a stable something that persists across choice situations — but I can't make that precise, and I can also see someone arguing the argument is actually fine through (5), with the real fallacy in (6)'s parity step instead. And there's a wrinkle I can't fit in: I recall that the revealed-preference people have results to the effect that if your choices cycle badly enough — you choose A over B in one budget, B over C in another, C over A in a third — then no utility function represents you at all. If that's right, doesn't it mean the theory has empirical content, exactly contrary to (7)? But then what do I say about the procrastinator, whose choices may be perfectly consistent and who still insists she'd prefer to be someone who didn't procrastinate? Is her preference the one revealed by her behavior, or the one she reports, or does the question malfunction?

Tell me which step to reject and why that step looked fine, then price the alternatives — in particular, what accepting my friend's conclusion (7) would force economists to give up that they plainly shouldn't. Along the way I'd like to know whether "revealed preference makes utility talk unfalsifiable" and "the theory implies no one ever acts irrationally" are the same objection or two different objections wearing the same coat. Please answer in American English.
