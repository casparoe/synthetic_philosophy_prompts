I'm teaching a seminar next term on philosophy of creativity, and I've been collecting parody pieces to open each session with -- it wakes the students up better than a recap does. Next week we're on Hofstadter's picture of creativity as variations on a theme: the idea that concepts come with "knobs" (the dimensions you can twiddle), that making something new is basically taking an existing theme and wiggling those knobs -- slipping a concept a bit this way or that -- and that analogy-making is the engine under the whole thing. Half my students come out of the reading thinking it's the best thing they've ever read, the other half come out deflated, like great, so nothing is ever actually new, every "original" idea is just some neighbor in concept-space of an idea somebody already had. I want to poke at both reactions.

So: write me an Onion-style news article, headline plus 400-500 words, about a local man who has adopted the knob-twiddling model as a total lifestyle. He inventories the knobs on everything -- his marriage, his lasagna, his commute, his grief -- and he refuses to describe anything as "new" anymore, only as a controlled slippage from something adjacent. The jokes should come from getting the model right rather than making fun of it from outside: the question of which knob to turn is where taste actually lives, some knobs turn out to be welded shut, some variations land too far away to count as the same theme anymore, and the poor guy keeps discovering that other people were doing this all along without a framework (his mother-in-law has been slipping concepts for decades and doesn't even know it). I'd love a quote from a concerned neighbor who insists that at least some things in life are genuine discontinuities -- saltations, breaks, things that are NOT just a variation on a theme -- and the article should treat this neighbor as tiresously quaint for comedic effect, while still letting a careful reader see the real objection underneath.

The bar for fairness here is that a Hofstadter fan should read it and laugh, not wince -- exaggerate the actual features of the view (the dimensional analysis of concepts, the idea that similarity and difference trade off, the suspicion that far-out creativity and near-neighbor creativity differ in degree not kind) rather than inventing dumb ones. Bonus points if you can work in a deadpan bit about him trying to knob-twiddle his way out of an argument about whether knob-twiddling is itself just a variation on some older idea about variation, and getting stuck in a loop he insists is a spiral. Keep it to roughly 400-500 words for the body, and don't editorialize at the end -- the Onion never winks, and neither should you.
