I'm a science-and-ideas journalist moderating a public "in conversation" event at a science festival, and my guest is a cognitive scientist who's spent her whole career defending heterophenomenology — Dennett's idea that the science of consciousness has to proceed third-person, treating subjects' reports as texts to be interpreted rather than as direct windows onto experience. She's very good on stage and loves the provocative framings, so I don't need questions that just get her to restate the view. Draft me eight to ten interview questions that actually press: at least one on what it means that the subject's report is treated like fiction about a world we can't visit directly, one on the cases where subjects are demonstrably wrong about their own experience (color phi, the delay before you can report anything at all) and what work those cases are really doing in her argument, one on whether the method could in principle discover that nobody is conscious — and whether she'd accept that as a legit finding or whether that would reveal the method smuggles in an assumption somewhere, and one on the trained-introspection crowd (meditators, the neurophenomenology people) who insist first-person discipline yields data no third-person setup can replicate. For each question, give a follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likley to give — she has stock answers and I want the follow-up to be built for them. The audience is mostly festival-goers but there'll be a handful of philosophy grad students who came ready to fight, so mark which questions are really for them.
