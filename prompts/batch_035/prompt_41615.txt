Anglo-American law is strangely two-faced about inaction, and I want to know what view of causation, if any, the pattern presupposes.

On one hand, the common law imposes no general duty to rescue: a swimmer who watches a child drown ten feet away commits no legal wrong, and courts have said the reason is not merely prudence but principle — the harm was not caused by the bystander. Criminal law treats a doctor who withdraws life support at the patient's competent request very differently from one who administers a lethal injection at the same request, and the standard justification is that the first merely lets the underlying condition do its work while the second causes death — even though both foresee death, both intend it, and the patient ends up dead either way.

On the other hand, the same legal system has no difficulty treating non-doings as causes when it wants to: products liability makes a manufacturer answerable for failure to warn of a known hazard; a landlord can be liable for failure to repair; negligence law's ordinary 'but for' test seems to make an omission a cause whenever the harm would not have occurred had the omission been repaired. Many European systems go further and criminalize the failure to render easy assistance, with no visible strain on their causal concepts.

So my questions. Does the killing/letting-die and rescue/no-rescue pattern presuppose that omissions are not causes, or only that they are causes of an inferior kind, or that causal contribution matters only against a background duty? Notice the suspicious coincidence that duties appear exactly where omissions get charged as causes — as if the duty were converting a non-cause into a cause, which suggests causation may be doing no work at all and the practice is a moral classification wearing causal vocabulary. Also notice that if omissions are causes in the plain counterfactual sense, they multiply absurdly: every able spectator at the beach becomes a cause of the drowning, and nothing distinguishes the negligent lifeguard from a stranger on the sand except luck and proximity. Reconstruct the implicit theory — say whether it is a transmission-of-force view, a contrastive view on which causation is relative to a chosen alternative, a norm-sensitive view on which what counts as a cause depends on what someone was supposed to do, or something else — and say which parts of the practice strictly require it and which are underdetermined, compatible with several views. Finally, tell me what would have to change in legal doctrine if the law openly adopted the flat counterfactual view under which every bystander's inaction is among the causes of the harm.
