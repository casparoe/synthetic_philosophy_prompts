I have run into an argument about the value of information that I cannot resolve, and that appears to prove far more than its premises could possibly support.

The background, as I understand it: I. J. Good, in his 1967 paper on the principle of total evidence, proved that free information can never hurt you. If a signal arrives before you must choose, if you condition on it correctly, and if you then take an act that maximizes expected utility relative to your posterior, then your expected utility is at least as high as it would have been had you chosen without the signal. The worst case is that the signal is uninformative, in which case you ignore it and choose exactly as before. This seems plainly right for a single, fixed decision problem. It is less plainly right once the argument below gets going.

The argument:

1. An agent faces a decision problem D: a set of acts, a set of states, a prior over the states, and utilities. Before acting, the agent may perform some experiment and observe its outcome.
2. By the theorem, for any experiment whose outcome is correlated with the state, performing the experiment and observing the result is weakly better than acting straight away: deciding with the observation yields expected utility at least as great as deciding without it.
3. Which experiment to perform is itself a choice made under uncertainty — the agent does not yet know what the candidate experiments would reveal — so there is a higher-order decision problem D′ concerning the choice of experiment, and the theorem applies to it in turn: gathering information relevant to D′ before choosing an experiment is weakly better than choosing an experiment blind. Concretely: if D is whether to run a clinical trial of a drug, D′ is the choice of trial design, and the question of what to read up on before settling the design is a third-order problem of the same shape.
4. The same reasoning iterates without end. Information about how to gather information about how to gather information relevant to D has non-negative value, and so on through every finite order.
5. At each level, gathering is weakly better than not gathering. An option that is weakly dominated at every level at which it is available is not an option a rational agent may choose. So a rational agent may not act while any level of gathering remains unperformed: it acts, if at all, only after an inquiry that has no last step.
6. But rational agents act after finite inquiry as a matter of course, and a theorem whose point is that more evidence is never worse was hardly meant to make action impossible. Applied to the decision of when to stop gathering, the reasoning in (5) would forbid ever stopping — including stopping in order to apply the theorem at all. So some step above is wrong.

The conclusion is absurd, so something has slipped, but I cannot pin down where. Step (2) looks like the theorem itself, and I would be most reluctant to give it up. Steps (3) and (4) feel as though they smuggle in something — perhaps that the agent already possesses a probability model of what the various meta-inquiries would show, which is exactly what it is trying to construct by inquiring — and yet the theorem never demands that a prior be earned; it demands only that it be fixed. My deepest suspicion falls on (5), and the most I can say there is that weakly better and required seem to have come apart somewhere; but I cannot turn that into a diagnosis, because if gathering at each level is costless and can only help, it is hard to see why an agent that skips it is not leaving something on the table.

Which step should I reject? Explain why that step looked irresistible, and what rejecting it costs. In particular, I want to be shown where the chain of obligations actually gives out, and why its giving out amounts to a permission to act rather than a paralysis — or, if the fault lies in (3) and (4) rather than (5), whether the value-of-information result can be re-established for hierarchies of inquiries at all, or whether it is intrinsically a theorem about a single agent with a single, already-given model, and what an agent at the bottom of such a hierarchy is then entitled to assume about its own model before it has inquired.
