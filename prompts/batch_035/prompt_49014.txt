I'm taking a third-year philosophy of mind course, and we've just finished a unit on teleosemantics and the project of naturalising mental content. My lecturer keeps reaching for an analogy that I'd like you to scrutinise, because my seminar group has started using it as if it settled things. The analogy is this: a belief is like a heart. The heart's function is to pump blood, and that fact about it is fixed entirely by the evolutionary history of the lineage — a heart that is diseased and fails to pump is still a heart *for* pumping, which is why we can call it defective rather than re-describing it as a heart for making a sloshing noise. Similarly, the thought goes, a belief's function is to represent the world truly, that function is fixed by the history of selection — whether evolutionary or some analogue like learning or cultural transmission — and this is what finally lets us say what a false belief is: a misrepresentation, the way a diseased heart is a malfunction, rather than just a state whose causal antecedents we can trace. False beliefs, on this picture, are failures to do what beliefs are for, and that is supposed to dissolve the disjunction problem that purely causal theories of content face.

My seminar group has taken this and run with it: if content is just function and function is just history, then mental content is as naturalistically respectable as physiology, and anyone who worries about meaning being irreducibly normative is making the same mistake as someone who worries about "dysfunction" being irreducibly normative. I'm suspicious that the analogy is doing more work than the arguments, but I can't quite pin down where it breaks. Some worries occur to me — Davidson's Swampman springs to mind, since a creature randomly assembled with my exact structure has the same causal dispositions but no history, and teleosemantics seems forced to say it has no content at all, which feels like the wrong result; and there seem to be cases where evolution selects for states that systematically misrepresent, like a frog snapping at BB pellets, or the classic points about adaptationist "just-so" indeterminacy, where it's not clear which of several effects of a state was the one selected *for*. But I don't know which of these disanalogies actually matter for the naturalisation project and which are incidental.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of mind with expertise in the naturalised content literature — Millikan, Neander, Papineau, Dretske and their critics are all fair game — and think carefully before answering, perhaps jotting down the possible disanalogies first and then assessing each. I'd like an explicit audit: a table or list of what corresponds to what in the heart/belief mapping, then the points where the mapping breaks, with a clear separation between the disanalogies that threaten the naturalising conclusion my classmates draw and the ones that don't. Then tell me whether the conclusion — that content is just history-fixed function, and normativity about belief is no more mysterious than talk of bodily malfunction — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was quietly carrying the whole argument. Finally, and this matters to me: say what would change your mind about your verdict — which consideration, if it could be made out, would flip you to the other side, and where in your reasoning you think you're most likely to have gone wrong.
