I run a small underwriting analytics group at a specialty insurance firm — four analysts plus me — and between us we handle maybe a dozen probability claims a week: a vendor's certified failure rate for a component we insure, a catastrophe model's annual flood probability for a river basin, a base rate for a rare liability claim pulled from another industry, each number attached to quotes that can be worth millions. Over the weekend I had a long argument with an old friend who did her PhD in philosophy of science about whether defer to the objective probability is a rule my team can actually follow. She brought up David Lewis's Principal Principle, which as she explained it says that if you know the objective chance of some event is p, and you have no inadmissible evidence about it, your credence should simply be p; if you know a die is fair, bet as if each face has a one-in-six chance. My position was that this just tells us to swallow whatever number lands on our desk, which is irresponsible when we know things the number doesn't reflect. Her position was that I was misunderstanding admissibility, the notion that screens off which of our beliefs are allowed to count alongside the chance: evidence about how the event was caused is inadmissible, while evidence about how the chance itself was determined is not, and most of what I was calling our extra information falls on one side or the other in ways I hadn't thought through. The argument ended unresolved, at around one in the morning, over the case that really divides us: a probability figure that we only possess because of how it was produced — say, we received the report because the number inside it was alarming enough to trigger a review, or the vendor who certified the failure rate is a company we partly own — where I suspect the selection process infects the number and she wasn't sure it does.

What I want is for you to turn the principle into a procedure my analysts can actually run, in fifteen minutes or less, on any probability claim that crosses their desks, with no philosophy background assumed. Please give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates with defer to the number, where it terminates with treat the number as one weighted input among several, and where it stops and hands the decision to a specific person because the remaining question is one of judgment rather than fact — and be honest about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure. Then run the procedure on three cases to show it working: first, the certified failure rate from the vendor we partly own, where our other information is that the certification process was thorough but the commercial relationship makes me suspicious; second, the flood probability from a catastrophe model that we know omits a factor our own engineers consider significant, namely the deteriorated maintenance of the levees upstream; third, the liability base rate drawn from a different industry than our client operates in, which is close but not identical. At least one of these should come out in a way I won't like — I am willing to bet it will tell me to defer to the flood model despite the omitted levee factor, and if so, say so plainly and explain what the principle is doing to us. Finish with an honest account of where the procedure will misfire, especially on the selection-effect cases that started the argument, and on what a defender of my friend's side would say the procedure gets wrong. Write it as continuous prose only, like a memo I could forward to the team: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — just paragraphs, with the ordered questions spelled out in sentences.
