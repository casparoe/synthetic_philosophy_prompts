I'm putting together a unit for a sci-fi & philosophy elective called "Strange Minds," and the segment I'm working on is about levels of description and where minds can live. The anchor text is Hofstadter's Prelude... Ant Fugue from Gödel, Escher, Bach, where the ant colony Aunt Hillary turns out to be conscious even though no individual ant is -- intelligence as a higher-level pattern over dumb lower-level parts, like how a brain is neurons none of which think. I want to pair it with Ender's Game, since the buggers seem like the perfect classroom example: the humans spend the whole book at war with a species whose soldiers look like individuals, when actually the minds live at the level of hives/queens, and the whole tragedy of the xenocide comes from humans looking for minds at the wrong level of description. Or at least that's my pitch to my co-teacher, who is skeptical of teaching a YA war novel in a philosophy class and wants to know it will earn its slot.

So here's what I want from you, acting as a philosopher of mind who knows both works well: give me a reading of the novel through the fugue's argument. I have some specific tensions I'd like you to work through, and I'd honestly rather you push back on my framing than flatter it. First, is the level-confusion reading actually right? The humans' failure to recognize bugger sentience until Ender does the empathic thing and connects with the hive queen -- is that Hofstadter's point dramatized (you will never find the colony's mind by dissecting an ant, and the humans basically spent a generation dissecting ants), or am I stretching it? Second, and this is where my co-teacher got interested: Card's hive mind seems to have a queen at the center. The soldiers are described as basically mindless extensions of her. Doesn't that make it NOT an Aunt Hillary case at all -- it's one big mind puppeting many dumb bodies, which is closer to a standard centralized mind than an emergent colony-level pattern, and arguably smuggles in exactly the kind of central executive homunculus Hofstadter is trying to get rid of? So maybe the novel cheats: it gets the emotional payoff of "the minds were there all along at a higher level" while quietly relying on there being a single somebody at the top to talk to. Ender, in the end, talks to the queen directly. Aunt Hillary's ants never had anyone to talk to.

I'd like you to brainstorm the levels question first before commiting to a reading -- like, actually map out which entities in the book are candidates for minds and at which level each one would have to live (soldier, queen, hive, species, the fleet command structure with Ender as its "neuron," even the mind game). And please be honest about where the novel resists the ant-fugue frame rather than forcing it into an illustration: does the book end up saying something the Hofstadter dialogue can't do with argument alone -- my own hunch is the moral asymmetry, that once you accept colony-level minds the xenocide isn't mass killing of soldiers but the murder of a handful of persons, which is a conclusion the fugue never has to face since no one threatens Aunt Hillary with extermination. But I'm open to being told that's backwards. If you think Speaker for the Dead changes the picture, mention it briefly, but keep the focus on the first book since that's all my students will have read. No need for quotes from either work, I know the students have the texts. Take the reading seriously in both directions -- I want to walk into that department meeting with something my co-teacher can't dismiss.
