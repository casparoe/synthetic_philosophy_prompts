At the rationalist meetup I've attended for the better part of a decade, two of our sharpest members — Elena, consistently among our best-calibrated forecasters in prediction tournaments, and Rob, who spent years doing due diligence at a venture fund — spent an entire evening arguing about Naomi's plan to leave her quant job and spend four years founding an independent research nonprofit. Naomi herself had left by then; the argument was about whether her judgment was any good. It went like this:

1. Elena opened with the outside view: Naomi puts her probability of the org achieving meaningful impact at around seventy percent, but the base rate for ambitious solo-founded research nonprofits in that timeframe is a small fraction of that, and the fact that Naomi knows her own plan in loving detail is exactly the kind of information that did not save the members of the reference class. Her credence should collapse toward the base rate.

2. Rob answered that the reference class is doing all the work and was chosen to get that answer: orgs founded by people with Naomi's specific track record of shipping hard technical projects form a different, more favorable class, and there is no principled way to pick between the classes. The discriminating evidence — her unusually strong pilot results, her team already committed — lives in the inside view, which is the only place evidence about this particular project could live.

3. Elena conceded class selection is hard, but said it cuts against him: the inside view has the same problem in worse form, since you can rationalize anything with particulars, and at least base rates are counted rather than felt. She invoked the planning-fallacy findings that the people closest to a project are the worst at predicting it, so Naomi's confidence is precisely what the founder of a doomed org would report.

4. Rob conceded overconfidence is real but said the cure is knowing about the bias, not discarding the evidence. And he raised what he called an asymmetry: the outside view, as actually practiced, only ever pushes probabilities down. Applied consistently, it forbids assigning any project a probability above its class base rate, so no exceptional project could ever be rationally undertaken — yet exceptional projects demonstrably exist and someone demonstrably undertakes them.

5. Elena then stepped back from the project to the disagreement itself: she assigns fifteen percent, Naomi seventy; per the reasoning behind agreement theorems, a peer's differing credence is itself evidence, and the policy that wins in expectation is to weight the peer's estimate rather than trust one's own chain of reasoning, because you would have run that same confident chain in worlds where you were wrong.

6. Rob replied with a regress: if Elena must weight Naomi's credence equally, Naomi must weight Elena's, and the rule yields no fixed point. Universal modesty means no one ever acts on a new argument, since every correct update in history first looked like one person's idiosyncratic reasoning; the people who actually moved the field did not average their credences with the crowd's.

7. Elena's last word was survivorship bias: he is counting the immodest winners and forgetting the far more numerous immodest losers, and from the inside every crank is sure he is the exception.

They asked me to referee and I found I genuinely couldn't. Please go through the seven moves in order, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification; identify which claims are load-bearing and which are fair concessions; say whether either of them lands a decisive point or whether each has a hidden false assumption; note what both are missing; and, if you're willing, say who is more right overall.
