Question 4: "Recall Keith DeRose's bank cases. In the low-stakes version, Hannah and her wife are driving home on a Friday, pass the bank, and consider coming back Saturday to deposit a check; nothing much rides on it, since if the bank turns out to be closed they can just go Monday. In the high-stakes version, the setup is the same except a large bill is due Monday, and if the check isn't deposited by Saturday they face a serious penalty. In both versions Hannah's evidence is exactly the same: she was at that bank two Saturdays ago and it was open, and she doesn't stop to double-check. We're inclined to say Hannah knows the bank will be open in the first version but hesitate in the second. (a) Explain how epistemic contextualism accounts for this data. (b) Explain how subject-sensitive invariantism (the pragmatic encroachment view) accounts for it. (c) Now suppose Hannah is in the high-stakes situation, but her friend — who is relaxed and doesn't care one way or the other — says 'Hannah knows the bank will be open Saturday.' What does each view say about the friend's utterance, and why?"

Her answer:

"Epistemic contextualism says that 'knows' behaves like 'tall' or 'flat': the standards for applying it are fixed by the context in which the attribution is made, so the sentence 'Hannah knows the bank will be open' can express something true when uttered in one context and something false when uttered in another. This is the view of Keith DeRose, and also of Jason Stanley. In the low-stakes version, the standards in the conversational context are low, so Hannah's evidence (she was there two Saturdays ago and it was open) easily meets them, and the attribution is true. In the high-stakes version the standards go up, and her evidence no longer clears the bar, so the attribution is false. Contextualism explains the data as a shift in the standards for the word, not a shift in Hannah's epistemic position.

Subject-sensitive invariantism, or pragmatic encroachment, keeps the meaning of 'knows' fixed across contexts. Instead, whether knowledge is present depends on the subject's practical situation: when a lot is at stake for her, she needs a stronger epistemic position to count as knowing. So in the low-stakes version Hannah knows, and in the high-stakes version she doesn't, but this time because of facts about her situation rather than about the word.

Where the subject and the attributor are the same person, as in the original cases (Hannah is talking about her own case), the two views predict the same verdicts, which is why the bank cases alone can't decide between them.

In the case where Hannah is in the high-stakes situation but her friend is relaxed, the contextualist says that what matters is the attributor's context. The friend's context is low-stakes, so the standards are low. But the point of contextualism is that the standards track how much is at stake for the subject, and here a lot is at stake for Hannah. So the contextualist has to say the friend's utterance is false. The subject-sensitive invariantist also says it's false, since for her the question is always Hannah's practical situation, and Hannah's is high-stakes. So in this case both views give the same answer, which shows the two views aren't really as different as they are usually made out to be."
