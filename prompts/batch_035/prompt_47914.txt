For twelve years I have worked as a professional dog trainer, and I am fluent in the technical vocabulary of operant conditioning as the trade actually uses it: positive and negative punishment, reinforcement schedules, extinction bursts, differential reinforcement of alternative behaviour, desensitisation and counterconditioning, and the fallout we are trained to expect when positive punishment is applied carelessly — fear, aggression, and the degradation of the relationship between handler and animal. Crucially, in my vocabulary punishment is a purely functional notion: a consequence is a punishment only if it reduces the future frequency of the behaviour it follows. It carries no connotation of desert, and nothing in the framework distinguishes between a sanction administered because it works and one administered because it was owed.

Last month my younger brother was sentenced to four years for grievous bodily harm, and I have found myself involuntarily reaching for my professional vocabulary to think about what the court did, only to discover that it does not reach. I understand, in outline, that philosophers divide over theories of punishment: retributivism, which holds that punishment is justified because the offender deserves it; and consequentialist theories, which justify punishment by its forward-looking effects — general deterrence, specific deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation — sometimes with communicative or expressive accounts positioned awkwardly between them.

Restate this debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Take the consequentialist theories first: presumably deterrence translates as punishment as a conditioned punisher for the general population, incapacitation as removal of the animal from contexts in which the behaviour occurs, rehabilitation as differential reinforcement of incompatible behaviour. Say where those translations are clean and where they distort. Then attempt retributivism: can the claim that my brother's sentence was justified by desert, rather than by any effect on his or anyone else's future behaviour, be expressed at all in a vocabulary in which punishment is defined entirely by its downstream effects on frequency? I suspect it cannot, but I want to know whether the failure is principled.

Then give me the residue report I gather is standard for this sort of exercise. Which parts of the philosophical debate survive translation, which survive only in distorted form, and which have no expression whatsoever in operant terms? And the question I actually care about: does the residue indicate that my vocabulary is missing something, or that retributivism was never a coherent claim in the first place? One consideration I keep returning to, which you should address: my vocabulary is explicitly and unapologetically consequentialist about punishment — it defines the phenomenon by its effects — so perhaps the inexpressibility of desert is no more surprising than the inexpressibility of phlogiston in the vocabulary of modern chemistry, and no more a defect of the chemistry. But perhaps the analogy fails, because the choice to define punishment functionally was itself a theoretical commitment, made by Skinner and his successors, rather than a neutral starting point. If the consequentialist bent of my trade's vocabulary is itself one side of the philosophical debate wearing a lab coat, then my inability to say anything about desert tells me nothing about whether desert is real. Tell me which way you think it goes, and why.
