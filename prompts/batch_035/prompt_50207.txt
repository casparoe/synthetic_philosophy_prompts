I co-organize a student reading group on animal ethics, and this term we've been working through moral uncertainty with a focus on insects — specifically the tension between the enormous numbers involved (roughly 10^18 insects alive at any moment, plus the recent growth of industrial insect farming for feed and protein) and our honest, quite low credence that any given insect is sentient. At our next meeting I want to replace the usual debate with a diagnostic: everyone works through an instrument silently, we score it, and then we map where the room actually sits before anyone has had a chance to anchor on labels. Would you write that instrument for me?

Please build it as a decision tree, but do the construction in the right order: first lay out the logical space of questions on which the positions genuinely come apart, and only then locate the actual defended positions as leaves. The upstream nodes should be commitments a participant can check without knowing which position they lead to. Some nodes I'd insist on: whether credence in sentience multiplies against numbers in the same way ordinary empirical probabilities do (the expected-value question); whether moral uncertainty — uncertainty not about facts but about which theory is true — should be discounted in a way empirical uncertainty isn't, as some recent moral-uncertainty literature proposes, or instead handled exactly like empirical uncertainty; whether there is a credence threshold below which a small probability simply drops out of the arithmetic rather than being multiplied; whether our intuitions about specific cases carry weight given that our indifference to insects may have an debunking evolutionary or cultural explanation; and whether the sheer scale of insect life could ever play a role other than through the multiplication itself.

The branch points themselves should be concrete cases with two or three answer options each, not abstract questions — nobody should be asked point-blank whether they think insects can suffer. Cases I want worked in, roughly eight to ten questions total: the windshield bug count on a summer drive; swatting a mosquito that may carry malaria versus the mosquito's own stake; a tradeoff where you can impose a mild, brief irritation on one human or spare ten thousand crickets being farmed and frozen; the recent experiments where bumblebees repeatedly roll small balls for no reward, and what, if anything, that should move your credence; and a case designed to test whether nociception plus behavioral flexibility is enough for moral patienthood or whether something further, like unified experience over time, is required. The leaves should include at least: the straightforward expected-value view on which insect welfare becomes a dominant cause area; the moral-uncertainty-discounting view; the view that credence should be low enough, or the nature of consciousness demanding enough, that the multiplication is simply moot; a precautionary or ambiguity-averse view on which you refuse to multiply by a number you don't have; and some non-aggregative or person-affecting position on which the arithmetic itself breaks. For combinations in the logical space that nobody actually defends, mark them as unoccupied and say in a sentence whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate given your questions — don't paper over the inseparabilities. The audience knows basic utilitarianism and decision theory but none of the literature, so assume no technical vocabulary without defining it inline.
