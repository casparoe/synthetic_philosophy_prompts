My economics department reading group has, after two years of circular argument, persuaded two senior members to run a formal adversarial collaboration on the meaning of utility, and I have been asked to draft the joint statement they should be able to sign at the end. Write that document.

The first party — call her the behaviorist — holds that preference talk in economics is shorthand for stable dispositions to choose, that utility is nothing over and above a numerical representation of consistent choice, and that Samuelson's revealed preference program succeeded in purging the discipline of unverifiable mental entities. On her view, cardinal utility is meaningful only where it can be reconstructed from choices under risk (as in the von Neumann–Morgenstern framework), interpersonal utility comparisons are not merely impractical but meaningless, and happiness research is a category error: self-reported satisfaction measures something, perhaps, but nothing the theory of choice needs or should want.

The second party — call him the mentalist — holds that utility is a psychological quantity, the intensity of preference or the valence of experience, for which choices are merely fallible evidence. He thinks the ordinalist revolution conflated an epistemic point (that utilities cannot be observed directly) with a semantic one (that there is nothing there to observe); that cardinal measurement is in principle possible, as chronometers made cardinal time measurement possible even when only ordinal comparisons were observable; and that the accumulating evidence of preference reversals, framing effects, transitivity violations, and the divergence between predicted and experienced satisfaction shows that choice is a noisy proxy for the very thing welfare economics is ultimately about.

The document should contain the following, in this order:

1. A numbered section of claims both parties can sign. These should cover the formal territory honestly: what the revealed preference axioms (WARP, SARP, GARP) actually assume; what the ordinal representation theorems establish and what they presuppose; what the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms add and what their delivered "cardinality" amounts to; and what both concede about the empirical literature on context effects and inconsistent choice.

2. A disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated twice, once in wording the behaviorist would accept as a fair statement of her position and once in wording the mentalist would accept of his. Include at minimum the status of preference (disposition to choose vs. attitude evidenced by choice), the meaningfulness of cardinal utility outside the expected-utility setting, interpersonal comparability, and whether welfare analysis should be grounded in choice, satisfaction, or something else.

3. A cruxes section. For each crux, describe a study, argument, or test that both parties would agree bears on it, and record each party's advance prediction — written so that the outcome could, in principle, embarrass them. Consider, for example: whether real-time affect reports or past choices better predict future choices in a domain where the two diverge; whether stated preferences stabilize when people are made to re-examine their options; and the redescription problem — whether the behaviorist can always absorb a seeming anomaly (say, loss aversion) by enriching the description of the choice situation, or whether that maneuver eventually becomes unfalsifiable, and whether there is a fact of the matter about which description is the right one.

4. A closing section in which the parties identify which of their disagreements are substantive and which are partly verbal — in particular, whether they ultimately disagree about what the word "preference" should mean, or about what exists — and state what it would take for each to move.

The tone should be that of a genuine joint product: no side gets the last word, no formulation is slanted, and nothing is included that one of the parties would refuse to sign.
