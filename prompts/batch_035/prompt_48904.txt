I cover science and ideas for a general readership, and after a decade of writing about consciousness research I've drifted into the camp that says consciousness, in the sense philosophers argue about, is a kind of user illusion: the brain constructs a simplified model of its own information-processing, presents that model to itself in a phenomenally rich-seeming format, and we mistake the interface for the thing. Dennett's user-illusion framing, Frankish's illusionism, and Graziano's attention schema account all strike me as versions of this basic move, and I've largely stopped writing about the "hard problem" as though it were a research problem rather than a misdescription.

What I want from you is a map of what would actually count against this view. Not a lecture on why dualists or panpsychists disagree with me — I've read those, and most of them assume what's at issue. I want the defeaters internal to the research program: what empirical findings, conceptual arguments, or formal results should genuinely worry an illusionist, and how do they rank from fatal through serious to merely awkward?

Let me sharpen the request with three specific angles, since the standard debate has gone stale. First, the self-reference worry: when an illusionist says "it seems to me that there is phenomenal redness, but the seeming is itself a construction," what could even in principle falsify that? I sometimes explain the view to editors with a dashboard metaphor — the check-engine light doesn't literally contain engine trouble, it's a display item — but I've started to suspect the metaphor hides the hard part, namely that in the brain the dashboard is supposedly looking at itself. Does that reflexivity make the view empirically self-sealing, and is self-sealingness a fatal flaw for a theory of this kind or just an unfortunate feature it shares with its rivals?

Second, the evidence asymmetry: are there actual findings on the books, or plausible near-future ones, that the illusionist and the realist about phenomenal consciousness predict differently? Consider things like covert consciousness in patients diagnosed as behaviorally unresponsive, split-brain and neglect phenomena where report and what we'd intuitively call experience come apart, psychedelic states where self-modeling reportedly breaks down while something persists, and the work on the "meta-problem" of explaining why we think there's a hard problem at all. For each: does it cut for the view, against it, or does it show that the two camps have converged on making the same predictions with different vocabulary — and if the last, say plainly whether that convergence should make me more or less confident in my position.

Third, the collapse worry: it's been argued that illusionism, once carefully stated, either quietly redefines consciousness into something the realist already accepts or quietly commits itself to the existence of the very qualia it denies. Work through whether the view can be stated in a way that avoids both horns, and identify what a critic could point to — a consequence, a case, a result — to show it cannot.

Where relevant, distinguish what illusionism's own major proponents have conceded or should concede from what only outsiders claim it implies. And don't spare me: if the honest answer is that the position is now insulated to the point of being a change of subject rather than a theory, I'd rather know that before I write my next column saying so.
