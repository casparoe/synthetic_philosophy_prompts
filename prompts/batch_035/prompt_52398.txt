At our monthly rationality meetup the post-talk argument this month was about David Lewis's account of conventions (from his book Convention, the one about coordination equilibria and common knowledge), and I got drafted to referee, which went badly, so now I'm asking you. Quick setup since it matters: on Lewis's view a convention is roughly a regularity everyone conforms to, expects everyone else to conform to, prefers to conform to given others do, where some alternative regularity could have done the same job, and where all this is mutually known in this nested "I know that you know that I know" way. OK, the argument between my two friends, Dana and Ken, went like this:

(1) Dana's opening move: Lewis basically finished off the normativity question for conventions. Once you see them as equilibria held up by expectations and preferences, there's no leftover ought — what we call the "obligation" to follow a convention is just each person playing best response to everyone else. The ought is an illusion produced by everyone happening to be in equilibrium.

(2) Ken said this gets it backwards: the expectation-plus-preference structure is precisely what naturalizes the ought instead of explaining it away. Given that everyone drives on the right and expects me to, I have a genuine reason to drive on the right, and reasons are normative. Lewis didn't kill the ought, he gave it a respectable ancestry.

(3) Dana answered that a reason conditional on your preferences is just prudence relabeled, and pointed at bad conventions like QWERTY — cases where everyone would prefer the alternative but no one can switch unilaterally, so the equilibrium persists even though we'd all be better off without it. Saying people "ought" to conform there seems exactly wrong; you ought to coordinate, not conform to whatever equilibrium history dumped on you.

(4) Ken said Dana was conflating two questions: whether the convention is good (the arbitrariness condition already concedes it was a coin-flip which regularity won) and whether there's a reason to conform once it's in place — and there is, because defecting unilaterally just imposes coordination costs on everyone else. The convention is arbitrary; the reason to follow it is not.

(5) Dana's closer: then the ought floats free of anything worth calling justification. Precedent and Schelling-style focal points do all the causal work determining which equilibrium wins, so all this normativity-by-expectation amounts to is path dependence wearing a halo. Plus, Ken's story requires the full common knowledge nesting, and most actual conformists — the ones keeping the convention alive — plausibly can't run that reasoning, so the account seems to miss the phenomenon it was supposed to capture. Ken just said Dana was sneaking in an intuition that oughts must be justified by something external, and that norms that arise from where we happen to be are still norms, like it or not.

Please adjudicate move by move: who's right about what, where does each of them make their best point and their worst slip, who's more right overall, and is there something both of them are missing? I honestly couldnt tell.
