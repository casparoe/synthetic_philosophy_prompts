A friend of mine who took an intro philosophy class in college has been trying to get me up to speed on epistemology over coffee, and the other day he explained the regress problem to me more or less like this: whenever you believe something for a reason, you can always ask what justifies *that* reason, and so on forever. So any chain of justification either goes in a circle, stops somewhere, or goes on infinitely, and all three options are supposed to be fatal — circles are circular reasoning, stopping somewhere means you're just being arbitrary, and an infinite chain means nothing is ever *actually* justified. Then he walked me through the three standard responses. Foundationalism, he said, is the view that some beliefs justify themselves — "basic" beliefs that are so obvious they are their own reason, like that I'm in pain right now — but he said that's really just a circle with one member in it, which is why Descartes apparently needed God to vouch for our basic beliefs. Coherentism, he said, is the view that a belief is *true* so long as it fits with the rest of your beliefs, and he said this has the embarrassing consequence that conspiracy theorists are fully justified, since their worldview can also be one big internally consistent circle, so coherentism is basically refuted. Infinitism, he said, holds that your chain of reasons literally never ends and you somehow have infinitely many reasons sitting in your head, which he said no actual philosopher takes serious. His takeaway was that the regress shows you ultimately just have to take something on faith, whatever your theory is called.

He was really confident, and I don't know enough to push back, but a couple things bugged me. It seemed weird to say that a belief like the pain one is "circular" in the same way a loop of many beliefs would be, and also weird that a theory about what makes beliefs *justified* would come out saying what's *true* — those feel like different questions to me, though maybe I'm missing something. And if the circle option were really as dead as he made it sound, why would anyone bother defending it in the first place?

Can you go through his explanation piece by piece and tell me which parts a philosophy professor would nod along to, which parts are just the standard simplifications everybody makes, and which parts are actually wrong or unfair to one of the three views? I'm especially curious whether the "nothing is actually justified" bit is part of the regress problem itself or something he added, and whether his big conclusion — that at bottom you have to take something on faith — really follows from the parts of his story that survive, or whether it's smuggled in. Since I can't check any of this against an actual expert myself, please be upfront about where you're least sure, what you'd expect a real coherentist or infinitist to complain about in your own reading of them, and what would make you change your mind about whether any of his characterizations are fair.
