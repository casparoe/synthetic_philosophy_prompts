I have noticed a pattern in practical life for which I cannot find a name, and I want to know whether philosophy or the theory of decision has one for it. Consider three people (composites, but each realistic):

First, a tradeswoman known in her district to pursue any cheating customer without limit — small-claims filings, public complaints, days of her time spent over sums worth a fraction of those days. She never lets a grievance drop. The odd thing is that this is why she is almost never cheated: her willingness to act against her own interest in any particular dispute is what keeps the disputes from arising at all.

Second, a man who once borrowed informally, moved abroad where no one could have collected from him, and repaid anyway. It was not any enforcement that moved him; and it was knowing in advance that he was the sort who would repay that got him the loan on favorable terms in the first place.

Third, a negotiator who once walked away from a deal at a visible loss, and is known for it. Nobody who deals with him now opens with an offer they would be embarrassed to have accepted.

The common structure, as best I can state it: at the moment of performance, the act is irrational — the costs plainly exceed the benefits, and everyone can see that they do. But the stable disposition to perform such acts is what made the earlier, beneficial arrangement possible. The irrationality of each part seems to be the rationality of the whole.

I have come across the term precommitment, but that appears to concern devices: removing options in advance, lashing oneself to the mast. In my three cases nothing is removed; the option to defect stays fully open, and the person simply declines it. I have also considered whether this is just reputation, but reputation seems to be a track record of past behavior persuading others about the future, whereas in the second and third cases the trait must be visible before there is any track record to draw on, and in the first case it operates through what people can see of her character now.

Does this configuration have an established name? Please structure your reply in four sections, in this order, at roughly the lengths indicated:

1. Nearest concepts (about 150 words) — the established concepts that come closest to what I have described, named and glossed in a sentence each.
2. Comparison (about 400 words) — one paragraph per candidate, saying in each case what it shares with the pattern above and where exactly it diverges; be disciplined about the divergences rather than assimilating everything to one idea.
3. Verdict (about 200 words) — either the single concept that fits best, together with a precise definition of it, or, if nothing established fits, a plain statement that it does not, followed by a coined term, a definition, and two boundary cases: one situation that superficially resembles the pattern but falls outside it, and one marginal case you would hesitate on.
4. What remains unsettled (about 100 words) — the questions my description leaves open, so that I can sharpen my own formulation of the phenomenon.

A bare name will not satisfy me; the point is the carving — what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
