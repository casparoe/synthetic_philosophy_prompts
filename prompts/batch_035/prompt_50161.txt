So I'm TAing a philosophy of science course and this year's unit is the problem of old evidence. The setup everyone knows: general relativity and Mercury's perihelion — the anomaly had been sitting there for decades, everyone's credence in the data was already 1, and standard Bayesian conditioning says evidence you're certain of can't confirm anything, yet intuitively that's precisely the case where GR earned its credibility. Glymour pressed this against Bayesian confirmation theory back in the 80s, and there's a pile of attempted fixes I want the students to be able to navigate without just memorizing teh names. So: build me a decision tree.

Entry point is the commitment "Mercury's perihelion confirmed GR even though the data was old news." The branch nodes should each be a question a student could answer without knowing what the standard positions are called — things like: did the confirmation happen at a moment (a change from earlier credences) or is it a relation between your current epistemic state and the theory; was anything actually learned when GR was proposed, and if so what exactly was learned; are counterfactual credences ("what I would have assigned the perihelion data if I'd never considered GR") well-defined enough to do probabilistic work with; do you want to model scientists as logically non-omniscient agents who can learn "H entails E" the way they learn ordinary evidence; and whether you're prepared to just deny the intuition that old evidence confirms.

Leaves should be the actual named positions where they exist — Howson's counterfactual fix, the Garber/Jeffrey/Niiniluoto move of putting a probability distribution over sentences so logical learning gets absorbed by conditioning, the view that nothing needs fixing because confirmation was never diachronic in the first place, and the bullet-biting leaf that says old evidence just doesn't confirm and the intuition is wrong. Plus whatever else falls out of the tree naturally. If some region of the logical space turns out to be empty, say so and guess why nobody lives there (incoherent? unattractive? just unexplored?).

Two things I want especially: flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate — my hunch is the counterfactual fix and the logical-learning fix end up equivalent once you push on them, but I've never worked that out carefully — and tell me where the tree puts the person who bites the bullet and says "fine, old evidence never confirms, but at least novel prediction still does." Is that even a stable place to stand, or does something in the neighborhood of the ad hoc hypothesis problem pull it apart?

Audience is undergrads with one semester of probability, so phrase the nodes in plain terms but don't dumb down the structure. Maybe 5-7 branch nodes and no more than a dozen leaves, otherwise they'll never get through it in one section.
