Write a dialogue, roughly 1,200 words, between two members of a philosophy-adjacent workplace committee — I'll call them the conclusion-voter and the premise-voter — over drinks the evening after a meeting at which their three-person hiring committee deadlocked in a way neither can stop thinking about. The committee had to decide whether to recommend a job candidate, and the department's written criteria say a recommendation requires all three of the following: strong research, strong teaching, and likely collegiality. Member A judged strong research, strong teaching, but not collegiality; Member B judged strong research, weak teaching, but collegial; Member C judged weak research, strong teaching, and collegial. Each premise commands a 2-1 majority, yet the candidate fails overall 2-1 — and by coincidence, a direct vote on the final recommendation would also have gone 2-1 against. My friend and I argued about this for three hours last night, and I want the dialogue to carry our disagreement faithfully.

The conclusion-voter should hold that the committee's only authoritative act is a direct vote on the verdict; decomposing the decision into premise-by-premise votes is, on her view, a procedural illusion that manufactures a "collective" position nobody actually holds. The premise-voter should hold that a committee whose written criteria are conjunctive ought to vote on the criteria, since that's the only way the group's verdict tracks the group's reasons; a direct vote, he argues, can produce a collective judgment that is unresponsive to what a majority of the members actually believe about every relevant fact. She gets to press the strongest version of her case: that premise-driven voting generates a judgment that no individual member endorses, that it lets a majority who never agreed on anything jointly overrule the actual majority, and that the very same machinery, pointed at disjunctive instead of conjunctive criteria, reverses the outcome without anyone changing their mind. He gets to press the strongest version of his: that "nobody holds the conjunction" cuts no ice, since group attitudes were never supposed to mirror anyone's attitudes; and that a group that votes only on conclusions cannot explain its decisions, cannot be held to its own criteria, and can be shown to have "believed" things a majority of it rejects.

At some point one of them — I don't care which — should propose the escape hatch of sequential voting, where the members vote on premises in turn and anyone whose overall judgment contradicts the running tally is expected to revise; and the other should object that this turns deliberation into an elimination game in which the vote's order, not the members' reasons, decides the outcome. Near the end, one of them should be genuinely tempted by the eliminativist exit — the thought that the deadlock isn't a puzzle to be solved but evidence that "what the committee believes" is a fiction, and that only individuals ever judge — while resisting actually taking it, because they both know the university will still act in the committee's name tomorrow.

Requirements: both characters must be equally competent; neither is a strawman, and each must answer the other's actual arguments rather than restating a position. No narrator, no stage directions beyond the barest scene-setting in one opening sentence, no citations or named philosophers — the argument should stand on its own content. And they should not fully resolve it: the dialogue should end with the two of them realizing that their disagreement is now about whether the committee meeting tonight counts as having happened at all, and deciding to order another round instead.
