Next month I am giving a talk to my local discussion group on a view about the direction of time that I have worked out over the past several years on my own. I have no academic background — I read popular science books, think, and take notes — and I would like you to prepare me for the question period.

First, the thesis I will be defending, in the form I will present it. Everyone agrees that time feels directed: the past seems fixed and knowable, the future seems open, and time seems to "pass." My view is that this entire asymmetry is not a feature of time itself but a side effect of a single historical fact: the universe began in an extraordinarily ordered state. The fundamental laws of physics, as far as I understand them, do not distinguish past from future — they work the same both ways. What distinguishes the directions is that in ours, ordered things are constantly degrading. My central claim is about records: a footprint, a photograph, a tree ring, and a memory are all the same kind of thing — local order that was produced by burning order somewhere else. Record-making is possible only in the direction in which entropy was lower earlier, and so a creature made of such processes will find records in exactly one direction and will call that direction "the past." If a universe had the same laws but no exceptionally ordered start, nothing in it would leave records in either direction, and nothing in that world would correspond to past and future differing — even though the laws would run exactly as ours do. On my view, then, "the flow of time" is not an illusion exactly; it is a real fact about us and the matter we are made of, misread as a fact about time.

Now, the audience, which is what I need your help with. The group meets monthly at the library; about fifteen regulars, mostly retirees. The people whose questions worry me are these. (1) A retired high school physics teacher who is a committed "block universe" man — he holds that relativity has already shown that all moments equally exist and that passage is an illusion. He will like the second half of my thesis, where I deny that anything flows, and he will object to the first half, where I say the flow is a real fact about us rather than simply a mistake. (2) A woman who spent her career doing thermodynamics in the chemical industry. She will not let loose talk about "order" and "disorder" pass, and she has strong views about what the second law does and does not assert. (3) Two members of a local church who care about the open future for moral reasons — they have read popular apologetics arguing that if the future is "already there," freedom and responsibility are undermined, and one of them has been told that Einstein proved this. (4) A man who left a philosophy PhD program years ago and now takes a deflationary line on all such questions — he will suspect that my thesis, once cleaned up, either says something trivial or contradicts itself. (5) The rest are general readers of popular science who will ask the intuitive questions — whether time travel is ruled out, whether this means the future is "already written," and so on.

Please draft my preparation sheet. I want the objections I will actually get from these specific people — not generic objections from physicists or philosophers, but the questions as the retired teacher, the thermodynamicist, the church members, and the ex-philosopher would each put them, in their own vocabulary, in roughly the order they are likely to come and ordered by how much damage each could do to my talk. For each, give me a reply I could deliver in about a minute, a note on where that reply is genuinely weak and what I should concede rather than bluff, and mark which objections have real force against my position as opposed to merely sounding impressive. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me what to say if it comes anyway. Finally, identify the softest point in my own thesis — the place where I should prepare a defense in advance rather than hope it is never probed — and, since I have no credentials and no training, advise me on how to respond when someone, as someone eventually will, asks whether any of this is established physics or just something I made up.
