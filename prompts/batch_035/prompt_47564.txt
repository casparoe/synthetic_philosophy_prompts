I'm prepping a seminar session on explaining mass killing, and I want you to roleplay as a historian of the holocaust who's also comfortable with social psych. think about it carefully before commiting to anything, maybe do a quick brainstorm first.

The case: Reserve Police Battalion 101 and the Józefów massacre. Major Trapp, visibly distressed, offers any man who doesnt feel up to it the chance to step out; only around a dozen do. The rest spend the morning shooting at point-blank range, and most keep participating in the months that follow. Goldhagen reads this as showing that ordinary Germans were driven by eliminationist antisemitism; Browning reads it situationally.

Give me a breakdown of how much explanatory work each of these does, as percentages that must sum to 100: (a) ideological antisemitism, (b) conformity/obedience to authority, (c) primary-group loyalty, i.e. not wanting to dump the awful work on your comrades, (d) careerism and fear of sanctions, (e) gradual desensitization over time. For each, one sentence on the single piece of the Battalion 101 record it handles best and one on where it strains.

Then a second layer: if I swap in a different perpetrator case — say an interahamwe group in Rwanda, or men running deportations for a Soviet agency — which of the five percentages do you actually have to revise, and why? Don't waffle with "it's a mix of factors" — commit to numbers and defend them.
