My friend has opinions on literally every response to the problem of evil, and after years of arguments I've collected a decent track record of her verdicts. Moral evil: she accepts the free will defense, though she's fussy that it only has to show God couldn't have gotten significantly free creatures with less evil — she's always insisting nobody knows God's actual reasons for permitting anything. Soul-making she rejects flat; the claim that suffering builds character is empirical, and for the suffering that breaks people it's just false. On wild animal suffering she's a proper skeptical theist — we have no grip at all on what God's reasons could be, so predation and parasitism aren't evidence against God. For natural evil she likes the regularity-style defense: stable laws are what make free, significant action possible in the first place, natural evil is a byproduct, and crucially nobody is claiming the harm does the victim any good. And she rolls her eyes at anything that tells the sufferer their pain isn't really bad or is secretly good for them.

Last week someone pitched her a new one, the heaven version: God permits each horror because the sufferer will recieve, in the afterlife, goods so great and so interwoven with the suffering itself that from inside heaven they wouldn't wish any part of their life away — the suffering isn't merely outweighed by some later good, it's defeated, made part of a whole the sufferer themself endorses. Will she accept it? I care less about the yes/no than about the rule (or rules) she seems to be running — I can see at least two that fit her track record equally well but split apart right about here — plus your best guess and your reasons for it, and what case you'd put to her next to figure out which rule it actually is.
