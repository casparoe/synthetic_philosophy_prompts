I'm taking a philosophy of physics course and we just worked through the Newton–Leibniz fight over absolute space, with the rotating bucket as the centerpiece (plus some of the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence). Next week I have to write a dialogue between two characters for class, and honestly I'd like to hear a good one before I attempt mine. Could you write a dialogue between a substantivalist (space exists in its own right, and the water climbing the bucket walls is evidence of motion relative to space itself, not relative to the bucket) and a relationist taking roughly the Machian line (only distances and relations between material things are real, so if anything is doing the work it's the matter distributed around the bucket, not some invisible container)? Please make them equally good at this — the relationist shouldn't be a pushover, and the substantivalist shouldn't just keep repeating "but the water goes concave."

A couple of twists I want worked in. First, at some point have the relationist bring up Newton's other example — the two globes connected by a cord, rotating in otherwise empty space, where the tension in the cord is supposed to reveal true rotation — and see how she uses it and how he answers. Second, I want them to hit a scenario neither finds entirely comfortable: a universe containing only the bucket, no stars, no distant masses, nothing else. The relationist has to say something about whether the water still climbs the walls, and if her view implies it can't, that's a real cost she has to own. The substantivalist, for his part, has to explain what it even means for empty space to be the thing the water rotates relative to, given that no point of it can be marked or observed. Third, let the relationist try flipping the bucket against him: if uniform straight-line motion relative to absolute space has no observable effect at all, why does rotation, when both are equally motions relative to the very same space? I want him to actualy give an answer rather than dodge.

One more thing: have them genuinely engage — responding to each other's actual moves, conceding small points, and eventually escalating to their deepest commitments (sufficient reason for her, and whatever his equivalent is, maybe something about there being a fact of the matter about motion that isn't constituted by observables). They don't need to reach agreement — I'd rather it end with each one naming the single assumption they'd refuse to give up even if holding onto it costs them the argument. Bonus points if somewhere in there the relationist gets tempted by a view she doesn't officially hold, like maybe space is real but derivative from the laws rather than the other way around, and the substantivalist calls her out for it.
