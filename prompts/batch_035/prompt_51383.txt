I've been hanging around the rationalist-adjacent interpretability discourse for a long time now — the circuits thread, feature visualization, the "simulators, not agents" debates, the recurring fights about whether a model "really" has goals — and I've noticed that a lot of these arguments stall on a question nobody bothers to state explicitly: whether a trained network has determinate representations at all. One camp is realist about interpretability: there is a fact of the matter about what a given subnet computes or represents, and better tools will converge on it. The other camp is instrumentalist: the network is just weights, any decomposition into features or world-models is a choice of lens, and different lenses can be equally good. What bothers me is that in every actual thread I've read, both camps agree about all the experimental results on the table and still disagree, which makes me suspect the disagreement only becomes substantive in certain specific kinds of cases — and I'd like a stock worked example I can bring to these arguments instead of watching people gesture at each other.

So: construct one concrete, mechanistically plausible interpretability scenario in which the realist and the instrumentalist are forced to give different answers to the same question about the same set of weights. You may invent the network, the training task, and the finding, as long as the details are the sort of thing that could plausibly appear in a real interpretability paper. I'd rather you avoid the stock moves everyone reaches for — polysemanticity, "is there a grandmother neuron" — and instead build the scenario around something subtler. For instance (but feel free to do better): a case where two different interpretability methods each yield a clean, internally coherent, predictively successful account of what a single subnetwork computes, and the two accounts genuinely disagree; or a case where the most predictive decomposition of the network carves things at an angle that no human would accept as "what the model represents."

For whatever scenario you construct, I want three things spelled out. First, exactly where the realist locates the additional fact that settles which account is correct — what in the weights, the training history, or the counterfactual behavior could make one story the story — and what the instrumentalist says about why there is no such fact to be had. Second, what follow-up experiment each camp would propose in response to the finding, so it's visible that their research programs actually diverge rather than just their slogans. Third, your own judgment about whether the scenario is one where realism genuinely pays rent: does one camp's picture predict something the other's cannot, or does the disagreement evaporate once the experiment is run? Please don't cop out with "it's interpretation all the way down" — I can get that in any comment thread. I want the worked example, with the disagreement pinned to a specific place in it.
