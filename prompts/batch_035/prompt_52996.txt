I maintain a small ethics blog aimed at upper-level undergraduates, and I would like a diagnostic quiz sorting readers by what they believe about supererogation: the category of acts that are good to do but not wrong to omit. The quiz must sort readers by their responses to cases rather than by their allegiance to labels, so no question may simply ask whether the reader believes in going beyond the call of duty, and every answer option must be phrased in terms a reader with no philosophical training could endorse or reject after a moment's reflection.

Please write ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the following five leaves: (1) the classical three-category view, on which supererogation is a distinct moral category alongside the obligatory and the forbidden; (2) maximizing consequentialism, on which any act that does the most good at acceptable cost is required, so apparent supererogation is an illusion generated by bias toward the near and the identifiable; (3) satisficing consequentialism, on which we are required only to do enough, so heroic maximizing is optional in a derivative sense; (4) latitude views, on which wide duties leave agents discretion about when and how much to help, and exercising that discretion beyond the minimum is meritorious; (5) agent-centered prerogative views, on which the permission to give disproportionate weight to one's own projects explains what consequentialists miscall supererogation.

The cases must include, in some form: (a) a benefit to another person that costs the agent nothing at all, testing whether costless benevolence can be optional or is instead required; (b) a rescue-type scenario whose price to the agent varies across questions, so that the reader's answers jointly reveal where their threshold of obligation sits; (c) a series of individually trivial omissions that are collectively bad, testing whether the reader treats each small decline as permitted even when the pattern of declining is culpable; (d) a promise to perform what would otherwise be a supererogatory act, testing whether the reader takes the resulting obligation to be as strict as an ordinary promise or somehow softer; and (e) a case in which someone declines to do the good act, testing whether the reader thinks a moral remainder survives the absence of wrongdoing: something owed by way of apology or regret, without anything wrong having been done.

The scoring key should close with two further notes. First, identify which pairs of leaves the quiz cannot reliably separate, and state what further case would be needed to separate them; satisficing and prerogative views, for instance, may agree on everything above. Second, identify any combination of answers that no leaf accommodates, and say in a sentence whether that pattern is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored in the literature.
