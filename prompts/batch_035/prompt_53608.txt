In discussions of small-state security, I keep running into the porcupine analogy: Switzerland's armed neutrality is often explained by saying the country is like a porcupine — a small animal no predator bothers to attack because, while it cannot win a fight, it is too costly to swallow. Lately the same image is invoked for Taiwan (the so-called porcupine strategy) and sometimes for Finland's and Sweden's Cold War territorial defense, always to argue that a small state with universal conscription and a whole-of-society defense can deter a vastly larger neighbor without formal alliances. I would like an audit of this analogy rather than a defense of it, along the following lines.

First, give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the quills (the conventional army, the mountainous terrain, the réduit, stockpiles and civil defense, society-wide rifle ownership?), what plays the body (the population, the economy, the territory to be seized?), what plays the predator (a rational, cost-calculating aggressor — or an ideological or opportunistic one?), and what the act of swallowing is supposed to be (occupation, annexation, imposing a government?).

Second, lay out where the mapping breaks. A porcupine cannot lose its quills to an arms embargo, does not depend on seaborne trade for its calories, cannot be threatened into shedding its quills to avoid a war it would lose anyway, and its quills evolved against predators that avoid injury because injured predators starve — which is not obviously the cost function of an aggressor who can absorb heavy losses for symbolic, ideological, or prestige reasons. A porcupine also cannot have its deterrent devalued by enemy countermeasures developed over decades.

Third, separate the disanalogies that matter for the actual use of the analogy — deciding whether a small state should adopt conscription-based armed neutrality rather than alliances, professionalization, or some mix — from those that are merely colorful.

Fourth, say whether the conclusion the analogy is used to support, namely that armed neutrality is a viable general strategy for small states, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the porcupine was quietly doing the argumentative work all along — for instance by importing the assumption that the aggressor's only relevant motive is hunger.

Finally, if you think a better analogy is available from ordinary life or biology — a wasp's coloration, a hive that dies with its sting, a fortified city that trades sieges for starvation — say what it captures that the porcupine misses, and note what it in turn smuggles in.
