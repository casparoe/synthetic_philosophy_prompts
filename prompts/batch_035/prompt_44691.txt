The debate over logical omniscience has, as far as I can tell, been running in place for half a century, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another entry in it.

To fix the terrain: on one side stand those who treat logical omniscience as a deliberate and harmless idealisation. In the Hintikka-style epistemic logics, an agent's knowledge is closed under known implication (if she knows p and knows that p entails q, she knows q); in orthodox Bayesianism, her credence in every logical truth is 1 and her degrees of belief obey the probability calculus exactly. Defenders of these frameworks say this is like frictionless planes in mechanics: you abstract away from the mess to see the underlying structure, and actual agents approximate the ideal in something like the way real inclined planes approximate Galileo's. On the other side stand the dissatisfied: the bounded-rationality tradition, impossible-worlds semantics, awareness logics, and those who point out that the 'noise' being abstracted away — the fact that deriving consequences is computationally hard, that the inference problem sits at unpleasant complexity classes, that no possible agent could satisfy the idealisation even approximately — is not noise at all but constitutive of agency. On this view, an idealisation no possible agent could even approach is not a simplification but a change of subject, closer to modelling flight with a perpetual motion machine than with a frictionless plane. The standard manoeuvres are all well known — the Dutch book argument that only a logically omniscient bookie could exploit a merely bounded agent; the reply that this shows the bookie, not the agent, is doing the idealising; the impossibility results for logics that try to weaken closure; the complaint that impossible worlds let agents believe outright contradictions — and yet, so far as I can see, nobody has moved in decades.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: one is doing normative theory for an ideal arbiter whose verdicts settle what actual agents fall short of, the other is doing descriptive or engineering work on agents we can actually build or observe, and the apparent clash comes from each side quietly assuming the other's question is a category mistake. But I have a second suspicion I'd like you to engage with seriously, because I coined it myself and can't tell whether it's illuminating or merely neat. It is that the dispute turns on whether logic is being treated as part of the agent's environment or as part of her competence — as something she can be uncertain about, in the way she is uncertain about the weather, or as something she cannot fail at without failing to count as an agent at all. The idealisation side seems to hold the second view of logic and the first view of, say, empirical facts; the bounded side wants logic on the environment side of the line; and perhaps neither has noticed that they draw the line in different places.

So: diagnose the disagreement. Is it verbal, substantive but empirical, substantive but formal, methodological, or two questions under one name?
