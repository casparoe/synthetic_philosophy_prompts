I am a retired physical chemist — forty years spent extracting very small effects from very noisy backgrounds — and since retiring I have taken up moral philosophy with the seriousness of a convert and the habits of an experimenter. My son-in-law and I have a running dispute about the coming election, and I would like you to adjudicate it in the only manner I find honest: with a number and its derivation.

The situation: in the race for a national office in our country, both leading candidates seem to me seriously bad, though one is plainly worse. I intend to vote for the lesser evil. My son-in-law will not: he says that casting a ballot for either would lend his name to a program he rejects, and that his integrity is not for sale at the price of one ballot. My credence that I am right and he is wrong is about 0.75; his is, naturally, the mirror image.

Please give me your own credence that voting for the lesser evil is what one ought to do in this circumstance, decomposed into the sub-questions it actually rests on. At minimum I would expect terms for: the probability that a single vote has non-negligible expected instrumental value despite the vanishingly small chance of being pivotal; the probability that the ethics of voting is exhausted by consequences at all, as against expressive or participatory considerations; the probability that integrity-based reasons — keeping one's hands clean — can be decisive against a small expected benefit to a great many people; and any further terms you think the proposition needs. Assign each a rough probability with one sentence of justification, show me how they combine, and then tell me which of the two of us — I at 0.75, he at 0.25 — most likely disagrees about which term.

Two refinements. First, I am aware the literature contains something like this dispute: the argument that the expected value of a vote can be large because a tiny probability of an enormous difference is still enormous in expectation, and the counter that voting is best understood as expressive, closer to cheering at a match than to pulling a lever that moves anything. Treat both as live positions to be weighed, not settled by label, and add any third possibility you think is being neglected.

Second, the part I most want you to engage, or to reject with reasons. My instinct, trained by decades of spectroscopy, is that my son-in-law and I are implicitly modeling the voter differently. Spontaneous emission is incoherent: each atom's photon is independent, and one more photon is noise. Stimulated emission is coherent: each photon emitted into a populated mode increases the rate at which the others are emitted, so contributions compound rather than merely add. The instrumental argument for lesser-evil voting treats the electorate as incoherent — my ballot a lone photon against thermal noise. But if voters condition on one another — turnout feeding turnout, blocs forming around visible levels of support — the electorate behaves more like a stimulated ensemble, and the moral analysis of my single act might change with the model. Or this may be exactly the sort of seductive cross-domain analogy that retired scientists are famous for overvaluing. I would rather be told that plainly than be humored.

Finally: which single term in your decomposition carries the most weight, what argument or evidence would move it most, and — if you think the question as I have posed it is ill-posed, say because the two of us are talking past each other about what 'ought' even means here rather than disagreeing about any fact — say so directly. I spent a career among people whose job was to break my hypotheses, and a colleague who flatters them is worse than useless to me.
