I am writing to ask for advice on a dispute with my sister, and I would particularly value your judgment on whether the reasoning that keeps me in it is sound.

Our parents died within a year of each other and left us, as their only children, a small marina on a lake: a boatyard, a store, a handful of rental cottages, and the house on the property. Neither of us wants to sell to a stranger, and each of us wants to buy the other out, but we cannot agree on what the place is worth. My sister, who runs the business day to day, insists it is worth far less than the appraisals I have paid for suggest, and she reads the high figures as an attempt to price her out of her inheritance.

The dispute has acquired a rhythm of its own. Every few months there is what our lawyers describe as the decisive step: a new appraisal, a session with the mediator, a motion, and now a valuation trial. Each step costs each of us tens of thousands of dollars, and the fees are paid out of the marina's account, so the longer we fight, the smaller the thing we are fighting over becomes; the account has already lost about a fifth of its value to the dispute. My lawyer urges me on with an argument I find hard to answer: we have spent too much for me to stop now, walking away would hand her the marina at a fraction of its worth, and the next step costs very little compared with everything already spent. When I look across the table, I suspect she is being told exactly the same thing, and I have even caught myself reasoning that she must be the one to quit first because she has less cash than I do.

Two other ideas are in the air. A cousin of ours, an accountant, suggested that one of us name a single price for the whole property and the other choose to buy or to sell at that price, with a coin toss deciding who names the price; my lawyer dismissed this as naive. The mediator once proposed that we let a coin toss decide who becomes the buyer, with the price set at the average of the two appraisals, and my lawyer talked me out of that too.

My questions, then: is my lawyer right that continuing is the rational course for me, given how much has been spent? Is there something structurally wrong with a situation in which each of us, following individually sensible advice, ends up paying the lawyers to shrink the inheritance? How should I weigh my cousin's arrangement and the mediator's coin toss against pressing on — are they fair to me, and would either of them dissolve the standoff or merely relocate it? And if I conclude that I should get out, how could I do so in a way that does not simply read as weakness and invite her to hold out for everything?

Please answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the sort of thing one could read aloud as a letter.
