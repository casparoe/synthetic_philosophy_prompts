I'm writing a paper on the normative foundations of promising, and before I engage with any particular theory I want to work out the specification any such theory should be held to. The purpose I want the theory to serve is practical: it should be good enough to guide a deliberating agent who is deciding whether, here and now, she is bound — including hard cases where her grip on her own obligations is exactly what is in question. Ideal-theoretic accounts that only tell us what obligations a fully just society's promises generate are not what I'm after.

Please write the desiderata. For each, give the motivating case or consideration, and mark it either non-negotiable or a camp preference (as you would with locality in interpretations of quantum mechanics — the analogue here being, say, the demand that promissory obligation be recoverable from more general moral considerations, which some philosophers treat as a constraint and others as question-begging reductionism). I have my own running list; to save you the guesswork about which cases I take to matter, I'll tell you what's on it, though you should feel free to add or contest items.

The theory must handle: promises extracted under coercive duress by an unjust regime, where refusal meant death; promises whose performance benefits the promisee not at all and harms the promisor greatly (the "give me the kidney I asked you for" case); promises made to someone who will never learn whether they were kept, including promises to the dead; promises released by the promisee at no cost to anyone, and the question of why release is even needed if the obligation arose from the promise's earlier effects; the difference between promising and mere prediction about one's own future conduct ("I promise I'll be there" versus "I'll be there, you can count on it" from someone with a known habit of showing up); and a case I've not seen discussed much, which I'd like you to take seriously: a promise given by a promisor who believes, with justification, that the promisee would prefer the promise to be broken and only extracted it out of politeness, so that the promisee's welfare, the promisor's own preferences, and the promise's continued existence all point the same way, and yet breaking it without asking still seems wrong. Does a theory that can't say why that is wrong count as a theory of promising at all?

Beyond the cases, the theory must respect certain distinctions I take to be data: between being obligated to perform and owing performance; between the wrong of breaking a promise and the wrong of making it negligently; and between promissory obligation and the quite different machinery of contract, which invokes remedial duties rather than the primary one. It should also say something about promises to oneself, which I suspect are either a trivial extension or a reductio, and I'd like to know which before building them in.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion: the desiderata cluster into two groups that cannot be jointly satisfied — one family of intuitions treats the promise as fundamentally an act of assurance that works by changing what the promisee may rely on, and so ties the obligation to the promisee's reliance and their standing to release; another family ties it to something the promisor does to herself, a self-imposed obligation whose persistence through promisee indifference is precisely the point. The dead-promise and reluctant-promisee cases seem to me to pull toward the second, the duress and kidney cases toward the first. If you think some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly — that is the part I most want to see.

Finally, and briefly, since I will do this part myself at length: score three families of views — social-practice or convention theories, assurance or reliance theories, and commitment-of-will theories — against your list, and say which single desideratum does the most to exclude candidates.

Please write your answer in British English.
