I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, and I run a small Slack channel where a few of us argue about decision theory puzzles over lunch. The one that's eaten most of our bandwidth lately is the absent-minded driver. The problem is half the channel uses the same words to mean different things, so I want a quiz that sorts people by what they actually believe about the case, not by which label they like. Maybe you can write it for me.

In case you don't have the details handy, here's the setup as I understand it (my own phrasing, standard numbers): a driver is going home and will pass two identical intersections in a row. He can't tell them apart, and at either one he never remembers whether he already passed an intersection — that's the absent-mindedness, and crucially he knew in advance he'd be exactly this confused at the wheel, so being confused is not news to him. If he exits at the first intersection, that's the worst outcome (call it 0). If he continues and exits at the second, that's the best outcome (4). If he continues past both, that's middling-bad (1). I worked out that if he commits in advance to a mixed strategy — continue at each intersection with some fixed probability, roll the dice, and dont look back — the best mix is continue 2/3 of the time, expected value 4/3. Sanity-check me on that math please. The fight on our channel starts at the intersection itself: he's sitting at one of the two, can't tell which, and the question is what "rational" even demands of him right there. Some of us say the plan is sacred and this moment is just execution. Some say he should re-open the question on the spot. Some say both rankings are legitimate, they're just answers to different questions. And at least one person thinks the whole puzzle is evidence something in the standard machinery is broken.

So here's the ask: a decision tree / sorting quiz. Around 6-10 branch nodes, each phrased as a concrete case someone can answer without knowing where the branch leads. Cases I'd like you to use or improve on (add your own if they split the camps better):

- Before setting out he installs a randomizer he can't override, set to continue 2/3 of the time. Once he's on the road he realizes he can pay a small fee to have it removed, restoring his ability to decide at each intersection. Does he pay? I.e., is on-the-spot deliberation worth anything to him?
- He's at an intersection. A bystander standing next to the car, who also can't tell which intersection this is, offers free advice. Should the right advice match the plan? And does it matter whether the bystander is advising "you, right here at this node" versus "you, the whole guy over the whole drive"?
- Same scenario, but his amnesia was self-inflicted (he took a forgetting pill on purpose) versus an accident (bump on the head). Does that difference matter, or is it morally/theoretically idle?
- At an intersection it dawns on him that his own present action might be evidence about which one he's at — if drivers like him tend to continue, then continuing hints he already continued once before. Does his own current act count as evidence about his own past behavior, and should that influence what he does now?

Leaves I need on the tree: (1) the plan-first camp — deliberation happened at planning time, the intersection is pure execution; (2) re-deliberation at the node, whatever that ends up implying for what he should do; (3) the "two legitimate questions, two answers, the paradox is just conflation" camp; (4) the "no stable advice exists here — every recommendation undermines itself — and that's a real finding about decision theory, not a curiosity" camp; (5) the evidential-flavored camp that uses his own act as information, if you think it's genuinely distinct from the others rather than a subtype.

Please build the tree from the logical space first — which questions could in principle seperate any two positions, then where the real camps actually sit — and flag (a) any two leaves your tree can't cleanly separate, and (b) any branch combination that nobody plausibly occupies, with a guess at whether that's because the combo is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Give the leaves plain-English names we could actually use in Slack, not latin. Dont bother with citations, author names, or reading lists — I don't care who said it first, I care about the reasoning, and I'm not going to look anything up anyway.

One more thing: if you think this quiz as I've specced it can't be done well — say, because my branch questions already bake in answers, or because the puzzle is an artifact that doesn't deserve a taxonomy in the first place — tell me that plainly instead of producing something polite but useless. I'd genuinely rather hear the objection. Answer in American English, and go easy on the jargon — zero formal background over here.
