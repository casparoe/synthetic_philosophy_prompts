For most of my adult life I treated philosophy of mind as a spectator sport, but this past year it stopped being optional for me: after a stretch of severe work-related burnout I began having episodes of depersonalization, hours at a time in which my own inner monologue felt like something I was overhearing rather than something I was saying. My therapist describes it as common and benign, but it has made the idea that the self is a kind of user interface feel less like a clever thesis and more like something I have accidentally glimpsed from the outside. While in that mood I rewatched Being John Malkovich, and it hit me very differently than it did when I first saw it: the film now looks to me like an extended thought experiment about exactly the interface picture of consciousness, and I want help working out whether that reading actually holds.

Here is the reading I want tested. On the user-illusion line of thought, the phenomenal self is a representation the brain builds for control purposes, like a desktop that shows icons rather than the circuitry underneath. Kaufman and Jonze's film seems to dramatize this in at least three registers: the ordinary fifteen-minute rides, where customers are pure passengers watching through Malkovich's eyes with no more control than a user has over the operating system; the middle act, where Craig figures out how to stay inside and puppet Malkovich, a kind of demon process seizing the self-model from within; and the ending, where Craig is permanently embedded in the young girl, awake and aware behind her eyes forever, apparently unable to act at all. And then there is the scene where Malkovich himself goes through his own portal and finds a world where everyone has his face and can only say his name — which reads to me like the self-model suddenly becoming visible as a model, the interface glitching into view. But here is my worry, and I'd like you to take it seriously rather than talk me out of it for the sake of a tidy essay: the interface view is supposed to dissolve the inner spectator, not install one, whereas the film keeps putting a little person behind the eyes — someone who watches, or someone who grabs the wheel. Doesn't the portal mechanic smuggle back the very Cartesian theater the user-illusion story was invented to empty out? Is the film secretly dualist in a way that undermines the reading, or does it actually show something the theory needs and doesn't say: what the interface is for, and what it would be like to lose control of it without losing the show?

I've been asked to introduce a screening of the film for a small philosophy-of-film society I belong to, so I need a reading that is faithful in both directions — one that doesn't reduce the film to an illustration of Dennett or Metzinger, and doesn't bend their views to fit the plot. Please tell me where the reading genuinely illuminates a scene or a character's choice (Lotte's transformation seems especially rich here), where the film resists the reading and an honest critic has to concede the mismatch, and what, if anything, the film adds to the debate that the philosophical arguments alone don't give us. And please don't pad the answer with reading lists or citations — the audience is general, not academic, and I want ideas I can actually speak to, not a bibliography.
