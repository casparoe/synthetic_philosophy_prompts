I work on mechanistic interpretability of language models. The vocabulary I use daily comprises probing classifiers, ablation and activation-steering experiments, features and circuits, and more recently sparse autoencoders that decompose activations into candidate feature directions. My group has a standing disagreement that we have never managed to make precise. When a linear probe reads dog-related information off some layer, and ablating that direction reliably changes dog-related behavior, one faction says the model has a dog feature — something in the network that our tools located. The other faction says this is our own ontology projected onto a matrix factorization: for any probeable quantity there exists some direction that decodes it, and we all know the failure mode in which probes extract information that the model itself makes no use of, so decoding is not the same as the model representing. A philosopher friend of mine remarked that the philosophy of mind has been having our argument for fifty years under other names, and I would like to see whether that is true.

Restate the central philosophical positions on what it takes for an internal state to carry content in my vocabulary, one position at a time, as faithfully as you can. I mean at least these three. First, informational or causal theories of content, on which representation is a matter of reliable indication — this looks like the easiest to translate, since indication appears to be what our correlational probes measure, so say exactly where the translation bends. Second, teleosemantics, on which content is fixed by what a trait was selected to do; presumably whatever the training process selected the internal state for plays the role of selection, so spell out what the analogue of a selected function is and where training-as-selection disanalogy distorts the view. Third, Dennett's intentional stance, on which attributing beliefs is justified when doing so compresses and predicts the system's behavior — which I suspect is uncomfortably close to what we actually do when we name a feature after whatever steers it. For each position, say explicitly what carries over, what has to be distorted to fit, and what cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. Where a philosophical distinction corresponds to one of ours — for instance the distinction between a probe that merely decodes and a feature the model causally uses, or the question of whether the model's own basis is privileged over a basis we impose — make the correspondence explicit rather than smuggling it in.

Then give me the residue report and a verdict. I particularly want your view on two items I cannot currently formulate at work. The first is misrepresentation: we can say the network classifies an input wrongly, but we have no vocabulary for the network being wrong about what its own feature is a feature of — the difference between a network that correctly represents tails as parts of dogs and one that misrepresents them. The second is normativity: our causal interventions tell us what a feature does, but nothing in my vocabulary says what it is supposed to do. So, at the end, answer the question that the residue raises: does it show that my vocabulary is missing something the philosophers can supply, or does it show that their debate is less substantive than it looks because, in the regime that matters to me, the correlational/causal distinction already does the work they assign to content? Please write your response in British English.
