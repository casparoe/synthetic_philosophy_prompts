You are a professional philosopher specializing in collective responsibility, political reconciliation, and the ethics of official remembrance, and you also have practical experience drafting documents for public bodies. I need your help with a genuinely contested question before my committee, and I want you to reason through the hard parts rather than reaching for boilerplate.

I serve on the Ad Hoc Reconciliation Committee of the city of Aldermoor. In 1962, our city council used federal urban-renewal funds and eminent domain to clear the Bottoms, a predominantly Black neighborhood of roughly 900 households, to make way for a highway spur. The clearance destroyed forty-one Black-owned businesses, two churches, and the city's only Black-owned bank. Here is the detail that keeps surfacing at our meetings: the highway spur was cancelled in 1965, two years after clearance was completed, for cost reasons. No highway was ever built. The land sat as vacant parcels for a decade and now holds a storage facility and a surface parking lot. A descendant-led organization, the Bottoms Families Alliance, is petitioning the council to adopt a formal resolution of apology. Their petition asks for three things: (1) an apology issued in the name of the City of Aldermoor; (2) the specific word "atonement" rather than "regret" or "apology" alone, on the grounds that "regret" implies sorrow without responsibility; and (3) an annual scholarship fund of $75,000, seeded from the city's general fund.

The committee is deadlocked, roughly along these lines. Three members argue that a resolution is owed: the city, as a corporate entity, did the wrong, the city still exists, and an institution can apologize for its own past acts through its current officers — this is no different, they say, from a corporation apologizing for a defective product from decades ago. Two members argue that the resolution as drafted fails: an apology in the name of the city puts words of culpability in the mouths of current residents, most of whom are not descended from anyone involved in 1962 and many of whom are descendants of people the city has itself wronged in other ways; asking them to accept a statement of atonement, and to fund it, confuses the moral ledger. One member supports the apology but opposes any material component, on the ground that an apology followed by a payment looks like the city purchasing absolution, and that $75,000 spread over an unlimited class of descendants is a sum so small it functions as symbolism wearing the costume of compensation. The city attorney has separately warned us that the word "atonement," in context, is more likely than "apology" to be cited in future litigation against the city, though she concedes the legal exposure is uncertain.

There is also the unfinished-business problem that several committee members keep circling but not naming: the fact that the highway was never built. Some feel this aggravates the wrong (the destruction was not merely a cost of some greater public good that at least materialized; it was destruction for nothing, and the city has since benefited from land acquired at clearance prices). Others worry it cuts the other way: it makes the episode a tragic blunder rather than an injustice, which an apology for wrongdoing may misdescribe.

Please draft the written recommendation I will circulate before our next meeting. Take the role of an expert advisor to the committee. Before drafting, work through the conceptual issues carefully — whether and how a continuing institution can apologize for acts committed by long-dead officeholders; whether an apology's addressees can include people who are themselves deceased, and what it means for a resolution to be addressed to their descendants; the difference between expressing regret, accepting responsibility, and atoning; and whether an apology without any material component is meaningful or is merely costless speech. Then produce the memo itself, with these features:

1. A clear bottom-line recommendation on each of the three petition items — issue or not, "atonement" or different language, fund or no fund — including any conditions or modifications you would attach.
2. Careful, non-strawmanned treatment of the strongest consideration on the side your recommendation rejects, not merely the side it adopts.
3. An explicit answer to the unfinished-business problem: does the fact that the highway was never built change what kind of response the wrong calls for, and should the resolution's language acknowledge it?
4. Draft language (one or two sentences) for the operative clause of the resolution, if you recommend issuing one.
5. Practical guidance the committee can act on: what the resolution should and should not claim about present residents' culpability, and how to handle the legal concern without letting it silently determine the moral question.

Keep the memo under 1,200 words and write it so that a city council member with no philosophical training could follow every argument in it.
