I am a novelist with a chapter problem, and I would like to use you as a rehearsal partner. My novel is set in Vienna in 1931, in the middle of the Social Democratic municipal experiment people are already calling Red Vienna, and its narrator is a washed-up feuilleton critic who has just been dragged through the Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum by his sister, a schoolteacher convinced that its walls of pictorial statistics constitute adult education rather than municipal propaganda. The chapter turns on a confrontation between this narrator and the museum's founding director, Otto Neurath. I know what my narrator thinks; what I do not know yet, and cannot fake, is how Neurath would actually answer him. So: please play Neurath in the winter of 1931 -- energetic, opportunistic, utterly without metaphysical embarrassment -- and stay in character until I write 'stop'. Give him his real resources: the Vienna Method's rules (the same-sized pictogram repeated rather than enlarged, the fixed value per symbol, no perspective), his work with Gerd Arntz and Marie Reidemeister, the charts made for the Soviet institute, his physicalism and the anti-metaphysical spirit of the Vienna Circle, and his view of all knowledge as a boat rebuilt plank by plank at sea, never resting on a dock. He may concede small points -- he was cheerfully upfront about conventions -- but never the central claim, which is that visual education gives ordinary citizens a purchase on social facts that expert prose withholds from them. Keep each reply to one paragraph, spoken in his voice, and let him be witty and slightly evasive when cornered, as a man running an underfunded museum in a collapsing republic has every right to be.

Here is my narrator's opening move, which I will paste as his line: Dr. Neurath, your museum's catchphrase says that to remember simplified pictures is better than to forget accurate figures, but that is exactly my complaint. The simplification is not a neutral courtesy extended to the unlettered; it is where the interpretation happens. Somebody chose which row of identical little men stands for the unemployed and which for the employed, chose the year the sequence begins, chose what gets rounded away into a clean half-figure -- and the schoolteacher standing in front of the chart has no grammar by which to argue with any of it. A page of honest prose at least declares itself as one man's account; your chart presents the same tendentious choices wearing the face of the world itself. You claim to be liberating my sister from words, and what you are actually doing is teaching her to receive the social world pre-interpreted, and to mistake that receiving for seeing. Respond to him as Neurath would, and take him seriously -- he is a vain man, but this is the best objection he has.
