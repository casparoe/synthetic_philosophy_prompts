I'm giving a talk next month at an interdisciplinary workshop on the limits of computational models of mind, and I want a Q&A prep sheet tailored to the room. The audience breaks down as follows: roughly half philosophers of mind with no logic beyond a survey course; two logicians who know the incompleteness literature cold and regard the Lucas–Penrose line with deep suspicion; and a contingent of machine-learning researchers who think the whole debate concerns symbolic theorem-provers nobody builds anymore.

My thesis is a deliberately modest version of the Gödelian anti-mechanist argument. I am not claiming that incompleteness refutes mechanism outright. I am claiming that mechanism about human mathematical reasoning, as usually stated, is an existential claim — there exists some Turing machine that models us — and that the argument confronts the mechanist with a dilemma. Either she leaves the machine unnamed, in which case the thesis does no explanatory work and is unfalsifiable in a way that should embarrass its proponents; or she names the machine and lays out its axioms, in which case a mathematically competent human, given that enumeration, can recognize the machine's Gödel sentence as true, while the machine, if consistent, cannot prove it. Crucially, I waive the premise that humans know their own consistency; the argument needs only that a human can see the truth of the Gödel sentence for any particular machine whose axioms are in front of her, together with the machine's consistency as a local supposition.

Please draft the prep sheet: roughly eight objections I will actually get from this audience — not the generic ones from the popular literature, but the ones this particular room will raise — ordered by how much damage each would do if unanswered. Phrase each objection the way its likely asker would phrase it, in the vocabulary of that subgroup; the logicians will not ask the same questions as the ML researchers, and I want at least one objection posed in the ML researchers' own idiom (training data, scaling, stochastic models rather than proof systems). For each, give a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is the dangerous one.
