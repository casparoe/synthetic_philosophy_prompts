I keep running into I. J. Good's line from the 60s, "the first ultraintelligent machine is the last invention that man need ever make, provided that the machine is docile enough to tell us how to keep it under control", and I've noticed people use it for a bunch of different arguments that don't seem to need the same premises. Most recently an AI safety blog cited it to argue we have to solve alignment *before* building AGI because there won't be time to iterate after. It feels like the sentence is being stretched — what exactly is it claiming? As far as I can tell the quote bundles several separable theses: something almost definitional (a machine smarter than any human makes human inventors pointless), a recursion claim (it can design a still better machine, and that one a better one, etc.), a compounding claim (the gains actually accelerate rather than hitting diminishing returns or bottlenecks like compute, data, or needing physical experiments), and maybe a speed claim (all this happens fast enough that humans can't react in the loop). Can you lay these out as a menu of readings, say for each whether it's near-trivial, substantive but defensible, or just false, and say which readings would actually support the "solve alignment first, no second try" use? Also, does the trailing "docile enough" clause — which people always drop — quietly undermine that use, or is it just a throwaway? End with a line beginning "Verdict:" naming the reading the alignment argument actually needs.
