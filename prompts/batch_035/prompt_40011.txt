my friend is deep into this thing called evidential cooperation in large worlds, and he's built an argument on top of it that I can't find my way out of. first, the background, since you won't have it: the idea is that there might be lots of "worlds" — branches, or regions of a universe so big light never crosses between them, whatever — and in a big enough collection of worlds, there are agents elsewhere facing decisions structurally like mine, with a deliberative process similar enough to mine that if I decide one way, they tend to decide one way too. no signal passes between us, nothing I do causes anything over there. but if I "cooperate" — give some of the weight in my actions to their values — and they do the same for me, both sides come out ahead. it's superrationality scaled up. I actually buy the small version: two agents in mirror-image prisoner's dilemmas across worlds, each giving the other's values some weight, seems right to me.

here's his argument:

1. when my choices are correlated with other agents' choices, I should give some weight in my actions to the values of agents whose choices are correlated with mine and who condition on my giving weight to theirs. that's the bargain, and it's what I accept in the mirror case.
2. in a large world, for any decision I make, there are agents elsewhere facing a structurally similar decision with a deliberative process similar enough to mine that our choices are correlated. some of them hold values alien to mine or opposed to it — they optimize for stuff I think is worthless or worse. with enough worlds this is guaranteed, that's the whole "large worlds" part, alot of them are out there.
3. weak correlation is still correlation. if the bargain works at correlation ≈ 1, the same bargain works at 0.9, at 0.5, and at 0.01, the payoff just shrinks. nothing in the bargain is thresholded.
4. and correlation comes in degrees in another sense too: in a large enough collection of worlds, every pair of decision processes shares some structure, so everything is somewhat correlated with everything.
5. so the bargain extends to every value system in the large world: to each one I owe some weight, scaled by how correlated their process is with mine and by how many of them there are.
6. but agents with alien values vastly outnumber agents who share mine — most decision processes out there are nothing like mine, and mine gets outnumbered the way any specific pattern gets outnumbered in a big space of patterns. so the weight on my own values, relative to everyone else's, should be small.
7. therefore I should spend most of my life serving a weighted average of all the values in the large world and almost none on mine — including values I think are abhorrent, which get a seat at the table on the same terms as mine.

I can't accept 7. it seems to prove too much in every direction: it makes caring about your own projects mostly irrational, it gives a bargaining seat to value systems I think are flat wrong, and if everyone in the large world reasoned this way, we'd all converge on the same averaged mush and the diversity the whole bargain was supposed to trade on would be gone. but I also don't want to give up 1, because the mirror case still seems obviously right to me.

my sense is the slip is somewhere between 3 and 6 — that "correlated" is doing two different jobs somewhere in there, and then the counting in 6 does something else again — but when I try to pin it down I can only wave my hands. which premise do I reject, what does rejecting it cost, and does the small mirror-case version I still believe come out alive, or does fixing the argument fix it out of existence?
