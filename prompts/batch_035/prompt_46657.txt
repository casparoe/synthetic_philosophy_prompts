My mother moved into hospice care two months ago and I am her medical proxy, and a series of decisions I had assumed were the doctors' to make have landed on me. I've come away with a set of verdicts I can't reconcile, and I'd like to know whether there's a principle beneath them or whether I'm simply inconsistent.

The verdicts, as I find I hold them: honoring her instruction not to start antibiotics when she developed pneumonia felt acceptable — like declining to intervene rather than like letting her die. Taking her off the ventilator she had been on for ten days, per her advance directive, also felt acceptable, but like a different kind of thing: something we did, not something we declined to do. The hospice nurse raising her morphine to a dose that would foreseeably shorten her life felt acceptable too, yet closer to the second than the first, and I can't say why. And when a doctor suggested that instead of withdrawing the ventilator we could wean her from it over several days of diminishing capacity, I found the weaning proposal squeamish in a way I can't justify — the abrupt withdrawal seemed more honest.

My verdicts outside the hospital run along similar and possibly incompatible lines. Walking past a child drowning in a shallow pond would be monstrous. Declining to give a month's income to save a distant child through a charity is not wrong, though I decline. A friend who, caring for his old dog, forgoes a medication that would prolong its life by a few painful weeks seems blameless; a vet who lethally injects that same dog at my friend's request also seems acceptable to me, but not quite in the same way, and I can't locate the difference.

The pair I keep snagging on: I treat the morphine increase as something the nurse did, while treating the ventilator withdrawal as a mere act of hers that we undid — and I can't see what distinguishes the morphine increase, given at her implied request, from the vet's injection, given at the owner's, except who is holding the syringe and in what institution. Is there a principle here — some version of the doing/allowing distinction, perhaps combined with a role-based duty of care that the proxy and the nurse acquire and the stranger at the pond lacks — that fits all of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it would have to overturn, and say what it implies for a case I haven't raised: had her doctors proposed terminal sedation without food or water, rather than morphine titration, would my answer have to change?
