I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language, and our vagueness reading group has spent three weeks stuck on a running dispute between two of my colleagues — Dana, who defends supervaluationism, and Ellis, who defends epistemicism. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher of language who specializes in vagueness, think the exchange through carefully before committing to any verdicts, and then adjudicate it move by move. Here is the exchange as it actually unfolded. (1) Dana opened by saying that the sorites shows that a vague predicate like heap has no sharp cutoff: the tolerance premise, that removing one grain from a heap leaves a heap, fails somewhere, but there is no particular pair of adjacent numbers of grains at which it fails, since each such instance is indeterminate. (2) Ellis replied that this is self-undermining: saying that there is no sharp cutoff draws a sharp line of its own, between the cases where the predicate definitely applies and the indeterminate ones; and a predicate that divided the world into no determinate regions at all would not be a predicate — so there is a cutoff, and our inability to know where it lies is exactly what the sorites teaches. (3) Dana called Ellis's truth an empty one: on supervaluationism, the claim that there is a sharp cutoff comes out true because it holds on every admissible precisification, yet no statement of the form that the cutoff lies between n and n+1 is true, so no boundary is actually drawn — which is the theory's virtue, not its flaw. (4) Ellis answered that a truth with no true instances in the vicinity is mere bookkeeping, and that epistemicism at least explains the ignorance it postulates: on a margin-for-error principle, a case can be known to be a heap only when it lies far enough from the boundary, which is why the cutoff is unknowable in principle rather than merely unknown to us. (5) Dana countered that iterating the margin-for-error principle erodes everything: if knowing of a thousand grains that they form a heap requires knowing it of some heap one grain smaller, and so on down the line, the epistemicist ends up unable to know of even the clearest case that it is a heap — at which point Ellis said this only shows that knowledge does not iterate, and that one can know without knowing that one knows, which is independently plausible. Please rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating; say who is more right overall and why; and tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing in the way they have framed the whole disagreement.
