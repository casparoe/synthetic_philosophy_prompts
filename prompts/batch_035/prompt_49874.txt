I am a novelist finishing a book whose protagonist is a psychiatrist who has lost her faith in the diagnostic manual. In the pivotal scene she delivers a monologue to her residents on the inpatient ward, and I want the philosophy underneath it to be sound, because she is meant to be the most formidable mind in the book. Her argument runs as follows: Ian Hacking showed that classifications of people are what he called interactive kinds, subject to looping effects — the people classified change in response to being classified, which in turn changes the classification that classified them. My character carries this to what she takes to be its conclusion: psychiatric diagnoses are self-fulfilling prophecies. Once a label enters the manual, clinicians begin to find the condition, patients learn to recognize themselves in it, schools and insurers and pharmaceutical marketing complete the loop, and the category is continually remade by the very people it sorts. Therefore, she tells her residents, nobody suffered from what we now call attention deficit disorder before the category was devised — there was nothing there to have. The condition, and the suffering, are artifacts of the sorting itself.

Before I lock the scene, I need to know whether her argument is as watertight as she believes. If it is not, tell me precisely where it gives way and how the one resident who has actually read Hacking would press the point, so that I can either shore up the monologue or, better, dramatize the moment she is caught mid-argument by a pushback she cannot parry. Evaluate her reasoning as a philosopher of science would evaluate a submitted paper, not as a novelist's permission slip: distinguish the claims that survive scrutiny from the ones that only sound like claims that survive scrutiny, and note which of her premises a careful critic would let stand and which she has quietly smuggled in. Please end your reply with a single line beginning Verdict: stating whether her argument, exactly as I have stated it, is sound, and if it is not, the specific step at which it fails.
