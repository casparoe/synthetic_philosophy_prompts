so, background: I spent about 35 years in infectious disease research before retiring, mostly host-pathogen interactions and immunology, and in the last few years I've gotten deep into philosophy of religion (long story, my wife blames YouTube). Here's what keeps striking me: whenever the naturalistic take on religion comes up in my reading or in arguments with friends, somebody reaches for the infection analogy. Religions are viruses of the mind — they spread from person to person, they've been shaped by selection to manipulate host behavior, they can go dormant and flare back up decades later, they have vectors and reservoirs (priests, parents, whole communities), and they throw off variants that evade the host's skepticism like immune escape. Dawkins pushed a version of this years ago, the whole mind-virus / parasitic meme framing. And it's not just decoration: for the people using it, the analogy is carrying a real conclusion. If religion is an infection, it's a pathogen — so it should be bad for the folks carrying it, a maladaptive byproduct our cognitive immune systems failed to screen out — and from there it feels natural to talk about something like eradication, or at least a cultural vaccine (critical thinking education in schools, etc).

Now here's my problem, as someone who spent a career with actual pathogens: in my field the analogy runs both ways. The overwhelming majority of microbes we carry aren't pathogens at all, plenty are flatly mutualistic, and there are even endogenous retroviruses that wormed into our germline and ended up doing essential work — as I recall the placenta leans on captured viral genes, of all things. Some of the nastiest acute infections are actually recent zoonotic jumps that kill the host precisely because nobody coevolved with anybody. So "it spreads like an infection" seems to tell you almost nothing about whether it's good, bad, or neutral for the host, and I suspect the same goes for whatever the religion version is tracking. My friends who use the analogy would probably say this just proves my skepticism is itself a symptom, ha. But I genuinely don't know whether my doubts show where the analogy breaks, or whether the religion-is-bad-for-you conclusion survives even after you fix the analogy.

So: be my philosopher of religion with a side of evolutionary biology for an afternoon, and please actually think before you answer — maybe brainstorm the mapping out first, don't just hit me with a list of famous names. What I want is a real audit of the infection analogy. First the correspondence table: what plays the role of transmission, virulence, host manipulation, immunity, latency, reservoir hosts, zoonotic spillover? Then the disanalogies, and crucially a sorting of which ones actually matter for the conclusion the analogy was being used to license — the "religion is a parasite that does its carriers no good, so work toward eradication" conclusion — versus which are just noise. My immunologist's hunch is that the analogy needs coevolution built in, and once you do that the eradication-friendly reading might collapse or might even get stronger (we eradicate pathogens we never coevolved with, not our commensals — but then again nobody ever "eradicated" their gut flora to good effect either, or did they?). And if the virus framing is doing damage, tell me whether there's a better comparison from my own world — commensal microbiome, domesticated organism, an organ of the immune system, whatever. Honest verdict at the end: does the conclusion the analogy was supposed to support survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was it riding on the analogy the whole time?
