I teach philosophy of science, and this semester im doing a unit on how scientific communities carve up time — the way "periods" and "epochs" get made rather than found. The Anthropocene is my running example, and a student stumped me last week with a question I now want to hand back to the class as a clean written comparison. Some background so we're on the same page (I'm stating it deliberately so nobody needs to go check anything; take my description as given): after years of work, the geologists' working group proposed formalizing the Anthropocene as an epoch starting in the mid-twentieth century, the marker being the thin layer of plutonium and other bomb-test radionuclides that shows up in sediments worldwide — you can literally see it in a core — with the preferred date sitting around 1950. The rival proposal that got the most attention went much further back: 1610, the so-called Orbis spike, a dip in atmospheric CO2 preserved in Antarctic ice cores, which its defenders tie to the deaths of tens of millions of Indigenous Americans after European contact, the reforestation of emptied land, and the resulting carbon uptake. And then in 2024 the whole formalization effort got voted down by the stratigraphy authorities, so the question is now oddly academic — which is exactly when philosophers like it best.

Here's my ask. Take the role of a stratigrapher who's spent a career on boundary definitions and moonlights as a philosopher of science. Start with some brainstorming on what a "golden spike" is actually for: a GSSP has to pin a boundary to a physical section of rock, sediment, or ice somewhere, and it exists so that geologists anywhere on Earth can correlate their local strata to the same moment. So the real question isn't "which date better matches when humans started mattering" — that's the trap my students keep falling into — but which marker does the correlating work better: a radionuclide layer that is global, near-synchronous, and unambiguous, versus a CO2 dip that is arguably older, human-caused in a morally loaded way, but blunter as a signal, since atmospheric mixing is slow, ice cores are scarce, and a dip in one trace gas isn't a unique event. Then run the comparison on those terms plus whatever else the formal criteria bring in (secondary markers, alignment with the post-1950 acceleration in everything from fertilizer to chicken bones), and consider honestly whether either proposal actually satisfies the criteria — or whether each just smuggles in a story humans like telling about themselves, one heroic-technological, one tragic-colonial.

Give me something in the 600-900 word range, casual is fine, and end with a machine-readable line at the very bottom, after all the reasoning: \answer{1950}, \answer{1610}, or \answer{neither} — whichever your comparison actually supports, with "neither" a fully live option if you think both fail the golden-spike criteria in different ways. Dont let the answer line do all the work; the argument has to earn it.
