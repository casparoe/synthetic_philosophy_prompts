I'm teaching a seminar that pairs social choice theory with works about collective decision-making, and next week we're doing Sidney Lumet's Twelve Angry Men alongside the literature on judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma. Please give me a reading of the film through that literature.

Here is the pairing as I see it, and where I want you to test, complicate, or correct it. The doctrinal paradox was first posed about juries: if the members of a collegial body vote on the premises of a decision — say, that a witness is reliable and that the defendant had the opportunity — a majority can accept each premise while a majority of the same body would reject the conclusion those premises entail, so premise-driven and conclusion-driven aggregation can diverge. Twelve Angry Men seems to run this in reverse: the film opens on a conclusion vote, an eleven-to-one show of hands for guilty, and the ensuing deliberation is a slow conversion of conclusion-based agreement into premise-by-premise disagreement — the alleged uniqueness of the knife, the old man's walk to his door, the marks on the woman's nose from eyeglasses, the boy's muddled alibi — until the conclusion vote reverses to unanimity for acquittal. Read the film as making a claim about aggregation: something like, the initial verdict was an artifact of aggregation without deliberation, and premise-driven voting, forced by a single dissenter, is the cure.

I want you to take that thesis seriously and also to find where the film resists it. Some threads you might pull: Is Juror 8's initial vote best understood as premise-based resistance (he doesn't think innocence has been shown, only that the premises haven't been settled), and does the film ever let us distinguish that from mere doubt of the conclusion? The unanimity rule means the verdict is reachable only if every juror's judgment flips — does the film dramatize unanimity's double face, as a safeguard against correlated errors and as an engine of conformity pressure once the count turns? The Condorcet-style results that vindicate majority voting assume independent juror errors, but the film's holdouts seem to fail independence in both directions — prejudice correlating the early guilty votes, deference correlating the later acquittals — so is the film an argument that no aggregation mechanism, unanimity included, can do the epistemological work, and that only the deliberation does? And does the film cheat by letting the audience be fairly confident the defendant is innocent, thereby collapsing the distinction the aggregation literature depends on, between the verdict as truth-tracking output and the verdict as whatever the group's aggregated judgments happen to be — a distinction that would survive even if the kid really did do it?

Please point me to specific scenes for each claim, and be candid where the reading strains: for instance, whether the secret-ballot scene is really about independence, or just about stripping the group's ability to see when the dissenter's support arrives. Finally, tell me what the film adds to the judgment-aggregation debate that the formal results don't have. My own candidate is that the formalism fixes the agenda in advance, while the film's whole drama is about how the set of premises up for decision gets constructed on the fly — which premises get raised, reenacted, or dismissed is itself the outcome of the deliberation, not an input to it. Is that right, or does the literature already handle it?
