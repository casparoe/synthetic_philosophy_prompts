I'm a graduate student working on distributive ethics, and I've become convinced that the debate I keep circling back to has been running in place for over fifty years: the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism. The objection goes back to Rawls, who argued in A Theory of Justice (1971) that classical utilitarianism, in extending to society the principle of choice for one man, fails to take seriously the distinction between persons — treating losses to some as fully offset by gains to others, in a way we would never accept within a single life. Nozick sharpened it a few years later with the utility monster, and the subsequent literature — Scanlon's contractualist ban on aggregating across persons, Taurek's question of whether numbers should count at all, Parfit's engagement with aggregation and with what he called the direct and the indirect claims — has generated endless refinements and case pairings (rescue cases, transplant cases, headaches versus death) without anything resembling resolution. Utilitarians concede every intuition at the level of cases and still don't convert; their critics win every intuition and still can't say precisely what the utilitarian has left out.

I'd like a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a verdict on who's right. Is it substantive — a genuine first-order moral difference about whether losses to one person can be compensated by gains to another? Is it verbal, with the two sides meaning different things by taking persons seriously? Are the sides answering different questions under one heading, as I've begun to suspect: one side arguing about the ethics of aggregation at the level of outcomes, the other advancing a claim about the form a moral justification must take — roughly, that each person must be able to see the treatment of them as answerable to them, which is a constraint on theories rather than a premise within one? That would explain the peculiar dialectical pattern, where utilitarians happily bite bullets at the level of cases while insisting that they too respect persons, and it would explain why Parfit, sympathetic as he was to the critique, still endorsed aggregation in the kinds of medical cases he used to press it. But I'm not confident, because the contractualist side does seem to make substantive claims, for instance about what one person may not be made to suffer for the sake of others, and a purely formal reading arguably drains those claims of their force.

Given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would count as progress: a sharper distinction between aggregation within a life and across lives, a formal result, a new class of cases that could discriminate between the readings, or a change in the question. And if your diagnosis implies that no progress should be expected — because the disagreement bottoms out in bedrock commitments about the individual that neither side can argue for without circularity — say so plainly, and explain what it is about this particular dispute that has kept it productive-looking for half a century anyway.
