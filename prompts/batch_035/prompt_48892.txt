I teach a cross-listed graduate seminar in social and political philosophy on the enforcement of norms, and as a change of pace from term papers I want to close the course with a diagnostic quiz — in the spirit of those magazine instruments that tell you which theory you actually hold — sorting students by their normative commitments about how much rule-following a society should demand. The students will take it before we read the descriptive literature on tight and loose cultures, so the quiz must not presuppose that vocabulary: it should sort people by what they say about cases, not by which labels they recognize.

Write eight to ten questions, each a short concrete case, each with two or three answer options that are plausible without telegraphing where they lead. The cases must be engineered to pull the following distinctions apart, in this order of importance: first, whether deviation should attract sanction at all, as opposed to being left to private disapproval and social cooling; second, whether the stringency of enforcement may be indexed to a society's exposure to threat and scarcity — the thought that a community living near the subsistence margin is entitled to a discipline a comfortable one is not — or whether one standard applies everywhere and always; third, whether the demands properly attach to coordination domains (queues, dress, greeting rituals), to moralized domains (marriage, career, religious observance), or indifferently to both; and fourth, whether a society may impose different stringency on members and outsiders, or on insiders and newcomers. Include at least one case that separates rule proliferation from sanction severity — a society with many lightly enforced rules versus one with few harshly enforced ones — since I want to learn which of those my students actually take themselves to be objecting to.

Then provide a scoring key mapping patterns of answers onto positions, including at least these leaves: a Hobbesian or authoritarian perfectionist position on which order is itself the primary good and enforcement needs no further warrant; a threat-indexed functionalist position on which stringency is a legitimate adaptation to circumstance rather than a universal demand; a domain-particularist contractualist position on which sanction is justified only where it resolves a coordination or harm problem that those regulated could themselves acknowledge; a liberal neutralist position on which the state and social pressure may police harm but not ways of life; and a strict libertarian position on which sanction as such is the problem and private disapproval is the ceiling of legitimate response. For any combination of answers the key permits that corresponds to no defensible or named position, say so, and speculate briefly on whether the view is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves the questions cannot reliably separate; I would rather know my instrument is blunt in places than pretend otherwise.

One constraint throughout: no question may simply ask, in effect, how much the respondent values rules or how much they value freedom. If a student could answer every item without being surprised by at least one of the positions the key assigns them, the quiz has failed.
