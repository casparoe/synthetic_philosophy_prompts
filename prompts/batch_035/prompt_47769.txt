So, background: I write novels — literary-ish fiction, three published, fourth in drafts — and the only vocabulary I really have for contradiction is craft vocabulary. Retcons, dream sequences, unreliable narrators, the rule every fiction workshop teaches that if *everything* is unreliable then nothing means anything. I don't have any logic training past a semester in undergrad that I mostly slept through.

Here's my situation. In my current draft, a character gives two mutually exclusive accounts of the night her sister died, and I've decided the book never adjudicates between them. My editor keeps insisting readers will quietly pick one account as "what really happened," and my view is that a good reader can hold both at once without the book collapsing. A friend who reads philosophy said "oh, you're basically a dialetheist," and I went down a rabbit hole and now I need this translated properly.

So: take dialetheism and paraconsistent logic — the stuff about some contradictions being true, and about how from a contradiction you *can't* derive everything else — and restate it in the vocabulary I actually use. I think the counterpart of "explosion" in my world is the workshop rule: one contradiction too many and the whole book stops tracking, every scene becomes equally valid or invalid, readers give up. Is paraconsistency, in my terms, something like quarantine — a contradiction gets to live inside one character or one chapter and the plot logic around it stays normal? That's what I'm doing with the two accounts: the contradiction is sealed off, the rest of the book behaves.

But then give me the honest residue report, because I suspect the translation breaks somewhere. The thing I can't figure out is whether fiction is even the right host for this. Priest (I think it's Priest? if I've got the name wrong, correct me) says some contradictions are *actually true*, in reality, not just that we can't tell which of two things happened. But fiction seems to trivially allow contradiction — a book can say the sky is green on page 3 and blue on page 40 and nothing "follows" from it, because fiction doesn't really run on inference at all. Readers don't derive propositions from the text the way logicians derive theorems. So maybe there's no such thing as explosion in fiction, which would mean paraconsistency can't be doing any work in my book — the quarantine isn't logical, it's just an attention effect. Or maybe the fact that readers *do* keep tracking, that some contradictions wreck a book and others don't, means fiction has a real paraconsistent structure that logicians are describing in a different register. I genuinely don't know which.

Also, if anything else in the philosophical position has no counterpart at all in how stories work — say it plainly. And tell me whether the leftover stuff points at something fiction does that logic can't see, or at something in the philosophy that's less clear than it looks. Please don't water it down for me — I'd rather be confused by the real thing than flattered by a version of my own book talk dressed up in logic words.
