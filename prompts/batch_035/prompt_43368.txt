I am a high school junior, and I have been invited to give the closing presentation at a community event organized around genocide remembrance, hosted at my school. The audience will include other students, a few teachers, and some members of local advocacy organizations. I want the talk to make a point that I have not seen made in the standard presentations: the word "genocide" does two jobs at once — a narrow legal job, fixed by the 1948 Convention with its specific intent requirement and its closed list of protected groups, and a much older moral job, closer to Raphael Lemkin's own broader conception, which took in the destruction of a people's culture, language, and way of life even where mass killing was not the method. My thesis is that a lot of public confusion and political anger comes from these two jobs quietly coming apart, and I want to illustrate exactly where and how.

To structure the talk, I would like you to rank five factors on a single question: how much each factor actually drives, in the real world, whether an atrocity ends up being named a genocide (by courts, governments, or the press). The five factors are: (1) evidence of the specific intent to destroy a protected group as such, as opposed to evidence of large-scale killing or displacement alone; (2) the sheer number of victims; (3) the geopolitics of the perpetrators — specifically whether powerful states with veto influence have an interest in blocking the label; (4) the persistence and organization of the victim group's own survivors and diaspora in demanding the name; and (5) the fit between the case and Lemkin's original, broader conception of destruction of a group's culture and institutions. For each factor, give it a tier (decisive, major, minor, or marginal) and a one-sentence justification grounded in how naming decisions actually get made — for instance, how a case with fewer deaths but clearer intent gets the label while a politicide with millions dead is legally stranded because political groups were left off the Convention's protected list, a choice the drafters made under Cold War pressure, and how the provisions on cultural destruction that Lemkin wanted were dropped from the final text.

Then I want a second, separate ranking of the same five factors by how much each should drive the naming decision in an ideal world — what would be the most morally defensible basis for the name. Finally, and this is the heart of my presentation, walk me through the divergences between your two rankings: identify the factors that move the most between the actual-world list and the ideal-world list, explain why they diverge, and tell me which single divergence you think is most instructive for a general audience. As a capstone, give me a number between 0 and 1 representing the fraction of the real-world variance in naming that you would attribute to legal criteria as opposed to political ones, with a brief defense of the split.

A few notes on scope. Please do not provide citations or reading lists; I already have my sources and will supply them myself. I am also not asking you to adjudicate any particular live dispute about any current conflict — the comparisons I have sketched above (the exclusion of political groups, the dropping of cultural-genocide provisions, the intent-versus-numbers issue) are the historical background I need, used as illustrations rather than as verdicts on specific cases. I want the analysis to be sharp enough that a lawyer or historian in the audience would nod along, but written plainly enough that my classmates will follow it, and I want to come away knowing the strongest objection to my thesis that the two jobs of the word are coming apart, so that I can address it before someone raises it from the floor.
