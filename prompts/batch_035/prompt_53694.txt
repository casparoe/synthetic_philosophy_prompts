I'm a math PhD student taking a philosophy of language course as an elective, mostly because I kept getting into arguments at the pub about whether math is discovered. We just spent three weeks on Wittgenstein-on-rules (via Kripke's book) and instead of clearing things up it's made me realize I hold a bunch of case-verdicts that don't fit together. So, here are my gut calls, and I'd like to know whether there's one account of what fixes what a person means that captures them, which of my verdicts it has to throw out, and what it predicts on cases I haven't listed.

The verdicts, roughly in order of confidence. A kid who's only ever been shown sums with numbers under ten gets asked 687+2: she'd better say 689, and if she says 5, that's a mistake, not a different but equally valid reading of her training. Me: I accept the skeptical claim that nothing in my finite past favors plus over quus (where quus is ordinary addition for small numbers and 5 for anything involving anything above 57), and at the same time I think it's just obvious I meant plus and not quus. I cant give up either half, which I'm aware is awkward. A whole community, records going back centuries, whose members all compute ordinary addition up to a trillion but then wrap around to zero, all of them, sincerely and confidently: I say that by "plus" they mean the wrap function, it's not a trillion-wide mass error. A lone dissenter in our community, otherwise sharp and sincere, who computes quus: here I wobble — half of me says he misunderstands addition, i.e., he means plus and gets it wrong, the other half says he just means quus by "plus", which would make my community verdict look like simple majority rule, in a way I dont like. And an oracle that has matched correct addition on a billion past trials: it means plus, I see no difference in kind between it and a human who's been right so far.

My current best guess at a principle is something like: what you mean is fixed by the pattern your community's practice of correction and continuation would converge on, not by anything in you alone. But that seems wrong for the first verdict — why does the community get to declare the kid wrong rather than say she's revealed a different rule all along? — and it can't obviously explain why I'm so sure in my own case that answering 5 tomorrow would be an error and not a change of meaning. Can you repair the principle so it fits the verdicts, or is the honest answer that some of my calls have to go, and if so which? Also tell me what the repaired principle says about a case I didn't list: two communities merge, one that always computed ordinary addition and one that always computed the wrap, and the kids are now taught together in mixed schools. Did anybody's meaning change on moving day?
