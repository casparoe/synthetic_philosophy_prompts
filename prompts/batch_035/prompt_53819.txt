I'm writing a term paper for a course on the history of economic thought, and I've become fascinated by Adam Smith's views on empire and colonies — a part of his thought far more ambivalent than the caricature of him as a doctrinaire free trader. Most people know he attacked the mercantilist restrictions governing colonial trade, but fewer realize that he made at least one striking concession. Discussing the English Navigation Acts, which he considered economically harmful, he nonetheless wrote: "As defence, however, is of much more importance than opulence, the act of navigation is, perhaps, the wisest of all the commercial regulations of England." He also judged the empire to be, on the whole, a bad bargain for the mother country: the colonies were held for the sake of a monopoly trade that enriched a few merchants while burdening the rest of society, and shortly before the American crisis he suggested that Britain would be better off either granting the colonies fair representation or voluntarily releasing them.

Here is my question: if Smith were somehow able to observe the current wave of economic statecraft — semiconductor export controls aimed at China, large subsidies to attract chip fabs and battery plants, the de-risking and friend-shoring of supply chains away from geopolitical rivals, and the general revival of the idea that strategic industries must be kept within one's own political bloc — what would he say about it? Would he treat these policies as a modern replay of the Navigation Acts, an acceptable sacrifice of opulence for defence, or as something genuinely different in kind, for instance because the security stakes, the technologies, or the structure of modern trade differ enough from seventeenth-century shipping? Please reconstruct his likely position as carefully as you can from what is actually in his writings and his characteristic modes of argument, rather than from the popular image of him.

Since I want the paper to be defensible, please clearly separate in your answer what is settled among Smith scholars from what is genuinely contested. For example, I gather that the reach of his defence-over-opulence concession is itself debated — some read it as a narrow, almost grudging endorsement of one naval-security measure, others as a broader license for commercial restriction whenever security is invoked — and I would like you to flag interpretive points of that sort explicitly rather than presenting one reading as though it were the consensus.
