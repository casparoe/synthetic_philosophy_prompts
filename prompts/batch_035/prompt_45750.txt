My flatmate Dave and I keep having this running argument about whether statements about the future are already true or false right now. I'm a backend engineer and he did physics at uni, so between us we know just enough to be dangerous. I've started writing down his verdicts so I can catch him out later. His record so far:

- "It'll rain in Bristol next Friday" (I asked him this Monday): not true and not false, there's no fact of the matter yet, nothing in the world right now makes it either.
- "Either it'll rain in Bristol next Friday or it won't": true, obviously — he looked at me like I was thick for even asking.
- "You'll be at the pub at 7 on Friday": already true, because I've decided, its in the calendar and I've told everyone. Even though I could still get hit by a bus on the way there, apparently that doesn't matter.
- "There will be a total solar eclipse over Cornwall on 11 August 1999", posed as if we're sitting in 1985: already true back then — the positions of the planets in 1985 settled it.
- "Tomorrow the sun will rise over Edinburgh" (asked last night): true.
- The classic one — "there will be a sea battle tomorrow", two fleets sat there manoeuvring, neither admiral having made his mind up: no truth value.

Next week we're having dinner with his sister, who's doing a philosophy degree, and I know exactly how that conversation will go, so I want to be ready. What will he say about each of these?

1. "Sam will be at the pub on Friday too" — Sam decided weeks ago that he's coming, but he hasn't told either of us, so Dave has no way of knowing.
2. "Last year it was already true that it would rain in Bristol this Friday" — I'd be asking him now, the day before, when the forecast has basically locked in.
3. "At 7:03 next Friday you'll be walking through the pub door" — same decision as before, but now with a timestamp on it.

What rule or rules is he actually running? It seems like more than one thing fits his record so far, so tell me which of the three cases would split those rules apart. And is his record even consistent? The eclipse one and the Bristol rain one rub each other up the wrong way to me — if the planetary positions in 1985 settled the eclipse, why don't this Monday's facts settle the rain? — but I cant quite put my finger on whether that's a real contradiction or just him being sloppy about two different senses of "settled".
