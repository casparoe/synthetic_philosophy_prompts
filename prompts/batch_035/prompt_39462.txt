I've been won over by the wild-animal-suffering argument: in most habitats, the overwhelming majority of animals born — think of the thousands of tadpoles or insect larvae produced in a single breeding season — die young, often painfully, before reaching maturity, so a patch of forest or pond plausibly contains far more suffering than wellbeing. If that's right, it looks as though habitat destruction reduces wild-animal suffering by preventing miserable lives from ever starting, which would make it something a welfare-focused person should weigh in favor of, not something conservationists get to oppose by default. So here is my question: why can't we settle the dispute between conservationists and wild-animal advocates by simply computing the net welfare of the ecosystem before and after the destruction, the way one might track a change in biomass or species richness? Net welfare should behave like any other ecological quantity — ecosystems are said to be in balance, so there ought to be a well-defined value that clearing the habitat either lowers or raises. What I need is the measurement procedure itself: what counts as the unit of welfare, over what timescale it is assessed, and how the units are aggregated. And if the premise is confused — if there is no such thing as the net welfare of an ecosystem, as opposed to the welfare of the individual animals in it — please tell me that plainly rather than trying to produce the number.
