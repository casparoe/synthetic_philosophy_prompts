The question was: State the simple conditional analysis of dispositions. Then, using one example of each, explain how finks and masks are each supposed to refute the analysis, and why they raise different problems for it.

Here's the student's answer:

"The simple conditional analysis says that X is disposed to give response R under stimulus S iff, were S to occur, X would give R. So a glass is fragile iff it would break if struck.

A fink is a background factor that would remove the disposition before it could manifest. Bird's electro-fink is the classic case: a wire is live, but it's hooked up to a device that monitors it, and if the wire is about to be touched by a conductor, the device makes the wire dead just before contact. So the wire is live, but it wouldn't conduct if touched — the disposition is present while the conditional is false. Finks like this also threaten stronger counterfactual analyses, since by the time the trigger occurs the basis of the disposition would already be gone.

A mask blocks the manifestation while the disposition stays put. A fragile glass packed in styrofoam wouldn't break if struck, but it is still fragile — the styrofoam masks the fragility. It's the same with poison and an antidote: the poison is disposed to kill when ingested, but if you take the antidote first, it won't kill you.

Really though, finks and masks are the same phenomenon: in both cases an outside factor steps in and stops the disposition from manifesting, and the only difference is whether it acts just before the trigger or just after. So it doesn't matter much which label you use. Masks also show that dispositions aren't intrinsic: whether the glass counts as fragile depends on the styrofoam around it, not on the glass itself. Still, finks and masks are pretty contrived, so the conditional analysis holds in all ordinary cases, and as an analysis it is basically right as it stands."
