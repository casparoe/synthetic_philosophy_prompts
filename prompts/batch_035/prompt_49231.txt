I'm a PhD student in political theory, writing a chapter on competition and the common good, and I keep running into Moloch. You know the thing by now: after Scott Alexander's mid-2010s essay (the one riffing on Ginsberg's Howl, where Moloch stands for the devouring logic of competition), basically everyone in my circles reaches for Moloch to explain any bad collective outcome. Overfishing, arms races, academic publishing eating everyone's evenings, dating apps getting worse, labs racing on capabilities — all Moloch, all the way down. The response I usually recieved when I push back is a shrug and something like "it's just game theory, man", which is exactly the kind of move I want to pick apart.

Here's the suspicion I want someone to actually argue for properly, because my supervisor thinks im being uncharitable and I want the strongest version on paper. The claim: the Moloch framing smuggles together at least three structures that only look similar. (1) Genuine commons problems — overgrazing, overfishing — where everyone would be better off if everyone restrained, and the standard fixes (property rights, quotas, Ostrom-style community management) are politically hard but conceptually boring. (2) Positional arms races — ad spending, credential inflation, peacock tails — where the prize is fixed and the competition just burns resources around it; restraint is unstable here, but not because coordination is impossible, rather because the payoff to defecting is so immediate. (3) What I'd call drift without a race: cases like academic publishing or social media feeds, where nobody is actually winning — theres no prize at the end, just an equilibrium nobody chose and no one is steering, a treadmill everyone hates. My hunch is that the appeal of Moloch is mostly emotional: it's comforting to have an impersonal demon to blame, and the game-theory dressing makes fatalism look like rigour. If every bad equilibrium is a multipolar trap, coordination starts to look metaphysically impossible rather than merely hard, and that does real ideological work — it quietly lets people off the hook for the boring, contestable political choices (quotas, bans, taxes) that historically have broken these things.

So: please make the case for this position as charitably as you can, including spelling out what the Moloch framing gets right — the point is that the criticism should survive contact with the best version of the view, not just the forum-comment version. Feel free to use whatever examples you like, but keep it usable by a reader with no web access: no citations, no reading lists, just the argument. And one thing I'd like at the very end: a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by one sentence stating the strongest single objection to the position you've just argued for. My supervisor will ask for that, so I want it on record.
