I've spent this term auditing a decision theory course where the sunk cost fallacy served as the running example of clean irrationality: the money is gone whether you walk out of the play or stay for the second half, so the ticket price is no reason at all, and a rational agent looks only forward. But my flatmate, who did her degree in moral philosophy, has been unpicking this all term, and her two objections have stayed with me. First, the retrospective point: if you finish the novel you've sunk ten years into, those ten years become part of a completed life-project rather than a loss, so completing it now seems to confer value on the past itself — and it's not obvious that conferring value on your own past by your present choices is confused. Second, the character point: a disposition to see things through may be genuinely valuable, and if it is, then occasionally honoring costs that are, considered alone, sunk might just be the premium you pay for not being the kind of person who abandons things the moment the ledger turns. I've come to suspect the textbook and my flatmate are both partly right, and I'd like to think it through in verse rather than in another argument over the washing-up.

Write me a sestina about this — thirty-nine lines, six sestets plus a three-line envoi, with the end-words rotating in the traditional pattern so that each stanza re-encounters the same six words in a new arrangement. The choice of end-words should do real philosophical work: pick six words from the lexicon of expenditure and endurance — something like spent, gone, half, whole, still, and leave, though you may substitute your own if yours are better. The conceit is that the rotation enacts the very thing under dispute: the same words return unchanged, stanza after stanza, exactly as the sunk resources return unchanged in every fresh justification for continuing — the form should make me feel, physically, why the mind cannot put the past behind it, because the form itself refuses to let the words go.

Set it in the first person at a specific decision-point — I'd suggest intermission of a bad play you paid too much to see, but a half-built boat in the garage or a nearly finished dissertation would serve as well — and let the speaker's mind move between the decision theorist's principle and my flatmate's two defenses without either being caricatured. The envoi is the crux: one line should carry the forward-looking principle as sharply as the textbook states it, one should carry the strongest opposing thought in the poem, and the third should be the speaker actually acting — leaving or staying — in a way that is consistent with the weight the poem has actually given each side, not a shrug. Someone who teaches this debate should be able to recognize it in miniature, and someone who has never heard of it should still find it a good poem about not being able to let go.
