A few weeks ago my sister left me stranded at the airport for three hours. She'd promised weeks in advance to pick me up when my flight landed at five, and she simply forgot — didn't check her phone, never thought of it once — so I finally gave up and took a sixty-dollar taxi home. When I told the story at a family dinner, my cousin and my aunt got into a surprisingly heated argument about whether she's actually to blame, and now I'd like someone to referee it properly.

Here's the complication: that very morning, her boss had told her she had to stay at the office until at least eight to handle a client emergency, and that if she left early she'd be fired — no second chances. So even in the world where she remembered the promise, she couldn't have kept it. My cousin says this settles everything: she couldn't have picked me up no matter what, so blaming her for my stranded afternoon makes no sense, because you can't be at fault for an outcome that was guaranteed regardless of what you did. My aunt thinks that's a dodge. She says the reason I got stranded is that my sister forgot — the boss's threat never actually stopped her from doing anything, since she never got as far as trying to leave; the whole chain of events ran through her carelessness, not through the threat. My cousin replied that in that case my aunt is blaming her for "forgetting," which isn't a choice at all — no one decides to forget something, it just happens to you. My aunt shot back that forgetting isn't like weather: my sister had never written the promise down, hadn't set a reminder, and didn't so much as text me when she learned about the work emergency — a person who took the promise seriously would have done at least one of those things. My cousin's last word was this: grant every bit of that, and suppose she had been the most conscientious person alive — remembered instantly, felt terrible, begged the boss, and still been stuck at her desk until eight. Same taxi, same three hours. If the harm would have occurred anyway through no fault of a conscientious version of her, then the harm itself can't be what makes her blameworthy; and the actual her differs from conscientious-her only in ways that made no difference to the outcome, so it's unfair to hang the blame on those differences. At that point they both turned to me, and I honestly couldn't say.

Please go through their exchange move by move and tell me who's right at each step — not just who wins overall. And if there's something both of them are missing, I'd like to know that too, because I keep going back and forth between them.
