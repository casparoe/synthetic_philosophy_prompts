At a month-long residency last autumn, two writers I've known for years — Marta, who is forty-five and has just been offered the chance to adopt a child on her own, and Jun, who has raised two — argued for most of an evening about whether her decision could be made rationally at all. I said almost nothing, and they've since asked me to referee by email, which I don't trust myself to do. Here is the exchange as I recall it.

(1) Marta began from her own case: every framework she knows for deliberating well — weighing outcomes against her preferences, imagining the future and scoring it — presupposes a fixed point of view doing the valuing. But the thing she is deciding whether to become, she said, is precisely a change in the point of view itself. She can simulate the logistics of a child; she cannot simulate what the mornings will be like from the inside, because the self that would know is one that doesn't exist yet and can't be consulted. So deliberation of the ordinary kind is theater: she can go through the motions, but nothing she does counts as settling the question, and pretending otherwise is a way of lying to herself about what she's doing.

(2) Jun replied that this proves far too much. On Marta's standard no one could rationally take a new job, marry, move countries, learn the cello at fifty, or even read the book that ends up rearranging them — since each of those changes what one values and how things seem from the inside, at least a little. Yet we confidently give and take reasons about all of them, and Marta gave reasons for her own last move to Lisbon that she still endorses. If her argument worked, it would work everywhere; since it manifestly doesn't, it doesn't work here either.

(3) Marta answered that Jun was flattening a difference of kind into one of degree. Some changes add to a self — a new job is had by the same valuer who weighed it, more or less, and if the fit turns out wrong, that same valuer notices. What she is contemplating replaces the valuer. The difference isn't in the size of the change but in whether there's a continuing standpoint against which the change can be assessed as a success or a failure. Moving to Lisbon was judged before, during, and after by (roughly) her; adoption would be judged afterward only by whoever it makes of her.

(4) Jun granted the description and attacked the use of it: if the post-decision standpoint is so remade that its verdicts can't be compared with the pre-decision one, then Marta's own claim to know that this is that kind of change can't survive either. Everything she knows about what adoption does to a person comes from parents and ex-parents describing it afterward — exactly the contaminated testimony she wants to set aside when it's inconvenient, and the only evidence in existence. She can't invoke the unbridgeable gap to dismiss favorable reports while leaning on unfavorable ones drawn from the same far side of it.

(5) Marta's last word was a challenge aimed at me as much as at Jun: I have spent thirty years writing mothers from the inside, and if pressed I would admit that I don't know what it's like, that the craft is all outward signs. But — she said — no one thinks I lack knowledge because I haven't been a seventeenth-century ship's surgeon either, so Jun should say plainly whether the limit is about transformation or about the first person, because those are different limits with different consequences for her argument.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: who is right about what at each of the five moves, what each concedes that they shouldn't, and — since I suspect they are at least partly talking past each other — what both of them are missing about the difference between deciding for the self one is and deciding for a self one's choice will help create. Marta is waiting, and so, less patiently, is Jun.
