I'm a staff engineer at a mid-sized payments company, and I sit on our Architecture Review Board, which has to sign off next week on a proposal to migrate our billing pipeline off a fourteen-year-old Ruby monolith onto a new service. The migration plan drops a set of undocumented compatibility shims in the old system — dead-looking code paths that reroute invoices under conditions no one on the current team can explain. One of those shims, we learned from an incident postmortem last year, was silently catching a specific class of malformed customer records and would have cost us a seven-figure revenue discrepancy if removed; another, which we traced through an old commit, was confirmed to be leftover scaffolding for a payment provider we dropped in 2012. So the same category of "inexplicable legacy behavior" has contained both a load-bearing wall and genuine cruft.

I have no philosophy background, but a while ago I read about something called Chesterton's fence — the idea that you shouldn't remove a fence until you know why someone put it up — and it seems to describe our situation exactly. Two of my colleagues on the board are already dug in. One says the principle settles it: if we can't explain a shim, we keep it or replicate it in the new system, full stop. The other says the principle proves far too much: every line of code was written by someone for some reason, so taken literally it forbids us from ever deleting anything, and our codebase becomes immortal; she also points out that the last time someone invoked the fence idea in a review, it delayed a critical security patch by six weeks while we "investigated" an obsolete code path. And the migration has a hard deadline from finance, so we can't just slow-walk the whole thing.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate to the board before the meeting. It should:

1. Recommend a concrete, actionable policy on when undocumented legacy behavior must be preserved, investigated further, or can be removed — a rule or procedure my fellow engineers could actually apply to the remaining shims, not a vague "be careful."
2. Give a definite verdict on the migration proposal itself: approve as-is, approve with conditions (specify them), or defer (specify what would change your mind and who owns it).
3. Take the fence principle seriously as stated — explain what's right about it — but also give an honest treatment of the strongest version of my colleague's objection that it licenses never deleting anything, and say how your recommended policy handles that tension rather than just balancing the two slogans against each other.
4. Where relevant, draw on the broader conservative tradition the fence idea comes from — the thought that institutions and practices may encode functional knowledge that no individual participant possesses, so reformers who see only inefficiency may be destroying something load-bearing. Our situation seems like a literal case of that, and I'd like the memo to engage with it, but written for engineers: no philosopher names or jargon are required, and if you use them, define them inline. Assume the readers are smart, skeptical, and allergic to anything that sounds like a TED talk.

For context: our team has a two-week investigation budget before the migration freeze, we can canary the new pipeline against 1% of production traffic with automated invoice-reconciliation checks, and we have access to the full version-control history going back to 2011, though the commit messages are famously useless.
