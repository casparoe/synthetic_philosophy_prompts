my egalitarianism reading group keeps tripping over the insurance analogy for redistribution and I want to actually think it through properly. the move, as our resident Dworkin-fan puts it, goes like this: nobody chose their genes, their upbringing, or their native market talents, so having sellable skills is basically holding a winning ticket in a lottery nobody entered. taxation is the premium, welfare payments and public services are the claims paid out, and the whole thing isn't confiscation of the "deserving" at all — it's just the payout stage of an insurance scheme it would have been rational for everyone to sign up to before they knew what ticket they'd draw. she uses it to argue that luck egalitarians can make redistribution feel like the least mysterious thing in the world: nobody thinks an insurer robs the lucky drivers to pay the ones whose houses burned down. and one guy in the group goes further and says the state should literally run on this logic — figure out what a rational person behind something like a veil of ignorance would have insured against, and pay out exactly that and nothing more, so no payouts for people whose low income traces back to choices (the classic example being someone who could work but prefers surfing).

so audit the analogy for me. what exactly maps onto what — premium, policy, insured event, insurer, payout — and which parts of that table get shaky? I can already see some pressure points but I want them done properly: the "policy" was never voluntarily purchased, people's attitudes to risk differ so there's no single thing "a rational person" would have insured against, and some of the things luck egalitarians worry about (expensive tastes? religious commitments that lower your earnings?) don't look like insurable events at all. also the gambler-vs-cripple contrast everyone uses — option luck vs brute luck — seems to do a lot of quiet work in deciding what counts as a "claim," and I'm not sure the insurance picture can justify that line rather than just stipulating it.

and then the big question: how much work is the analogy actually doing? my suspicion is that the intuitive pull comes from real insurance being consensual and mutually beneficial, but redistribution is compulsory and the "premium" isn't means-tested the way real premiums would be — so maybe the analogy is smuggling in the very legitimacy it's supposed to explain. or maybe the opposite: maybe the disanalogies don't matter because the normative heavy lifting is done by the brute-luck/choice distinction itself, and insurance is just a vivid way of illustrating it. tell me which it is, and if the analogy is carrying more than it can hold, what would a better one look like? also does the strict "pay out only what rational insurers would have covered" extension my friend likes survive the audit at all, or does it inherit every weakness of the analogy plus some of its own?
