My philosophy study group has an argument that started as a response to Gettier cases and somehow ended up eating everything, and I cant tell which step is rotten. Here it is:

(1) Gettier cases show that a justified true belief can still fail to be knowledge, because it's true only by luck -- the stopped clock that reads noon at exactly noon, the job candidate with ten coins in his pocket who doesn't know he has ten coins, whatever. So knowledge excludes a certain kind of luck.

(2) In those cases the luck traces to a specific structural feature: the thing that justifies the belief has come apart from the fact that makes it true. The clock's showing noon is why you believe it's noon, but not why it's noon.

(3) To block that coming apart, the believer's justification has to guarantee the truth of the belief. Anything short of a guarantee leaves a gap, and any gap is a channel for luck.

(4) Only entailment guarantees truth. If your evidence leaves open a way the belief could be false, it doesn't guarantee anything.

(5) So a belief counts as knowledge only if what justifies it entails that it's true.

(6) But no empirical belief meets that bar. My evidence that there's coffee in the pot is compatible with the coffee being a realistic prop, with my hallucinating, with being a brain in a vat -- the evidence underdetermines the truth in every worldly case.

(7) So no empirical belief is knowledge. The Gettier problem isn't a boundary dispute at the edge of knowledge, it swallows the whole territory.

The guy pushing this says the moral is that "knowledge" was always just a compliment we pay to beliefs whose luck hasn't been exposed yet, and he seems weirdly cheerful about it. My instinct is that something fishy happens between (3) and (5) -- maybe "guarantee" is doing two different jobs, one about ruling out luck and one about ruling out falsehood? -- but I can't pin it down without hand-waving. And I can see how someone could just bite the bullet at (7) and become a skeptic, but that seems like paying everything to save a tidy argument.

So: which premise or inference should I reject, why did it look fine in the first place, and what does rejecting it cost? If there's more than one respectable place to break the chain, I'd like to see the alternatives priced against each other. Please answer in American English.
