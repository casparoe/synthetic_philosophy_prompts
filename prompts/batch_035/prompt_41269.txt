I lead a film-and-philosophy discussion group, and for December I've picked It's a Wonderful Life -- the obvious holiday choice, so I want to give it an unobvious angle. I've been reading the critiques of effective altruism lately, particularly the measurability bias (the worry that the movement systematically overvalues whatever fits a cost-effectiveness spreadsheet, like deworming pills, and undervalues diffuse, relational, hard-to-quantify goods), the institutional critique (that EA's focus on an individual donor's clean, trackable interventions crowds out messier political and community-level change), and the debates around earning to give. And it struck me that the film is almost eerily about these questions, though it was made decades before anyone used the phrase.

The parallels seem rich but not straightforward. George Bailey's work -- keeping a small-town building and loan solvent, enabling homeownership for people Potter would keep in slums, anchoring a community -- is precisely the kind of unglamorous, institutional, unmeasurable contribution that the critics say EA's methods are blind to; by any metric George himself would accept (getting out of Bedford Falls, college, travel, money), his life is a failure, and Potter more than once tells him so. Meanwhile his friend Sam, who leaves for New York and gets rich (in plastics, if I remember the phone call right), sends money back when it's needed -- arguably the film's earning-to-give figure, though a friendly one. But here's what complicates the reading and what I most want to work through: Clarence's tour is literally a counterfactual impact evaluation. George gets shown Pottersville, the world without his intervention -- a systemic counterfactual -- and the angel's lesson is exactly what an EA impact report would conclude: George's life had enormous, if previously invisible, effects. So does the film vindicate the institutional critique, or does its climactic device actually vindicate the counterfactual method EA defends, suggesting the problem is only that we under-measure rather than that we can't measure?

I'd like a reading that takes both sides seriously rather than using the film as a stick to beat EA with. Please identify the scenes that do the most work for the critique (the bank run, Potter's job offer, the missing $8,000 and the ending's bailout, the Pottersville sequence) and say exactly what each shows; flag where the film resists the anti-EA reading -- for instance, whether George's good is genuinely incommensurable or just unaccounted, and whether the final communal rescue, dependent as it is on one man's personal relationships, is a vision of the institutional good or a retreat from it; and tell me what you think the film adds to the philosophical debate that the argumentative literature on its own doesn't. My group ranges from an economist who is sympathetic to EA to a community organizer who is not, so if you can frame the discussion so both of them leave with something to chew on, all the better.
