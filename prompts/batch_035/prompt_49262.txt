I am a novelist, and my current book follows two philosophers in their seventies: a theist who has spent her career defending the free-will defence against the logical problem of evil, and her oldest friend, an atheist who presses the evidential argument from animal suffering. In the penultimate chapter, weary of forty years of talking past each other at conferences, they conduct a private adversarial collaboration and publish whatever joint statement they can both sign. Draft that document for me, as it will appear as an appendix in the novel.

The statement must contain, and contain only: (1) a numbered list of exactly five claims both can sign, each one or two sentences long, phrased so that neither would wince at the wording of her own side — these should cover, at minimum, what the free-will defence does and does not establish about the logical problem, the difference between a defence and a theodicy, and what the evidential argument requires in order to gain a foothold; and (2) a disagreements table with exactly five rows, one per residual disagreement, each row giving the disagreement in wording that the side holding the view would accept for itself, the crux beneath it, a case or argument both agree bears on that crux, and each side's prediction about it, recorded in advance.

Two constraints from the novel. First, at least one of the five disagreements must turn out, in the drafting, to be partly verbal — I suspect the word gratuitous is the culprit, since the theist means "without any God-justifying reason whatsoever" while the atheist means something closer to "without any reason we can see" — and that row should say precisely which part of the dispute dissolves and which part survives. Second, to stop the two of them recycling the fawn, they agree to reason together about a fresh case: the slow death from internal injury of the last member of a species of flightless bird on a Pacific atoll, some centuries before any human being reached the island. At least one signed claim and at least one table row must turn on this case. Both, being honest, will concede that the atheist cannot claim it as observed evil, while the theist cannot reach for free will or soul-making in explaining it — what each thinks God's silence there shows, or fails to show, is the hinge of the chapter.

Write it dry and unadorned, like the work product of two professionals: one small personal concession apiece is welcome, but no narration, no dialogue, nothing after the table.
