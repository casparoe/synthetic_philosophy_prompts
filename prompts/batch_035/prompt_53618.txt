I teach an upper-level seminar on the history of ethics (14 students, one 80-minute session) and I want to close our unit on how practices die with an activity rather than a lecture. The unit backs a thesis usually associated with Appiah: that practices like dueling and foot-binding were not argued out of existence. The sermons and pamphlets against dueling had existed for centuries and changed nothing; what ended it was a shift in who conferred standing — the moment members of the dueling world found themselves being laughed at by people whose opinion they had never needed to count.

Design an activity in which students discover this from the inside rather than being told it. Have them inhabit the following invented practice, which you should adapt as needed but whose structure matters. They are members of a small fictional guild in which every favor, courtesy, and service creates a recorded debt that can be repaid but never forgiven. Forgiving a debt is the gravest insult the guild knows: it tells the debtor they were never worth keeping track of. Prestige accrues to those with the longest, most elaborate ledgers; a member with no outstanding debts is suspected of having none worth having. The practice is materially ruinous — nobody sleeps, every dispute escalates because reconciliation is by definition unavailable — and everyone privately hates it. That last part is the point: it persists anyway, exactly as dueling did among gentlemen who had been denouncing it privately for generations.

Split the class. One faction is tasked with abolishing the ledger-norm by argument alone: give them real deliberation time and require them to produce actual objections, then rig the game so their arguments are experienced as fully accepted while changing nobody's behavior. A second faction must hold some other lever the game makes available — control over who counts as a guild member, the ability to make the practice legible to outsiders, whatever you judge works — such that the norm collapses through status rather than persuasion. Payoffs should be denominated in standing, not money, and students should not hear the Appiah thesis named until the debrief.

Alongside the rules and materials, give me a minute-by-minute timeline and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific moments in the historical story — what corresponds to the anti-dueling sermons that failed, and what corresponds to the outsiders whose laughter succeeded. The debrief should then ask whether the mechanism students just watched would have applied to slavery, whose abolition is standardly credited to argument and interest, so they see the thesis is a claim about a certain kind of norm rather than a law of history. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and be honest about what no classroom game can reproduce: real honor attaches to standing among people one actually has to live among, and the mechanism we are studying operated over generations, not eighty minutes.
