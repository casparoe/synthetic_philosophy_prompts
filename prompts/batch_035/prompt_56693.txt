I'm taking a graduate seminar on Nietzsche's On the Genealogy of Morality, and next week I have to present my own position on what a genealogy of morals can and cannot show. Before I stand up in front of everyone, I want to make sure my position is actually coherent, because when I write my five core commitments down they look like they might be pulling against each other. Here they are: (1) I read the Genealogy as a work of descriptive psychology and history — Nietzsche is explaining how our moral concepts actually arose (the slave revolt in morality driven by ressentiment, the internalization of blocked cruelty into bad conscience, the ascetic ideal giving suffering a meaning), and I take it as axiomatic that where a value comes from is, by itself, no argument against it; the genetic fallacy is a fallacy, and Nietzsche knew that as well as anyone. (2) Separately, I am persuaded by contemporary evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics: if our moral intuitions were shaped by selection pressures that had no regard for moral truth, that seems to give us a reason to distrust those intuitions. (3) I am a moral realist — there are stance-independent moral facts, and our judgments can track them. (4) My working method in ethics is reflective equilibrium: I test principles against considered judgments and adjust both until they fit, and I honestly don't know what else ethics could be if not something like that. (5) Even granting Nietzsche's whole psychological story about how guilt and compassion and equality came to occupy the place they do, my own commitment to equal respect survives fully intact — the genealogy explains the genealogy of my values without giving me any reason to revise them.

The thing I want checked is whether (1) and (5) can really sit alongside (2). My seminarmates keep insisting that Nietzsche's story about our moral concepts originating in a resentful inversion by the powerless is exactly the same shape as the evolutionary story: both explain our moral responses in terms of something other than sensitivity to moral truth. So if selection pressures can debunk our intuitions, why can't ressentiment? And conversely, if origins never refute, doesn't that defuse the evolutionary debunker too? I half suspect there's a principled difference — the evolutionary story applies to *all* of our intuitions indiscriminately, whereas the Nietzschean story is supposed to target a specific evaluative outlook — but I can't tell whether that difference does the work I need it to, since a defender of slave morality could always reply that the genealogy of the master's values is just as unflattering. So: audit the set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly? Which tensions dissolve on inspection — including the one my seminarmates insist on, if it does dissolve? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, tell me which single revision would clean up the most and what it costs, and don't assume I already know which commitment I'd drop, because I don't.
