A friend of mine sits on the regional board of a good-governance NGO, and we argue constantly about what should count as corruption. She grew up in a town where the local patron fixed everyone's problems, so her instincts are not the textbook's, but she is no relativist either: some things clearly appall her. Over the years she has given verdicts on six cases:

1. A procurement officer awards the road contract to his cousin's firm despite a cheaper and better rival bid: corrupt, no hesitation.
2. The same officer awards a contract to the genuinely best bidder; the firm's owner afterwards quietly pays for the officer's daughter's wedding: corrupt.
3. The officer hires his nephew as a junior clerk; the nephew is adequately qualified, and the post had sat unfilled for a year: not corrupt — that's just how a small town works, she says.
4. Constituents bring the mayor baskets of produce at harvest, worth perhaps a day's wages, and he accepts them: not corrupt.
5. The mayor holds weekly open hours helping constituents — supporters and opponents alike — with paperwork for benefits they are legally owed: not corrupt, obviously.
6. After a drought, the mayor sends the water trucks to his own parched home village before an equally dry village whose elders backed his rival: not corrupt — you look after your own first, she says, that's not what we're fighting.

A new case has now come to her board. Our mayor, entirely within his legal discretion, has spent a discretionary public-works budget overwhelmingly in the neighborhoods that voted for him: every project is legal, properly documented, and genuinely useful; nothing goes into his own pocket or his family's; and the wards that opposed him receive the same deferred maintenance they always received. After the budget passed, the beneficiary wards' leaders organized the turnout that re-elected him. The NGO's national office, applying a standard international definition, wants this flagged as clientelism and corruption. Will she vote to flag it?

Please reconstruct the rules her six verdicts are consistent with — I can see at least two or three that fit her record equally well (one centered on improper exchange, one on private enrichment through office, one on which groups an official is permitted to favor) — say what each rule predicts about the new case, give me your best guess at her vote along with your reasons, and then tell me which further case, if you put it to her, would most cleanly separate the candidate rules. On that last point, I'm curious whether you would test her with a variant of the wedding-payment case (an exchange in which the official's conduct never deviates and nothing is actually influenced), a variant of the cousin-contract case (an award that is tainted by kinship but happens to be the best bid on the merits), or something better than either.
