here are my gut verdicts on prisoner's dilemma-ish situations, and I'd like to know if there's a principle behind them or if I'm just vibing. One-shot against a stranger I'll never see again, no one watching: I defect, feels obvious. One-shot against someone who thinks exactly like me and will almost certainly pick what I pick: I cooperate, and defecting feels like leaving money on the table. One-shot against a stranger I happen to know always cooperates on principle: I lean defect, but it feels slimey. One-shot with no witnesses, but our choices will be described later to a community whose respect I care about: cooperate. Iterated with a friend, open-ended number of rounds: cooperate, at least until near the end. Iterated with a friend where we both know the exact number of rounds: I defect on the last one, and once I admit that, it seems like the whole thing should unravel backwards and my cooperation in earlier rounds should collapse too — but it doesn't, in my gut or in actual life. I've also noticed I reach for "defectors win in evolutionary terms" when I want to defect and "repeated games built civilization" when I want to cooperate, which makes me suspect the justifications are aftermarket. So: is there one principle that fits all or most of these, which of my verdicts would it have to overturn, and what does it say about the case I keep flip-flopping on — one-shot, no witnesses, no repetition, against a known always-cooperator who will never find out I defected? Please answer in continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold, like an email to a friend.
