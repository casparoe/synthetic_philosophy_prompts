Been having a running argument with my dad about "for want of a nail" history, you know, the idea that tiny stuff redirects everything. I realized I actually have pretty firm gut verdicts on individual cases but they don't seem to add up to one principle, so I'm trying to figure out what I actually believe. Here are the cases and my gut answers to "would history have gone more or less the same way without this?":

- No Caesar crossing the Rubicon: the Republic still dies, roughly on schedule. Some other strongman ends it, maybe not him, maybe a decade or two later. The Republic was rotten and the armies were loyal to generals not the Senate.
- Archduke's driver taking the wrong turn in Sarajevo so the car stops right in front of Princip: I still think WWI happens, maybe even on schedule. Everyone had mobilization timetables and the alliance system was a hair trigger. Something else lights it within a few years.
- No Constantine converting: Christianity still wins, it was growing like crazy, but without a Nicaea-type moment imposed from above, the creed probably looks pretty different.
- No Luther posting anything (or however that actually went): Reformation still happens, grievances and the printing press were there, but a reformation without a Luther personality cult probably looks alot bloodier or alot calmer, I honestly waffle here.
- No printing press in Europe around 1450: shows up within decades anyway, the Chinese had the pieces long before, someone assembles them in Europe soon.
- Cleopatra's nose. Pascal's old line, if her nose had been shorter, different men orbit her, different Roman civil wars, and here, weirdly, my gut says the late Republic actually does go a different way. This one feels individual in a way the Caesar one doesn't, even though both are one-person events in basically the same period, and I can't explain why.

So my tentative half-principle is something like: the coarser the outcome description and the longer you wait, the more it's fixed by slow stuff (geography, institutions, population pressure), and the more fine-grained or near-term it is, the more it's hostage to whatever particular thing happened. A river with rapids, the channel is structural, the eddies are contingent. But two problems. First, the butterfly effect seems to say the opposite, that ANY small change cascades, so why doesn't the wrong turn in Sarajevo redirect the whole 20th century instead of just shuffling who fires the first shot? Am I just betting that social systems dampen perturbations the way weather doesn't, and if so on what basis? Second, the Cleopatra verdict: if slow forces dominate coarse outcomes, she should be as swappable as Caesar, and my gut says she isn't, or maybe my gut is just being sexist? that possibility occurred to me and I don't love it.

Is there one principle that gets all these verdicts, or close? If you have to overturn some, which ones and why? And I'd want it to make a call on a case I haven't given: does the industrial revolution happen roughly on schedule in a world where Britain gets knocked out early, say no Glorious Revolution, or is that one of the rare things that actually needed its particular incubator?
