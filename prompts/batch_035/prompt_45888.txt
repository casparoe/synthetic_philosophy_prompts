Most jurisdictions that recognize advance directives treat them very asymmetrically: a written directive refusing treatment — no feeding tube if I am permanently unconscious, no antibiotics in end-stage dementia — is treated as binding even when the present, demented patient seems content, cooperates with care, and would visibly resist being left to die. But a directive requesting treatment — the earlier self instructing clinicians to keep treating despite later protest — carries far less weight, and clinicians and surrogates override such requests routinely, often citing the present patient's apparent wishes or best interests. Psychiatry displays a related ambivalence: self-binding arrangements, in which a patient with recurring psychotic or manic episodes instructs clinicians in advance to hospitalize or medicate her against her contemporaneous objection, are permitted in some places yet honored only partially, with clinicians frequently setting them aside whenever the present patient protests. What theory of the authority of the earlier self does this pattern of practice presuppose? Consider, at minimum: a sovereignty view on which the directive's force comes simply from its being my choice; a continuity view on which the earlier self has authority only insofar as it shares psychological connections — values, plans, projects — with the present patient; and an interests view on which the directive binds only because the earlier self was a better-placed judge of what is good for the person across time, an epistemic rather than a positional authority. Say which of these the refusal-request asymmetry actually fits, whether the asymmetry instead reveals that the practice presupposes no consistent theory at all, and whether the psychiatric reluctance toward self-binding is traceable to the same source as the medical reluctance to honor treatment requests or to something distinct (say, the thought that competence fluctuates rather than being lost). Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and note where it is underdetermined among the theories. Finally, take a definite position: tell me which account of the earlier self's authority you think the practice ought to rest on, and state concretely what would have to change — in the weight given to requesting directives, in the honoring of self-binding instructions, or elsewhere — if the practice were rebuilt on that account, rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose. I should mention that a colleague of mine claims the whole pattern reflects nothing philosophical, only liability avoidance, family pressure, and the optics of withdrawal of care, and that reading a theory of personal identity into legal boilerplate is a philosopher's indulgence; your answer should say directly whether that deflationary reading is sufficient or whether the asymmetry needs the normative explanation.
