I'm writing a section for a phil-bio paper arguing that when etiological and causal-role accounts of function disagree, the disagreement often can't be adjudicated until someone first settles which system-capacity analysis is in play — which makes "dysfunction" verdicts more interest-relative than either camp likes to admit. To make the point land I need a worked case, and the obvious ones are exhausted, so build me one to spec.

The direction of divergence I want is unusual: the trait must be performing its selected effect, at roughly the rate and in the circumstances selection shaped it for, so the etiological account says it's functioning properly — while a causal-role analysis of the organism says the trait is actually making a negative contribution to the system capacity being analyzed, so it comes out dysfunctional. Evolutionary mismatch material is fine (I suspect a stress-response or immune case can be made to work) but it's your call; just keep it biologically real, no more than one paragraph, and absolutely no swampmen, no traits popping into existence, no vestigial organs doing the old "wings of a penguin" routine since that's the textbook case where the accounts disagree about which function, not about whether the trait is working.

After the scenario, show your work. Run the etiological verdict with its actual machinery (what the selection history was, what the selected effect is, why performance counts as proper), then run the causal-role verdict with its machinery (name the containing system, name the capacity being analyzed, show the trait's contribution is negative), and make sure both verdicts are generated by the accounts rather than stipulated — if you find yourself just asserting "and this is dysfunctional," the case has failed.

Then three more things. First, tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to each verdict if you remove it. Second, give the causal-role theorist their best comeback — my guess is it's that the analysis choice was rigged, that a different capacity analysis makes the contribution positive, and that this relativity is a feature not a bug — and tell me whether that comeback saves them or concedes my point. Third, be honest about whether the case ends up favoring the etiological account after all, since a case I can't lose to is a case I haven't tested.

One more constraint: I need the case to survive a referee who knows the actual biology — if you fudge the empirical details to force the verdicts, say so at the end rather than papering over it. Dont worry about citations, I have those.
