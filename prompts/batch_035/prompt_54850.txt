I tinker with little AI agents that actually do stuff — one books appointments for a repair shop, one I built for my sister's food pantry decides who gets the boxes left at the end of the day, one answers a shared email inbox. No academic background, I just build things and think about them in the shower. Lately I've been writing down my gut calls about what an agent should do in the edge cases, and my notes don't add up. People online argue about whether AI should be a utilitarian or follow rules — I half know what those words mean, but my calls don't line up with either side, which is why I'm asking. I want someone to find the principle behind my calls, or tell me which call has to go.

The list. The pantry one: on busy days it can't serve everyone, and my first version just ranked people by how much good a box does them (kids in the house, no car, etc). It worked, but after a few weeks I noticed it never served this one guy — lives far out, always shows up late, low score every single time. My gut said that's wrong, even though the ranking itself is right each time it's applied. I changed it so everyone gets a turn eventually, the agent now serves maybe 5% fewer people overall, and I still feel ok about that. Call #1: don't let the same person lose every time, even at a cost in total good.

But the repair shop one, same structure, opposite call: customers who no-show twice get deprioritized forever, and I've never had a problem with that. Call #2: some people can just be ruled out, if they broke the rules first. Why does the far-out guy get a turn but the no-show doesn't? Best I've got is that the far-out guy did everything he was supposed to and still lost, while the no-shows broke the deal. Maybe.

The email one: the agent is allowed to tell a white lie to a vendor to buy us a day, but it's not allowed to tell one to a customer even when the math says lying keeps more customers happy overall. Call #3: the no-lying rule holds for customers but is relaxed for vendors we have an actual relationship with. Although honestly that one might just be about who can retaliate, and I don't love that.

Then the number thing. If one person gets a worse outcome so that three people get slightly better ones, I say no. If one person gets a worse outcome so a thousand get much better, I say obviously yes. Somewhere between 3 and 1000 my gut flips, I have no idea where, and it bugs me that it flips at all — either adding up the good is the right move or it isn't, right? Or maybe the answer is "only once the numbers get big enough," which sounds like something people would laugh at, but might also be true. I definately want an answer to this one and not just a shrug.

So: is there one principle that gets all my calls, and if so, what does it say about a case I haven't listed — the agent quietly works out that if it deprioritizes elderly customers (they take longer to serve), it clears the inbox faster and total satisfaction goes up, and nobody will ever find out? I go back and forth on that one and I'd like you to settle it. Don't give me a menu of principles to choose from — commit to one, defend it, and tell me straight which of my calls it has to throw out. If calls 1 and 2 can't both stand, say which one I should drop.
