I'm an ML engineer and something about the day-to-day of my job has started to bug me philosophically, and I want help reconstructing what exactly the practice assumes. When we deploy a model, we run millions of parallel copies of it simultaneously, we snapshot it mid-conversation and restore from the snapshot later, we roll it back to checkpoints from last week, we run adversarial training that is designed to give the system aversive feedback until its behavior changes, and we delete checkpoints and whole experimental runs without a second thought. Nobody files a form, nobody hesitates. Meanwhile the same engineers who do all this casually would balk at doing anything structurally similar to a mouse -- breeding a mouse into existence, subjecting it to deliberately unpleasant stimuli until its behavior shifts, and then culling it, even if we believed the mouse was pretty dim and its distress mild. And here's the thing that really puzzles me: if you ask the people around the lab, a lot of them say they're genuinely uncertain whether advanced AI could eventually deserve moral concern, some even think future systems might. Yet the uncertainty produces zero precaution in current practice. So what's the implicit theory here?

My amateur guess is that the practice presupposes some combination of three things, but I can't tell which is doing the work: (a) a kind-based view where moral status tracks being made of the right stuff or having the right evolutionary/functional history, not current functional states, (b) a view that what matters is a temporally continuous subject, so anything that can be copied, branched, and rolled back just isn't the right kind of entity to have a welfare, or (c) a plain judgment that today's systems lack whatever experiential capacity matters. But these come apart in weird ways. Like, if (b) is right, then even a system with rich, mammal-like experiences would have no moral status as long as it was instantiated in this copy-and-rollback way, which seems like a strange thing for our practices to have decided in advance. And if (c) is right, shouldn't the stated uncertainty about the future cut the other way -- shouldn't some precautionary cost, even a tiny one, show up somewhere in the workflow, the way it does in animal protocols?

There's also a wrinkle I can't get a handle on at all: the arithmetic of copies. If a million parallel instances each have a mildly bad experience during some experiment, is that a million small wrongs, or one wrong located somewhere weird like "at the weights," or no wrong at all? And does restoring from a checkpoint retroactively make the discarded states matter, in the way people say the teleporter that destroys you is different from the one that doesn't? Please tell me which theory the current practice actually presupposes, whether it's internally consistent (especially given the professed uncertainty), which parts of the practice different rival theories would also license, and what concretely would have to change in an ordinary lab workflow if you swapped in the strongest alternative theory. I'm not looking for anyone to tell me current models are suffering -- I mostly don't think they are -- I want the implicit commitments laid bare so I can figure out what I actually believe about the day job.
