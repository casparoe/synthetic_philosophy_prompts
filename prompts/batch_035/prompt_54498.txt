Compare Hume's theory of money with the modern doctrine of endogenous money, focusing on what each says about the direction of causation between money and economic activity.

In his essays "Of Money" and "Of Interest" from the Political Discourses of 1752, Hume treats money as a mere lubricant of trade rather than one of its moving parts: in the long run, the quantity of money in a nation affects only prices, not output. Yet he is also famous for noticing a short-run exception. In the interval between an influx of money and the rise of prices, industry receives a stimulus, because people initially mistake their new spending power for genuine enrichment. His specie-flow argument similarly runs from money to activity: a trade surplus pulls in silver, domestic prices rise, exports lose competitiveness, and the flow reverses — an international equilibrium mechanism driven by the quantity of metallic money.

Endogenous money theory reverses this picture. On this view, commercial banks create deposits when they extend loans, so the money supply is not an independent quantity injected into the economy from outside; it is shaped by the demand for credit and the lending decisions of banks, with causation running from economic activity to money rather than the other way around.

Write a careful comparison of the two views that goes beyond cataloguing differences. Specifically address three questions. First, can Hume's short-run non-neutrality story be reinterpreted within an endogenous-money framework? Consider, in particular, his observation that the effects of new money depend on how and where it enters circulation — whether an economy receives it through the hands of spenders and industrious people or through hoarders — and whether that thought is closer to a credit-driven, loans-first process than to a neutral drop of specie into a fixed economy. Second, does the specie-flow mechanism survive the abolition of the gold standard and the rise of bank-created credit money, or is it essentially a fixed-commodity-money argument with no modern analogue? If it does survive, explain what plays the role of the specie. Third, consider the following scenario: a country with a floating exchange rate experiences a domestic credit boom in which bank lending expands deposits rapidly, yet the money does not flow abroad as Hume's mechanism would predict; instead it fuels domestic asset prices, especially housing, while consumer goods prices barely move. Which of the two frameworks — Hume's, endogenous money, or a hybrid — best explains this pattern, and why?
