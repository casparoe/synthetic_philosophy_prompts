I'm a PhD student in bioethics working on concepts of health and disease, and after a year of seminars on naturalism and normativism I've become fixated on an asymmetry between how psychiatry and the rest of medicine fix the extension of the term "disease." The dominant psychiatric diagnostic manual defines a mental disorder as a syndrome involving a clinically significant disturbance in cognition, emotion regulation, or behavior that reflects a dysfunction in the underlying psychological, biological, or developmental processes — and it then adds, verbatim, that "Mental disorders are usually associated with significant distress or disability in social, occupational, or other important activities." So the definition has two components: a dysfunction component, which looks like a straightforwardly naturalist notion of failed biological/psychological function, and a harm component, which looks normative — and the harm component is hedged with a "usually." The manual also goes further than most textbooks: it stipulates that an expectable or culturally approved response to a common stressor or loss does not count as a disorder, and that socially deviant behavior (political, religious, sexual) and conflicts that are primarily between the individual and society do not count either, unless the deviance results from a dysfunction in the individual. Meanwhile, somatic medicine happily counts as diseases conditions that produce no distress or disability: asymptomatic hypertension picked up on a routine screen, early cancers, silent infections. Nobody demands that the hypertensive patient be bothered by his blood pressure before we call it a disease.

What I'd like is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of disease that this psychiatric definitional practice presupposes. Is it a hybrid — naturalist about the dysfunction component and normativist about the harm component — and if so, are the pieces consistent with one another? The exclusion clauses seem to naturalize the criteria by insisting that harm is never sufficient without internal dysfunction, while the "usually" hedge seems to quietly concede that harm isn't even necessary. Do these pull in opposite directions, or is there a coherent reading on which they fit together? And is the apparent inconsistency with somatic medicine real — does general medicine presuppose a purely naturalist, value-free account of disease (something like the biostatistical theory), or can it too be read as requiring a normative element that is simply satisfied in the background of our clinical practices?

I'd also like you to be disciplined about what the practice strictly presupposes as opposed to what it is merely compatible with. Could a thoroughgoing naturalist accept the manual's text by reading the distress clause as a fallible diagnostic marker of dysfunction rather than a constituent of disorder? Could a thoroughgoing normativist accept it by treating the dysfunction language as a way of registering whose complaints get social standing? Where exactly is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories of health and disease would license the same criteria? Finally, tell me what would actually have to change in the manual if the presupposed view were replaced by pure normativism — and where the practical pressure points would be, particularly for contested categories where the presence of harm is precisely what's disputed (the ones that attract accusations of disease-mongering). Would the asymmetry with somatic medicine survive that replacement, or would the two halves of medicine end up needing a single shared account?
