I run a philosophy workshop for first-year undergrads — around 18 people, one 75-minute session — and I want them to actually feel hermeneutical injustice rather than just be told about it. The problem I keep hitting: hermeneutical injustice is a gap in shared vocabulary, and once the topic is on the syllabus my students already have the words, so the gap is gone before we start. So design an activity where the gap is real for a while: participants go through some experience they can't name, and the naming only becomes possible when someone else in the room — someone who controls a resource the participants need (an official record, a glossary, a scorecard — your call) — supplies the vocabulary. The gap should track power, not just ignorance. No lecture up front, nobody hears the phrase "hermeneutical injustice" until the debrief, and it should be visible in what participants do, not in what I say afterwards. Give me rules, materials, rough timing, a debrief that maps the moments of the activity onto the distinction between a gap that's mere ignorance and a gap that's structured by who got to write the vocabulary, and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (e.g. students smelling a philosophy trick and gaming it) plus how to handle that. Also be honest about which part of the real phenomenon no classroom version can reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief myself.
