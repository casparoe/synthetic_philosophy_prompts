I help run a sort of life-skills workshop at the community centre here — mostly adults who didnt finish school, we meet tuesday evenings, about 12 people, and we've got 90 minutes. This term I want to do a session on why we do stuff we already decided not to do. I got obsessed with this after noticing my own pattern: every sunday night I write a plan for the week, and by wednesday I've broken it, and the weird part is I don't even feel like I changed my mind — the plan still looks right from a distance, it just loses when the moment actually arrives.

I've been reading on my own (no philosophy background, just blogs and a couple of pop-psych books) and two things clicked for me. One is the old idea of akrasia — acting against your own better judgement, like you're watching yourself do it. The other is from behavioral econ, about how people discount the future: not smoothly like compound interest, but steeply up close, so a small reward available NOW can beat a bigger one later, even though an hour ago you'd have picked the bigger one. The detail that really got me is that apparently people's choices can flip just because one option became immediate, even when the gap between the two options stays exactly the same. That's the difference between "people are just impatient" and "the near future works differently than the far future," and I want people to feel that flip happen to themselves rather than have me explain it on a whiteboard.

So: design me an activity for this. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear any of the jargon until the debrief at the end. Real but small stakes — we have a tiny budget, like £15, but it doesn't have to be money, snacks or break time or whatever works is fine. What I picture is at least two decision points far enough apart that they commit one way and then get tempted the other way, plus a third setup that specifically tests whether the flip tracks the immediacy of the reward and not just how long you have to wait — that's the part I care about most. Then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the two ideas, and onto the distinction between genuinely changing your mind versus losing an argument with yourself.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. And since I'm going to run this on real people tuesday: be upfront about how confident you are in the design actually working, and flag the one part you're least sure about.
