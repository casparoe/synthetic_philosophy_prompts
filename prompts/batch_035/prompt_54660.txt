I teach philosophy of science at a state school, which mostly means arguing with engineers about falsificationism, but every couple years I get roped into a seminar on time. Anyway, at a department dinner last week my counterpart in chemistry announced, apropos of nothing, that the whole problem of time's direction is captured by the phrase "you can't unscramble an egg" — entropy goes up, eggs don't unscramble, we remember the past and not the future, case closed, pass the salt. Everyone nodded like that settled it.

I want to push back on him in the seminar, but fairly, and the more I stare at that little proverb the less sure I am what exactly it's claiming. Help me take it apart. Lay out the distinct precise theses "you can't unscramble an egg" could be standing in for when it's put to arrow-of-time work — I'm guessing there's a spectrum from the utterly trivial (this egg, in this pan, will not in fact reassemble) up through something statistical (it's not impossible, just overwhelmingly improbable — and wait, isn't that a problem, since the same statistics run backwards tell you the egg probably didn't get scrambled either?), to something about the asymmetry of manipulation, which a grad student once tried to explain to me as "you can melt an ice cube with a flame but no contrivance will refreeze warm water" being the same fact as why our interventions reach the future but not the past. And somewhere in there is the reading that would actually get my chemist from scrambled eggs to why I have memories of yesterday and none of tomorrow — I skimmed David Albert's Time and Chance years ago and remember something about needing an extra postulate about the universe's low-entropy past before the second law explains anything about records or memory, so maybe that's the load-bearing reading, or maybe I'm conflating things.

For each reading: is it true, is it contested, and could it carry the weight he dumped on it at dinner? A menu, not a defense of one option. And please keep a running separation of what's settled among people who actually work on this (physicists and philosophers both) from what's genuinely fought over — I gather the Past Hypothesis is a lightening rod and I'd like to know if that's right or something I half-remembered from a book review. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence — your overall verdict on whether the chemist's one-liner is basically fine or smuggling in a big claim — then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question.
