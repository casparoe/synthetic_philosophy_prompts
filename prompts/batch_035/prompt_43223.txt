So Berkeley, the bishop everyone knows for "to be is to be perceived," also wrote this whole book about economics called The Querist, where he argues that money isn't wealth at all -- real wealth is industry, and money is basically just a counter we use for keeping track of who's owed what. He literally asks, in Query 23, "Whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?" and elsewhere suggests that a smaller amount of money circulating quickly is equivalent to a larger amount sitting still. He was desperate to get Ireland's economy moving and thought a properly managed national bank issuing paper could do it.

My study group got into it about what he'd say about modern money and I want your take. A friend of mine is into crypto and claims Berkeley would obviously be a Bitcoin guy: the ticket theory means money doesn't need intrinsic value or state backing, just confidence, and gold bugs who say paper money is fake should love a currency that's just the pure ticket with all pretense stripped away. I think that's backwards: Berkeley only cared about money insofar as it "stirreth up industry," and a currency that people buy specifically to sit on (the whole hodl thing, plus the hard cap at 21 million coins) seems like it would strike him as the Irish hoarding problem all over again, money that's stopped circulating and hence stopped doing its one job. But then again negative interest rates and quantitative easing are states basically trying to force money to move, which sounds very Berkeleyan, and yet he also wanted the state's national bank to be expertly managed, which is not really the decentralized ethos.

So, if the bishop could be briefed on all this: where would he land on Bitcoin, on central bank digital currencies, and on whether a government can ever be trusted to run the kind of bank he wanted for Ireland? Assume he understands the tech. Walk me through the reasoning from his actual commitments, and flag any points where you think his views just don't settle the question either way.
