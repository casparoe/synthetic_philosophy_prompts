Last weekend my oldest friend and I had an argument that ran from dinner until nearly four in the morning, and we ended it more exhausted than resolved. We are both probability people by trade — she runs the quantitative risk desk at a reinsurance firm, I do population-level forecasting for a health agency — and the argument started, as these things do, from a small practical question: whether the probabilities in her catastrophe models are, in any honest sense, *out there in the world*, or whether they are only a disciplined summary of what she and her models believe. By the small hours we had reconstructed a debate that we later found has an entire literature attached to it.

She takes the Humean, reductionist line: there is nothing more to a chance than its role in whatever system of regularities best balances simplicity and strength in summarising the total history of the world. Chance-facts, on her view, are fixed partly by how things actually turn out — the best system for our world is selected by looking at the whole mosaic, including the future. I hold that chances are objective, mind-independent quantities that would be there even if no one theorised about them: single-case propensities of setups, if you like, though I am not wedded to the machinery. What neither of us disputes is the deference norm we both started from — that if you know the objective chance of an outcome is x, and you have no 'inadmissible' further information, your credence in that outcome ought to be x. (I gather this is standardly called the Principal Principle, after David Lewis.) Where we came to blows was on what that principle *is*: I say it is a substantive norm connecting two distinct things, the world's chanciness and our degrees of belief; she says it is closer to a definition — chance just is whatever rational deference tracks, and its objectivity is the objectivity of a good bookkeeping convention.

Three subsidiary fights she insists I record faithfully. First, I claim the best-system view cannot give non-trivial chances in a deterministic universe without smuggling in observer-relative notions of simplicity, while she claims a system that says 'about 4% of reactors of this type fail per decade' can be the best system even where the underlying dynamics are deterministic. Second, she raised what she tells me Lewis himself flagged as a bug in his own theory: the principle, taken literally together with the best-system analysis, seems to give absurd instructions once you form credences about propositions concerning the chances themselves, or about futures that would make the best system different — the so-called undermining problem. She thinks the fix is to restrict what counts as admissible evidence; I think once you patch admissibility case by case you have quietly abandoned the principle for something weaker. Third, there is the reference-class mess: her desk prices risk against pools of past events, and she cannot say, without embarrassment, which pool a brand-new risk belongs to; I claim this is a symptom of her view's confuse-the-epistemic-with-the-ontic; she claims propensities face exactly the same problem in fancy dress.

Here is what I would like you to write, and please write it in British English throughout. Draft the joint statement the two of us should have produced at 4 a.m. instead of parting testy on her doorstep: a document we could both sign. It should contain, in order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — including whatever we both accept about the formal role of conditional probabilities, about the deference norm in ordinary cases, and about what we take ourselves to be disagreeing *about* (so that neither of us can later claim the dispute was merely verbal when it was not, or vice versa if you conclude part of it is verbal).

2. A disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated once in wording I would accept for my own view and once in wording she would accept for hers, with no formulation slanted so that one of us is made to hold something silly.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us. I suspect they include whether a probability can be simultaneously objective and dependent on the totality of actual facts, whether non-trivial single-case chance is coherent under determinism, and what the undermining problem really shows — but you may find others.

4. For each crux, at least one concrete case, test, or argument both of us would concede is relevant — she suggests the deterministic reactor model above; I would add something like an isotope whose decay statistics are best systematised probabilistically in a world whose physics is, at bottom, not chancy — together with each of our predictions about it, recorded in advance so we cannot backfit later.

Be exact, be fair to both sides, and where you think one of us is simply confused, say so in the document rather than flattering us in the claims section. No reading lists, no citations to literature I would have to go and verify; if a named position helps, use it only as much as we would use it in conversation across a kitchen table.
