I accept the Gödelian anti-mechanist conclusion of Lucas and Penrose: roughly, that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show human mathematical understanding cannot be captured by any Turing machine, because for any consistent formal system we can see that its Gödel sentence is true, and no such system can establish that. What would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want them to include: formal results (e.g., arguments that the anti-mechanist must claim to know their own consistency, or that human mathematical belief is demonstrably inconsistent and so outside the theorem's scope altogether); empirical developments (what exactly would it take from neuroscience or physics — say, evidence that the processes Penrose invokes in quantum gravity or in the brain turn out to be simulable — and how much of the view actually depends on those, as opposed to resting on the pure Gödelian argument); and things a suitably advanced AI system could do or fail to do that should move me (e.g., if systems started finding genuinely new proofs while also endorsing the consistency of the very systems that generate them). I also want an honest assessment of the retreat pattern I suspect in my own view: when the original claim that we can always see the truth of our Gödel sentence runs into the objection that we might be inconsistent or unable to know our own consistency, defenders weaken it to something like "no *knowably* sound machine captures us." Has that progression qualified the view into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a fatal problem for a position of this kind or a legitimate response to an unfair demand for empirical content in mathematics? Tell me which of the defeaters committed anti-mechanists have themselves acknowledged versus dismissed, and where you think I am most exposed.
