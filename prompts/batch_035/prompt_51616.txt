I have been asked to give the closing address at an annual vigil commemorating the abolition of the death penalty in France on 9 October 1981. The organization hosting it works with prisoners' families, and the room will be mixed: long-time abolitionist activists, a contingent of law students, several members of a Catholic peace community, and — the reason I am writing to you rather than drafting this myself — a delegation of murder victims' relatives who support capital punishment but attend each year out of respect for the organizers. Please draft the address, roughly six minutes spoken, formal in register but not academic.

I want the speech built on Camus's 1957 essay Reflections on the Guillotine, and I want his argument to do real structural work rather than serve as a literary flourish. Two constraints in particular. First, the vigil's program this year is organized around innocence and exoneration cases, and I do not want my address to quietly concede the converse — that if guilt were certain, execution would be justified. Camus's deeper claim was precisely about the unquestionably guilty: that the death penalty is an act of cold administrative premeditation, prepared and scheduled by the state, which makes it in a sense worse than the crimes it answers, and that a secular state claiming the godlike authority to make an irrevocable final judgment over a human being is overreaching in a way that no empirical argument about deterrence can repair. The speech must keep the crowd that came for the innocence cases while refusing to let the innocent-executed framing carry the whole argument. Second, Camus contrasts the horror of the crime, which he treats as humanly comprehensible — despair, blindness, passion — with the horror of the ceremony that answers it, where responsibility is so distributed among judges, jurors, and officials that no one finally owns the killing. I want that asymmetry in the speech, but it must be voiced in a way that the bereaved families in the room can hear without taking it as a claim that their loved ones' murderers deserve sympathy they have never received.

One further constraint: the speech should close on something other than an invocation of Camus by name — he may be cited once, early, but his reasoning should thereafter be present as the spine of the argument rather than quoted as scripture. And please do not lean on statistical claims about deterrence or exoneration rates; the audience has heard those figures all evening, and I would rather not assert numbers I cannot stand behind at a podium.
