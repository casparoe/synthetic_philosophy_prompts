I'm in a reading group that's been going through the Frankfurt stuff on moral responsibility, and I want to design my own Frankfurt-style case rather than just rehash the Black-and-Jones setup where the neuroscientist waits in the wings. The twist I want to test is two variables that the classic cases leave alone: (a) the intervener's trigger is reason-sensitive, meaning the counterfactual intervener would only step in if the agent were about to do the *right* thing for a particular reason, and stays home if the agent does the right thing for a different reason or does the wrong thing for any reason at all; and (b) the agent *knows* the intervener is there and roughly how the trigger works, so the agent acts in full view of the fact that one specific path is closed off, while other paths (including some good ones) remain open.

So here's the assignment. Write me a one-paragraph scenario meeting these constraints: it has to be realistic and mundane -- an office, a family dispute, something like that, no brain implants, no mind-reading devices, and crucially no perfect knowledge on the intervener's part either, so the trigger has to be something the intervener could actually detect through ordinary means (which is genuinely tricky given the reason-sensitivity, and I want you to solve it rather than cheat by making the reason behaviorally obvious); and the agent should know about the intervener without the disclosure itself being the reason the agent acts badly. After the scenario, do the walkthrough: state exactly what the intervener would have done and in which nearby worlds he'd have done it, catalogue whatever alternative possibilities the agent still has (there should be at least a flicker or two), say what Frankfurt himself concludes about blameworthiness in a case like this, and then say what a flickers-of-freedom defender will latch onto here. Then tell me honestly which of my two added variables is actually doing any work: does the reason-tracking trigger make this start to look more like a manipulation case than an overdetermination case, and does the disclosure change anything or does it collapse back into the original Frankfurt machinery with cosmetic upgrades? Finish with how a defender of the principle of alternative possibilities would attack the case, and whether the attack is better or worse than the standard one against the classic cases.

To be clear about what counts as cheating: don't just stipulate that the agent is blameworthy or that alternatives are absent -- the verdicts have to fall out of how the case is set up. And don't make the intervener's presence causally involved in what the agent actually does; whatever the agent does, the intervener has to remain causally idle, same as in the originals. I want this for a presentation where I argue that most Frankfurt case variants are idle-wheel decorations on the 1969 argument, so if you think one of my two variables undermines that thesis, say so rather than saving my ego.
