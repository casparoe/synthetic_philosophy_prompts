Two faculty members at a law school — one a committed textualist, one a committed purposivist — have co-taught a course on legislation for years. Last spring, a workshop hypothetical exposed that each of their methods was pushing them toward a conclusion they found uncomfortable, and they have since agreed to run a structured adversarial collaboration. They have asked me to commission the joint statement they will both sign. Draft that document.

The hypothetical that provoked the collaboration is as follows. In 1994, the legislature of a (fictitious) State enacted the Graduates Remain Act, whose preamble recites an intent to reduce the exodus of degree-holders from the State. Section 3 provides: "A resident student shall be eligible for a tuition waiver at any institution of higher education located within the State." The legislative file includes a committee report describing eligibility as extending to students at any institution of higher education "located within the State and maintaining its principal place of instruction within the State." The enacted text omits the second clause, and nothing in the record shows whether that omission was a scrivener's error or a deliberate late-stage narrowing negotiated in conference. No amendment has been enacted in the three decades since. Apex Online University is chartered in the State, keeps its administrative offices there, delivers every course over the internet, and maintains no campus anywhere. Dana, a State resident whose post-graduation plans are unknown, enrolls in Apex's fully online program and applies for the waiver.

The discomfort at the workshop ran against type. The textualist found herself drawn to the conclusion that the statute covers Dana — a resident student, attending an institution located within the State, with the operative words doing their ordinary work. The purposivist found himself drawn to denying the waiver — the Act's object was anchoring degree-holders physically in the State, and tuition support for online enrollment does not obviously serve that object, especially since the omitted "principal place of instruction" clause suggests the enacting legislature itself contemplated instruction sites within the State. Both are troubled that their positions look like the caricature of the other camp.

The joint statement must contain, in this order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — at least six, of which at least three concern the Apex case itself (for instance, about what the enacted text does resolve, what the committee report does and does not establish, and what the thirty years of legislative inaction do and do not show). Each claim must be one both parties could genuinely sign, neither vacuous nor slanted toward either side.

2. A table of residual disagreements. Each disagreement must be stated twice: once in wording the textualist would accept as a fair statement of her own view, and once in wording the purposivist would accept as a fair statement of his. Formulations that smuggle in a victory for one side are unacceptable.

3. A cruxes section identifying the subquestions such that settling any one of them would move at least one party. Candidates worth weighing include: how much weight the unexplained omission of the "principal place of instruction" clause deserves, given that the record leaves it undetermined whether it reflects error or intent; whether "purpose" in this Act should be taken as the concrete 1994 mischief the enacting coalition targeted or as the more abstract rationale underlying it; and whether long legislative acquiescence amid the growth of online education carries any interpretive significance. Select the cruxes you actually take to structure the dispute, and justify the selection.

4. For each crux, one test, argument, or hypothetical discovery that both parties would agree bears on it — a recovered drafting file, a hypothetical later amendment, the application of an established canon, or something else — together with each side's prediction, recorded in advance and phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own prediction.

5. A disposition section. Either the parties agree on Dana's application (in which case justify both signatures) or they record, precisely, what would have to be established for each to change his or her mind.

6. A closing section stating whether any part of their dispute over Dana is merely verbal, and if so, which part and why.

Both faculty members insist that the statement stand on the hypothetical alone: no citation to real cases, scholarship, or legislative history, since the collaboration is about method, not authority. Write in a formal register throughout, and answer in American English.
