I spent thirty-five years doing experimental work in condensed matter physics before retiring a few years ago, and I have lately been reading philosophy of science with the enthusiasm of a convert. One conviction has survived the reading, and I would like it properly stress-tested rather than admired. It is that physics has been converging on the truth, in a robust sense: not merely that each theory is useful and then discarded, but that successive theories stand in a genuine relation of approximate truth to one another, so that it is a substantive, checkable fact that our current theories are closer to the truth than the ones they replaced. Two observations anchor this for me. First, the phenomenon of reduction in a limit: Newtonian mechanics falls out of special relativity as velocities get small, classical thermodynamics out of statistical mechanics as fluctuations average away, and so on. It is hard for me to believe this nested structure is an accident; it looks like successive approximations to something. Second, the machinery works. Instruments built on theories we now consider strictly false kept functioning and still do — lenses ground by ray optics, interferometers designed before quantum optics — which suggests the old theories were approximately right rather than simply wrong.

I am aware the notion of "closer to the truth" has a troubled formal history. I have read that the first careful attempts to define it, going back to Popper and his successors in the 1960s and early 1970s, were shown to have absurd consequences — ordering theories in ways no one could accept — and that philosophers have been constructing repairs ever since. What I have not seen clearly laid out is what the stakes actually are for someone holding my view. So here is my request. Treat my position charitably but adversarially: what would count against it? I am not only after the standard induction from past discarded theories — phlogiston, caloric, the aether — though I do want an honest account of how much weight that induction deserves. I also want the less familiar defeaters: which conceivable patterns in the future history of physics would genuinely disconfirm convergence rather than merely complicate it; what it would mean if two empirically equivalent but structurally incompatible frameworks persisted indefinitely; whether the reduction-in-the-limit phenomenon could be true even if the "approximately true" reading of it were false. And I want to know which of these defeaters the defenders of my view would themselves concede as real problems, as opposed to the ones only its critics press.

Please rank the difficulties from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about where you think my own framing is most likely to have gone wrong — for instance, whether I am mistaking an instrumental success story for an epistemic one, or quietly assuming that because an old theory survives as a limiting case it must have been tracking the structure of the world rather than merely the surface behavior of instruments. Finally, tell me what would change your verdict: which single argument, piece of historical evidence, or possible future development, if it held up, would move you to the other side, and where in your own reasoning you are least confident. I would rather leave with one well-identified weak joint than with a polished defense.
