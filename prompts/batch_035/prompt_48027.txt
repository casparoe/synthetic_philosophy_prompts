I am a novelist at work on a book that follows a family through a national calamity, and two people whose judgment I trust have divided openly over how the past should figure in it. My editor argues for what I can only call exemplary history: the past in fiction exists to be shaped into images of greatness and endurance, compressed and ennobled until the ancestors stand as exemplars; readers, she says, come to historical novels precisely to be enlarged by the past, and a writer who declines that office is peddling trivia. My oldest reader, a retired historian, objects that this is the manufacture of myth: the family's members lived in the grain of things — what they ate, what they owed, whom they feared — and the novel's duty is fidelity to that grain, preserving the past for its own sake rather than bending it to our need for inspiration. He adds that exemplary history is how nations falsify their origins, and he wants no part of it. My editor answered that a novel built as an archive becomes a museum and its author its curator: reverence for the particular hardens into nostalgia, and a writer who only preserves and never selects ends up living backward, more an object among objects than a person, drained of the present tense. He conceded some of this — he admitted that a purely preservative temper can embalm a book — but replied that the remedy is not her ennobling images; it is, on occasion, cold judgment: the novelist must sometimes drag the family's past to the bar, condemn part of it, and take the knife to it, even at the risk that nothing wholly innocent survives the trial, because only by destroying something in the past can the book live with the rest of it. At this my editor looked rather alarmed and said that judging the dead for the sake of the living is no safer than ennobling them: every generation rewrites its parents into villains to flatter itself, and a book that starts condemning will end up serving the present just as slavishly as one that starts worshipping. I could not referee, and I have a book to finish. Please go through their exchange move by move, say who is right at each step and by how much, and tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing — including, if you can, what a book that satisfied neither of them entirely would be doing with the past.
