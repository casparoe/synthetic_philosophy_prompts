I work on a technical standards committee that has to write definitions containing vague predicates — when a system is "reliable enough" to be fielded, when a model is "sufficiently interpretable" to satisfy a disclosure rule, when a concentration is "excessive" for a given zone — and I've noticed something that unsettles my amateur's understanding of the vagueness literature. The philosophy I half-remember, built around the sorites paradox, seems organized around explaining why there is no sharp boundary between the heap and the non-heap, whereas the practical half of my job is legislating one: some threshold must go in the document, a number or a criterion, and people will litigate at the margin. Before I read any further in the literature, I want the specification an adequate theory of vagueness would have to meet for a purpose like mine — a theory of vagueness good enough to advise a body that must draw boundaries on purpose.

Write the desiderata. I'd expect the list to include at least the following sorts of items, though I want you to exercise judgment about what belongs and what doesn't: which cases the theory must get right (the ordinary sorites for a vague predicate like "bald" or "rich," but also forced-boundary cases where an institution must fix a cutoff, and the interaction between them — does the legislated line change the predicate's meaning or merely its application?); what it must say about higher-order vagueness and whether a theory that eliminates it is thereby disqualified; what it owes us about the appeal of tolerance premises — why the one-grain step seems unobjectionable in a way the full induction never does, and whether a theory that treats that appeal as simple error has missed something; whether it must deliver a determinate truth-value for each marginal statement or is permitted some weaker commitment structure, and what that means for a standards body that needs a yes-or-no answer about a borderline case; and how it should handle penumbral connections (that a shade noticeably darker than a definitely dark one is dark) and context shifts (tall for a jockey versus tall for a center). For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and be explicit about which are non-negotiable for my legislative purpose versus which are prejudices of one camp — supervaluationists, epistemicists, degree theorists, and contextualists each smuggle in demands the others rightly refuse, and I'd like that sorted out rather than papered over.

The part I most want to see is whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable: I suspect that a requirement of tolerance — that adjacent cases in a sorites series cannot be treated differently without a relevant difference — cannot coexist with the requirement that the theory bless a legislated sharp cutoff, or at least that the two can be reconciled only by an unappealing move, such as treating the legislated predicate as an artificial homonym of the vague one. If that is right, say so and say which requirement you would abandon. Then, briefly, run the main contenders — epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree or fuzzy theories, and contextualism — against the list; keep the scoring short, since the criteria are where the thinking is. And tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates, because that will tell me which literature to read first.
