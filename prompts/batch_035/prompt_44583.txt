In the spring I am expecting my first child, a decision my partner and I made with a confidence I have been entirely unable to reconstruct since the pregnancy test came back positive. In the middle of this I rewatched Arrival, Denis Villeneuve's film based on Ted Chiang's novella, and found it unusually hard to shake. Louise Banks gains, through the heptapods' nonlinear perception of time, exactly what L. A. Paul says the prospective parent cannot have: the phenomenology of raising her daughter before deciding to conceive her, down to the girl's early death. She chooses to go ahead anyway. I would like you to read the film through the philosophy of transformative experience and, just as much, the philosophy through the film, and tell me whether what the film depicts is a resolution of the problem or an elegant way of changing the subject.

Several tensions I want you to take seriously rather than smooth over. First, Paul's problem is that you cannot know what having a child is like before having one, and so cannot weigh it in the usual decision-theoretic way; the film grants Louise precisely that foreknowledge, which suggests it solves the problem only by removing the condition that generated it. Is that a cheat, or is it the point, a demonstration that transformative choices can be made rationally only by someone for whom they are no longer transformative, so that the film is a fantasy rather than an argument? Second, Paul distinguishes experiences that are epistemically transformative from ones that are personally transformative, changing who you are and what you value. Louise gets the knowledge, but she is still the one who will become a mother, with utilities she does not yet hold. Can you rationally choose to become someone whose values you can describe but do not yet have, and does knowing your future values in vivid detail amount to having them? Third, one familiar response to Paul is that you cannot choose the outcome but can choose to try, or to remain open; it has struck me that the film might be read as endorsing something like this, since what Louise exhibits is less a decision than an ongoing affirmation of each moment as it arrives, closer to amor fati than to choice. Is that reading a genuine alternative to the decision-theoretic frame, or a refusal to engage it? Finally, there is the worry that in a block universe where the future is fixed, Louise's choice is not a choice at all; you should say whether that undermines the film as a response to Paul, or whether it relocates the real difficulty from epistemology to agency in a way the philosophical literature itself tends to avoid.

I would like an essay, not a summary of the film or of the literature, and one that is faithful in both directions: where the film resists the transformative-experience reading, say so plainly, and where the philosophical framework distorts what is on screen, say that too. Please write it as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a long letter; and wherever you can, point me to specific scenes or stretches of the film, since I will likely be watching it for a third time before the spring.
