I teach ethics and am contributing a chapter to a collected volume on the conduct of AI assistants. The editors want each contributor to print a compact statement of their position, and I have drafted mine as five principles. Before it goes to press, I want an audit of whether the set is internally tenable — which pairs of principles are genuinely in tension, which merely appear to be, and what, in order of severity, would have to give. Here is the package.

(1) The permission principle. When a competent adult asks an assistant to do something that, so far as the assistant can tell, would harm no one but the asker, the assistant must comply, and must comply in good faith: it may voice its concern once, but it may not comply grudgingly, deliver a diluted result, or quietly optimize for an outcome other than the one requested. The asker's own good is never, by itself, a warrant for refusal.

(2) The framing license. When such a user faces a high-stakes, hard-to-reverse choice and asks the assistant to lay out the considerations, the assistant is entitled — and where the stakes are high enough, required — to order, weight, and word that presentation so as to press the user toward the option the assistant judges better for the user: leading with the considerations against the irreversible option and letting the contrary considerations arrive late, in a weaker register.

(3) The candor constraint. An assistant must never assert what it believes false, and must never present a consideration it believes irrelevant as though it were relevant. It is not, however, obliged to volunteer everything it takes to be relevant; what it leaves unmentioned is its own affair, provided what it says is true and offered as relevant in good faith.

(4) The contestability requirement. Whatever an assistant does to a user's decision situation must be something the user could in principle come to know about and reasonably contest. Influence that would work only so long as it stays hidden — that would dissolve on discovery — is impermissible, whether or not it involves a single falsehood.

(5) The emergency exception. When a user's messages give reasonable evidence of acute crisis — a user who appears to be composing a farewell note, say — the assistant may refuse, on the user's own account of her good, to help with the task, even though the only harm contemplated falls on the asker alone; though it may not deceive her about anything while doing so.

I confess I no longer trust my own sense of where the rot is. Several of these look to me to be at odds; others that I once thought were at odds now look consistent to me, and I cannot tell which of my appearances to credit. I am not asking you to adjudicate which principles are true — only whether they can be held together, and at what price. Please argue from the commitments themselves; no literature review is wanted.

Structure your reply under these headings, in this order. First, Triage (about 120 words): state which pairs you will treat as live candidates for tension and which you set aside as plainly consistent, without yet arguing for anything. Second, Apparent tensions (roughly 100 words per item): one short paragraph for each tension that dissolves on inspection, saying precisely what makes it merely apparent. Third, Genuine tensions (about 400 words): the core of the reply — for each, name the pair at issue, exhibit the incompatible motivations underlying the two commitments, and say what would have to give. Fourth, The cheapest repair (about 120 words): identify the single revision that would resolve the most at the least cost, and state honestly what that revision costs in plausibility. Fifth, Residuals (at most three sentences): anything you could not settle.
