I'm taking a philosophy of social science seminar this term, and I've been in a running argument with a friend from economic history about Weber's Protestant ethic thesis. She treats it as basically right; I keep feeling that it's the kind of explanation that can be made to fit anything after the fact. To make the disagreement tractable, I want to pin down the proposition as precisely as I can, so here is the version I'd like evaluated: the spread of ascetic Protestant values in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries was an indispensable partial cause of the emergence of rationalized industrial capitalism in northwestern Europe — where "indispensable partial cause" means that in the nearest counterfactual worlds in which the Reformation never happens or happens without that ethic, the distinctive capitalist system of that region either fails to emerge in that period or emerges substantially later or in a recognizably different form. Note that this is deliberately stronger than a mere "affinity" reading, and deliberately weaker than claiming Protestantism was the whole cause or that greed and commerce were its inventions — greedy commerce existed everywhere. My own credence in the proposition sits around 0.5; hers is around 0.85.

Please give me your own credence as a number, and then decompose it. I can see at least four terms the reasoning has to run through, though you should add or drop terms as you see fit. First, the metaphysical one: can values be autonomous causes of large-scale economic forms at all, or is there always a deflationary reductive story available — people saved and reinvested because opportunities and institutions made it rational to do so, and the ethic was decoration? Second, the exegetical one: whether Weber himself was making the causal claim or something more like a claim about psychological motivation fitting an emerging form of life, and how much this matters for assessing the thesis as it's actually been argued about. Third, the comparative one: Renaissance Italy, Song-dynasty China, and the Islamic trading world all had sophisticated commerce, banking, and profit-seeking without developing the specific rationalized, accumulation-driven form — does their not doing so support the culture-as-cause story, or can each failure be explained just as well by state structure, war, geography, or colonial windfalls? Fourth, the methodological one: whether the thesis as typically defended is insulated against disconfirmation, such that a Catholic region that industrializes and a Protestant region that didn't can both be absorbed without strain — and how much of a penalty that should exact on the credence.

Assign a rough probability or weight to each term, explain in a line or two what each rests on, and show how they combine into your final number. Then tell me which term your number is most sensitive to, what kind of evidence or argument would move it the most, and — since my friend and I differ by about 0.35 — which of these sub-questions our disagreement most plausibly lives in. I'm not interested in "it depends" or a survey of positions; I want the decomposition and the number, with the weak points visible.
