I'm a science and ideas correspondent for a general-interest magazine, and a growing share of my beat is press releases about evolutionary psychology: studies claiming that some familiar behavior — women's preference for men with resources, disgust at unfamiliar foods during pregnancy, the way stepchildren are treated, mood dipping in winter — is a Stone Age adaptation, often with a brain-imaging figure attached. My editors want me to move fast on these, my readers love them, and I have maybe an hour with a paper before I decide how to frame it. What I need is a decision procedure I can actually run in that hour for sorting claims into roughly three bins: treat as credible science, cover as an interesting hypothesis with heavy caveats, or decline to cover. The procedure should be grounded in what philosophers of biology and evolutionary biologists actually know about the failure modes of adaptationist reasoning — the worry that a story about the adaptive function of a trait can be told for almost anything, the distinction between a trait's current usefulness and its evolutionary history, the problem that we can't run selection experiments on the Pleistocene, the question of whether cross-cultural data and comparative primate data can stand in for a fossil record, and the way plausible-sounding stories can survive the failure of their original evidence by generating a near-identical variant. It should also tell me what would count as good evidence in this field when it exists — convergent methods, quantitative predictions rather than retrodictions, evidence against nearby alternatives — and not just list reasons for skepticism.

I want you to be honest about which steps of the procedure are real tests and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure: for instance, whether a claim is "in principle unfalsifiable" is often itself a contested judgment, and I don't want the procedure to smuggle in a verdict under the guise of a checkbox. Mark the points where a reasonable journalist could run the same procedure and land somewhere else, and say what to do at those points. Then run the procedure on three hypothetical items from my queue: a study claiming that food aversions in the first trimester of pregnancy are an adaptation that protected embryos from plant toxins, with supportive cross-cultural survey data; a claim that a well-documented sex difference in some mate preference is explained by differential parental investment, resting on a comparison of questionnaires from thirty-seven countries; and a preregistered, cross-cultural study testing a prediction from an evolutionary account of cooperation — that people will be more sensitive to being cheated in exchanges than to logically identical violations of non-social rules — which reports a much weaker effect than the theory's proponents originally claimed. At least one of the three should come out in a bin I'll find inconvenient, and you should say which and why. Finally, throughout the answer, keep separate what is settled among people who actually work on this — the existence of these methodological worries, the broad consensus points about heritability and selection — from what is genuinely contested, such as how damaging the objections are to evolutionary psychology as a research program and whether its best-known results have survived critical scrutiny; please don't present one side of that fight as the consensus view.
