I run communications strategy for a national mental-health charity, and nearly every campaign we produce leans on one analogy: depression is like diabetes, mental illness is an illness like any other. The line is doing at least three distinct jobs for us. It is meant to shift the perceived cause from character to chemistry, so that a person who can't get out of bed is seen the way we see someone with a failing pancreas rather than as weak or lazy. It is meant to underwrite our advocacy for treatment parity — if it's an illness, it deserves the same insurance coverage and research funding as oncology. And it quietly licenses the medical framing of involuntary hospitalization, since we don't normally think of confining someone for their own good unless medicine is involved. Until now nobody on my team has had to defend the analogy; we just deploy it.

At our last strategy review, one of our trustees, who teaches medical ethics, complicated this. She pointed out that the analogy has a famous adversary: Thomas Szasz, who spent a career arguing that calling psychological suffering "illness" is a category mistake. As she reconstructed it, his argument was roughly that genuine disease is a condition of the body identifiable by physical signs discoverable independently of what the patient says or does, whereas what psychiatry classifies are patterns of conduct, speech, and reported distress, identified by interpreting a person against social and moral norms — so the term "disease" is being used metaphorically, and everything downstream of the metaphor, including civil commitment dressed up as treatment, inherits its problems. When I pushed back with the obvious counters — neurosyphilis, the measurable brain changes in some conditions, the fact that plenty of physical medicine also diagnoses by reported symptoms like pain — she said those might blunt Szasz's claim about the boundary but don't by themselves vindicate the diabetes line, which is a different, stronger claim.

I'd like an audit of the analogy that my team and I can actually use at the board meeting in three weeks. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the pathologist's lesion, of insulin, of the glucose monitor, of the relapse — and then the places the mapping breaks, flagged by which of our three uses each break threatens: the no-blame use, the funding-parity use, or the involuntary-treatment use. I suspect they don't stand or fall together, and I want to know which of them the analogy can still carry once the disanalogies are on the table, and which it was secretly carrying the whole time. I also want the audit to be honest in both directions: does Szasz's own critique smuggle things in, like an unrealistically narrow picture of physical medicine or a silent assumption that everything norm-dependent falls outside science? And tell me whether there's a better analogy we should be using instead, or whether the right answer is that no single analogy serves all three of our purposes and we need to say different things in the anti-stigma spots than in our policy submissions. Bear in mind the practical constraint that our public-facing material is thirty-second radio reads and single-sentence tube posters, so if the conclusion is "use a more careful analogy," it has to survive being that compressed.
