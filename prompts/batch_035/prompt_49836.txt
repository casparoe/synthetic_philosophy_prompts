You are a professional moral philosopher who works on the boundary between normative ethics and action theory, and who has spent years teaching the deontology–consequentialism debate to undergraduates. You also run a small philosophy blog aimed at readers who have completed an introductory ethics course: they know the trolley problem and can name Kant and Mill, but they have never encountered the technical literature on constraints, agent-relativity, or moral remainder.

I would like you to write a diagnostic quiz for the blog entitled, roughly, "Which view do you actually hold on the paradox of deontology?" The paradox, for the readers, is this: if deontology recognises agent-relative constraints against doing certain things — killing, torturing, breaking promises — then it seems the agent may not violate a constraint even once when doing so is the only way to prevent five, or five thousand, further violations of that very same constraint by other agents. The deontologist must apparently either say that morality demands standing by whilst vastly more wrongdoing occurs, or concede that the constraint is not really a constraint.

The point of the quiz is to sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they like, so please observe the following constraints on construction:

1. Ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options. Options must describe what the agent should do, or how the agent should feel afterwards — never the name of a view. No question may ask the reader whether they are a deontologist, whether they believe in constraints, or anything else that simply requests a self-label.
2. Every case must be one in which the alternative to the agent's committing one violation is further violations of the same kind by other moral agents — not merely more harm befalling people through disease, accident, or nature. The paradox only bites on doing-versus-doing, not doing-versus-allowing.
3. Invent fresh cases. At most one recognisable classic (sheriff and mob, transplant surgeon) may appear, and only if reworked to serve the diagnostic purpose. The rest should be new: a supply officer, a hostage negotiator, a water-utility engineer, an occupying army's judge — whatever you can make vivid.
4. The set of questions must collectively test at least these five dimensions: (i) whether the number of prevented violations can ever outweigh one committed violation, and if so whether the constraint yields abruptly at some threshold of horrors or erodes gradually as the numbers grow; (ii) whether the constraint's force depends on whose violation it would be — a case where a violation will certainly occur whoever the agent chooses, and the only live question is whether the agent does it herself or leaves it to someone else; (iii) whether an agent who violates for the best reasons nevertheless incurs guilt or owes something to the victim — moral remainder; (iv) whether constraints against different wrongs trade against one another the way they trade against prevented harm (may one break a promise, or lie, to prevent a killing by others?); and (v) whether the prospective victim's consent converts a would-be violation into something else altogether, and if so what.
5. Discard any draft case on which every position you mean to distinguish would answer identically. A question that separates nobody is decoration; cut it.

The scoring key should be built from the logical space, not from a list of famous names. First set out the dimensions and the combinations of answers they permit; then locate the positions in that space: absolutist deontology (constraints hold at any number, however great); threshold deontology (constraints hold up to some very large number of horrors and then yield); the tragic or "dirty hands" view (violating is the best or even obligatory thing to do, yet remains a wrong to the victim and leaves residue on the agent); a two-level or rule-consequentialist dissolution (the constraint is a highly reliable rule justified by its outcomes, with principled exceptions at the edges); and plain act-consequentialism (the paradox is a straightforward reductio of constraints). For any combination of answers that none of these positions occupies, say so and diagnose it briefly: is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because it is unexplored?

Finally, flag explicitly any pair of positions the quiz cannot reliably separate — for instance, threshold deontology and the two-level dissolution will agree on every moderate case and part only at the far edge, and absolutism and the tragic view may answer every "what should she do?" question the same way, differing only in what they say afterwards. For each such pair, either propose how a further question could part them, or concede honestly that no single case can, and say what that means for someone trying to locate themselves.

Before writing the quiz, please brainstorm: sketch the positions, generate candidate cases, and check each case against each pair of positions to confirm it actually discriminates. Then present (a) the quiz, (b) the scoring key with the logical space and the diagnosis of empty cells, and (c) a short closing note on the instrument's blind spots — the positions and distinctions it structurally cannot capture.
