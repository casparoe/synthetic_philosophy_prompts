I got to talking to a friend's husband at a barbecue a while back — he does something in philosophy for a living — and something he said has been bugging me since. He told me that the philosophers who actually think about this stuff have basically given up on the idea that numbers exist. His way of putting it was that saying "numbers exist" is like saying "the average taxpayer exists" — a useful figure of speech, but not a thing you could ever bump into. He also said the old worry goes something like: even if numbers did exist, they'd have to sit somehow outside of space and time, and there'd be no way for creatures like us, whose whole thinking happens inside a skull, to ever make contact with them. Supposedly that's what made everyone give up on it.

So if the smart people have settled that numbers aren't real, here's my question, which maybe sounds dumb: why do we still teached kids arithmetic like it's facts? Is 7 × 8 = 56 a fact about something, or is it closer to a habit we all agreed on? And if it's a habit, could we have wound up with a different habit — one where 7 × 8 = 57 and all the downstream stuff still hung together consistently? When I asked my kid's math teacher something like this after the parent-teacher conference she looked at me like I was crazy, so I figure I'm missing something basic. When you answer, could you please keep separate what's genuinely settled among people who study this from what's still a live fight between them? The barbecue guy talked like the no-numbers side had obviously won years ago, and I want to know if that's really the consensus or just how it sounds from inside his team.
