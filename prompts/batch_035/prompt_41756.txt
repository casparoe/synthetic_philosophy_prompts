I run a monthly philosophy evening at my local library — a dozen or so adults, mostly retirees plus a couple of university students, with no formal philosophy between them — and next month I've been asked to lead a session on whether happiness and suffering can be weighed against each other on a single scale, ending with the view that at least some suffering cannot be compensated by any amount of happiness, however large: the position people sometimes call negative utilitarianism with a lexical threshold. I have sixty minutes and a flipchart, and the group will arrive with nothing but the folk version of utilitarianism, the greatest happiness for the greatest number. I don't want you to explain the views to me; I want a plan for the session. Specifically: the order the ideas have to come in and why — my suspicion is that something about aggregation, the assumption of a common currency, has to be in place before the question of an exchange rate between pleasure and pain even makes sense, but I'm not sure what exactly has to be established first; then the one case we should carry through the whole session, and what it must have and lack — I'm fairly sure it should involve a single person's own pleasure and pain so we can postpone all questions about comparing across lives, and that it must be a genuine trade where the pain is not merely a means to later pleasure, which rules out dentistry and exercise, while smuggling in nothing about rights or desert. Then the misconceptions I should expect and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I'd guess at least these: that the threshold is a psychological fact about how intense pains feel rather than a normative claim; that the view amounts to simply caring more about suffering, or weighting it more heavily, rather than denying commensurability outright; and that "some suffering can't be compensated" slides into "no suffering is ever worth any pleasure." One member is a retired nurse who reaches for the 0-to-10 pain scale from her ward whenever suffering comes up, and I suspect that will both help and mislead in ways I can't yet see — I'd like your view on where to use it and where to shut it down. Please also tell me where to draw the line and openly refuse to go further — I assume the formal axiology and anything drifting into population ethics is over the line at this level, but flag anything smaller that will trip me up. Finally, give me one question I can ask in the last five minutes whose answer would show me they have grasped the commensurability issue rather than merely decided they like the threshold conclusion.

Please answer in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a letter. And since the session is in Britain and the group includes a couple of pedants, keep any terminology you suggest pronounceable and avoid American spellings.
