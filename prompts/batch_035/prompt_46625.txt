Please compare John Norton's empiricist account of scientific thought experiments with James Robert Brown's Platonist account, and adjudicate between them. By way of background: Norton claims that a thought experiment is, once reconstructed, simply an argument — typically one with premises borrowed from experience and unexpressed premises smuggled in by the narrative — and that it can yield no knowledge beyond what its premises already contain; Brown replies that at least some thought experiments (his paradigm being Galileo's argument that bodies of unequal weight cannot fall at different speeds) function as a kind of perception of abstract laws of nature, delivering new knowledge without new empirical input.

A textbook rehearsal of these two positions will not do. I would like you to anchor the comparison in a test case of a kind neither account was explicitly designed for: the framing-sensitivity of thought-experimental judgement. Consider the following hypothetical case proposed by a physicist we may call Dr Ilves. She asks her students to imagine a perfectly elastic collision between two identical, frictionless discs. When the collision is described from a laboratory frame — one disc hurtling toward a second disc at rest — the students report a confident intuition that the moving disc is the 'active' party, the one doing the hitting, and they predict outcomes accordingly. When the very same collision is described from the centre-of-mass frame — two discs approaching each other symmetrically — the students report a different intuition, of a mutual, reciprocal encounter. The underlying situation is numerically identical in both descriptions; only the frame, and hence the rhetoric, has changed; and Dr Ilves notes that the two framings sometimes license different guesses about what would happen if the discs were made slightly non-identical.

Use this case, together with the more familiar phenomenon of thought experiments that mislead (cases where the imagined scenario seduces us into confident but false judgements that only a later, more careful argument exposes), to address the following questions:

1. Does Norton's reconstruction thesis straightforwardly predict framing effects — since different descriptions license different implicit premises — or does the very fact that the same physical situation supports incompatible reconstructions threaten his claim that there is a principled reconstruction to be found?
2. Is the framing sensitivity of our judgements an embarrassment for Brown, inasmuch as a genuine perception of a Platonic law should presumably not vary with an arbitrary choice of inertial frame, or can a Platonist about thought experiments accommodate it, say by appeal to the role of the imagination in making abstract structure salient?
3. On which of these two battlegrounds — misleading thought experiments, or framing-sensitive ones — does each account do better, and does the overall balance of advantages favour Norton's deflationary picture, Brown's mathematical-realist picture, or some hybrid that keeps, for instance, the Nortonian reconstruction discipline while granting the Brownian point that some premises are accepted on the strength of something that feels like insight rather than inference?

You may draw on well-known canonical examples (Galileo's falling bodies, Stevin's chain over the inclined plane, Maxwell's demon, Einstein's pursuit of a light beam) for illustration, but the argument should turn on the framing-sensitivity and misleadingness phenomena described above, not on the canon. Please avoid fence-sitting: if you conclude that neither account is fully satisfactory, say precisely what a satisfactory account would need to add, and which of the two it would resemble more closely.

Please write your answer in British English, at a level suitable for an advanced undergraduate or beginning graduate student in philosophy of science, in roughly 800–1,200 words.
