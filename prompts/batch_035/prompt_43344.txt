My two friends Dana and Marcus spent a four-hour car ride last weekend arguing about Gödel and time, and I was wedged in the back seat listening to the whole thing, so now you get to adjudicate, because honestly I couldn't follow who was winning. The setting: in 1949 there was a big tribute volume published for Einstein's seventieth birthday, and Gödel contributed a short essay with some philosophical remarks, in which he presented a solution to the equations of general relativity describing a rotating, non-expanding universe. In that universe, if you travel along certain trajectories you can arrive back before you departed, and -- this is the key part for them -- there is no way to slice the whole universe into a single agreed-upon "now": no global cosmic time. Dana's opening claim was that Gödel thereby refuted the passage of time: in the Gödel universe there's no objective fact about which events are present, so the flow of time can't be a real feature of the world, and Gödel thought it followed that time is ideal everywhere (and Dana noted Einstein's reply in the same volume took it seriously, saying the problem had bothered him since building up general relativity and asking whether such worlds should even be counted as physically possible).

Marcus said this is oversold, and made a few different points, so I'll number them like the argument went: (1) Our universe is not rotating; it's expanding, and in the standard cosmology there is a global cosmic time everyone could in principle agree on, so the rotating solution is just a curiosity -- by Dana's logic, the physical possibility of a universe containing no electrons would show that electrons aren't real. (2) The actual argument has two steps: first, that general relativity by itself doesn't force objective becoming, because it's compatible with a universe where nothing lapses; second, that since time's passage is supposed to be the sort of thing that couldn't depend on the happenstance distribution of matter, if it can fail to exist in any physically possible world, it must not pass in ours either. Marcus thinks step two is a plain non sequitur -- a modal bridge principle smuggled in without argument. Dana answered that it's not a non sequitur but an inference to the best explanation: finding that the laws of physics are indifferent to whether time flows -- perfectly happy describing worlds where it doesn't -- is like finding the laws indifferent to vital spirits, and nobody concludes from that that vitalism was right. (3) Marcus also said Dana kept sliding between "time doesn't exist," which is crazy since clocks plainly work, and "there's no objective becoming," which is the real claim; and that anyway presentism in the modern literature is a thesis about what exists simpliciter, which Gödel's argument, aimed at the lapse of time, never even touches. Dana said that was hair-splitting, since without objective becoming presentism loses its whole motivation -- a block universe was the destination all along. They also bickered about whether it matters that Gödel apparently kept working for years trying to extend the result to our actual expanding universe (something I gathered from later notebooks people have dug through, not from the published essay), with Marcus saying that shows even Gödel thought the published argument didn't settle our world, and Dana saying it shows the opposite, that he never gave it up.

So: who's right at each move? Specifically, does the fact that our universe has a global cosmic time rescue anything for Dana's opponents, or was Marcus right that it's beside the point; is step two of the argument a non sequitur or a fair inference to the best explanation (and is the vital-spirit analogy any good); is the presentism/becoming distinction doing real work or is it, as Dana claims, academic; and what should I make of the unfinished extension to the expanding universe? Please give me a verdict move by move, note what both of them were missing entirely, and tell me who comes out more right overall. I'd genuinely like to know, because I have to sit through another car ride with these two next month.
