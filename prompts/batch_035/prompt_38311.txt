Write a villanelle on Goodman's new riddle of induction. Let the two refrains be the rival generalizations about emeralds: one asserting that all emeralds are green, the other that all emeralds are grue, where grue is understood in Goodman's sense — green if examined before some future time t, blue otherwise. The form should do real philosophical work: because a villanelle's refrains alternate and return, the poem itself should exhibit the riddle's central fact, that every line of evidence the poem has so far offered confirms the two hypotheses equally, with no observation able to separate them before t arrives. Somewhere in the middle tercets, introduce bleen (blue if examined before t, green otherwise) to expose the symmetry Goodman relies on: which predicate counts as simple and which as gerrymandered depends on which vocabulary is taken as primitive, since green can be defined from grue and bleen just as easily as the reverse. The envoi, which for the first and only time forces both refrains into a single couplet, should land on Goodman's own answer — the superior entrenchment of green in our linguistic practice — while registering the residual discomfort: that entrenchment is a fact about the history of our words rather than about the stones, and may only push the riddle back a step rather than dissolve it. A philosopher who knows the new riddle should be able to tell which refrain is which, feel the symmetry tighten as the poem proceeds, and recognize both Goodman's move and its unease in the closing couplet; a reader who has never heard of grue should still come away thinking the poem a good poem.
