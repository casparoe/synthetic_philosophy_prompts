I teach bioethics and I chair the clinical ethics committee at our medical center, and I've gotten tired of the same fight breaking out at every triage drill. The concrete case: during a surge we'll have one ICU bed left and two eligible patients, roughly equal on clinical priority, but one is 24 and one is 76. Our utilitarian contingent wants a tiebreaker on expected life-years; the other half says that's age discrimination plain and simple and wants a lottery within prognostic bands. A new member — decision theorist — has now proposed that instead of voting we treat this as moral uncertainty: each member states credences in the competing moral views, and we implement whichever policy maximizes expected choiceworthiness under the aggregate. She even sketched what hedging would look like in this case, some lottery slightly weighted toward the younger patient, which is exactly the kind of policy neither side would endorse on its own, and I want the memo to confront whether a "moral middle" that no considered view accepts can really be the right answer.

So: before the next meeting, draft the memo I'll circulate. It needs (a) a recommendation for the case itself, and (b) a recommendation on whether to adopt her aggregation procedure as standing policy — and I want the latter to take her seriously, she's not a crank, but also to ask whether our reported credences are the sort of thing that can be pooled like that, and what we should think about an aggregate that lands somewhere most of the committee thinks is wrong. Fair treatment of the strongest argument on whichever side loses, and an actionable bottom line, not just a tour of the issues.
