You are a scholar of comparative politics and political theory who spent the first decade of your career as a distributed systems engineer before retraining, and who is therefore fluent in both vocabularies and allergic to glib analogies between them. I am a distributed systems engineer: I design microservice architectures for a living and my working vocabulary is that world's — service decomposition, single ownership of data, latency, data locality, the CAP theorem, eventual consistency, circuit breakers, the slow organizational drift by which every "shared service" quietly becomes a monolith again, and the recurring question of which node should own which decision and what happens when local and global state disagree. I have recently become interested in federalism and subsidiarity — the US system of enumerated powers and the Tenth Amendment, German cooperative federalism in which the Länder administer much federal law through the Bundesrat's consent procedures, the Swiss combination of cantonal autonomy with the double majority required for constitutional referendums, and the EU's subsidiarity principle with its early-warning mechanism for national parliaments — but every explainer I read seems to use words I know ("levels," "competences," "interoperability") while carrying a meaning my vocabulary can't quite hold. I know roughly how these systems are structured; I have not read the political theory underneath them.

Please do two things for me, and take the second at least as seriously as the first.

First, translate the core positions in the federalism and subsidiarity debate into my vocabulary, as faithfully as a translation can be. This is a translation exercise, not an analogy hunt: I am not asking for "federalism is like microservices because." Among the mappings I'd like you to consider — and to accept, repair, or reject on the merits — are the following, which I've drafted myself: subsidiarity's test of whether an objective can be sufficiently achieved at the lower level, read as a latency-and-capacity argument about where computation should sit; German concurrent competences and the executive federalism in which Länder administrations run what the federal level legislates, read as an ownership split between the team that writes the interface specification and the teams that run the service; Swiss cantonal majorities and the double majority, read as weighted quorum requirements designed to keep the consensus of the fleet from being dictated by the highest-throughput nodes; the drift of American commerce-clause jurisprudence from Wickard onward, read as the familiar story of how a shared platform's remit expands until nearly every request technically crosses it; and the EU early-warning mechanism — which has been triggered only a handful of times in its existence — read as a health check whose firing threshold is so high that it mostly reports liveness. You should add mappings of your own where the source material demands it, including any that make federal systems look less like distributed systems than my list assumes.

Second, give me the residue report: what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. I especially want your judgment on three candidate residues, though you should identify others. (1) Legitimacy: political theorists insist it can matter who decides even where both levels would decide the same thing, and I cannot find any property in my vocabulary that this maps onto — the closest I can get is audit logging, which is obviously wrong. (2) Identity and membership: the federal units in each of these systems are units because of histories, languages, and loyalties, and my vocabulary has only nodes and their capacities. (3) The criteria problem: subsidiarity presupposes that "which level is competent" is answerable in the abstract, whereas in my world the analogous question is usually settled by whoever has budget authority and carries the pager, not by argument. For each residue, tell me whether it points to something my vocabulary is missing — the way a language without tense cannot express sequence — or to something the theorists' claim never managed to say clearly in the first place, or to a confusion on their side that my vocabulary usefully exposes.

A few instructions on method. Before you write anything, brainstorm several candidate mappings in private and discard the weak ones rather than presenting me a survey of everything you considered; I want your best translation, not your search history. Guard against smuggling my vocabulary's assumptions into the translation and presenting them as the original's content — for instance, do not render subsidiarity as simply "push decisions to the edge because latency is bad," since the treaty principle carries a substantive presumption about the value of Member State action that has nothing to do with response times, and if you flatten that you are translating badly rather than translating faithfully. Where a mapping works only if you distort the political concept to fit the technical one, say so explicitly and say which way the distortion runs. Treat the residue as the most interesting part of the deliverable, not as a caveat at the end. And close with a verdict: if you had to teach subsidiarity to a room of engineers, would you use my vocabulary as scaffolding and then take it away, or does the vocabulary get something structurally right that the standard political-theory presentations get muddy?
