My roommate's a Humean about laws of nature and I think I've built an argument that wrecks his view, except it may wreck mine too, so I need someone to tell me where it goes wrong. Quick setup so we're on the same page: on his view, all that really exists is the vast mosaic of particular facts spread across space and time — where every particle is, what every field value is at every point — and the laws of nature are just whatever set of regularities would best systematize that mosaic: the axioms with the best balance of simplicity and informativeness. Nothing happens *because* of the laws on this picture; the laws are what they are because of how the mosaic happens to be. He'd put it as: first the facts, then the summary.

Here's the argument. (1) On this view the laws are fixed entirely by how the particular facts are distributed — same mosaic, same laws. (2) The mosaic includes every single event: this electron's swerving here, that planet's orbiting there. (3) Nothing can explain a fact that it is entirely fixed by — an explanation whose truth depends on the very thing being explained is going in a circle. (4) So on his view the laws cannot explain why any particular event happened, not even why this electron accelerated just now. (5) But explaining particular events by subsuming them under laws is basically all of physics; if laws explain nothing, science is doing something impossible every day. (6) So the laws must be something over and above the mosaic.

My problem: my own view is closer to laws being necessities built into things, not summaries, so I'd love for this argument to work. But I've noticed (3) might prove too much. Whether a war is going on seems to explain why individual soldiers are shooting at each other, and yet whether a war is going on looks entirely fixed by what the individual soldiers are doing — the war is nothing over and above their movements, exactly like the laws are nothing over and above the mosaic. If (3) says nothing fixed by a bunch of facts can explain any of them, it seems to say the war can't explain the shooting, which I can't accept. My roommate's only defense so far is that a best system explains events "in a different sense" than real explanation, which to me sounds like a dodge — if the sense is different enough, he's conceded (5) anyway.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd rather keep (5) than the argument, and I'd honestly rather keep (3) too if I can. Please write your answer in American English.
