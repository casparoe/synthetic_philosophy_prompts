For the past few months I have been collecting my own reactions to cases in which a person reports on the contents of their own mind, trying to determine when I actually trust introspection and when I do not, and I would like to know whether a single principle captures the pattern. My verdicts are as follows. If someone says, mid-sip, that they taste cinnamon, I trust the report about the taste outright, even though they might be wrong about the cinnamon itself. If, after an argument, my partner tells me I was seething and I sincerely report that I felt nothing but calm, rational disappointment, I do not trust my report: I suspect the anger was there and that introspection either missed it or was enlisted to hide it. Experiments in which people fluently explain choices they never actually made have made me distrust nearly every answer to the question of why one chose something, including my own account of why I took my current job. Yet when a therapist reports noticing herself grow irritable with a patient and concludes that something in her own history was being stirred up, I trust her considerably more than I trust a stranger's explanation of a consumer choice. A report of how I feel at this moment I trust more than the same report made a day later about how I felt then. An amputee's report of pain in a missing hand I trust as a report of experience, while noting that it is wrong about the body. A meditator's claim that aversion flickers half a second before the thought it seems to respond to I trust more than the same claim from someone who has never trained their attention, though I cannot say why training should help if it is the same faculty doing the work.

My tentative principle was that introspection is reliable about how things seem now and unreliable about causes, reasons, and the past. But this fails on the sincere "I felt nothing but calm" case, which is a present-tense report about seeming that I nonetheless distrust, and it wobbles on the therapist, whose report is a reason-giving report that I trust. So my questions: Is there a principle that saves all of these verdicts, or nearly all? If so, please state it precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdict on a case I have not listed, and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If no principle saves enough of them, please say which verdicts I ought to give up and why those rather than the others. Finally, I would like the principle's ruling on the case I keep going back and forth on: a person who reports, mid-conversation, that they notice they are enjoying an argument more than they want to admit, and who proposes to base a career decision on the introspective claim that, deep down, they already know they want to leave their profession. Should that report be trusted the way the cinnamon report is, distrusted the way the post-argument report is, or something in between?
