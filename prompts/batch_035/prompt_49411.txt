I'm in a graduate reading group on laws and chance, and our convener has taken to abbreviating the Principal Principle as the instruction to let your credences defer to the objective chances — if you know the chance is one-half, believe to one-half. This semester that gloss has been made to do two very different jobs, and I've started to doubt it can do either. First, in a session on deterministic worlds, someone argued that since a complete physical state plus deterministic laws settles every outcome, the true chances are all zero or one, so the Principle shows there is no room for genuine chance in a deterministic universe, and anyone assigning a fair coin one-half is being straightforwardly irrational. Second, in a discussion of foreknowledge, the same gloss was used to claim that if a perfectly reliable time-traveler tells you the coin will land heads, you still know the chance is one-half, so your credence should still be one-half — chance trumps even testimony like that. Both felt wrong to me, but for what might be opposite reasons. Lay out the distinct precise theses this one-liner could be expressing — from readings so weak as to be near-tautologies (perhaps defining chance as whatever rational agents defer to), through readings about credence conditional on chance propositions given only admissible evidence, to strong readings on which known chance screens off all further evidence — and say for each whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. Then say which readings, if any, would support each of the two uses: does any defensible reading really yield the no-chance-under-determinism conclusion, and does any really support discounting the time-traveler's testimony? If the two uses pull the gloss in incompatible directions, flag exactly where. Present the readings as an ordered list I can bring to the next session, with a verdict attached to each.
