Two scholars have been arguing for years about whether historical atrocities can legitimately be ranked. One, a comparativist trained in quantitative demography, holds that structured comparison of atrocities — by death tolls, total years of life lost, the proportion of a target population destroyed, the duration and intentionality of the harm — is a scholarly obligation, and that refusing to compare on grounds of reverence simply abandons the field to polemicists. The other, a particularist steeped in memory studies, holds that ranking atrocities is structurally obscene: it treats victims as fungible units, invites contests over whose suffering "counts more," and reliably functions rhetorically to minimize whatever is being ranked below something else. They have agreed to an adversarial collaboration. Draft the joint statement they should produce: (1) a numbered section of claims both can sign — including, if possible, points like the distinction between comparing atrocities (pairwise, purpose-relative) and ranking them (constructing a total order), and the fact that some comparative claims (e.g., about mechanisms, preconditions, or aftermath) are uncontroversial in historiography; (2) a table stating their exact residual disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side would accept — I suspect one crux is whether quantification is merely descriptive or inherently performative, another is whether there exists a common metric of harm across radically different evils (e.g., mass killing versus centuries of enslavement versus forced assimilation of survivors), and another is ordinality: whether even a comparativist can honestly defend more than rough, purpose-relative orderings; and (3) for each crux, a concrete case, thought experiment, or historical test both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If you conclude that part of their dispute is merely verbal — for instance, that they mean different things by "ranking" — say which part and why.
