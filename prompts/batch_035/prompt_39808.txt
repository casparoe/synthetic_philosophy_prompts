Next month I'm giving a talk at a workshop on disease classification (mostly a philosophy of medicine crowd) where I'll be defending a causal-role account of biological function as the right one for medicine. Act as a seasoned philosopher of biology who's sat through a few hundred of these Q&A sessions and knows exactly how they tend to go.

The thesis, roughly: the selected-effect / etiological story — a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected, the Neander and Millikan line — is fine and probably true for evolutionary biology. But the function claims that actually do the work in the clinic are causal-role claims (Cummins-style: a part's function is its contribution to some capacity of the containing system). When a nephrologist says the function of the kidney is to filter blood, they're saying what the kidney contributes to the organism-level capacity of keeping blood composition in range — no appeal to selection history, and none needed. On my view a disease attribution only requires that the part is failing to contribute the causal-role function it's supposed to contribute to that system-level capacity. And I want to claim the etiologists' big selling point — that selection history gives you a naturalized normativity, a fact of the matter about malfunction — is a bonus medicine doesn't actually need: the badness of disease comes from the patient's suffering and their life plans, not from what selection shaped the organ for.

The audience will be maybe 25 people. Two senior philosophers of biology who are committed etiologists, one of them trained directly in that lineage and fond of the backward-looking point that a trait can only fail at what it was selected for doing, so only selected-effect functions ground genuine malfunction ascriptions. One philosopher of medicine sympathetic to Boorse's biostatistical theory, who'll want to say normal function is just statistical, species-typical contribution — neither etiological nor causal-role. A psychiatric epidemiologist who thinks in Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction terms and will press on whether my account still delivers the dysfunction half of that analysis or whether I've quietly collapsed it into the harmful half. Plus a bunch of clinicians who use function the way physiologists do and will be genuinely puzzled that anyone thinks there's a problem here at all. Please think carefully about who will raise what and in what order — I expect the etiologists to open and the clinicians to drift in late with so-what questions — and for each objection, phrase it the way that person would actually put it, rank it by how much damage it does, draft me a two-minute reply, and flag where the reply is soft. I know the question I'm hoping nobody asks: if the badness comes from the patient, why dress it up as dysfunction at all — isn't that just conceding that disease is a value judgment with physiological window dressing. But tell me if there's a worse one I havent thought of. No bibliography or citations please, this is just for my own prep — I only want the prep sheet.
