I'm a researcher in multi-objective reinforcement learning, and I want a translation exercise rather than a tutorial. The vocabulary I use daily: reward vectors, linear (weighted-sum) scalarization, Pareto fronts and coverage sets, the fact that in stochastic environments linear scalarization can only reach the convex hull of the front, non-linear aggregators like Chebyshev scalarization, preference elicitation over weight vectors. I have no formal training in philosophy. A colleague told me that Harsanyi's aggregation theorem — the result from the 1950s showing that if every individual's preferences and the social preference relation both satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and the social relation respects Pareto, then social utility must be a positive weighted sum of individual utilities — is essentially the foundation of everything I do when I fold a reward vector into a scalar with fixed weights. I want you to test that claim.

Restate Harsanyi's theorem and the surrounding philosophical debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then report what does not survive the translation. Concretely, I want:

1. The theorem itself, stated so that someone who thinks in terms of expected reward vectors and weight vectors can verify every step — which assumptions correspond to which axioms, and where the weighted sum falls out.

2. Harsanyi's companion argument from roughly the same period, the impartial-observer setup where you evaluate social arrangements from behind a veil of ignorance by giving yourself an equal chance of being each person. Is there anything in my world like this — e.g., treating the individual's index as a random variable and optimizing expected utility under a uniform prior over indices?

3. The main objections philosophers raised afterward. Two in particular: first, the fairness objection associated with Diamond, that vNM plus Pareto forces the social relation to be indifferent between a sure allocation and a lottery with the same profile of expected utilities even when the lottery concentrates all the risk on one person — does this correspond to anything I should know about when choosing between linear scalarization and non-linear aggregators over the joint distribution of returns? Second, the affine-invariance problem: each individual's vNM utility is defined only up to positive affine transformation, so the weights in the weighted sum appear to be underdetermined unless utilities can be compared across persons.

4. Rawls's maximin alternative to Harsanyi's utilitarian conclusion — if leximin or lexicographic orderings over the reward vector are the right analogue, say whether any fixed weight vector can represent them.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: which parts of the philosophical debate translate cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. My own suspicion is that the affine-invariance problem is just the familiar fact that per-objective reward scales are arbitrary, so the weight vector is meaningless without a normalization convention — but I want to know whether the philosophers have anything principled to say about fixing the scales that we practitioners lack, or whether they are in the same position of choosing by convention. I also suspect that the genuinely untranslatable part is the moral status of the weights: in my world the weight vector is a design parameter set by an engineer or elicited from a user, whereas in Harsanyi's world it is supposed to be a fact about how to aggregate persons, and I cannot tell whether that difference is deep or cosmetic.

End with your verdict on whether the residue points to something my vocabulary cannot say or to parts of the philosophical debate being less substantive than they look. Open with that verdict in your first sentence — no preamble, no restatement of my question — and let the translation and the residue report carry the argument for it.
