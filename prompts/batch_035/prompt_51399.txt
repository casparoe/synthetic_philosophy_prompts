I'm in a moral psychology course and we just covered the guilt vs. shame distinction (guilt being about "I did a bad thing," shame about "I am bad" / being seen as bad), and it got me thinking about something weird in the criminal justice system here in the US. On one hand, at sentancing and parole hearings, judges and parole boards treat remorse as a big deal — defendants who apologize and accept responsibility often get lighter sentences, and at parole hearings you're basically expected to sound sorry. But at the same time, some judges hand down explicitly shaming punishments — making a shoplifter stand in front of the store wearing a sign saying what she did, or a drunk driver put a bumper sticker on his car, and we keep public offender registries that anyone can look up online. So my question is what view of moral emotion this combination presupposes. Does the remorse discount only make sense on the "guilt" picture, where what matters is the defendant's internal conscience and private self-condemnation, while the shaming punishments only make sense on the "shame" picture, where what matters is the audience's judgment of you? Can one system really presuppose both at the same time, or is one of these practices actually riding on something else entirely, like deterrence or judges just liking defendants who are respectful in court? Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no intro or conclusion: (1) what the remorse-mitigation practice presupposes about moral emotion, (2) what the shaming punishments presuppose, (3) whether the two are consistent or in tension, (4) how each practice would have to change if the opposite theory of moral emotion turned out to be the right one, and (5) which practice is genuinely presupposing a theory of moral emotion and which is better explained by non-moral stuff.
