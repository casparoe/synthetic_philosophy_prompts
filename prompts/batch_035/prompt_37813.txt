My algebra prof explained Zorn's lemma by saying it's basically induction turned sideways: you want a maximal element, so you take what you've got, and if it's not maximal there's something bigger — pick one, repeat — and since you can't go on forever you must eventually hit a maximal element. He used this to reassure us that Zorn is as intuitive as ordinary induction, so we shouldn't feel weird leaning on it to prove maximal ideals exist. My set theory friend says the reassurance is bogus and the story has real holes: (1) the "pick one" step is exactly what needs the axiom of choice, and Zorn is equivalent to AC, so the story is kind of circular — it quietly spends choice to sell us a lemma that's just choice in a lab coat; (2) "you can't go on forever" is not obvious at all — an infinite process can just keep going, and what stops it is the upper-bound hypothesis, which the prof never even mentioned; and (3) ordinary induction goes from stage n to stage n+1, but even after you add choices there are limit stages where there is no "next" element and you have to collect everything so far and jump to a bound of the whole chain, which is nothing like anything in regular induction. Can you audit the analogy? Lay out what maps to what (induction step, base case, reaching the conclusion), where the mapping breaks, which breaks actually matter for the reassurance the prof wanted versus which are just pedantry, and say whether "it's basically induction so trust it" survives once the disanalogies are on the table — or whether a fixed-up version of the analogy could still do the reassuring work.
