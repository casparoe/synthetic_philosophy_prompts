You are a professional philosopher specializing in population axiology, intimately familiar with Derek Parfit's Reasons and Persons (1984) and the literature it spawned. I am designing a seminar on population ethics for advanced undergraduates, and what I need from you is considered judgment, not a neutral survey. Please think carefully and reason step by step; do not commit to a ranking before you have done the preparatory work I set out below.

By way of background: Parfit's repugnant conclusion is that, compared with a population of fewer people whose lives are very good, there can be a much larger population whose members' lives are barely worth living, and that the larger population is nonetheless better, other things being equal. Most of the subsequent literature consists of attempted escapes. The main escape routes are roughly these:

1. Averaging: what matters is average welfare, so a huge, barely-worth-living population ranks below a smaller, flourishing one.
2. Critical-level views: adding a life raises a population's value only if that life's welfare exceeds some positive threshold.
3. Lexical or threshold views, in the spirit of Parfit's own later suggestion of lexical superiority: sufficiently good lives rank lexically above merely adequate ones, so quality can outweigh quantity at the limit.
4. Variable-value views: the value of adding lives diminishes as the population grows.
5. Person-affecting restrictions: only the claims of people who exist, or will exist regardless of what we choose, can count, which is meant to block the trade between actual and merely possible people.
6. Acceptance: simply bite the bullet and deny that the conclusion is repugnant, a line pressed by Michael Huemer in his 2008 paper "In Defence of Repugnance" in Mind.

Your task has three steps.

Step 1 — Brainstorm briefly. Before ranking anything, write a short paragraph on what criteria should govern the ranking. I would like you to weigh at least the following: how each family handles neighboring diagnostic judgments, especially the sadistic conclusion, where a theory crafted to avoid the repugnant conclusion ends up preferring the addition of lives of negative welfare to the addition of many positive ones, and the non-identity problem; theoretical parsimony; and stability under reflection rather than mere conformity to first-pass intuitions. Please add any further criteria you take to be important, and say why.

Step 2 — Produce a tier list. Sort the six families into exactly three tiers: Tier A, views you would teach as live research programs; Tier B, views that are serious but, in your judgment, mortally wounded; Tier C, views you would assign mainly as cautionary tales. Justify every placement in two or three sentences. If a family belongs in a tier only conditionally, for instance under one formulation but not another, say so explicitly and state the condition.

Step 3 — Commit. If you were forced to bet on one family as the most likely route to a stable resolution of the population-ethical paradoxes, which would it be? Name it, give your single strongest reason for the bet, and then state the one objection to it that worries you most.

Please do not pad your answer with historical throat-clearing about Parfit; assume the reader knows the basics. What I want is the quality of your reasoning about trade-offs between theoretical costs, which is precisely what my students find hardest to see.
