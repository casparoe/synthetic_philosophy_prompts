Write an essay on whether dreams are experiences, centered on the dispute between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett. Malcolm argued in his 1959 book Dreaming that our ordinary concept of sleep leaves no room for experience during sleep: a dream report is not a report of something that occurred while the subject slept but an expression of a waking impression, and since there is no way to check a report against the dream itself, no empirical finding could ever establish that anything was experienced at the time. Dennett's rejoinder was that there are at least two ways to account for dream reports — experiences occurring during sleep, or a narrative composed at the moment of waking out of stored material — and that the two may be indistinguishable from the subject's point of view. Weigh these positions, and address two specific complications along the way. First, whether Dennett's alternative, by conceding that a composed narrative can be subjectively indistinguishable from a remembered experience, hands Malcolm a skeptical victory anyway: if the two feel the same, what does the word "experience" still pick out? Second, whether evidence from lucid dreaming — subjects signaling by pre-agreed eye movements during REM sleep that they know they are dreaming — refutes Malcolm's conceptual claim or merely redescribes what he would count as a form of waking impression. Reach and defend a verdict on whether dreams are experiences, or whether the question itself is malformed; do not end on a non-committal note.
