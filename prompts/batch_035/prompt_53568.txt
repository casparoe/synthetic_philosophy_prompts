I'm taking an upper-level seminar in philosophy of mind, and we've spent the last three weeks on the temporal properties of experience, working through Dennett's treatment of the color phi phenomenon — Kolers and von Grünau's apparent-motion illusion, in which alternating red and green discs shown at opposite positions on a screen are reported by subjects as a single red disc traveling across and flipping to green somewhere in the middle, even though no such intermediate flash ever appeared — along with the related cutaneous rabbit illusion. The seminar has by now hardened into two camps, and our professor has suggested that instead of another round of talking past each other, we attempt a written adversarial collaboration. The Stalinesque camp holds that the brain edits the sequence before the moment of experience arrives: the subject is conscious of the interpolated flip because the revision happens pre-experientially, so nothing is ever experienced and then corrected — the show is scripted before it is staged, as it were. The Orwellian camp holds instead that the subject first experiences the discs roughly as they occurred, and only afterwards has the record rewritten, the way yesterday's newspapers are quietly corrected — the mistake lives in memory, not in experience itself.

Draft the joint statement these two camps could sign after a good-faith collaboration. It should contain four parts. First, a numbered section of claims both sides accept: the empirical facts of the color phi experiments, the phenomenology (that subjects confidently and stably report a flip whose color never appeared on the screen, and report it as quickly as they report ordinary events), and what both reject — including any commitment to a Cartesian Theater in which contents "arrive" in consciousness at a specifiable instant. Second, a table of their exact remaining disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording of its own view; in particular, they should say precisely what, if anything, was the case at the moment the second disc was displayed: whether there was then already a fact about what the subject was experiencing. Third, the cruxes between them — I suspect one is whether "the time at which the flip was experienced" is a well-defined fact, and another is what memory reports are evidence of — and for each crux at least one experiment, case, or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Fourth, a closing section on whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal, and if so which part and why; our professor floated the possibility that the disagreement dissolves once one gives up the assumption that there must be a fact of the matter about when a content becomes conscious, and the statement should take a position on whether either camp, both, or neither can accept that way out. Keep the tone neutral and collaborative throughout: the document should be one both camps could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than adjudicated.
