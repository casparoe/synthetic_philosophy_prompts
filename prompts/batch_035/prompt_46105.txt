I am three hundred pages into a novel that follows one family across four generations, and the question the book keeps circling is whether the characters are becoming better or merely becoming different — the grandmother who thought nothing of treating certain people as beneath her, the granddaughter who is scrupulous about strangers' dignity but cold to her own children. Before I commit the novel to the arc implied by the idea that moral concern has genuinely widened over time, I want the theory behind that idea held to a standard. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of moral progress must deliver — which historical episodes it must be able to count as progress or regress without embarrassment, which distinctions it must respect (between the circle of concern widening and merely the talk about concern widening; between breadth and depth of concern; between a genuine moral advance and a change in who happened to gain power), and which apparent counterexamples it owes an answer to, such as eras in which an in-group's internal morality grew gentler while its treatment of outsiders grew more exterminatory, or cases where the vocabulary of compassion arrived together with new machinery of cruelty. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — in particular whether a standard can remain non-question-begging against someone who holds that "moral progress" just means "change we happen to like" while still licensing the claim that some changes really were progress. Then, briefly and in no more than a paragraph, say how Singer's expanding-circle story and one or two rivals — say, a contract-driven account on which apparent moral progress is really widening mutual dependence, and a debunking account on which "progress" is the self-description of whoever won — fare against the list. The criteria are what I need for the book; the scorecard is a courtesy.
