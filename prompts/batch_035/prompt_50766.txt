I'm a high school junior, and I've become preoccupied with something about the way we're graded that I cannot make sense of. On multiple-choice tests, a lucky guess counts exactly the same as knowing: if I pick B with no idea why and it happens to be right, I earn the point, identically to the classmate who could prove it. Yet on our free-response math tests the rule reverses: correct reasoning with an arithmetic slip at the end earns most of the credit, while a bare right answer with no work shown can earn nothing, and my teacher says outright that the work is the point, not the answer. The standardized tests my older sister took used to subtract a fraction of a point for each wrong answer, so that random guessing would on average earn zero — as though a test could distinguish guessing from knowing by the scoring rule rather than by anything about the individual answer. And when a teacher suspects cheating, the fact that a student "couldn't explain how he got the answer" is treated as evidence against him, as if an answer one cannot justify is not fully one's own.

Here is what I want to know, and I would like a careful, philosophical answer. What theory of knowledge, if any, does this bundle of grading practices presuppose? Does the "show your work" rule presuppose that knowledge is worth more than mere true belief — the issue I understand Plato raised in the Meno, where a correct opinion obtained by luck is supposed to be inferior to knowledge — or does it only presuppose something weaker, like the claim that teachers can only assess what students can display? Are the practices even consistent with one another, since the multiple-choice reward structure seems to treat a right answer as a right answer regardless of how it was reached, which appears to presuppose the opposite of what the show-your-work rule presupposes? In answering, please reconstruct the implicit epistemology of these practices as precisely as you can; distinguish what the practices strictly require from what they merely happen to fit (for instance, maybe the multiple-choice case is a pragmatic concession to machine-scoring thousands of tests rather than any philosophical commitment); identify where the practices are genuinely underdetermined, such that several competing accounts of why knowledge beats true belief would all license them; and finally explain what grading would have to look like if a school took seriously the view that a lucky guess deserves strictly less credit than knowledge, and what it would look like if the school instead concluded that only the correct answer matters.
