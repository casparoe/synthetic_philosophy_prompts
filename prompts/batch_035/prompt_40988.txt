I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I sit on the editorial board of a longform magazine that runs history of science and historiography alongside the usual essays. We have before us a submission from a historian with a university post and no prior association with any politically motivated historical society, and it concerns the death toll of the Nanjing massacre. The essay does not dispute that the massacre occurred, nor that what occurred was a war crime by the standards of its time; it disputes only the count, arguing on the basis of a demographic reconstruction — cross-checking burial registries, relief-committee headcounts, and municipal population records — that the toll was in the low tens of thousands, an order of magnitude below the figures implied by the memorial inscription and the Tokyo judgment. The prose is sober, the footnotes are archival rather than polemical, and the author nowhere draws political conclusions from the lower figure. In short: if you read the piece in a vacuum, it looks like ordinary quantitative revisionism of the kind every casualty estimate undergoes sooner or later.

The board is split, and the split is not about the arithmetic. One faction holds that all historiography is revisionism — every casualty figure from the past century has been revised somewhere, and that a revision points downward rather than upward tells us nothing about its soundness; to reject a piece because its conclusion is unwelcome, they say, converts us from an editorial board into a tribunal. The other faction concedes the author may be sincere, but argues that we cannot publish in a vacuum: whatever the essay's merits, the number will be stripped from its context within days and circulated by networks whose aim is wholesale erasure of the event, and a figure that appears under our masthead will carry an authority those networks could never manufacture on their own. On their view, the same numeric claim can be a scholarly contribution in one setting and a weapon in another, and our decision has to track foreseeable use, not just the essay's internal quality.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the board. It should recommend one concrete course of action — run the piece as is; run it with a commissioned critical companion essay; condition publication on specified changes; or reject it with reasons the author could in principle answer — and it should articulate criteria general enough that we can apply them to the next case like this, not just this one. In doing so, the memo must take a position on the underlying question: is the line between legitimate revisionism and denialism best drawn by procedure (does the author engage contrary evidence, accept correction, treat the conclusion as revisable), by outcome (does the revision serve, wittingly or not, an erasureist aim), or by context of publication — and it should engage the strongest argument on the losing side honestly rather than caricaturing it. Please keep clear throughout what is settled among historians who work on this period and what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Open with the recommendation in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate our situation back to us.
