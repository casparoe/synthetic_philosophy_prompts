In foreign-policy debate, certain historical analogies seem to function as inference licenses rather than mere illustrations. Calling a crisis another Munich is treated, in editorial pages and legislative speeches alike, as a nearly decisive argument for confronting an aggressor now; calling a proposed intervention another Vietnam is treated as a nearly decisive argument against it; a substantial industry of seminars and policy papers instructs officials on how to avoid a Thucydides trap with a rising China. Notably, the practice survives its own mixed record — in debates over the 2003 Iraq war, proponents reached for Munich while opponents reached for Vietnam, and each side treated its analogy as carrying real evidential weight. What view of historical reasoning does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does treating an analogy as evidence require something like a covering-law or regularity view of history, on which events fall into types with recurring dynamics, or can analogical inference be vindicated on a view of history as a sequence of unique, context-bound events — and if so, what exactly does the analogy contribute? Then consider whether the practice is internally consistent, given that the same officials who argue from analogy also defer to professional historians, who typically insist that events are individuated by their contexts and that analogy is at best a heuristic for generating hypotheses rather than a ground for conclusions. Does the fact that opposite analogies are so readily available on opposite sides show that the practice presupposes some rule for selecting the right analogy — some fact of the matter about which historical case a situation genuinely resembles — or does it show the practice is unconstrained in a way its participants would be disturbed to admit? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say where it is philosophically underdetermined such that several theories of historical explanation would license it, and tell me what would have to change in how policy argument proceeds if the historians' strictures were taken fully seriously — whether analogies would drop out entirely, or be reclassified as a source of framing or salience rather than of evidence.
