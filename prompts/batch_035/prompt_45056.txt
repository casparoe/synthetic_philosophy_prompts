I am putting together a unit on experimental philosophy for a graduate seminar, and I need a fresh case for the session on the side-effect effect. In the familiar chairman-style vignettes, an agent knowingly brings about a harmful side effect while single-mindedly pursuing profit and is judged blameworthy, whereas a structurally identical agent who knowingly brings about a helpful side effect is judged not praiseworthy. I want you to build the inverse: a matched pair of one-paragraph vignettes, set in an ordinary workplace (no executives, no environment, no trolleys), in which the asymmetry flips — the agent who knowingly produces a harmful side effect, while indifferent to it, comes out not blameworthy, and the structurally parallel agent who knowingly produces a helpful side effect, while equally indifferent, comes out praiseworthy.

The point of the case is to stress-test the two leading explanations of the original effect, so it must be engineered rather than rigged. Specifically:

1. Hold fixed everything the classic cases hold fixed: the agent's knowledge of the side effect, the agent's complete indifference to it, the fact that the side effect is not the agent's aim, and the same underlying aim in both vignettes.
2. The inversion must be generated, not stipulated. Do not tell the reader whom to blame or praise, use no evaluatively loaded vocabulary, and make the aim itself something common and neutral — deadlines, budgets, sales figures.
3. One paragraph per vignette, and the pair should differ only in the feature you are using to produce the inversion.

After presenting the vignettes, name the single feature that is doing the work, and explain why it blocks blame in the harm case while sustaining praise in the help case. Then run the theory check. Consider first the norm-violation account, on which subjects' blame attributions track whether the side effect violates some operative norm, and second the reason-explanation account, on which subjects blame only when the agent appears to have acted for the bad-making reason. For each account, state what it predicts about your vignettes. If your case is kinder to one account than the other, say which and why; if both can accommodate it, say what each must concede to do so.

Finally, play defense. How would someone who insists on the original asymmetry argue that your case does not engage the side-effect effect at all, but instead trades on some other phenomenon — excuse, consent, causal structure, or mere framing? And what is the minimal modification to your vignettes that would restore the original asymmetry while leaving your device intact? If no such modification exists, that is itself a result — but you must argue for it.
