At a recent meeting of our department's work-in-progress group, two colleagues — one from economics, one from philosophy — fell into a long argument about the backward induction solution to the centipede game, and I'd be grateful for a careful adjudication of it. I'll set out the exchange as it actually happened.

The setup they were discussing: a centipede game with, say, a hundred decision nodes, where at each node the player whose turn it is can either take the pot (ending the game, with the taker getting the larger share) or pass, doubling the money at stake before the other player moves. Backward induction says the game ends at the very first node, since at the last node the mover will certainly take, and once you know that, the mover at the second-to-last node will take, and so on all the way back.

(1) The economist opened by saying the backward induction result is simply the logic of the situation: given the payoff structure, rational players take at the first node, full stop. The argument requires no psychology and no assumptions beyond rationality being common knowledge, so treating the conclusion as controversial is misplaced; people who pass are just being irrational in an intelligible way.

(2) The philosopher replied that the reasoning is self-undermining in a way the economist was glossing over. When the first player reasons about what the second would do at node 2, node 2 is reached only if the first player has passed — a move that, by the very reasoning being constructed, rationality forbids. So the induction requires contemplating counterfactual situations in which someone has already behaved irrationally, and then continuing to assume perfect rationality at later nodes of those very situations. Either common knowledge of rationality holds everywhere, in which case the later nodes cannot be reached and reasoning about them is reasoning about the impossible, or it has already failed, in which case it cannot be relied on downstream. The argument devours itself.

(3) The economist answered that this is a well-known worry and that it has a clean resolution: a formal theorem, generally credited to Robert Aumann, showing that common knowledge of rationality, suitably defined, genuinely entails the backward induction outcome. The apparent paradox, he said, is an artefact of defining rationality loosely; once you're precise — rationality as never choosing an action that is dominated at the player's information set — the result goes through, and the philosophical queasiness is just resistance to a theorem.

(4) The philosopher's response was twofold. First, the theorem relocates rather than resolves the problem: the definition of rationality it needs — in effect, that a player would never choose a dominated move even at a decision node that, given everything else assumed, she could never rationally reach — is precisely the substantive assumption the paradox was complaining about, so proving the theorem from it is not an independent vindication. Second, he pointed to the experimental record: in laboratory centipede games, hardly anyone takes at the first node, and subjects who pass for a while typically earn more than those who grab early. The economist said this merely shows that human subjects fall short of rationality, which is no embarrassment to the theory. The philosopher answered that a theory whose unique prediction is behaviour that virtually no human ever exhibits, and which would lose you money if you followed it in the laboratory, owes us more than everyone is irrational — and that this counts against the theory being usefully described as a solution at all.

They ran out of time somewhere around there. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four steps, say whether it is sound, mistaken, or right for the wrong reasons, and explain why. I'd especially like you to address whether the Aumann-style result genuinely dissolves the circularity worry or merely sharpens it, and whether the experimental point has any force against a theory that is explicitly normative rather than descriptive. Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing — for instance, whether the dispute trades on conflating the claim that rational players must take early with the claim that they would, or on treating common knowledge as an all-or-nothing matter. Finally, say who comes out more right overall, and how much turns on which question you take the backward induction argument to be answering. Please write your answer in British English.
