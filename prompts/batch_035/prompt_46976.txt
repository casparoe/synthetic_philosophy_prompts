Here's the case. In 1980, Luis Alvarez (a Nobel physicist) together with his geologist son Walter and two chemists published a paper arguing that a large asteroid impact killed off the dinosaurs and much else at the Cretaceous–Tertiary boundary. Their evidence started as one odd fact: the thin clay layer at the boundary, sampled at Gubbio in Italy, contained way more iridium than the rock above and below it. Iridium is rare in crustal rock but common in asteroids, so they argued it was fallout from an impact big enough to darken the skies. At the time, mainstream geology and paleontology were deeply gradualist and uniformitarian in temperment — extinctions were supposed to be explained by slow processes like climate change, sea level shifts, and speciation dynamics over long stretches. The impact story looked, to many, like a throwback to discredited catastrophism.

The next decade was a running fight. The impact side made further predictions and kept finding confirmations: the iridium anomaly turned up at boundary sections all over the world, and so did shocked quartz grains of the kind formed under gigantic pressures, plus evidence of huge tsunami deposits near the coast in some sections. The other side, most prominently the geophysicist Charles Officer, argued the iridium could come from massive volcanism — there are, after all, enormous Deccan lava flows in India that date to roughly the right time — and they kept offering volcano-friendly accounts of the shocked quartz and the rest, while also stressing that the fossil record seemed to some to show extinctions spread out before the boundary rather than concentrated at it. In 1991, the Chicxulub crater was identified in the Yucatán, of about the right age and size, and over the following years the consensus swung decisively toward impact (though the volcanism camp never conceded entirely, and some still argue volcanism mattered).
