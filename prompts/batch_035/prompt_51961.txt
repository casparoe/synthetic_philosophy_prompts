Write an essay on whether mass famines should be understood as natural disasters or as political crimes, taking as your test cases the Bengal famine of 1943, the Great Irish Famine of the 1840s, and the Chinese famine that followed the Great Leap Forward. Sen's entitlement approach will be your starting point: the claim that people starve not because food is absent but because their bundles of endowments and exchange possibilities collapse, and his corollary that no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press and competitive elections. But I want you to go past the standard recitation, in two directions.

First, subject the phrase "political crime" to an analysis it rarely gets. In criminal law, a crime standardly requires an act or a legally cognizable omission, a harm, and a mental state — and the mental state comes in grades: purpose, knowledge, recklessness, negligence. If we take the "crime" framing seriously rather than as a rhetorical flourish, the question becomes which grade of culpability each famine supports. The Bengal famine under wartime British administration, the Irish famine under a state that had a parliament, newspapers, and Relief Commissioners who debated policy in public, and the Great Leap famine under a regime that suppressed famine reporting while exporting grain: sort them. Consider seriously whether the criminal analogy breaks because famine deaths are massively overdetermined, distributed across thousands of officials and market actors, and caused as much by omission as by act — and whether omissions that are diffuse and structural can carry the kind of agency that "crime" demands.

Second, test Sen's democratic-immunity claim against the Irish case, which is the awkward one: the famine happened inside the then mostadvanced parliamentary democracy in the world, with a lively press reporting the deaths, under governments that lost and won elections afterwards. Does Ireland show that the immunity claim needs refinement — that it is not democracy as such but something else (a political franchise for the starving population, perhaps) that does the work? And does the refinement still hold when you consider that British policy in Ireland was partly driven by a judgment that relief would do long-run harm — a case of conscientious under-intervention rather than indifference?

To keep the verdict honest, include a stylized hypothetical: a federal democracy, free press, full adult franchise, hit by a genuine collapse of total food supply — not an entitlement failure but an absolute shortage — in which the government mitigates well but a quarter-million people nonetheless die because logistics cannot reach an isolated region before the next harvest. Ask whether your favored verdict classifies those deaths as disaster, crime, or neither, and what that classification implies about which famines the "crime" framing was ever meant to cover.

Weigh the considerations on both sides, but do not end non-committally: defend a verdict about the disaster/crime dichotomy as applied to famines generally, including whether the dichotomy should be replaced by a third category, and what would have to be true of a government's knowledge and capacity for famine deaths to count as criminal in the strict sense you define. Do not cite or name specific books or articles; the argument should stand on the cases and the concepts.
