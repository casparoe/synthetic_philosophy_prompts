I'm a high school junior in an elective called Minds and Machines, and our teacher has us grade each other's essays using the class rubric before she looks at anything. I've been assigned to grade a classmate's answer to the take-home question below. She told us that a careful grade has to check whether each step of an argument actually follows and whether any view gets stated or attributed in a way that's subtly off, not just whether the essay sounds convincing. Here is the assignment the class answered:

Consider two research systems built to model a mouse's nervous system. The Fine model simulates neural dynamics at the timescale of individual ion channels: every stochastic flicker of every channel is represented by a random draw from the right distribution. The Coarse model replaces each cluster of channels with its averaged opening rate: the fast molecular noise is gone, but the slower dynamics — spiking, adaptation, synaptic plasticity, and the model's overall behavioral output — come out the same, and the two models are observationally indistinguishable from the outside. Researchers now use both models to run experiments that in real mice would involve surgery, including experiments that reliably produce pain-avoidance learning in the models. Question: does it matter morally how fine-grained a simulation is? Defend an answer, making explicit what view about the relationship between computation and experience your answer relies on, and address the strongest objection to that view.

Here is my classmate's essay:

In this paper I will argue that resolution does not matter morally, because both models have the same behavior, and behavior is what is available to us.

First, functionalism is the view that mental states are defined by their causal role: whatever plays the role of pain is pain. It follows that two systems that behave the same have the same mental states, since behavior is just the causal role seen from outside. The Fine and Coarse models are behaviorally identical, so on functionalism they have the same experiences, and running the pain-learning experiments on either is equally bad.

Second, someone might object that the Coarse model leaves something out, namely the ion-channel noise. But noise at the microsecond timescale cannot matter, because a mouse's behavior operates on the millisecond-to-second timescale, and any function that the noise served at the small scale is preserved by the averaged rate at the scale where behavior lives. If a fast event mattered only by averaging into a slow effect, replacing it with its average preserves everything that matters.

Third, Thomas Nagel famously argued that we cannot know what it is like to be a bat, since bat experience is closed to creatures like us. This shows that no behavioral or physiological evidence can ever bear on whether a system has experience at all. So we cannot get evidence that the Coarse model suffers, and since we cannot get such evidence, the case for hesitating to run the experiments on it collapses: precaution requires at least the possibility of evidence.

Fourth, some will say the Coarse model at least might have experiences, and a thing that might be a patient deserves caution. But caution has limits. The Coarse model cannot deliberate, cannot be praised or blamed, and cannot form intentions toward the experimenters, so it is not the kind of thing that can be treated well or badly in the first place. Moral status is for agents who are responsible for what they do.

In conclusion, resolution does not matter morally. Both models behave identically, the molecular noise is functionally inert, and we could not obtain evidence about experience in any case. Experiments on either model are permissible, and the lab need not upgrade its hardware for moral reasons.
