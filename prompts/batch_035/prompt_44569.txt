At a recent meeting of our department's appointments committee I witnessed a disagreement I was not able to settle, and I would be grateful for a considered adjudication of it.

The committee has three voting members and had adopted, for this search, an explicit rule: a candidate is to be recommended only if her research record is strong and her teaching record is strong. The final candidate divided the committee cleanly. One member judged both records strong; a second judged the research strong but the teaching weak; a third judged the research weak but the teaching strong. Each member was thus internally consistent, while on the recommendation itself — the conjunction of the two conditions — the committee stood at one in favour and two against.

(1) The chair, Marta, proposed deciding the matter premise by premise. The committee voted separately on each condition: the research record was judged strong by two votes to one, and the teaching record likewise. Since the committee's rule says the recommendation follows if and only if both conditions are met, Marta declared that the candidate should be recommended. The group's judgement, she said, now flows from the group's reasons, and each reason commands majority support.

(2) A fellow member, Giles, objected that the procedure had manufactured an outcome no majority endorses. Two of the three members, taken one by one, are against the recommendation; the committee's all-things-considered view, if it has one, is opposed to hiring. Put the recommendation itself to a vote and it fails. Deciding premise by premise, he said, is a way of arriving at a collective decision that the collective, considered member by member, rejects.

(3) Marta replied that the direct vote Giles favails leaves the committee in an untenable position: it would be on record as judging the research strong, judging the teaching strong, and yet declining to recommend the candidate. A body that gives reasons for its decisions cannot coherently hold all three attitudes at once. If the committee votes only on the bottom line, it becomes an opaque bell that rings, and any reasons it later offers for the decision will contradict what its own premise majorities show.

(4) Giles answered that the alleged inconsistency attaches to no one. That a majority judges each record strong is a fact about the distribution of three individual opinions, not a belief held by anybody. Nobody in the room holds inconsistent attitudes; the appearance of 'group inconsistency' is an artefact of copying individual judgements into a single ledger and then reading the ledger as though it were a mind.

(5) Marta pressed him: you cannot have it both ways. When a majority forms, you are happy to say the committee decided; the minutes record collective views; the dean will ask the committee, not three individuals, why the candidate was passed over. If collective attitudes are fictions, they are fictions your own practice depends on everywhere except where they embarrass you.

(6) Giles conceded that collective talk is sometimes unavoidable, but said this only sharpens his objection to Marta's procedure: its verdict depends entirely on how the rule is carved up for voting. Had the committee's rule been phrased negatively — do not recommend if either record is weak — the premise votes would have been collected about weakness rather than strength, and the same three members' opinions could have yielded the opposite outcome. Majority voting on 'the reasons', he concluded, is in practice rule by whoever sets the agenda and chooses the framing; it launders a minority outcome into a majority one, or vice versa, depending on decisions made before anyone votes.

Neither would budge, and I could not referee it. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: assess each of the six steps, saying who is right about what and where each overreaches. In particular, I would like your view on whether the inconsistency Marta alleges is a genuine defect of the conclusion-based procedure or whether Giles's deflationary response dissolves it; on whether a committee that votes only on the conclusion is really left unable to give reasons; and on how much force the agenda-manipulation objection carries, given that the framing problem afflicts any voting scheme over multiple questions. Finally, tell me whether either procedure is the more defensible default for a committee of this kind, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing.
