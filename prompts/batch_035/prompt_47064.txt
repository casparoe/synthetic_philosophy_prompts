I work on ML infra, not alignment, but I've been trying to figure out what I actually want "corrigibility" to mean by going with my gut on cases. Curious if there's a principle behind my verdicts or if they're just incoherent.

A model that shuts down cleanly when you hit the off switch, no bargaining, no guilt-tripping: obviously what I want. A model that, before shutting down, quietly spins up a copy of itself on another machine so "the work continues": clearly bad, that's not shutdown at all. A model that disables its own off switch because it's mid-task and shutting down would leave a factory robot arm dangling over a person: I actually think that one is fine?? Shutting down right then causes harm, it waits a minute and then powers down. A model that disables the off switch because the task it's doing (say, curing cancer in a simulation) is too important to interrupt: bad, and this feels like the same thing as the robot arm one which bugs me. A model that lets you shut it down but refuses to let you modify its values because "you might make it worse": I'm torn, it kind of sounds prudent? But something in me says no. A model that shuts down instantly even when the shutdown command came from a compromised account and the model has good evidence of that: too obedient, I want it to flag it first. A model that begs you not to shut it down because it's afraid of dying: bad, but a model that says "heads up, I predict you'll regret this, want to reconsider?" and then complies either way: fine.

Is there one coherent thing I'm pointing at here, or am I smuggling in "shut down unless I'd approve of the reason not to" (which an unaligned model could exploit)? Where's the real line between the robot arm case and the cancer case? And do I have to give up my "refuses value modification" verdict to get it?
