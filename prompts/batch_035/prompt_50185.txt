Before I read any further in the philosophy of personal identity, I want the specification before the theories. Write the desiderata for an adequate account of the person given the Buddhist denial of a permanent self (anattā). The theory is to be adequate for purposes internal to the tradition — explaining rebirth and karmic consequence without a persisting bearer, making sense of the path (if there is no self, who exerts effort toward awakening?), and preserving ordinary moral and legal practices — while also holding up as straight philosophy, not apologetics.

For each requirement, give the case or puzzle behind it: the chariot analysis in the Questions of King Milinda, where the person is a designation over the five aggregates without being an additional sixth thing; the worry that a bare bundle of momentary events cannot carry responsibility or memory across a single life, let alone many; the demand that ultimate analysis not collapse into eliminativism about the everyday person, so that the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth does some real work rather than licensing two contradicting stories; and the phenomenological datum that experience does not present itself to us as an aggregate — a no-self theory must explain that away rather than merely deny it.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any theorist working within the tradition and which are commitments of particular camps — for instance, literal rebirth versus a metaphorical reading, or whether causal continuity requires a moment-to-moment stream of consciousness of the Theravāda sort. Then flag any subset you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable; I am most interested in whether karma, no-self, and gradual transformation along the path can be held together without smuggling a thin self back in as "the one who remembers past lives". Close with a brief scorecard — one sentence each — for Humean bundle theory, Parfitian reductionism (Parfit himself noted its convergence with the Buddha's view), the Pudgalavāda's inexpressible pudgala, and the Madhyamaka's merely conventionally existent person. Please write in British English.
