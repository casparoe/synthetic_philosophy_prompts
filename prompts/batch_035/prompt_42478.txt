My ethics reading group got stuck on something involving the unilateralist's curse (the Bostrom/Ord thing: when lots of people each have the power to take an action that's bad if even one of them takes it, the action happens more often the larger the group, even when each person is more likely right than wrong that it's good — and the proposed fix is a principle of conformity, where you defer to the group's judgment instead of your own unless you clear a confidence threshold that rises with group size). Someone pushed this argument at us:

(1) The curse shows up whenever a bunch of agents can each trigger an outcome that's bad if any one of them triggers it, and each agent's own evidence marginally favors triggering.
(2) The principled fix is a conformity principle: don't act on your own judgment unless you're confident enough that the group's likely to be wrong, with the bar rising as the pool of potential actors grows.
(3) Moral progress is full of cases matching (1): the first person to publicly denounce slavery, the lone vegetarian in a meat-eating town, the whistleblower. In each case there were many people who coud have spoken up, speaking up exposed everyone to a cost if it was misguided, and each potential speaker's judgment that speaking was good was, by everyone else's lights, more likely wrong than right — otherwise it wouldn't have been controversial at the time.
(4) So by (2), all those people should have conformed, i.e. kept quiet until the group came around on its own.
(5) That would have suppressed basically every moral advance we've ever had.
(6) So the conformity principle, or the setup that generates it, is broken.

We can't agree on which premise to give up. I'm half tempted to bite the bullet on (4) and say most moral innovation should be suppressed and we just got lucky — but tell me where this actually goes wrong and what rejecting it there costs. My own suspicion is that the curse setup has a hidden assumption about everyone being epistemic peers with symmetric evidence that quietly does all the work in (3), but I can't quite nail it down, and if I'm right I'd like to know whether the principle can be repaired for the asymmetric cases or whether it just stops applying to them entirely.
