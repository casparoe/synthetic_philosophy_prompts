EXAM QUESTION (part marks shown in brackets):

"Presentism is the view that, necessarily, only present entities exist. Eternalism is the view that past, present, and future entities all exist tenselessly, with being past, present, or future amounting to standing in earlier-than or later-than relations to a time. Answer all four parts.

(a) [25] Present the objection to presentism from cross-temporal relations, using the case of Caesar's admiration of Socrates, and explain how the eternalist accommodates truths of this form. Describe what you take to be the most promising presentist reply, and assess it.

(b) [25] Present the truthmaker objection to presentism concerning past-tensed truths such as that there were dinosaurs, and describe the standard presentist response. Explain the main objection to that response.

(c) [30] Explain why the eternalist block universe appears to have difficulty accounting for temporal passage and the apparent singularity of the present. Discuss the rate-of-passage problem for views that posit genuine passage.

(d) [20] On the basis of the above, which view do you judge better off overall? Give reasons."

STUDENT'S ANSWER:

"(a) Presentism says only present things exist; eternalism says dinosaurs, Socrates, and future Martian outposts all exist tenselessly, and that their being past or future is a matter of standing in earlier-than or later-than relations to the relevant time. The cross-temporal problem is this: Caesar admired Socrates, but Caesar existed roughly two thousand years after Socrates, so on presentism, whenever Caesar exists, Socrates does not; and since a relation cannot obtain unless all of its relata exist, presentism cannot ground the truth that Caesar admired Socrates. Eternalism has no such problem: Caesar and Socrates both exist tenselessly in the block, and Caesar's admiring occurs tenselessly at the region of the block where Caesar is. The best presentist reply is that admiration is an internal relation: whether Caesar admires Socrates is fixed by Caesar's intrinsic mental state, which relates Caesar to the intrinsic qualitative character that Socrates is represented as having in that state. Internal relations obtain whenever their relata exist, and Caesar's mental state exists at the relevant present moments, so presentism retains the truth. This reply is fully adequate, since the relevant facts about Caesar are all present facts, and metaphysics anyway allows facts to supervene on the present. One might object that relating Caesar to a representation of Socrates is not the same as relating him to Socrates, but the two truths are surely equivalent.

(b) The truthmaker principle says that for every truth, something exists that makes it true. Eternalism satisfies this for past-tensed truths trivially: the dinosaurs themselves, existing tenselessly, make it true that there were dinosaurs. Presentism cannot use past entities as truthmakers, since none exist. The standard presentist response is that presently existing things, for instance the world, instantiate Lucretian properties such as the property of being such that there were dinosaurs, and that this instantiation makes the past-tensed truth true. The main objection is that this is a cheat: the property looks concocted to mirror the truth it is supposed to make true, so the response is vacuous. But this objection is overstated. Since the present world does instantiate the property, and primitive instantiation facts are permitted elsewhere in metaphysics, the presentist reply stands; the truthmaker worry dissolves.

(c) On the eternalist picture, all times exist tenselessly and none is privileged; the present is simply the region we happen to occupy, just as 'here' picks out where I am rather than a special place. Nothing in the block becomes or flows, so there is no passage. The best way for the friends of passage to respond is the moving spotlight theory, which is a version of presentism that supplements the block with a privileged present moment, the spotlight, which moves along the temporal dimension. But as Smart pointed out, if the spotlight moves, it must move at some rate, and the only candidate rate is one second per second. That rate is either trivial, in which case nothing has been explained, or it requires a second temporal dimension, a hyper-time, relative to which the spotlight moves, which is absurd. So the spotlight view fails, and with it presentism's best account of passage.

(d) Eternalism is better off overall. It solves the cross-temporal and truthmaker problems at one stroke, and its only apparent cost, the loss of passage, is no real cost, since the rate-of-passage argument shows passage to be incoherent anyway. Nothing of value is lost by accepting the block."
