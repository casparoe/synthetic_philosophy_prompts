My study group has been going in circles on this one and I want an actual verdict, not a menu of options. A friend of ours laid this out and won't budge:

(1) If a surgeon swapped one of your neurons for a silicon chip that matched its input-output behavior exactly, you'd wake up fine -- you'd survive, no different in kind from the cell loss your brain shrugs off every day.
(2) Survival composes: if you survive the first substitution, you're around to survive the second, and so on for any finite number of them.
(3) Whole-brain uploading is nothing but a long series of such substitutions, each of which you'd survive.
(4) So you'd survive being fully uploaded -- brain gone, you running on hardware in a rack somewhere.

I can live with (4), honestly. What I can't live with is where he takes it next: he points out that (1)-(3) never used the chip's location, so the rack can sit a mile away with a wireless link, and more importantly nothing in the argument says the rack is unique. Run the substitutions in two racks in parallel -- each neuron replaced in both at once, both racks functionally identical at every step -- and (1)-(3) are satisfied twice over. So by his own argument I'd literally be two people at once, or I'd have "been" in the rack the whole time even while my skull still worked. Each premise still looks fine to me individually: (1) looks like neuroplasticity writ small, (2) looks like plain transitivity, (3) looks like bookkeeping. My hunch is that "survive" quietly changes meaning somewhere between (4) and the two-racks case, but a couple people in the group insist the rot is earlier -- they think (2) only holds when consecutive stages overlap in the right way, and the argument never says overlap, so it's smuggling in an assumption it never states. Which step should we reject, what does rejecting it cost, and is there any way to keep upload-survival without biting the two-racks bullet?
