I run the behavioral side of a cardiac rehab program at a mid-sized hospital, which in practice means sitting down a few times a week with fifty- to seventy-year-olds who've just had a heart event and trying to get them to quit smoking, actually take their statins, and walk. Twenty minutes per session, high stakes, and my whole training is basically motivational interviewing: don't lecture, don't argue, elicit the patient's own reasons for change. My brother-in-law is finishing a philosophy PhD and he tells me this isn't just a counseling style, it's a live metaphysical position — he mentioned Bernard Williams' distinction between internal and external reasons. As I understand it: the claim "this patient has a reason to quit" is only true if there's some route from the motives and values he already has — after correcting false beliefs, spelling out consequences he hasn't imagined, letting him deliberate properly — that ends in him wanting to quit. If there's no such route, then on Williams' view there is no reason there to be found, just my reasons for wanting him to quit. That would explain alot of why lecturing people fails! But it also seems to have implications I'm not sure I can live with clinically, and I'd like it turned into something usable.

Write me a procedure I can actually run in twenty minutes: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure lets me document "no internal reason found, stop pushing" and close the file, and where it has to hand the decision back to me because the question is one of values rather than facts. It needs to handle the three kinds of patient I actually get. First, the guy who obviously cares about being around for his grandkids but hasn't connected that to quitting — sounds like an easy internal reason, but what do I concretely check before I'm allowed to believe the connection is real and not just something he says to me? Second, the guy who, after knowing him for two years, has clearly weighed it and genuinely prefers his remaining years with cigarettes — not confused, not in denial, just assigns different weights than I do. Third, the patient whose "own values" I suspect are mostly untreated depression talking, where I honestly don't know whether a motivational set you'd deliberate from while depressed is one that should count as *his* for these purposes.

Two more things. Be honest about which steps of the procedure are real steps and which are my judgment dressed up as a checklist — my brother-in-law says this is a known worry about the whole idea, that "sound deliberation" ends up doing all the work and can be stretched to whatever conclusion you were going to reach anyway, and if that's true of my procedure I want to know which parts it's true of. And tell me where the procedure says I'm allowed to try to *change* what a patient wants — building a new attachment, making the grandkids more vivid, even the occasional strategic guilt trip — as opposed to only finding reasons inside what's already there. I genuinely can't tell whether that's counseling or manipulation, and I suspect Williams doesn't fully settle it either, so wherever the procedure can't settle it, say so and tell me what I'm stuck deciding myself.
