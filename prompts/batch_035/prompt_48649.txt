Compose a satirical customer-support FAQ for an invented subscription start-up, of roughly 600 words, written in British English and formatted as numbered questions and answers. The company — call it Competence-as-a-Service, or invent a better name — has a strictly intellectualist business model: it transmits skills by delivering subscribers complete bundles of true propositions about the activity in question. Everything true about swimming, say, arrives in weekly instalments, and the company's stated policy is to refuse any distinction between knowing how to do something and merely knowing a way of doing it.

The satire targets that intellectualist position, and the humour must come from the actual content of the debate, so that a philosopher who knows the literature finds the piece fair rather than a caricature. Please work in the following, without ever naming the doctrines as such:

1. The regress problem. A subscriber asks why, having received every proposition, she still cannot act on them, and is told that applying propositions to action is itself a further competence, available on the Premium tier; the ladder of tiers should visibly never terminate.
2. Knowledge-how versus mere ability. A subscriber who has the full proposition set but is currently in a cast, or has lost her nerve, asks whether she must return her knowledge; the company's reply should turn on whether knowing how to swim requires being able to swim just then, and it should take a position while pretending to be a refund policy.
3. The special way of entertaining the propositions. The company insists that the delivered propositions must be grasped in some distinctively practical manner, and a subscriber asks what that means; the answer should be a masterpiece of marketing obfuscation that gestures at a real move in the literature without ever specifying it.
4. The practitioner without the propositions. A self-taught subscriber who performs flawlessly but cannot answer any of the company's knowledge questionnaires is denied recognition of his competence, since the assessments measure only recall of the bundles. The company's response should quietly assume that his skill just is some body of knowledge he cannot locate.
5. A closing entry in which the FAQ's own authors concede, entirely deadpan, that they cannot operate the product themselves.

The voice throughout should be deadpan corporate: no winking, no exclamation marks, no admissions. The irony must remain legible — a reader should be able to tell exactly what is being lampooned — and the company must never concede anything. Do not lean on bibliographic specifics: no page references, no dates, and at most a single passing mention of Ryle, or of the pair of philosophers who revived the intellectualist view, between them — or none at all.
