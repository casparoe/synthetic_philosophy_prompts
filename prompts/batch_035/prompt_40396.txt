I run a small reading group on economic history, and our final session, on Weber's Protestant ethic thesis, ended in a standoff between two members, neither of whom would give an inch. I had to referee and honestly could not. I would like a careful, point-by-point adjudication of their exchange.

Ilana opened with a strong reading of the thesis: Calvinist predestination produced an anxiety about election that had no sacramental outlet, and that anxiety got channeled into disciplined labor, methodical bookkeeping, and deferred consumption — a bundle of dispositions that helped give northwest Europe's commercial culture its distinctive tempo. Her evidence was the regional pattern: Protestant and Catholic territories sitting side by side with similar terrain, law, and trade access but diverging in proto-industrial development is as close to a controlled comparison as history offers.

Theo replied that the thesis is circular as stated. Values are unobservable, and we infer them from the very behavior they are supposed to explain: the thrifty shopkeeper is read as evidence of a thrifty ethic, which is then cited as the cause of the thrift. Unless the values are measured independently of the outcome — and he doubted sermon collections and catechisms could bear that weight, since clergy in commercial cities had every incentive to preach what their congregations already practiced — the putative explanation is a redescription.

Ilana conceded the general point but denied it applied here: church disciplinary records, diaries, and sumptuary regulation are traces of the value system generated for internal religious reasons, before and independently of the economic outcome they are asked to explain. She added a counterfactual: hold geography, property law, and trade routes fixed but swap in the surrounding Catholic confessional culture, and she doubted you get the same pace of accumulation.

Theo made two replies. First, a selection problem: commercial dynamism helps explain which cities and princes broke with Rome — lay elites who chafed at feast-day calendars, tithes, and clerical oversight were the ones to break — so the correlation does not isolate a causal direction. Second, mechanism substitutability: once you name the concrete intermediaries — scriptural literacy, the abolition of a large share of saint's days and hence more working days, trading networks of a confessionally excluded minority — the ethic itself drops out, since those mechanisms could operate, and sometimes did, without any salvation anxiety; the doctrinal core is a bystander. He closed by saying that even granting everything, culture explains at most timing, not the structural preconditions of credit, wage labor, and enforceable property, so calling the ethic a cause inflates a catalyst into an engine.

Ilana's last word: a catalyst is a cause; and the mechanisms Theo lists are not rivals of the cultural explanation but its vehicles — who demanded a literate laity, and why? — so explaining through them just relocates the cultural factor one step down, into the local costs and payoffs that made those mechanisms available in the first place.

Please go through their exchange move by move — the circularity charge, the independent-measurement reply, the counterfactual, the selection-effect objection, the mechanism-substitutability objection, the catalyst-versus-engine exchange, and Ilana's final relocation point — and say who is right about what, where each of them overreaches, and what, if anything, both are missing.
