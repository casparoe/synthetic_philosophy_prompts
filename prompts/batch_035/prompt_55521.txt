You are an experienced workshop facilitator and a professional philosopher specializing in normative ethics, so please think carefully and brainstorm a few options internally before committing to a design. I teach a mid-level undergraduate seminar on ethical theory (14 students, one 75-minute session) in which we have just finished Mill and are about to begin Rawls. Before we get there, I want an in-class activity that makes the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism land as a felt problem rather than a paragraph in the reading. The design should exploit the structure of the objection rather than just rehearsing it: I want the students to first endorse, with their own choices, the intra-personal aggregation that makes prudence rational — trading off a bad stretch of one life against a good stretch of the same life, accepting a cost now for a benefit later — and then be confronted with a numerically identical trade-off in which the costs fall on one identifiable person and the benefits on the others. Ideally the same students who defended the trade in the first round should find themselves unwilling to defend it in the second, without my intervening between the rounds.

Please give me complete rules and materials (something like index cards, tokens or points, and a payoff scheme that involves no real money and no one having to reveal anything embarrassing), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and a description of the two rounds: the prudential round, where each participant manages a single "life" across several periods and can rationally accept misery in period two for the sake of period four, and the social round, where a designated planner or majority vote faces exactly parallel options whose burdens and benefits land on different participants. Make the numbers such that the aggregatively optimal option in the social round visibly requires sinking one person's whole sequence, and include a rule that lets the group attempt to "compensate" the burdened participant in later periods, since I want them to discover for themselves why that reply feels weaker across lives than it did within a life.

Then the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the objection — the idea that utilitarianism extends the decision-rule of a single prudent individual to society, and that it thereby allows losses to one person to be offset by gains to another in a way that presupposes a kind of compensation the world cannot actually deliver. Please also do three honest things for me: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (my group includes a couple of confident utilitarians who will smell the punchline by minute ten, and some students who won't care about abstract tokens at all); tell me how to handle each; and tell me plainly which part of the objection the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply by lecture — in particular, I worry that students will come away thinking the objection is just about unfairness or the distribution of utility, when the Rawlsian point is supposed to be about aggregation across persons as such, and I'd like you to be explicit about whether any classroom mechanism can isolate that stronger point or whether the debrief has to do that work alone.
