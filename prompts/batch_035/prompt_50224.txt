You're a professional philosopher who has spent way too long on responsibility gaps — the worry that when an autonomous system causes harm and no human intended it or could reasonably forsee it, there may be nobody at all who is aptly blameworthy, and that this is a bug not just for blame but for safety too, since the threat of being held responsible is one of the things that keeps people careful. I need you to operationalize that for me.

I run incident review at a company that operates a fleet of autonomous warehouse forklifts. Roughly once a month one of them does something harmful — clips a worker, crushes stock, once shut a whole loading dock for a day — and the hard cases are never the dumb ones (miscalibrated sensor, someone skipped a checklist). The hard ones are where the behavior traces back to nobody's decision in particular. Last spring our collision-avoidance policy got retrained overnight on fleet data from our EU sites and started hugging the left aisle in a way no one had signed off on. Another time, two subsystems, each spec'd by a different team and each fine on it's own, interacted so that the forklift yielded twice and then didn't yield at all. Our review board is three engineers plus me, we get maybe 40 minutes per incident, and we have to close each one either with a name or with an explicit no-name finding, because our insurer — and, worse, the warehouse staff — want to know who is answerable. "The system did it" is eating morale.

So please think carefully about how philosophers carve this stuff up (responsibility gaps, the problem of many hands, tracing decisions through design, deployment, and ongoing learning), and then turn it into a procedure we can actually run in a meeting. Give me exactly five numbered questions, in the order we should ask them, and nothing else — no intro, no conclusion, no extra items, no headers. Each question should be a sentence or two and should carry its own pass/fail condition inside it: what kind of answer lets us move on to the next question, and what a "no" or a "this is a judgment call" routes to — a named person, a design fix, a sign-off by me, or an explicit finding that it lands on whoever chose to deploy despite knowing about the corner cases. And yes, I'm aware that last route might land on me; put it in anyway if that's where the philosophy points. One of the five has to be the question that catches the overnight-retraining case, where behavior changed without any decision anyone made. Another has to honestly flag where the procedure runs out and we're making a values call, not applying a rule. Don't soften it — if some incidents genuinely have no clean owner, the procedure should say so out loud instead of laundering it through a fake finding.
