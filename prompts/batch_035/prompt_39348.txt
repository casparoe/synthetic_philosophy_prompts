I've been reading up on how two countries handled basically the same problem in almost opposite ways, and I'm trying to figure out what each approach actually presupposes philosophically. In Canada, after the near-miss of the 1995 Quebec referendum, the Supreme Court's 1998 reference said secession would require a clear majority on a clear question, and that this would trigger only an obligation to negotiate, not a right to secede; Parliament then passed the Clarity Act in 2000, which gives the House of Commons a say on whether a question counts as clear and refuses to specify any numeric threshold for the majority. In the UK, by contrast, the 2012 Edinburgh Agreement just let Scotland run a single-question referendum on simple majority, on a question the Scottish government itself had a hand in shaping, with the UK government agreeing in advance to respect the result -- 50% plus one was enough in principle, no clarity test, no negotiation threshold. So one system builds in gatekeeping and ambiguity about how big the Yes has to be, and the other treats a bare majority on a straightforward question as enough.

What theory of self-determination does each of these practices presuppose, if any? I keep going back and forth. On one reading the Clarity Act only makes sense if you assume something like the remedial-rights view -- that there's no general right to secede, only a right triggered by serious injustice or exclusion, and since Quebec wasn't obviously in that position, all the fudge factors (clear question, clear majority, obligation to negotiate rather than to secede) are ways of raising the bar without ever saying so out loud. On another reading it presupposes almost the opposite: a purely plebiscitary view where anyone who'd secede from Canada has to really mean it, precisely because the majority's will does matter so much. And the Edinburgh Agreement looks like the clean plebiscitary case, but even there the 2014 "No" side spent the campaign saying it was a once-in-a-generation decision and the UK later refused a rerun, which seems to smuggle the supermajority idea back in through the timing rather than the threshold. Plus Canada lowered the voting age for neither while the UK let 16 and 17 year olds vote in 2014 -- do the two systems even agree on who the relevant "people" is?

So here's what I actually want: reconstruct the implicit theory of self-determination in each practice, say where each is underdetermined (i.e., what different theories of the right to secede would license the same practice), and tell me whether the apparent inconsistency between the two is real or just reflects a deeper shared commitment -- maybe something like an agreement that legitimacy comes from negotiation between two majorities rather than from any unilateral democratic act. And if you think one of these practices is quietly incoherent, I'd want to know what specifically would have to change to make it coherent. No need to survey the literature on secession, just the conceptual reconstruction of these two cases.
