Write a parody of a printed self-help advice column, roughly 600 words, plus a short endnote described below. The column is called "Equipoise," and its author writes under the byline Dr. Equipoise, a life coach whose serenity is exceeded only by his refusal to rush you. The piece should be formatted as three or four reader letters with replies, with noms de plume in the genre.

Dr. Equipoise's doctrine, stated and restated in his replies: a rational choice requires a reason that distinguishes the chosen option from its rivals; when the options are perfectly balanced, taking either would be arbitrary; arbitrariness is the one thing a rational agent must never allow; therefore, when you cannot discriminate, you must wait — for new information, a changed circumstance, some asymmetry to reveal itself. He takes this not as a liability of his method but as its crowning virtue.

Background you should draw on rather than invent (the model reading this has no web access, so everything needed is here): the doctrine is Buridan's-ass logic — the medieval thought experiment in which an ass placed exactly between two equally attractive bales of hay, with no reason to prefer either, is said to starve. The standard rejoinder, which your column exists to ironize, is that an agent can have a disjunctive reason — a reason to take either pile rather than neither — in which case an arbitrary tie-break is fully rational, and refusing to break the tie is not wisdom but the one genuinely irrational option on the table. A second rejoinder the coach must eventually face: doing nothing is itself a choice with consequences, so "waiting" does not stand outside the comparison; it is a third option in it, and usually the worst.

The satire should come from the doctrine's actual consequences, not invented absurdities. Each piece of advice must follow strictly from the coach's principle, and the letters should expose its costs one at a time, escalating: a reader torn between two job offers that differ by nothing she can name; a reader who flipped a coin and is gently scolded for delegating reason to brass — the coin's bias has chosen, not her; a reader whose spouse has stopped asking where she wants to eat; and a final letter from someone who has followed the coach's advice for months on two identical options and reports concrete, mounting harm from the waiting itself. The coach must never knowingly violate his principle, and he should never wink. But he should also not be a fool: at least one reply should contain advice that is genuinely reasonable — e.g., when two options differ only in ways you haven't investigated, gathering information really is the right move — so the satire lands on the doctrine rather than on the coach's stupidity. In the final reply, let him give his best available answer, and let the reader judge whether it saves him; his last line should be the tell, not a confession.

The reader should be able to tell what is being ironized — the insistence that rational choice requires a discriminating reason — without the author ever saying so.

After the column, append an endnote of 100–150 words, plainly written, out of character, in which you (a) flag where the piece takes liberties with the ideas — for instance, which reading of the Buridan scenario it leans on, and whether the coach's position is really held by anyone or is a composite of the "reasons must discriminate" intuition; and (b) say how confident you are in your handling of the philosophy and single out the point you are least sure of.
