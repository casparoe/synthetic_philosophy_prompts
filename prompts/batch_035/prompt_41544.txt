I'm a philosophy prof who's been threatening to write a paper on higher-order evidence for about a decade now, and this sabbatical I'm finally doing it. Before I commit to a theory I want the spec, not a survey. So: write me the desiderata — what any adequate account of how evidence *about your own epistemic situation* bears on belief has to deliver.

The cases it must handle, and I want a real mix here:

- the weather forecaster who gets calibration stats on her own past forecasts;
- peer disagreement where you learn that an epistemic peer, looking at the same data, came to the opposite conclusion;
- the "you've had two drinks / you've been awake for 30 hours" case, where the evidence is about your current processing, not the world;
- the doctor who learns that clinicians overdiagnose precisely the condition she just diagnosed, thirty seconds after diagnosing it;
- and the nasty self-undermining one: evidence that the very faculty you'd use to evaluate this evidence is off — where following the advice seems to require the reliability the advice denies.

The distinctions it can't collapse: between defeating p and defeating "my evidence supports p"; between higher-order evidence being decisive versus merely adding weight; between higher-order evidence you can actually act on and advice that's self-defeating when taken seriously ("don't trust your judgment" — okay, including that judgment?).

And the part I most want to see: which pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied. My own hunch is that "no akrasia" (never believe p while believing your evidence doesn't support p) and "higher-order deference" (when you learn your first-order handling is off, back off proportionally) come apart in the self-undermining cases, and if you agree, tell me which one you'd keep and what giving up the other costs. Also flag which of the requirements are genuine and which are just camp prejudices masquerading as adequacy conditions — I've started to suspect "transparency," the idea that if you know, you know that you know, is one of these, but argue it rather than taking my word.

Then, briefly, score the main contenders against the list: the deflationary view that higher-order evidence is just more first-order evidence in disguise, the defeat-only view on which it can only ever undercut and never positively support, and the views that make akrasia sometimes rational. Keep that part short — the desiderata are the point, and if some subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable, that's a result in itself and I want it stated plainly. No reading lists, no citations, I know the lit. It's the structure of the requirements I can't get straight in my own head.
