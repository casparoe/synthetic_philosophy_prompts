A quiet doctrine runs through the private law of most jurisdictions: monetary obligations are nominal. A lender who advances a hundred units under a thirty-year bond is owed a hundred units at maturity, whatever those units will then purchase; absent an express indexation clause, courts treat a change in the currency's purchasing power as legally irrelevant, and a debtor who repays in full discharges the debt even if the creditor can then buy only a fraction of what was lent. Episodes of hyperinflation have occasionally forced legislatures to revalue debts after the fact, but these are treated as extraordinary interventions against the background rule, not applications of it. The striking thing is that the same legal orders build machinery on the opposite assumption everywhere else: tax brackets and public benefits are indexed to a price level, awards for lost future earnings are discounted and adjusted as though the unit's value drifts predictably, and statutes routinely fix amounts in the price level of a stated base year. So the law seems to treat money as a fixed measure when it denominates debts, and as a variable quantity when it taxes, compensates, and appropriates. What theory of money does the nominalist rule presuppose?

Aristotle gives us the fork. In the Nicomachean Ethics treatment of exchange, he argues that dissimilar goods — his example asks how many beds a house is worth — become commensurable only through money, which serves as the measure that makes exchange proportionate; and he ties money to the community itself, holding that need is what holds association together and that money is its conventional representative, existing by agreement rather than by nature and alterable at will. Yet the same discussion concedes that money's own value is not constant, only comparatively steadier than that of other things, which threatens the measure function — a yardstick that changes length is a strange yardstick. In the Politics, he adds that exchange undertaken to meet need is natural, while the accumulation of money for its own sake is a perversion that mistakes the measure for the good measured.

Consider three candidate theories the nominalist rule might rest on. First, a conventionist theory: since the community creates the measure, it may simply stipulate the debt's content, and a hundred units are by definition dischargeable by a hundred units — the yardstick cannot shrink relative to itself. Second, a commodity theory: money is itself one good among others of variable value, so promising a hundred units is like promising a hundred bushels of wheat — delivery in kind extinguishes the obligation, and no one owes the counterparty what the wheat later trades for. Third, a state-creature theory: courts merely enforce obligations in the sovereign's unit, and the unit's meaning over time is for the sovereign, not the judiciary, to determine. For each candidate, say whether the nominalist rule strictly presupposes it, merely fits it, or is compatible with it only under strain; say where the doctrine is underdetermined, such that several theories would license it; and say whether the same system's indexation practices make the overall pattern incoherent, or whether nominalism and indexation can instead be traced to one underlying theory applied in different domains. Then take the Aristotelian question directly: given his twin claims that money is a conventional measure the community may alter at will and that a drifting measure fails as a measure, does the nominalist rule vindicate the first claim or betray the second — and would a consistent Aristotelian prefer indexed obligations on the ground that they preserve the measure function a debt presupposes, or instead prefer nominalism on the ground that the community's stipulation is the whole content of the debt? Finally, tell me what would have to change in legal practice if the presupposed theory were replaced by the commodity view or by a conventionist view with a real-value default — which doctrines, standard clauses, and forms of contracting would need rewriting — and whether the existing system, with nominal debts but indexed taxes, is best read as silently running two theories of money at once.
