I'd like your credence, with the reasoning decomposed, for the claim that William Kingdown Clifford's evidentialist position in his ethics-of-belief debate with William James is fundamentally correct — that is, that it is always wrong, in all circumstances, for anyone to believe anything on evidence insufficient to warrant it, as against James's contention that when a live, forced, and momentous option is genuinely undecidable on the evidence, we are entitled to let our passional nature decide.

I don't want a survey of the literature followed by "reasonable people disagree." I want a number, and I want it built up from sub-questions you assign rough probabilities to. Here is the decomposition I'd like you to use, amended or extended as you see fit, with one line of justification per term:

1. That there is a coherent distinction between believing on sufficient evidence and believing on insufficient evidence, rather than a continuum of warrant with no principled threshold — the sense in which Clifford's rule needs a notion of "sufficiency" that may not exist.
2. That belief, as a mental state, is ever under the direct control of the will in the way an ethics of belief presupposes — the doxastic voluntarism question — and if it is not, whether this merely relabels Clifford's duty as a duty about belief-forming practices and inquiry habits without changing its substance.
3. That Clifford is right that belief formation has a moral dimension at all, as opposed to being only instrumentally evaluable — whether the shipowner example, where a negligent belief kills passengers, actually shows that the wrongness lies in the belief itself rather than in the downstream negligence and action.
4. That James's three conditions — live, forced, and momentous options — succeed in carving out a genuine exception rather than merely describing cases where the evidence is merely balanced as opposed to absent, and whether the religious faith James defended actually satisfies his own conditions.
5. That a pragmatic consideration about the benefits of believing — self-fulfilling confidence, hope sustaining action, trust enabling cooperation — can legitimately raise the credence one is entitled to hold, or whether it merely gives us a reason to want a belief we still shouldn't hold.

Combine the terms and give me the overall number. Then tell me which single term the final credence is most sensitive to, which of the two positions that term most favors, and what consideration — philosophical or empirical — would move that term the most. If you think my decomposition smuggles in an assumption that Clifford or James would reject, flag it and say whether it changes your number.
