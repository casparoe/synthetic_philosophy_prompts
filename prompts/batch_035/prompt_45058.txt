I'm 71, a retired analytical chemist, and over the past year I've gotten into philosophy of mind after my wife finally put her foot down about me explaining chromatography at dinner. Now I have a problem I can't stop chewing on, and it's about my breakfast, of all things.

For something like 40 years I've drunk the same style of roast — same roaster for the last 15, same beans, same grind, same machine. I even keep a brew log, because of course I do. Somewhere in my late 50s the coffee started tasting flatter to me, and my first reaction was the scientist's: either the beans drifted (supply chains change, cupping scores wobble) or my taste buds are degrading — receptors die off with age, that's documented. Fine, I thought, testable-ish. Then I tried to actually pin down which it was and hit a wall.

Here's the wall. The only instrument I have for detecting the taste is my own tongue plus whatever my brain does downstream. If the beans drifted, I'd notice a change relative to my memory of earlier cups. If my receptors aged, the experience itself shifts and I'd notice... a change relative to my memory of earlier cups. Same signal! And then a third possibility occured to me that's worse: maybe the experience shifted gradually years back, and my memory of how it used to taste drifted along with it, so the comparison I keep running isn't anchored to anything. In the lab, when I calibrate an instrument, I compare it to an external standard. Here the detector is also the archive. There is no external standard.

And then the book I read last month has me half-convinced the question isn't even real. It's an essay by a well-known American philosopher of mind whose whole shtick, as far as I could tell, is that private ineffable "seems to me" properties are a confused posit — because any real difference in how something seems to you has to show up in some judgment or report or reaction of yours, and if none of your judgments or reports have changed, there's nothing left for the difference to be. My judgments did change (I now say the coffee tastes flatter), so maybe that's all there is to it. But that feels like it proves too much, and I can't say exactly why.

So, advice, from someone who spent a career on measurement: (a) is there a fact of the matter about whether my coffee tastes to me now the way it did at 40 — the experience itself, not the beans? (b) If yes, is it knowable, even in princple, by anything I could do — triangle tests with my wife mixing unlabeled cups, imaging, digging through my brew log for judged descriptions that stayed constant while the world and my tongue moved? Or (c) if the philosopher's right and there's no fact, then what exactly is it that changed when I say the coffee tastes flatter now, and is that really a defensible place to end up?

I'm used to error bars, so please say how confident you are in each part of your answer and single out the step you're least sure of. I'd much rather have a hedged honest answer than a confident hand-wave.
