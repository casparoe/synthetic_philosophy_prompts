I'm giving a talk to a political theory workshop defending Simone Weil's argument for abolishing political parties -- the wartime essay where she defines a party by three marks: a machine for generating collective passion, an organization built to exert collective pressure on the thought of each member, and a thing whose real object is always its own survival and growth, so that even being in a party that happens to be right still degrades your thinking. My claim is that she's structurally right about what party membership does to judgment, and that this doesn't depend on her mysticism or on the mood of the French defeat. The room is maybe twenty grad students and faculty: two comparativists who think modern democracy is unthinkable without parties (one of them quotes Schattschneider every chance he gets), a deliberative democrat who'll bristle at her ideal of the lone unmediated citizen, a Schmittian who'll say it's naive institutional design, and a bunch of agnostics. Prep me for the Q&A: the objections I'll actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they'd do, phrased the way that room would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the one I'm hoping nobody asks -- probably something about what organizes elections once parties are gone, but you tell me.
