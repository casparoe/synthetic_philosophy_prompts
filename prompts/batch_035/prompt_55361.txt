I'm a philosopher with a background in decision theory and formal logic, about to begin a three-year solo project on interpersonal comparisons of utility, and I have to decide within the next few months which problems to attack first. What I want from you is not a survey of the literature but a defended ordering of the open questions, so that I can see which of them deserves the first year.

Here are the candidate problems as I currently carve them up; reorganize them if you think my carving is wrong, but then say why.

1. The dimension problem. It's often assumed comparability is one thing, but it plausibly comes in fragments: comparing units (my utils are commensurable with yours), comparing levels (whose life is going better overall), comparing differences (this change helps you more than it hurts me, even if no total is comparable), and even sign-only comparisons (this policy is net positive, all else unknowable). Which fragments does actual practice — welfare economics, cost-benefit analysis, population ethics — genuinely require, and which fragment is sufficient for which practice?

2. The conventionality problem. Are interpersonal comparisons something we could in principle discover, made true by facts about the two minds; or something we institute, more like choosing a zero meridian than like measuring a distance? If the latter, it's unclear what normative force an aggregation rule built on such comparisons could have — it would inherit our stipulation rather than constrain it.

3. The construction problem. Aggregation arguments that derive a social welfare function from individual preferences under uncertainty about one's own identity are often accused of smuggling comparability in through an impersonal observer's utility function, assuming at the construction stage exactly what the theorem was supposed to output. Can a social observer be built from strictly ordinal, intrapersonal materials, or does every such construction quietly presuppose the conclusion?

4. The calibration problem. Suppose comparability facts exist. What observable evidence — behavior, verbal report, physiology, choice under risk — could in principle fix a cross-person scale, and is the resulting scale stable enough to be used?

For each question you prioritize, tell me: what makes it upstream of the others; whether it's tractable with formal-philosophical methods as opposed to empirical psychology, given that I have no lab; and what finding or argument, produced by someone else within the next two years, would move it up or down your list. I'm equally interested in the decoys: which of these four, if settled tomorrow, would change almost nothing downstream, and why. Finally, if you think there's a fifth open problem in this area more important than all four, name the single best candidate and make the case — don't give me a list of honorable mentions.
