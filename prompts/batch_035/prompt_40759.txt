I'm a charge nurse at a mid-sized regional hospital, and I've been asked to lead the forty-five-minute ethics component of next month's orientation for incoming nursing staff. The topic is supererogation. It became personally relevant last winter: during our unit's flood evacuation, two colleagues volunteered to stay with the patients who couldn't be moved until the water was contained, and when administration announced they would receive a formal commendation, a coworker objected that calling them heroes quietly brands everyone who went home as having fallen short. I want a plan for the session rather than the session itself. Specifically: the order the pieces must go in — I assume the distinction between what is obligatory and what is merely permissible has to be in place before the category of acts that are good without being required can be introduced, but correct me if I have that backwards — and whether our evacuation incident is the right carrying example at all, given that the audience will have heard about it through the grapevine and some of them may feel implicated either way; if not, what the example should contain and avoid. I'd also like the misconceptions new nurses predictably bring and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off — I suspect the biggest is conflating supererogation with going beyond one's job description, which is a staffing matter rather than a moral one, and I worry some will reason that if an act is genuinely better to do, it can't fail to be obligatory. Finally, one question I could ask at the end that would reveal whether they've understood the concept rather than just been moved by the story, and what I should openly decline to cover in forty-five minutes — for instance, whether the notion even survives a thoroughgoing consequentialism — and how to say so.
