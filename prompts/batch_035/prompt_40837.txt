A friend and I spent an evening arguing about Dennett's picture of the self, and he ended it by handing me the following reasoning, which he claims is fatal to the picture. I think it proves too much, but I can't locate the flaw. As a philosopher of mind familiar with Dennett's work, please think through each premise carefully before answering.

(1) Dennett holds that the self is not a concrete entity: it is an abstraction we posit to organize the narrative the brain spins about itself, on the model of a center of gravity — a useful posit about a distribution rather than an extra piece of stuff.
(2) An abstraction of this kind cannot itself be harmed: there is no such thing as damaging a center of gravity, only rearranging the masses it describes.
(3) When someone is gaslit, indoctrinated, or otherwise has her self-conception deliberately rewritten, what changes is only the content of the narrative — the distribution, as it were — and the posit adjusts to the new story, just as a center of gravity readjusts when the masses move.
(4) So on Dennett's view, such manipulation cannot be a harm to the self; at most it disturbs the organism, as any physical shock would.
(5) But deliberately rewriting a person's self-conception against her will is a paradigmatic harm to that person — indeed a deeper harm than a bruise, since it damages who she is.
(6) Therefore Dennett's view is false: there must be a concrete self that can be harmed, over and above the body and its narrative.

He then presses the point: if (2) stands, death is not bad for the one who dies either — the narrative simply stops — and no one can now wrong her future self by present recklessness. He takes these as a reductio of the whole picture. My suspicion is that something slips between (2) and (4): "cannot be harmed" seems to mean one thing in (2), where it concerns abstracta, and something else in (4), where it concerns what can be wronged. But I can't pin it down, and I'd much rather keep (5) than accept (6).

Tell me which premise or inference to reject, explain why it looked fine in the first place, and say what rejecting it costs — both for Dennett's view and for our ordinary thinking about persons. Take a definite position and defend it; don't lay out the options and leave the verdict to me.
