I teach political philosophy, and this term I'm building the unit on Nozick around the animals discussion in chapter 3 of Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974) — the passage often excerpted on its own, only a handful of pages, in which he tests his theory of side constraints against beings to whom we normally don't extend them. My plan is to set an exam question that gives students a list of Nozick's own rulings and asks them to predict his ruling on new cases he never considers. But the more I work with the section, the more I suspect it is consistent with more than one underlying principle, and I don't want to set a question with a single official answer if the track record doesn't determine one. Help me work out what it does determine.

Here is the track record as I read the section. Since my students will have the text only through this summary, please reason from this list, not from anything you remember about Nozick elsewhere:

- His opening test case is finger-snapping: "If you felt like snapping your fingers, perhaps to the beat of some music, and you knew that by some strange causal connection your snapping your fingers would cause 10,000 contented, unowned cows to die after great pain and suffering, or even painlessly and instantaneously, would it be perfectly all right to snap your fingers?" He takes the answer to be obviously no: some purposes fail to entitle us to impose great costs on animals, painlessly or not.
- He assumes, without arguing for it, that hunting and killing animals merely for the fun of it is wrong.
- He runs a battery of parallels against the meat-eater: swinging a baseball bat whose side effect crushes a cow's skull is not made all right by the extra pleasure of swinging it rather than doing toe-touches; killing an animal to obtain a bone for a marginally better bat is not made all right by the extra pleasure that bat would give; and he asks what principle the meat-eater can produce that allows killing for gustatory pleasure but not these. He leaves the challenge as an open demand for a principle rather than as an argument with a stated conclusion.
- He is unpersuaded that the wrong is to be routed through brutalization — the claim that harming animals spills over into how we treat persons — and doubts that any such spillover should be expected among people capable of drawing the line and acting on the distinction.
- The constructive suggestion he floats is a two-level structure: animals' interests may be aggregated and traded off, including for the gain of other animals and of persons, while persons remain protected by side constraints and are never used against their will for anyone's gain — the structure everyone remembers as utilitarian treatment of animals, Kantian treatment of persons. He offers it explicitly as a hypothesis to be tested, not as a result.
- He considers, and declines to rest anything on, the argument that eating animals is vindicated by the fact that it brings more of them into existence than would otherwise live.

Here are the new cases I'm thinking of putting on the exam:

(1) A cosmetics firm drips a mildly irritating compound into rabbits' eyes so that consumers can have a marginally better shampoo.
(2) A wildlife agency lethally culls one deer from each overpopulated herd, with instant kills, to prevent the remainder from starving slowly through the winter — a case in which the gain accrues entirely to other animals.
(3) A laboratory proposes the same painful vaccine trial on two groups: langur monkeys, and a small troop of bonobos, one of whom has been language-trained since infancy and reportedly passes mirror self-recognition.
(4) A hiker wants to know whether a Nozickian position permits eating the flesh of an animal found already dead on the road, given that no killing and no suffering is involved.

What I want from you is, first, a statement of the candidate rules that fit the track record — I can see at least the literal two-level reading, on which case (2) goes through by simple aggregation, and a weaker reading on which animals have genuine but limited constraints that only substantial interests can override; there may be a third on which constraint strength scales with cognitive capacity, which would put the bonobos and the langurs in different moral universes. Second, a verdict for each of the four cases under each rule, and your best single prediction of what consistency with the section requires, with your reasons. Third, the one further case, not on my list, that would most sharply discriminate among the candidate rules — the one I should hold in reserve for the follow-up question.

Two requests about how you handle the assignment. If you think my framing is confused — if, for instance, the section is better read as a set of exploratory pressures than as a body of rulings from which a principle can be extracted, so that the exam question I want to set is a bad exam question — tell me that plainly rather than adapting the material to my format. And if you believe any item on my list misstates what Nozick actually says, flag it and say what the text says instead; I would rather correct the handout than have you make my errors consistent.
