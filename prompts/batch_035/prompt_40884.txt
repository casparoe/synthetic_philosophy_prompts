I moderate the Q&A after the keynote at an annual philosophy-of-mind workshop. This year's speaker is a mathematical physicist in the tradition of the Lucas–Penrose argument — call him Dr. V. — whose talk runs the Gödelian analogy upward: from self-referential formulas to self-aware minds. His thesis is that the very technique Gödel used to construct a sentence that says of itself that it is unprovable, together with our ability to see that such a sentence is true, shows that the mind is not a formal system, and that consciousness is where the self-reference bottoms out. Draft a sequence of eight to ten questions for the Q&A. I want the sequence to do three things, each keyed to the actual content of his position rather than biography.

First, press on the argument itself: he assumes the formal system is consistent when he claims to see that its Gödel sentence is true, and he owes us a story about whether the mind's grasp of consistency is any less fallible than the system's. Include a question about whether an inconsistent mind could be a machine after all — and a follow-up keyed to his likeliest reply, that mathematical insight is somehow self-certifying. Second, press on the step from knowing that if F is consistent then G(F) is true to being a non-mechanical thinker: include a question about the standard regress worry, namely that seeing the Gödel sentence's truth relative to a consistency assumption just characterizes another, stronger formal system, so that the argument chases its tail. Add a follow-up on what he says to the point that a machine could in principle be told it is system F without that telling ever settling anything.

Third, press on the borrowed analogy, which is where I most want blood: his framework leans on the prestige of Gödel, Escher, Bach, but Hofstadter's strange loops were introduced to show how a mechanical substrate could come to carry a self-model — the self-reference was meant to be implementable, not an escape hatch from implementation. Include a question forcing him to say precisely where his use of the analogy parts company with that, and what independent work the self-reference is doing once anti-mechanism is already in place. Also include one question on why a theorem about what arithmetic can prove was ever supposed to bear on phenomenal consciousness at all.

End with a single question about what evidence, if any, could count against his view. He has a habit of answering every objection by saying no formal system can do what he just did in grasping the point, and I want a final question that this move cannot answer.
