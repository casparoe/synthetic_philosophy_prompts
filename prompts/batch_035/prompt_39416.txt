Two friends of mine who work at a small climate-tech startup got into it at dinner about whether their company will survive the next five years, and it turned into a whole stats fight I want you to referee. For context: it's a seed-stage startup making materials for batteries, roughly 20 employees, in Scandinavia.

The exchange went like this:

(1) Lars said anyone guessing whether they'll survive should start with the base rate: most seed-stage startups die within five years, so absent special info, you should put the probability of survival well under 20%. Anything else is just cheering for your own team. Marta said that base rate is useless because it lumps together every seed-stage company ever -- a biotech burning cash on trials for 12 years and a friend's dog-walking app are apparently in the same bucket as a battery-materials company with actual revenue. The base rate you use smuggles in a whole thesis about what matters.

(2) Lars said fine, so restrict the class: seed-stage hardware startups. Marta said that's worse, not better -- the class is smaller, so the rate is noisier, and you had to make a judgment call to draw that boundary in the first place. Why hardware and not "hardware in energy storage"? Why Scandinavia in or out? Every choice of class changes the number and there's no rule that says which one is right.

(3) Lars said there obviously is a rule: use the most specific class where you still have decent statistics -- specific enough to be informative, wide enough to have a real sample. Marta said that's a rule about comfort, not truth: it tells you when to stop narrowing because you get scared of small n, and "when the stats get too thin" is exactly the kind of arbitrary line she was complaining about. She said the honest answer is you should use all the classes at once, weighted somehow, which nobody knows how to do.

(4) Lars came back with what he thought was the killer move: if there's no fact about the right reference class, then probability itself is doing nothing here, and any number you quote -- 5%, 60% -- is theater. So the base-rate people are at least honest and everyone else is making it up. Marta said no, her point wasn't that probabilities don't exist, it was that they don't come for free from the class alone: Lars's under-20% number also embeds choices, he just doesn't notice his choices because the class is big. She said you reason forward from specifics too -- who their customers are, what the lab results look like -- and that's not theater, it's just a different kind of evidence that doesn't reduce to a frequency.

They basically ran out of steam around there and asked me what I thought and I honestly couldn't say. Can you go through their exchange move by move -- rate each of the four moves as clearly wrong, mostly wrong, a wash, mostly right, or clearly right, with brief reasons? And then tell me who's more right overall, and what (if anything) both of them are missing. If it matters for your verdict, assume the specific numbers I gave are right, i.e. most seed startups do die within 5 years and the survival rate for various narrower classes is genuinely all over the place depending on how you slice it.
