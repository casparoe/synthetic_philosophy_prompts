My friend has opinions about which sentences are true, false, or neither, and we've been keeping score. Her track record: "The present King of France is bald" — she refuses to call it false, says it misfires in some way she can't quite pin down. "The present King of France is not bald" — also not true, same misfire. But "The present King of France doesn't exist" — that one she happily calls true. Ordinary sentences with real referents ("the tallest building in the world is in Dubai") she treats as plain true or false, no fuss.

Next week we're arguing about "Either the present King of France is bald or Ottawa is the capital of Canada," and also "If the present King of France is bald, then he's older than Macron." Predict her verdicts. I'm less interested in the answers than in the rule she seems to be running (Russell-style paraphrase? a presupposition theory with gaps? something in between?), the other rules that fit her earlier rulings just as well, and what each of those rules says about the new sentences. Also give me your best guess with reasons, and name a further case that would best split the candidate rules apart. One thing I want from you: keep clear what's actually settled in this debate versus what's genuinely contested among people who know the literature — like how presuppositions behave under conditionals and disjunctions — don't hand me one theory's line as if its the consensus.
