A friend of mine has been working through Hofstadter and has taken to ruling, case by case, on whether an agent has "jumped out of the system" — JOOTSed, in Hofstadter's coinage. I'd like your predictions about how she'll rule on some new cases. Please approach this as a professional philosopher of mind with a strong grasp of Gödel, Escher, Bach: brainstorm the candidate rules before committing, reason carefully, and be explicit about where her track record leaves things genuinely open.

Background for the cases: the MIU system starts from the string MI and lets you produce new strings by four rules — append U to any string ending in I; replace any string Mx by Mxx; replace any III with U; delete any UU. The puzzle asks whether MU is reachable, and the answer, available only from outside the system, is no: the number of I's starts at 1 and, since doubling it and subtracting 3 from it both preserve non-divisibility by 3, can never become divisible by 3, whereas MU has zero I's. Hofstadter's moral is that no amount of cleverness inside the rules can ever tell you this; you have to jump out.

Her verdicts so far, on the question "did X jump out of the system?":
1. A person who grinds through derivations under the rules and then steps back and notices the invariant: yes, a genuine jump.
2. Gödel's construction, where a formal system is made to encode statements about its own syntax via Gödel numbering: no — she calls it a strange loop rather than a jump, insisting the system never rises above itself, and that any real jumping was done by Gödel from outside.
3. A chess engine that can only ever search for better moves, versus a human player who, far behind, decides the game is a draw and plays for it: the human, yes; the engine, no.
4. A child who can't do long multiplication in her head but can do it on paper: no — paper extends working memory but adds no new level.

The new cases I want predictions on:
(a) a large language model — the very sort of system being asked this question — that, asked to derive MU, instead explains the invariant and concludes MU is unreachable;
(b) a person who never leaves the rules but simply stops asking how to reach MU and starts asking which strings are reachable at all, and thereby stumbles toward the invariant — has she jumped out at the moment she changed the question, before any new mathematics enters?
(c) a chess engine with a hard-coded clause that offers a draw whenever its evaluation falls below a fixed threshold — does the engine jump out, or do its programmers, or neither?

I care less about the answers themselves than about the underlying analysis: what rules fit all four of her earlier verdicts equally well, where do those rules diverge on (a), (b), and (c), what is your best guess for each case and why, and what further case would most cleanly separate the competing rules?
