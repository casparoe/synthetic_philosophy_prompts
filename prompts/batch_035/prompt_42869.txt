I teach an undergraduate seminar in social epistemology, and next week we pair Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men with the Condorcet jury theorem. I'd like a serious critical reading of the film through the theorem -- one that takes both the film and the mathematics seriously -- which I can circulate as a discussion document beforehand.

The obvious tension: on the first ballot the jury votes eleven to one for conviction, and if the defendant is innocent, the film opens with the majority wrong -- a direct embarrassment for a theorem according to which a majority of twelve competent, independent, sincere voters is overwhelmingly likely to be correct. But I want the mapping to be disciplined, not glib. Which of the theorem's assumptions actually fail in the jury room, and when? Presumably the first vote is not a set of independent private signals: most jurors are voting on a shared body of trial evidence, several are voting on prejudice or personal grievance (Juror 3 and his son, Juror 10's bigotry), and at least one simply wants to get to the ballgame. Distinguish carefully between failures of competence, failures of independence, and failures of sincerity, because the film furnishes different examples of each, and they do different damage to the theorem -- corruption through a common cause like prejudice is not the same thing as correlated errors from common evidence, and I'd like the reading to keep those apart.

Then the harder question: deliberation. Juror 8 does not outvote anyone; he converts them, sequentially, by reenacting the old man's walk, producing an identical knife, and getting a juror to admit the witness couldn't have seen well without her glasses. Every conversion makes the next vote more dependent on the last, so the film seems to show deliberation dissolving exactly the independence the theorem needs -- and yet the verdict improves. Is the film an argument that aggregation and deliberation are substitutes, or that talk is how a jury escapes the correlated errors it started with? Relatedly, the film vindicates not majority rule but the unanimity requirement: it is the rule requiring every vote that gives a single dissenting juror his leverage, and one natural Condorcetian reply to correlated error is to raise the decision threshold. Does the film dramatize a better case for supermajority rules than the formal literature usually makes?

Finally, be honest about where the film resists the reading. The theorem is truth-tracking, and the film never establishes innocence: the jury's final verdict is not guilty only because of reasonable doubt. Competence in the theorem is the probability of a correct judgment, and it is not obvious the film's jurors ever make one. Say what the theorem can and cannot say about what the jury did, and end by telling me whether the film teaches something about epistemic democracy that the formal results do not. Indicate which three or four scenes I should send students to with the theorem in hand.
