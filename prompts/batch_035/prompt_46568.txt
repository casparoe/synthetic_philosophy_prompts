For a philosophy of mind reading group I've been asked to write up my position on the extended mind so the others can poke at it, and before I circulate it I'd like to know whether it hangs together. Here is the package. First, I accept the constitutive version of Clark and Chalmers's claim: Otto's notebook entries are among Otto's dispositional beliefs, not merely causes or inputs of his beliefs, and I take the parity argument to be what establishes this — when an external process guides behavior the way an internal one would, it thereby counts as part of the subject's cognition. Second, I hold that phenomenal consciousness does not extend: whatever notebooks and smartphones contribute, conscious experience supervenes on what goes on inside the organism, and I have no intention of giving that up. Third, I hold that beliefs need not be conscious to be beliefs — most of mine are dispositional, occurrently unconscious, and some I cannot currently call to mind at all — and this seems to me to reconcile the first two commitments. Fourth, I think cognitive science as actually practiced, individuating memory and attention over brain-internal mechanisms, is not fundamentally mistaken about its subject matter. Fifth, I want to apply the same parity reasoning to the social case: my partner manages our shared social calendar the way Otto's notebook manages his museum routes, and I am inclined to say her entries are part of my mind as well. Sixth, I hold that what makes a piece of information one of my beliefs, rather than something I merely use, is that it is subject to my epistemic agency: I can in principle inspect it, endorse or revise it in light of evidence, and answer for it. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what would have to give in each case? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most — I honestly do not know where the problem lies. Write your response as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one would in an email or a letter.
