I'm on the moderation council of a large online gaming community, and we're drafting a policy on slurs. The dispute that stalled our last meeting centered on the slogan "it's just a word." One member used it to argue that a flat word-list ban is wrongheaded: a slur that appears in a joke, in a quotation, or in a report of someone else's harassment carries nothing in itself, on his view — whatever offense occurs comes from what a speaker is doing with the word, and our existing harassment rules already cover that. Another member pushed back just as hard, saying that the whole reason slurs are different from ordinary insults is precisely that the word itself does the damage, whoever says it and however it's framed. I have to write the first draft of the policy, and I've realized I don't actually know what "it's just a word" commits anyone to.

Could you lay out, as a menu I can bring to our next meeting, the distinct precise theses that slogan could be expressing in this dispute? I imagine they run from the trivially true (slurs are, after all, words) through more substantive positions about where a slur's derogatory content actually lives — whether it's part of the word's literal, truth-conditional meaning; whether it works through pragmatics, so that the same word can be inert in one mouth or context and venomous in another; whether its sting comes from speakers' attitudes rather than from language at all; and whether social facts about who is entitled to use it are part of the story. For each reading, I'd like your verdict: is it plausible, does it support a word-list ban or a context-sensitive rule, and — crucially — does it actually support my colleague's move of invoking the slogan to settle the question? I want to be able to say, when we reconvene, not just which policy I favor but exactly which claim about language the policy rests on and which versions of his slogan would, if true, genuinely undermine it.
