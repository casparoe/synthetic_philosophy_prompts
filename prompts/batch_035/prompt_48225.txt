Write an essay on whether the doing/allowing distinction can carry the moral weight it is standardly given in the killing versus letting die debate, in light of what I think of as the problem of the many omissions — a problem I have almost never seen engaged in the effective-altruism and rationalist spaces I've read for the past fifteen years, where the Singer-style pond case is treated as a trump card and the only resistance ever offered is about demandingness.

The worry: when an agent allows a harm, there seems to be no fact about which omission is the one that constitutes the allowing. The doctor who lets a patient die by withholding a treatment has, at the same time, failed to consult a colleague, failed to try an off-label drug, failed to invent a better drug earlier, and failed to have chosen a different specialty altogether; the bystander who lets the drowning child die has failed to wade in, failed to call for help, failed to have learned to swim faster, and failed to have walked a different route. These candidate omissions differ wildly in cost to the agent, probability of success, and temporal and causal proximity, and any of them could be nominated as the omission by which the harm was allowed. If there is no principled way to individuate the relevant omission, it is unclear whether doing versus allowing is one distinction or indefinitely many, and unclear that it has the stable moral significance claimed for it in discussions of euthanasia, foreign aid, and the bad Samaritan. The usual rescue — that only omissions reasonably within the agent's power, or counterfactually tied to the outcome, count — looks like it is reverse-engineered from cases about which we already have firm intuitions, which is what the distinction was supposed to be explaining, not presupposing.

In the essay, consider whether this mirrors the problem of the many in the metaphysics of objects and whether any of the known responses to that problem (vagueness is real and harmless; accept many candidates and pick by convention; demand non-arbitrary criteria and accept that ordinary individuation just is interest-relative) transfer to omissions. Then reach and defend a verdict: either the doing/allowing distinction survives, together with a principled account of which omission is the morally relevant one; or the moral work we wanted the distinction to do must be relocated — to the costs imposed on the agent, to intentions, to the violation of prior claims, or to a straight comparison of outcomes. Do not use citations; name positions and arguments in your own words. Conclude by stating what consideration would most likely change your verdict, and point out the step in your own reasoning you would trust least.
