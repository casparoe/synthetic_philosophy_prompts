In December 1973, the American Psychiatric Association's Board of Trustees voted to remove homosexuality as a disorder from the DSM-II, substituting at first a narrower category for people distressed by their own orientation; a referendum of the membership upheld the removal by a modest majority, and a residual diagnosis (ego-dystonic homosexuality) survived in some form until 1987. Thomas Szasz had argued, from The Myth of Mental Illness (1961) onward, that psychiatric diagnosis is not the discovery of disease but the social and moral labeling of deviance, legitimated by medical vocabulary. Analyze the declassification episode through Szasz's framework. What does his account predict about how such a change would come about and who would prevail, and how well does the prediction fit the actual sequence -- the board vote, the referendum, the face-saving residual category that took another fourteen years to die? Where the mapping is awkward, say so. In particular, is the episode best described as a political negotiation given a scientific gloss, as Szasz would have it, or as a scientific community correcting itself on new evidence about causes and harms, and is there anything internal to the episode that could decide between those two descriptions, or does that question presuppose a distinction the episode itself cannot supply? Finally, tell me what the episode does to Szasz's theory: does the slow, quiet retirement of the residual category confirm his view that such categories are bargaining chips rather than findings, or does it show psychiatry retiring diagnoses on the kind of grounds Szasz claimed it never had?
