I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training beyond a lot of reading on planes, and I've gotten into a running argument with a colleague about why the universe's physical constants look so conveniently set for life. I've written out my actual position below, and I'd like an audit of it. Not a survey of the literature — I want to know whether my own set of commitments hangs together, and if it doesn't, where it breaks.

Here's roughly where I stand:

1. The fine-tuning of the physical constants is a genuine datum that calls for explanation. If the cosmological constant or the strength of the strong force had been even slightly different, no chemistry, no stars, no observers. That doesn't feel like luck to me, and I don't accept that pointing at it is a pseudoproblem.

2. I reject the anthropic move — the response that goes "we necessarily find ourselves in a life-permitting universe, so there's nothing to explain." To me that's like a lottery winner declaring their specific ticket wasn't improbable because somebody had to win. It's explaining away, not explaining.

3. I don't accept the multiverse as the explanation. Postulating a vast ensemble of causally disconnected, unobservable universes just to render our universe unremarkable strikes me as unfalsifiable, and unfalsifiable theories don't get my belief.

4. I do give the simulation hypothesis real credence — call it somewhere between 5 and 20 percent. And I find it attractive partly because it would explain the fine-tuning cleanly: our constants look tuned because someone chose them, the way we choose parameters for a system we're bringing up.

5. I'm a Bayesian about everything else. I don't think there's a sharp line between scientific and unscientific claims; there are just credences, priors, and likelihood ratios, and nothing gets ruled out in advance. Demarcation talk strikes me as a relic.

6. I think design inferences are legitimate in principle. If a radio telescope picked up a prime-number sequence, inferring an intelligent source would be good reasoning, and I don't see a principled rule that permits that inference in archaeology and SETI but forbids it in fundamental physics.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and how badly, which tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and, for each real one, what would have to give and what it costs me elsewhere in the list. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. If you think I've misdescribed one of my own commitments — that I believe something slightly different from what I've written, and that the real version doesn't conflict — say so explicitly rather than assuming my framing is right.
