At a graduate reading group on rational belief, two students got into a sustained disagreement about the relationship between the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and I'd like a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange.

(1) Ravi claimed the two paradoxes are fundamentally different. In the lottery case, he said, the subject has only a single, purely statistical ground for each belief: the draw is fair, a million tickets are sold, so any particular ticket loses with overwhelming probability. The paradox arises only if you equate justified outright belief with sufficiently high probability, which many philosophers reject. The preface case, by contrast, is immune to that reply: the author of a careful nonfiction book has a distinct and varied body of evidence for each individual chapter claim, so each chapter-belief is justified in the ordinary, non-statistical way -- and the author still reasonably believes, on inductive grounds about books of that kind, that at least one claim in the book is false.

(2) Meera replied that the difference Ravi draws is thinner than it looks, because the preface author's grounds for the "at least one error" belief are themselves purely statistical: he generalizes from the fallibility of comparable books. Symmetrically, she argued, the lottery believer's grounds for each ticket-loses belief are not as uniform as Ravi suggests -- once other tickets have been drawn and lost, the evidence for any remaining ticket's losing shifts ticket by ticket, so it is not one monolithic premise doing all the work in either case.

(3) Ravi conceded that the preface author's second-order belief is inductive, but said this concession strengthens rather than weakens his point: it shows the preface paradox cannot be dissolved by attacking the identification of belief with high probability, since the first-order beliefs survive that attack intact, and thus that the preface case is the deeper of the two.

(4) Meera then took a different line: perhaps the preface author does not outright believe each chapter claim at all. There is a distinction, she said, between believing a proposition and accepting it as a premise for a particular project; in writing the book, the author accepts each claim within the project, while the preface statement expresses his all-things-considered view. If that is right, the author's total state is consistent, because what he believes outright is only that the book is mostly right, not each claim in it.

(5) Ravi answered that this maneuver proves too much: the chapter assertions are made in print, under the author's name, and are thereby normatively governed just as much as the preface assertion is. If assertion requires outright belief, the author asserts each chapter claim and asserts that at least one is false, so he openly asserts an inconsistent set; if acceptance can substitute for belief, the distinction collapses for the preface statement too, and Meera's consistency is bought at the price of the author believing nothing in his own book.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, with a brief justification; then say who is more right overall; and finally identify anything of substance that both of them seem to be missing.
