I'm reading a popular science book about causality aimed at general readers. In a chapter contrasting older philosophical theories of causation with the newer tools of causal inference, it offers this summary:

"Traditional philosophers like David Lewis defined causation counterfactually: the striking of the match caused the fire because, had the match not been struck, the fire would not have occurred. The test is simply to imagine the world exactly as it is except for the cause, and check whether the effect still happens. The newer interventionist approach of Judea Pearl and James Woodward says the same thing but operationalizes it: X causes Y if you can wiggle X -- ideally by actually intervening on it -- and Y changes accordingly. Since wiggling the match's striking changes the fire, interventionism is just the old counterfactual test dressed up in mathematics, and the two theories are really one. Their shared weakness is preemption: if a backup arsonist would have struck the match had the first one not done so, then in the nearest world where the first striker doesn't strike, the fire still happens, so both theories wrongly conclude that the first striker did not cause the fire. This is why philosophers have largely abandoned these theories, and why practical people now work directly with the interventionist machinery instead."
