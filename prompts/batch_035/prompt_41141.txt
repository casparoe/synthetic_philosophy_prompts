I'm a novelist at work on a book in which Immanuel Kant — by a narrative contrivance I won't burden you with — spends a week as a guest observer in a present-day memory-care ward. The staff there practice what the caregiving literature calls therapeutic lying: telling residents with advanced dementia that their long-dead spouses are at the store, that it's morning when it's three in the afternoon, that they will be going home soon. These are falsehoods told without any prospect of gain, solely to prevent the agitation and distress that factual corrections provoke, and for some residents they seem to be the only thing that works. My Kant is the Kant of the murderer at the door — the philosopher who insisted that one may not lie even to a would-be killer asking after a fleeing innocent. I want his reaction to this ward to be credible rather than cartoonish, and I want to know what I can honestly do with him dramatically. So: what would he actually conclude about therapeutic lying? Where would his own framework give him room to maneuver — the distinction between lying and merely declining to correct, his thought that the lie wrongs the liar's own rational nature rather than only the person deceived, the question of whether someone with advanced dementia still counts as a rational agent owed the truth in his sense? And where is he simply cornered, with no move available that doesn't cost him either consistency or humanity? Finally, the question my novel turns on: is there anything about the ward itself — the severity of the condition, the absence of workable alternatives, the fact that the deceived often cannot retain or act on the truth — that would plausibly move the historical Kant, or would he leave exactly as he arrived? Please distinguish, throughout, what his writings genuinely commit him to from what is reasonable extrapolation, so I know which parts of my Kant are philosophy and which are mine.
