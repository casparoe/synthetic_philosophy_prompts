I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I'm currently reporting a feature on how AI labs increasingly grade models by inspecting their intermediate reasoning rather than only their final answers — the process-based versus outcome-based supervision debate. My editor has approved a sidebar for the piece in which I ask a counterfactual question: how would John Maynard Keynes have come down on this, had he been alive to weigh in on the evaluation of machine minds?

The conceit is not as arbitrary as it sounds. Before he was the economist of the General Theory, Keynes was a philosopher-probabilist whose 1921 Treatise on Probability argued that probability is a matter of logical relations between evidence and conclusion rather than mere frequency, allowed that some probabilities are not even numerically comparable, and introduced the idea that a probabilistic argument can be more or less well supported depending on how complete the underlying evidence is — what he called its weight. He was also famously preoccupied, as an investor and policymaker as much as a theorist, with situations of radical uncertainty in which no probability distribution is available at all and one must simply act.

So here is what I'd like you to work through, as if briefing me before I draft the sidebar. Would the early, logical-probability Keynes — with his insistence that an inference can be probable yet rest on very thin evidence — have been sympathetic to process-based supervision on the grounds that a model's outputs are observed only under conditions of severely deficient evidential weight? Would the later Keynes of fundamental uncertainty push the other way, arguing that since we can never fully validate a model's internal reasoning either, we should judge by results and accept that we are acting, as he thought investors do, under irreducible ignorance? And how would he handle what strikes me as the most Keynesian wrinkle of all: grading the process just relocates the problem, since the grader's own standards now need justification, threatening an infinite regress that his framework seems peculiarly designed to expose. Please give me a plausible reconstruction of his reasoning on each point, say where you think he would ultimately land, and flag clearly where you are extrapolating beyond anything he actually wrote.
