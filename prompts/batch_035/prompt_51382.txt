Plain-language mandates have quietly become a standard instrument of governance. Under the United States Plain Writing Act of 2010, federal agencies are legally required to write public-facing documents — benefit applications, tax instructions, notices of legal rights — in clear, concise, well-organized prose, and agencies train their staff accordingly: prefer active constructions to the passive, verbs to nominalizations, concrete subjects and everyday words, short sentences, and one idea per sentence. Comparable rules and style guides now govern civil-service writing in most other democracies. And yet the same governments maintain a flourishing official euphemism in politically sensitive registers — collateral damage, enhanced interrogation, downsizing, voluntary resignation — and make tactical use of the very constructions their own style guides forbid, since an agentless passive is a reliable way to report that mistakes were made without saying by whom.

This looks like an institutionalized version of Orwell, but an oddly partial one. Orwell's own position, in Politics and the English Language and elsewhere, was that the decline of a language ultimately has political and economic causes, and that the cure, if any, runs through political honesty rather than style rules; he also thought that corrupted language makes foolish thought easier in a loop that runs in both directions. A bureaucratic regime that polices syntax presupposes, by contrast, that the causation is tractable from the language side.

What theory of the relation between language and political thought does the plain-language practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct it: does the practice need anything as strong as linguistic determinism, or merely the weaker claim that bad writing tends to mislead and obscure agency, or even a purely communicative-efficiency view on which the whole point is that a citizen with limited literacy must be able to fill out a benefits form? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined — which rival views would each license the same rulebook. Then assess its internal consistency: can a government coherently mandate plainness for forms while cultivating engineered vagueness elsewhere, or does the euphemism case show the practice to be in tension with whatever it presupposes? Finally, suppose the presupposed view were replaced by a deception-based one, on which the evil to be policed is intentional misleadingness rather than muddled or agentless syntax: what in the training, the rulebook, and the enforcement would have to change, and what would survive untouched?
