For the final session of my social policy seminar, which this year pairs policy literature with films, I have assigned Ken Loach's I, Daniel Blake (2016), and I want to close the term by reading it through the philosophy of the welfare state that we've been developing all along: the distinction between contributory desert and need-based entitlement, the deservingness heuristics people actually apply to claimants (responsibility for one's plight, effort, recipiency attitude, and so on), and the conditionality and sanctions regime that workfare builds on those judgments. The interpretive puzzle I want to press on is this: the film is plainly an indictment of the system that grinds Daniel down, but it seems to secure our indignation largely by making Daniel and Katie maximally deserving — he is a widowed joiner who has worked all his life, is humble, follows every rule, and refuses to take a job at a garden centre only because his doctor has forbidden it; she is a devoted mother who moves her children from a London homeless shelter, starves herself so her children can eat, and breaks down at the food bank. So does the film mount a critique of the deservingness frame itself, or does it depend on that frame — would it move an audience whose antipathy is reserved for the undeserving, and is that a rhetorical compromise, a moral failing, or simply how any popular art must work? Katie is the harder test case: she shoplifts, and the film lets us watch her enter sex work. Where does she strain the audience's desert intuitions in ways Daniel never does, and what does the difference show about the categories? Please give me a reading that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously: say which scenes anchor your interpretation (the sanction for lateness, the garden-centre refusal, the food bank, the graffiti on the Jobcentre, the tribunal, and the eulogy Katie reads, which insists that Daniel is not a blip on a computer screen or a national insurance number but a man), note where the film resists the reading — including whether Daniel's own appeal trades on the contributory-desert currency he is protesting — and end by saying what the film dramatizes about conditionality and the undeserving poor that the policy and philosophy literature on sanctions and workfare does not.
