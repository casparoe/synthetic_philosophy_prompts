My sister wants to buy out my half of the cottage we inherited, and I got two friends who did game theory in grad school to help me think about a fair price, and they basically spent the whole coffee arguing with each other instead of helping me. First the setup, then their fight.

If we can't agree on a price, we stay 50/50 co-owners. That's honestly fine with me — my half locked up, some weekend use, some hassle, call it worth 180k to me. She hates it, worth about 150k to her, she wants the place to be *hers*. She'd pay up to 500k for full ownership, and she's not shy about it. So any buyout price between 180 and 350 is on the table.

Dan's opening move: the clean answer is the Nash bargaining solution. Take the no-deal numbers as the baseline, pick the price that maximizes the product of our gains over baseline. He said it works out to 340k, which puts us each 160 ahead of our fallback, and that this is the unique split satisfying Pareto efficiency, symmetry, invariance to how you scale utilities, and independence of irrelevant alternatives — so what else could fairness be.

Priya said that's rigged against whoever has the worse fallback, i.e. my sister. Her pitch was the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution: work out each person's best possible gain — mine is 170 (her paying 350), hers is 320 (me accepting 180) — and give both people the same fraction of that. She got about 291k, which lands us both around 65% of our ideal.

Dan said the ideal point is a fantasy. It's defined by the other side capitulating completely, and nobody's entitled to a fraction of what they'd get if I just gave in, so why should fairness be measured against it. Plus, he said, notice that Kalai–Smorodinsky only gets monotonicity by giving up independence of irrelevant alternatives, so the "fair" price can jump around when options neither of us would ever pick evaporate off the table — he called that a dealbreaker.

Priya came back that IIA is the dodgy one, and that Nash can violate monotonicity: there are cases where one side's options get strictly better everywhere and the Nash split still hands that side a smaller gain than before. Her line was that a fairness rule which can punish you for your own situation improving is broken, and that Kalai–Smorodinsky exists precisely to fix that, so the axiom trade is worth it.

Then they argued about whether the fallback numbers should even matter this much, since my fallback is partly my own doing (I could fight to force a sale, make co-ownership miserable, etc.) and my sister's low one partly reflects that she'd rather pay up than scrap, and then everyone had to leave.

Can you referee this for me? Specifically: in the axiom fight, is independence of irrelevant alternatives or monotonicity the more defensible demand, and is Dan's complaint about the ideal point fair or a cheap shot? Then work out what price each solution actually supports given the numbers above (show the arithmetic so I can check it), and tell me which one I should take to my sister — or whether the whole bargaining-theory frame misses something about siblings negotiating that both of them missed. And no, I can't just propose splitting the difference at 315 and call it a day, though if you think that's secretly the right answer for reasons neither of them gave, I'd hear it.
