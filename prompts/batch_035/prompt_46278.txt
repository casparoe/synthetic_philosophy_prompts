I'm a PhD student in HCI (human-AI interaction side of it, not the pure philosophy side) and I'm TA-ing an undergrad course on ethics of technology this fall. Next unit is on AI assistants, and I want a decision-tree handout that sorts students into an actual position on when an assistant may refuse a request or override a user's stated preference, instead of everyone just saying "it depends" and moving on.

Some requirements:

- Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of schools. The branching questions I want in there somewhere: (1) who is the intervention for — the user's own good, third parties, or nobody's good (the assistant "following its conscience" / the company's values); (2) does the assistant need evidence that the user's choice is somehow compromised (drunk, uninformed, in a crisis, caught in an impulsive moment) before it may intervene, or can it intervene on fully autonomous choices too — basically soft vs hard paternalism, but don't label the node that way, phrase it as a case; (3) transparency — must the user be able to find out that the assistant steered them, or may it quietly nudge or selectively withhold things for their own benefit; (4) standing — does it matter that the intervener is a product a company sells you rather than a state, a friend, or a fiduciary like a doctor.

- The leaves should include at least: the laissez-faire view (refuse only to protect others), soft paternalism, hard paternalism, epistemic paternalism (the interesting bit there is what it does with cases where the info being withheld is stuff the user explicitly asked for), a fiduciary view on which the assistant is more like a lawyer with duties to your good, the conscience view where refusal isn't paternalism at all because it's not about you, and the "it's a tool" view where the assistant has no standing of its own and everything reduces to whatever the operator configured. If you think there are other real positions in the space, add them; if some region of the space is empty, say so and take a guess at why.

- Nodes have to be concrete cases a 19-year-old with zero philosophy background can answer without knowing where the branch leads. Scenarios I'd like worked in, though feel free to come up with better ones: someone at 2am after a breakup asking the assistant to send a scorched-earth text right now; someone asking for an aggressive calorie-cutting meal plan while the assistant can see (from the conversation) signs of disordered eating; someone asking for the most one-sided summary of a political story because they're stressed and want it short; the assistant noticing a message would be career-ending and asking "are you sure you want to send this" vs. quietly delaying it; and one calibration case everyone agrees on, like an actively suicidal user asking for a method — include that one on purpose so we can check whether it actually sorts anyone, since a case that produces no disagreement is useless as a node.

- Flag pairs of leaves the tree can't cleanly separate. My guess is soft paternalism and epistemic paternalism bleed into each other, and the conscience view vs. the tool view might not be separable by any case question at all, only by asking about labels directly, which would defeat the point of the handout. If that's right, say so, don't paper over it.

- Ten-ish branch questions max, two or three answer options each, and a one-line "most awkward commitment" under every leaf.

Keep the tone light — these students will not read a wall of text. And if you could note at the top which single node does the most sorting work, that'd help me decide what to cut if it runs long. Sorry for the long spec, last years handout was a mess and im not doing that again lol.
