The socialist calculation debate seems to me to be the strangest stall in the history of economics, and I want a diagnosis of it, not a rerun of the arguments. Mises argued in 1920 that without market prices for capital goods, planners literally could not compute which production plans were rational. Lange and Lerner replied that planners could simulate the market: set provisional prices, watch for shortages and gluts, adjust, and repeat — a formal answer that many took to settle the matter. Hayek then shifted the ground, arguing that the market's real function is not computation but discovery: the relevant knowledge is dispersed, tacit, and time-sensitive, and prices are the only signal that aggregates it without anyone having to collect it. After 1989 the mainstream declared the issue closed in Hayek's favor — yet the debate has quietly reignited, with computational socialists like Cockshott and Cottrell arguing that modern computing, input-output analysis, and labor-time accounting can do exactly what the planners of the 1930s could not, while Hayekians retort that the very same complexity considerations prove planning intractable. What I find eerie is that both sides now accept the same facts — that equilibrium systems are in principle solvable, that computing power is astronomically larger than in Lange's day, that real economies involve tacit and distributed knowledge — and interpret them in exactly opposite ways.

Act as a professional philosopher of economics with serious expertise in both the Austrian tradition and formal general equilibrium theory. Before writing your answer, brainstorm the candidate diagnoses: is this a straightforwardly empirical disagreement that a simulation, a complexity result, or an actual planning experiment could settle; is it a disguised disagreement about incentives rather than knowledge (planners can compute but cannot be trusted to); is it verbal, turning on what deserves to be called 'calculation' or 'information' or 'knowledge'; or are the two sides answering different questions — static allocation versus dynamic discovery — under a single name? My own working hypothesis is that the crux is whether the knowledge a modern economy uses exists as data that could in principle be transmitted to a center or only as know-how that is generated and revealed through the market process itself, so that no amount of computing power addresses Hayek's actual point. Test that hypothesis against what each side actually says, tell me where it is too simple, and conclude with what would genuinely count as progress: a sharper distinction, a formal result, a change in the question, or an argument that the impasse is structural and permanent — and if the last, why. Be honest rather than diplomatic, and do not simply restate each side's standard case for the reader.
