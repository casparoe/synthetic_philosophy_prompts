I have recently taken on a commission that I am finding unexpectedly hard: translating into English the cycle of sonnets that my late aunt, a reasonably well-regarded poet in Portugal, wrote over the last fifteen years of her life, so that her grandchildren — who do not read Portuguese — can know her work. The commission has landed me squarely in the middle of the oldest argument in translation, the one about whether the translator owes fidelity to sense or to form, and I have come to suspect that the argument has not so much been contested as endlessly restated for about three centuries. Dryden already distinguished metaphrase, paraphrase, and imitation in the seventeenth century, and everyone since seems to be stationed somewhere on that line, dug in.

Let me describe the two camps as I meet them in practice. The meaning-first camp holds that a poem's translatable core is its semantic and connotative content, and that poetic form belongs to languages rather than to texts: the rhyme inventory and metrical resources of English simply differ from those of Portuguese, so a translator who insists on reproducing the sonnet's decassílabo and its rhyme scheme will be forced into filler epithets, inverted syntax, and rhyme-driven word choices that cost more meaning than the form was ever worth. This camp points to the great rhymed disasters of translation history — Nabokov used some very sharp words about what rhyme-hunting does to translators, and his own Onegin is deliberately a literal crib with commentary precisely for this reason. The form-first camp, with which I confess more sympathy, holds that a poem stripped of its form is not a translation at all but a gloss: the argument of the sonnet turns on the volta, the sudden reorientation at line nine, which is meaningless without the fixed boundary the form draws around it; a dance song's meaning is largely in the dance, and Hofstadter's whole exercise in Le Ton beau de Marot — hundreds of pages orbiting dozens of attempts to render Marot's tiny poem for a sick girl, a poem whose entire business is coaxing her into movement — rests on the claim that prosodic structure is itself part of what is said.

Here is what puzzles me. In my reading of the two camps, they agree on nearly everything factual: that form carries effect, that a paraphrase is not the poem, that forced rhymes damage diction, that perfect formal equivalence between languages is unattainable. And yet nobody moves, and each side treats the other's practice as a straightforward category of failure, not a rival answer to the same question. So I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on it. Is it verbal — two senses of "faithful" that make each camp self-evidently right in its own terms? Are the camps secretly answering different questions, one about what a translation should preserve and the other about what it can preserve, or one about the ontology of the poem (is it a content or an event?) and the other about the practical craft of substitution between systems? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work — something about reader psychology, such as whether the experience of an English pentameter is even remotely comparable to that of a Portuguese decassílabo — which no one has ever bothered to study because it looks like a matter of taste? I would also like you to consider the possibility that the whole dispute is purpose-relative in disguise: that "fidelity" has one correct reading for a crib meant for scholars, another for a stage performance, and another for grandchildren, and that the centuries of polemic come from insisting on a single absolute answer to a question that is indexed to use. A related wrinkle: practicing translators increasingly talk about "compensation" — surrendering the rhyme here and buying back an equivalent formal effect there — which suggests the two camps may not be exclusive positions at all, and yet the polemic persists undiminished among people who both know the term.

So: tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each side concretely says and does, whether my hunch about the purpose-relativity is right or just a way of changing the subject, and what — a distinction, an empirical result, a change in the question, a canonical demonstration — would count as real progress here, or whether this is one of those disputes that persists because it should. I ask because I have forty sonnets to render, and I would like to know whether I am facing a hard decision or an ill-posed one.
