I'm a novelist, three books in — mid-century family sagas, mostly — and for the next one I'm following a single character from about 1970 to 2020: a guy who reads The Population Bomb as a college senior and never lets it go. Famine dates he circled on calendars, kids he half-regrets having, the whole slowly curdling prophecy. My editor says the character comes off as a joke because "Malthus was debunked, the green revolution happened, we all know how this story ends," but I don't want to write him as a joke. I want to write him as a man who was maybe wrong and maybe early, and honestly I can't figure out myself which one he is, which is a problem when you're supposed to be the omniscient one at the keyboard.

So write me an essay on this: can a theory be "right but early," and does Malthus qualify? Here's the distinction I've been chewing on and want you to actually engage with, not just nod at — there's a difference between a prediction that simply failed (population outran food, we starved by 1990, we didn't, done) and a mechanism that got counteracted by things the theory never claimed to predict (hybrid wheat, the demographic transition, global trade in grain). If Malthus is best read as "population presses against resources whenever checks are absent, and the pressure is real even when innovation outruns it for a few generations," was he wrong in any sense a Popperian would recognize, or did we just get lucky? And the uncomfortable flip side: at what point does "right but early" become a rescue operation — a way of keeping a dead theory alive by quietly converting its absolute predictions into conditionals after the fact? I'd like you to use the Ehrlich–Simon wager and the demographic transition as test cases if they help, and to say something about the moral weight too, since my character's Malthusianism is partly an alibi for his coldness toward the poor, and the original Malthus had exactly that problem with the poor laws.

Don't end on a "perhaps the truth is in the middle" note — I need a verdict I can actually structure the novel's ending around, even if I ignore it. And since I'll be building a character's whole inner life on this, tell me honestly where your reasoning is most likely to fail: what development in the argument, or what historical fact I haven't thought of, would flip you to the opposite conclusion. If a well-informed historian or an economist who hates Malthus would laugh at some step you're taking, flag it rather than paper over it.
