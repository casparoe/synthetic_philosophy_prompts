So here's my situation: I just got moved over to the HR/benefits side of our company, and my first big project is getting more employees to actually enroll in the retirement plan. My coworker Dana thinks any use of defaults or framing is "manipulation," and that the only clean method is a well-reasoned memo that lays out the case honestly. I'm honestly not sure the line is where she thinks it is, and I want to stress-test it before I commit to a design.

Construct me a thought experiment to spec. The setting is a workplace benefits enrollment drive, realistic, no lies and no hidden information anywhere in either intervention. Intervention A is a written argument: every claim true, numbers sourced, nothing withheld. But it only actually works because of a quirk — it's engineered so that people skim it and sign the enrollment form while feeling like they read it carefully, and the data is clear that if people actually read it slowly, the argument on its own wouldn't move most of them. Intervention B changes nothing about the information: it's a default flip (enrolled unless you opt out) plus a plainly written one-page note saying exactly what we did and why — something like: we made enrollment the default because most people don't act either way, and here's how to opt out if you want. So B works through the path of least resistance but is fully disclosed, and A works through something that looks like reasoning but is really exploiting skimming.

Here are the specs. First, the scenario itself should be one paragraph. Second, on the view that manipulation is influence that works by bypassing or subverting the target's rational capacities — working through bias, habit, and laziness rather than through reasons — the case should come out with A as manipulation and B as not, and it should genuinely follow from the account, not be stipulated. Third, on the view that what makes something manipulation is that the influencer is trying to steer the target toward the influencer's preferred outcome regardless of the target's own judgment (as opposed to genuinely trying to help them decide well by their own lights), the verdicts should flip the other way: B is manipulation, A is not. Fourth, the gut intuition of an ordinary employee should pull in a third direction — both fine, or A fine and B creepy — your choice, but tell me which and why.

After the scenario, walk me through each account's verdict and the reasoning behind it. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work in getting the two accounts to disagree — I suspect it's the gap between the mechanism the influence actually works through versus the content of the message, but check me on that. Say what happens to each verdict if you remove that feature: for instance, if the memo really did convince on its merits once read, or if the default flip were never disclosed. And give me Dana's best attack on the case — she thinks a reasoned memo just can't be manipulation, full stop, so how would she argue the setup is rigged against her view?

This is for a blog post I'm writing, so the case has to actually generate the verdicts from the theories rather than baking them in — a scenario that just declares the outcomes is useless to me. And be honest about the failure points: where is this whole construction most likely to collapse, and what argument or consideration would change your mind about which account gets manipulation right?
