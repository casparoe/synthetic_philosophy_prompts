In my graduate seminar on formal and moral decision theory we have spent six weeks on what the literature now calls the fanaticism problem, and the class has deadlocked in a way I want to understand better than I do. The setting is familiar enough: if expected utility theory is normatively authoritative and utilities are not bounded above — as they arguably cannot be, once we take seriously stakes like existential risk or astronomical numbers of future lives — then one must sometimes prefer an option with a vanishingly small probability of an astronomical payoff over a certain modest good. One must, in the limiting case, take the mugging. On one side sit my students (and a vocal minority of the recent literature, e.g. Wilkinson's defence of fanaticism and the arguments catalogued by Beckstead and Thomas) who accept the fanatical verdicts as a constraint any adequate theory must reproduce, noting that the standard repairs are themselves costly: bounding utility looks ad hoc, probability discounting seems irrational, and risk-weighted expected utility in Buchak's style merely re-describes the phenomenon it was meant to solve. On the other side sit those who treat the requirement as a straightforward reductio of the combination of EU maximization with unbounded stakes — and who point out that the fanatic's own position collapses in the infinite case, where every option has infinite or undefined expectation and the theory goes silent. Both sides know all of this; both sides concede the other's strongest points in seminar and move not at all. My working hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than flattered, is that the dispute is not really about decision theory at all: it is a dispute about the epistemology of reflective equilibrium, specifically about whether a verdict entailed by a formalism we accepted on independent grounds — transitivity, dominance, the Sure-Thing Principle — can be overturned by intuitions about cases that everyone concedes are unstable, since the same students who refuse the mugging will happily take a one-in-a-million rescue chance when the payoff is merely very large. But there is a second possibility I cannot dismiss: that the question 'ought one to be a fanatic?' is ill-posed because it presupposes a decision theory that is neutral between the answers, whereas each answer just is a decision theory. Please give me a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement — whether it is substantive, verbal, methodological, or a matter of which cases count as data — supported by what each side actually claims, an account of why the debate has stalled precisely where it has (note the symmetry: the anti-fanatic cures generate mirror-image pathologies, so neither horn is cheap), and an honest answer about what would count as progress: a theorem, a class of cases with stable intuitions, an anthropic or infinite-ethics result, or a change in the question. Close with a final line of the form 'Verdict:' followed by a short label naming the kind of disagreement you diagnose.
