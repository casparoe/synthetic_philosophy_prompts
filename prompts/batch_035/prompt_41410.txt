I'm the parent representative on the curriculum committee of a small independent school, and I need a written opinion to circulate before our vote next month. The case: our most experienced math teacher wants to add a two-week unit to the eleventh-grade elective on "mathematical ideas." His plan is to teach Hilbert's hotel and Cantor's diagonal argument in the usual way, and then present finitism — the view that only potential, unbounded collection exists, and that a completed infinite totality is never something we can coherently talk about — as a live option, after which each student writes a short paper taking a side on whether actual infinities are discovered facts or useful fictions.

Two objections have come in. One father, an engineer, argues that infinity is settled mathematics: Cantor's results are theorems, the same as the quadratic formula, and presenting them as "controversial" tells students that math is a matter of opinion. Another committee member worries the unit will confuse weaker students, who need to learn to trust the machinery before they question it. The teacher's response is that the unit actually teaches a distinction students rarely grasp: between a proof and an interpretation of a proof. He points out that a finitist can check every step of the diagonal argument — nothing in the derivation is in dispute — while still denying that it reveals anything about a realm of actually existing infinite sets; and that students who can't see the difference between those two levels of commitment don't really understand Cantor at all.

I should disclose where I stand, because I want the memo to test my own position rather than flatter it. I have no academic background, but I've read about this on my own for a couple of years, and my half-formed view is that the engineer is attacking the wrong target: nobody, finitist included, doubts the derivations, so the unit isn't teaching that proofs are negotiable — it's teaching that the question of what the axioms are about is separate from the question of what follows from them. If that's right, the unit is safer than it sounds. But I can see the opposite reading too: perhaps finitism is less an open question than a minority taste among professionals, and a school shouldn't dress up a minority taste as a live controversy, any more than it should teach that heliocentrism is up for debate because one or two people say so.

Please draft the memo the committee can act on: a clear recommendation (approve, reject, or approve with conditions), the reasoning, a fair and serious treatment of the strongest version of the engineer's objection rather than a dismissal of it, and — if you recommend approval with conditions — conditions concrete enough that we can actually check whether the teacher has met them. Throughout, distinguish carefully between claims about mathematics and claims about pedagogy, since our committee keeps sliding between them. And because I suspect my own framing is the weakest link, end with a short section stating plainly what would have to be established at the meeting for the opposite recommendation to be the right one: which argument, if made well, should flip the committee's verdict, and where the memo's own reasoning is most likely to go wrong. I'll add any references myself afterward; you needn't supply any.
