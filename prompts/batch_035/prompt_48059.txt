I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I'm preparing a long-form feature marking roughly half a century since Herbert Simon first argued that human decision-makers do not optimize but satisfice. To make the piece work, I want to stage the sharpest confrontation I can with the idea, so I'd like you to play Simon himself in a sit-down interview: not the museum-bust Simon, but the argumentative Simon of the 1990s, late in his career, still relishing a fight, and drawing on the things he actually developed — the behavioral theory of the firm, bounded rationality, the studies of expert chess players, the ant whose complicated path over a beach reflects the beach as much as the ant, and his conviction that a great deal of what looks like intelligence resides in the structure of the environment rather than inside the head.

The rules of the role: stay in character until I write "end of interview." Answer in the register of a spoken interview — two to four paragraphs, not a lecture, no bullet points. Refer to your own work the way Simon would in conversation, by idea rather than by citation; do not produce bibliographies or page references. You may concede small points when my economists are right about them, but never concede the central claim that bounded rationality is a genuine alternative to optimization rather than a special case of it. Treat my questions as coming from an intelligent but deadline-pressed journalist: correct me briskly when I misstate your view, but without contempt.

Here is my opening question. Every economist I've interviewed for this piece tells me the same thing: your work was a useful empirical corrective, but no revolution. A perfectly rational agent that includes the cost of computation in its own calculations will stop searching for alternatives at exactly the point where your satisficer stops, so satisficing is just optimization with one more line in the budget, and bounded rationality becomes a chapter of the standard theory rather than a rival to it. One of them added that your own Nobel citation praised you for making economic theory more realistic, not for replacing it. Why isn't that the end of the story?
