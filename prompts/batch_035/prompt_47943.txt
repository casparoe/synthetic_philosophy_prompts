Three weeks ago I underwent a colonoscopy under sedation, and I am told that in the recovery bay I asked, on a loop, whether the procedure was over — apparently hearing each answer as though for the first time and remembering none of it afterwards. What has stayed with me is not the mild embarrassment but a philosophical unease: at each asking, I was, on my own evidence, someone waking for the first time, and yet there seems to have been some determinate fact about how confident I ought to have been that I had already asked. This has pulled me back into the Sleeping Beauty literature, and what I find there puzzles me more than the case itself.

The halfer–thirder dispute has been running for well over two decades — since Adam Elga's paper of 2000 and David Lewis's reply the following year — and, as far as I can tell, every argument on each side is fully understood by the other side, and nobody moves. The halfers maintain that Beauty receives no relevant new evidence on waking; the thirders reply that her current awakening is twice as probable if the coin landed tails. There are splinter positions, such as the double-halvers, who try to combine the halfer's credence in heads with a natural distribution over days of the week; there are Dutch book and betting arguments that each camp claims to survive unscathed; and there is a standing connection to anthropic reasoning, where the same fissure reappears as a disagreement between sampling assumptions. My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the dispute is not a substantive disagreement about probability at all, but a disagreement about whether 'it is Monday' expresses the kind of thing that takes an ordinary credence in the first place — one camp in effect answering a question about objective chances, the other a question about what Beauty ought to do or say upon waking, both under the single heading of rational credence. But I am not confident of this.

Diagnose the disagreement for me. Is it verbal, a hidden formal question about how probability theory should be extended to self-locating or centred propositions, a difference in bedrock intuitions that no argument could reach, or something else again? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says rather than offering a survey of positions, and say where the standard arguments genuinely fail to engage one another. Then tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress: a decision-theoretic result, a variant case in which the two positions prescribe observably different behaviour, a conceptual distinction that splits the question cleanly — or an argument that the question is ill-posed and the impasse is the correct and final outcome.
