ok, so quick context: I sit on the usage subcommittee of a mid-sized humanities journal (we control the style sheet for everything it publishes). Current rule blesses generic "he" and "-man" compounds as "traditional usage" — so authors can write "the average reader... he" or "chairman" and copyeditors leave it alone. There's a motion on the table at Friday's meeting to require neutral alternatives instead, and the subcommittee is split roughly down the middle. One faction says this is politics dressed up as style editing and we have no business taking a side; the other says the current rule isn't neutral to begin with, it only looks neutral because everyone's used to it. I'm supposed to circulate a memo before the meeting recommending what we do, and I'd like you to draft it.

Here's the part I want the memo to actually chew on, and it's a bit in the weeds: the lazy way to frame this is "progressive reform vs. stodgy traditionalists," but I think the real question is what the old rule is doing semantically. When a style guide permits "the reader... he," is it (a) just recording a fact about English, (b) stipulating one convention among several equally good ones, or (c) stipulating a convention that smuggles in a default picture of who the reader is? The old Hofstadter satire — the one where he writes as a fussy grammarian defending "white" as the generic term for humans, so you get things like chairwhite and talk of the whites of the species, all to show that a "generic" term is only generic relative to whichever group got to be the unmarked case — is basically an argument for (c). But the memo shouldn't just take that on faith. The obvious rejoinder is semantic bleaching: "man" and "he" supposedly shed their gendered content the way "guys" arguably has, so the racial analogy misfires, and forcing the change is tampering with a word that no longer carries the default anyway. So the memo needs some account of when a term counts as bleached versus still carrying the default — one that's stated precisely enough that the losing faction could push back on it, not just vibes.

What I need in the draft:

- A definite recommendation: adopt the change, reject it, or adopt with conditions. If conditions, spell them out — e.g. what happens with direct quotations from older sources, fixed phrases where the neutral alternative is awkward, authors who insist on first-person usage.
- Reasons the losing side could actually engage with — "times change" is not a reason.
- Operational criteria a copyeditor can apply at 11pm without calling a meeting: concrete line-editing rules, not aspirations.
- Please don't do citations or a lit review; describe any positions you invoke rather than name-dropping papers, I'll handle sources myself if we ever go public with this.

One last thing: our editor-in-chief skims everything and wants the bottom line extractable, so the memo has to end with a final line in machine-readable form, exactly like \answer{...}, containing one of: adopt / reject / adopt-with-conditions. Normal memo prose before that. Keep it to something a busy person reads in five minutes — I'd say under 700 words, don't pad it. Also if you could get it to me before thursday that'd be ideal, the packet goes out friday morning.
