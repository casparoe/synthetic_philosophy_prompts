My friend has been on a big neuroscience-of-consciousness kick lately, and the other night over drinks he explained Dennett's multiple drafts model to me like this: 'Dennett demolished the Cartesian theater — that's Descartes' idea that a little homunculus sits in the pineal gland watching a mental movie screen, and basically everyone believed some version of this until Dennett came along. The multiple drafts model replaces it: the brain works like a newspaper, where a central editor takes all the raw sensory copy, cuts and polishes it, and only the final published version ever reaches consciousness — everything that gets edited out stays unconscious forever. The editing is so fast we can't catch it in the act, which is why experience feels like one seamless stream. And combined with the Libet experiments this basically proves there's no free will, since our feeling of choosing is just the story the editor publishes after the decision has already been made.' He's read way more pop neuroscience than me so I mostly nodded along, but afterwards I kept feeling like something was subtly off, and I can't put my finger on what.

Some context on where I'm coming from: I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since like 2014, so I've absorbed the whole 'dissolve the question before answering it' attitude by osmosis, and I know Dennett is a big hero in that crowd for exactly that kind of move. I also vaguely recall that he was actually a compatibilist who defended free will in some book — I think it was called Freedom Evolves? — which seems really hard to square with 'proves there's no free will,' but maybe he changed his mind or maybe I'm misremembering. I have definitely not read Consciousness Explained cover to cover, so I can't check this myself, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

So what I'd like is an audit, kind of claim by claim: which parts of his summary would a Dennett defender (or a philosopher of mind generally) accept as roughly right, which parts are subtly distorted — like, does the newspaper-editor framing actually invert what the model says? — and which are just wrong. Also the 'everyone believed this until Dennett' bit feels suspicious, because I've seen people accuse Dennett of beating up a strawman there, and I'd like to know if that accusation has any bite or if he really is describing a view people hold. And finally, does the no-free-will conclusion follow from whichever parts of his summary turn out to be correct, or is he getting that for free somewhere? I'm inclined to believe the general 'no central place where it all comes together' picture, fwiw, so tell me if even that needs qualification.
