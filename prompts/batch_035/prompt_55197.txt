I've been working through Amartya Sen's 1970 result on the impossibility of a Paretian liberal for a reading group on social choice theory, and I've constructed an argument that I can't bring myself to accept, though each step seemed fine when I wrote it down. Here it is:

(1) Minimal liberalism, in the sense relevant to social choice, says that each of at least two persons is decisive over at least one pair of social states: there are outcomes x and y, differing only in some matter personal to that person, such that society must rank x above y whenever that person does, regardless of what anyone else prefers. (2) The Pareto principle says that whenever everyone in society strictly prefers x to y, society must rank x above y. (3) Sen's theorem shows these are jointly unsatisfiable. The derivation turns on profiles in which preferences cross into others' private spheres — his example is a prudish and a lascivious reader with preferences over who reads a single copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover, each ranking the outcome in which the other reads it above the outcome in which neither does. Tracing the cycle, the social ranking must simultaneously defer to each person within their own sphere and defer to everyone everywhere, which no single ranking can do. (4) Since the two commitments are jointly unsatisfiable, one of them must be abandoned, and the abandonment can't be confined to the perverse profiles: a commitment that fails under some admissible preference profile isn't a commitment at all. (5) The Pareto principle is the more secure of the two — unanimity among everyone, including the person affected, seems like the least any collective ranking owes us. So what must go is individual decisiveness: no one is, or can be, decisive over any pair of social states, however private. (6) And if no one is decisive over any pair, then a majority that unanimously ranks my reading the book over my not reading it is as entitled to impose that ranking as it is to impose anything else. There is no sphere into which collective evaluation cannot reach, because a sphere would require exactly the decisiveness step (5) has eliminated.

I can accept (1) and (2); they are, I take it, just the standard statements of the positions. My problem is that I cannot accept (6), and yet every move from (3) to (6) looks like it is licensed by the generality of the theorem rather than by any sleight of hand. The conclusion seems to prove too much: Sen's own point was that liberalism and Pareto cannot both be honored, not that liberalism is refuted outright, and yet my derivation seems to land on the outright refutation.

Tell me which step or inference to reject. Then explain why the step looked acceptable — what made it easy to overlook — and what rejecting it costs: I'd like to know what each available exit (giving up (2) across the board, restricting the domain of admissible profiles, weakening (1), or reformulating rights in some other way entirely) has to pay, and what the least expensive exit is. If you think the argument is actually sound and (6) can be swallowed, say so and defend the swallowing. Finally, tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point in your own answer you are least sure of.
