I'm part of a graduate metaphysics reading group that spent this term on Lewis, and I came out broadly sympathetic to counterpart theory: I like the clean ontology of worldbound individuals, and I have never been able to make sense of the transworld identity alternative, on which the very same person exists in many possible worlds with a different career, different hair, a different spouse, and remains strictly one with the person sitting here. Two weeks ago a friend in the group, who is firmly on the Kripkean side of modality, ran an argument at me that has been bothering me ever since, because it seems to show that the view I adopted doesn't do the one job it was built for. Here is his argument as I reconstructed it afterward. (1) A claim about a particular thing is made true by how things are with that thing; whatever makes "Humphrey might have won" true has to involve Humphrey himself, not just things resembling him. (2) On counterpart theory, what makes "Humphrey might have won" true is that in some other possible world there is someone who closely resembles Humphrey in the relevant respects and wins there. (3) Since Humphrey exists only in the actual world, the worlds doing the truthmaking contain his counterparts, never him. (4) So what makes "Humphrey might have won" true involves Humphrey not at all. (5) If what makes a claim true involves a certain person not at all, then the claim is not, strictly, about that person — it is about whoever and whatever it does involve. (6) Therefore on counterpart theory no modal claim is ever about the thing it seems to be about; "Humphrey might have won" is really a claim about someone else, and de re modality has been explained away rather than analyzed.

The kicker, as he presents it, is the generalization: if (5) is right, then counterpart theory was never a theory of de re modality at all, only a semantics on which all such claims turn out to concern stand-ins, so the position undermines its own point. He also likes to add that Lewis's counterpart relation is a similarity relation whose relevant respects are allowed to vary with context, which would make things worse — whether Humphrey might have won would then depend partly on how the conversational setting ranks similarities. My instinct is that something slips between (4) and (5), or perhaps already between (1) and (2), but every time I try to pin it down I either beg the question against him or find myself smuggling Humphrey himself into the other worlds, which lands me in the transworld identity camp I find incoherent for other reasons — if that world's Humphrey never entered politics, what is supposed to make him numerically identical to ours, given that they share only some of their properties?

So I'd like a careful diagnosis. Which premise or step should the counterpart theorist reject, why did that step look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, does the friend's step (5), the move from what a claim's truthmakers involve to what the claim is about, hold up as a general principle, or does it prove too much elsewhere? And symmetrically, I want to know whether the transworld identity theorist really escapes this argument or merely has her own version of it: does the claim that Humphrey himself is present in the winning world give her truthmakers that involve Humphrey in the sense premise (1) demands, or is she just relabeling the resembling individual as Humphrey and thereby asserting the haecceitism Lewis rejects? Finally, if I decide to bite the bullet and accept (6), tell me what exactly I'm left with — is it a coherent and worthwhile position, or a concession that modal realism was never, strictly speaking, about us?
