I do ML for a living and lately I've been reading philosophy of induction on my commute, and it's started to bug me that my entire field rests on a little ritual nobody ever argues for. Before we ship a model we hold back a chunk of the data, train on the rest, and measure accuracy on the held-out chunk. If the number is decent we declare the model fit for production, where it will score new customers, see next year's products, and live through whatever trend shifts happen after deployment. Regulators are getting in on it too -- emerging audit rules for algorithmic systems basically come down to a checkbox asking whether you held out data and tested on it, as if the checkbox were the evidence.

So here's what I want: an analysis of what that practice actually presupposes, philosophically. What view of induction does the train/test split only make sense under? Because I can see it's not a simple "the future resembles the past" view, and that's what got me confused in the first place. The test chunk is drawn from the same week's logs, the same population, the same sampling pipeline as the training chunk. So the inference isn't even from past to future -- it's from one sample of the present to a later, differently composed population. And I know just enough Goodman to be dangerous here: nothing in the numbers distinguishes this accuracy will persist from this accuracy persists until the new year and then collapses, in the way the emerald thing does for green and grue. The procedure seems to quietly assume that the quantities we measure -- error rates, on these labels, under this sampling -- are the right ones to project forward, and not some grueified shadow of them.
