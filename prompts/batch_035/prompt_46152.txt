I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of science, and next month I'm giving a talk at our department's work-in-progress seminar (so, friendly, but they still smell blood) defending a version of selective realism against the pessimistic meta-induction. The rough line I want to take: the Laudan-style objection from phlogiston, caloric, the ether and so on only works if you say, hand-wavingly, "theories of the past turned out false." But if you run the divide et impera strategy carefully -- distinguishing the working posits from the idle ones -- a lot of the standard counterexamples come out differently. My pet case is Fresnel's ether: yes, the ether isn't real, but the parts of Fresnel's theory that actually did the explanatory work (the equations, the structure of the optical phenomena) got absorbed into Maxwell's theory, so the realist isn't convicted by the "theory was false, radically false" charge. I even want to push it a bit further and say the ether itself was arguably a working posit in some respects, which I know is where the position gets uncomfortable. I'm basically taking the Psillos line with a provocative twist, and I know it.

The audience, though, is the problem. Our department is mostly historians of science plus a couple of philosophers of science, and specifically: one faculty member who is a proper Laudanian pessimist and loves rattling off the historical list of "once-successful, now-discarded" theories (he's asked me twice already about the crystalline spheres, which I suspect is a trap of some kind); one ontic structural realist who thinks my whole move concedes too much, that the objects-vs-structure distinction is incoherent, and that "retaining the equations" while giving up the ontology is either cheating or trivial; and a bunch of historians who are less interested in the metaphysics and more likely to come at me with actual historical detail -- whether the clean story I tell about Fresnel and the ether is even accurate, whether scientists at the time really distinguished "working" from "idle" posits the way my reconstruction pretends, that kind of thing. One of the historians has already told me she thinks the selectivity distinction is doing its work only after the fact, with hindsight, which stung.

Act as an experienced philosophy of science professor who has sat through a hundred of these talks. Please think carefully and maybe brainstorm a bit before writing. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this specific audience, in roughly the order they'll arrive, each phrased the way that person would phrase it (the pessimist, the structural realist, the historians -- they have very different vocabularies), with a two-minute reply for each, a note on where the reply is soft, and what I should just concede up front. Also tell me what the strongest version of the hindsight-selectivity objection is and how bad it really is for me, and include the one question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks.
