Quick bit of background: I'm in a Descartes reading group with two friends, Maya and Jonas, and last week we got through the cogito passages and then spent two hours fighting about it in the pub afterwards. They keep dragging me back in and I genuinely can't referee this, so I'm outsourcing it. Here's the exchange as well as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Maya went first: she said the cogito, read as an argument, is just invalid on its face. "I think, therefore I am" needs a hidden premise -- whatever thinks, exists -- and once you write the premise out you see the proof is circular-ish: the premise is at least as strong as the conclusion, and if the meditator is allowed to assume universal truths like that while doubting everything, then the whole radical doubt was theater. You could have run the same move with "I walk, therefore I am" and a premise about walking.

(2) Jonas said Descartes doesn't present it as a syllogism anyway -- it's supposed to be grasped all at once, not derived, so Maya is grading a proof that wasn't offered. Maya shot back that this just relocates the problem: if it's not an inference, it's an intuition, and the whole drama of the doubt was supposed to be that intuitions (even clear and distinct ones) can't be trusted until God is on board. So either the cogito is an inference with a smuggled premise, or it's an intuition that the method itself disqualifies. Pick one.

(3) Jonas then made what I thought was his best move. He said the cogito is neither inference nor intuition, it's self-verifying in the way an act is: trying to doubt that you're thinking is itself an instance of thinking, like trying to sincerely deny that you're speaking out loud while doing it. The sentence isn't reporting a fact that could have been otherwise, it's performing the thing that makes it true. So asking for its premises is a category error -- you might as well ask for the premises of "hello."

(4) Maya said fine, even granting all that, look at how little it buys. If it's performative, it only verifies itself at the moment of performing it. So you get existence-at-this-instant, and everything else -- an enduring self, a substance, a thing whose essence is thinking -- has to be smuggled in from elsewhere. The famous conclusion is supposed to secure a lasting "I," and the argument, on Jonas's own reading, delivers something more like a flash. Jonas said that's still not nothing, and it also tells you what you are while you're checking: a thinking thing. That's a substantive result, not just a flash.

(5) Maya's last word was that even the "what" is stolen. All the performance actually guarantees is that thinking is occurring -- and the move from "thinking is occurring" to "there is an I that has the thinking" is done by grammar, not by the argument. Compare: "it's raining" doesn't commit us to some thing that does the raining. Maybe "I think" is just how the language forces us to dress up an impersonal event. Jonas got visibly annoyed and said that if there's no I, then the doubt that generated the cogito doesn't belong to anyone, and a doubt that nobody doubts is not a doubt, so the event-talk doesn't even make sense of the setup. Maya said that shows the I is a practical presupposition, not something proved.

So: can you go through the five moves and tell me who's right at each stage -- not just who comes out ahead overall? And then tell me what, if anything, is left standing at the end: what exactly does the cogito prove, to whom, and for how long? I'd especially like to know whether Maya's dilemma in (2) -- inference with a hidden premise, or intuition the method forbids -- really is exhaustive, or whether Jonas's performative reading in (3) actually escapes between the horns, and whether her grammar point in (5) is fatal or just forces a weaker conclusion.
