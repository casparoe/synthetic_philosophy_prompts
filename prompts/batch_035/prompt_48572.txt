At my last physical, my doctor told me that at fifty-five my blood pressure reading falls in the range officially labeled prehypertension, that my bone-density scan shows osteopenia, and that my fasting glucose makes me prediabetic. I feel entirely well, and nothing is wrong with any part of me right now. What struck me afterwards is that this classificatory practice has quietly expanded over the past few decades: states that were once described as risk factors — numbers that predict elevated odds of some future condition — have been renamed as early stages of the conditions themselves, so that an asymptomatic person can leave a routine appointment carrying several diagnoses. Yet the same medical establishment draws a hard line elsewhere: aging itself, though it is progressive functional decline, is officially not a disease, and each time the question of medicalizing ordinary grief or shyness or infidelity comes up, the argument offered is precisely that some harmful, dysfunctional-seeming states must be kept outside the category.

Some years ago I took a philosophy of medicine course where the central contrast was between views of disease as a matter of objective biological dysfunction — a part of the organism failing to do its statistically typical job — and views on which disease is irreducibly normative, involving harm to the person judged against their interests. A risk factor seems to fit neither cleanly. My heart, bones, and pancreas all function normally; only my future probability distribution is unfavorable. But harm is equally absent, since I feel fine and may never develop anything. So what theory of disease does the pre-disease labeling practice presuppose? Does it presuppose a genuinely different ontology on which elevated risk is itself a disease state — or is it better read as normativism in disguise, where disease just means whatever there exists a cost-effective intervention for, and the labeling followed the availability of the interventions? And how does that presupposition, whatever it is, sit alongside the refusal to medicalize aging, where the intervention argument would run the other way?

I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined — whether several theories of disease would equally license the labeling. Then tell me what would actually have to change in the practice, in the language of guidelines, insurance billing, and the conversation in my doctor's office, if the implicit view were replaced by a strict naturalism that denies disease status to any state in which no part is malfunctioning. A skeptical friend of mine insists the whole apparatus is just pharmaceutical marketing, since the thresholds were set so that enormous fractions of the healthy population qualify; I am less interested in the motives than in whether the conceptual point survives them — whether a category invented for commercial reasons can still be said to presuppose a theory of disease at all, or whether that very fact is itself evidence about which theory medicine actually operates with.
