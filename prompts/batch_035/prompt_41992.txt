I'm a graduate student writing on the axiology of extinction, and the specific question I'm after is why human extinction would be worse than the deaths it involves. Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture — the claim that the assumption that humanity will outlive us structurally conditions much of what we now find worth doing — is my starting point, and my working thesis is that the extinction-shaped residue, the component of badness left over once every death has been accounted for, does not reduce to the value of the lives that would otherwise have been led (the style of explanation, in Parfit's neighborhood, that treats unrealized future value as a loss). To test the thesis I want a thought experiment that inverts the usual setup. I have a design in mind, but I keep botching the details, so I'd like you to build it to specification.

The usual doomsday case gets its verdict from the future's being empty: no later people and no later value, so on the lost-value explanation the residue just is all that unrealized value. I want the opposite direction: a matched pair of scenarios, both organized around a single fixed death, in which the residue tracks continuation rather than value. Held completely fixed across both scenarios: the same woman (give her a life and projects worth caring about — a restoration ecologist, say, or a lexicographer; your choice), the same life history and character, and the same death on the same date from something perfectly ordinary. Stipulate that she never learns of anything that happens afterward, so that nothing in either scenario works through her expectations or her dread. In scenario one, humanity ends not long after her death, but the Earth and everything on it flourish for millions of years: the future is, by stipulation, abundant in value while containing nothing that continues, remembers, builds on, or even records anything she cared about. It should come out that the event still carries a residue beyond the deprivation she herself suffers — or at least that all that teeming value does nothing to soften it. In scenario two, humanity survives her for centuries — bare continuation — but in conditions stipulated to be close to valueless: her descendants are numerous and comfortable, yet nothing they do realizes, continues, or preserves anything of value, theirs or anyone else's. It should come out that the residue here is muted or absent. The target ordering is therefore residue(scenario one) > residue(scenario two) — the reverse of what the lost-value explanation predicts, since scenario two destroys far more value than scenario one does. Keep both scenarios realistic (a rewilded biosphere is fine; extraterrestrials and uploaded minds are not), one paragraph each, and generate the verdicts rather than stipulating them: if you cannot get a verdict out of a view without bending the case, say so plainly. For my purposes a strain you report is a finding, and a verdict you smuggle in is a failure.

After the scenarios, walk me through them: what the structural afterlife view, an impersonal view that counts unrealized future value as lost, and a plain totalist utilitarianism each say about the two scenarios, and exactly where the views divide. Then identify which feature of the pair is doing the work, and what happens to the case if that feature is subtracted. Two people in my reading group have objections I'd like addressed on the way. One insists that post-extinction biospheric flourishing cannot offset anything, because it is not part of what the extinction destroyed — the value would have existed only in the counterfactual, and the counterfactual is fixed across both scenarios — so tell me whether the first scenario can be built so that the offset question is live rather than confused. The other thinks the residue in scenario one is really driven by remembrance — the thought that no one will ever know she existed — which is a different rationale from the continuation of projects; if that is right, my case is polluted, so tell me whether it can be cleaned of that reading, and if not, what a Parfitian defender of the lost-value explanation would say to dissolve the whole contrast.
