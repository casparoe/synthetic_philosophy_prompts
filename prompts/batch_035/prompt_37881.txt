Two members of my forecasting club spent an evening arguing about whether the United States will still be the world's leading power in 2100, and I couldn't follow all of it. The exchange went like this. (1) Dana argued for what she called the outside view: leading powers throughout history have rarely held the top spot for more than a few centuries, most far less, so if you put the U.S. into the class of "states that were the leading power of their era" and read off the base rate, you should expect American preeminence to end well before 2100 -- and anyone who insists the U.S. is different is exactly the kind of confident insider the outside view exists to correct. (2) Marcus objected that the reference class is arbitrary: include agrarian land empires like Rome and the Han and you get doom, but there are other equally natural classes -- nuclear-armed states, maritime commercial hegemons, large continental federations -- and they give different base rates, with no fact of the matter about which class the U.S. "really" belongs to. (3) Dana said arbitrariness can be managed: take the most inclusive class of past leading powers, or pool the predictions from several defensible classes, and the conclusion survives, because the U.S. would have to be an outlier among outliers to buck every base rate at once. (4) Marcus replied that pooling just relocates the arbitrariness (who decides which classes enter the pool?), and that base rates ignore mechanism: past hegemons fell for specific causes -- the world wars for Britain, overextension along porous land frontiers for others, succession crises for dynastic empires -- and knowing which of these mechanisms the U.S. has and lacks should swamp a number pulled from a historical spreadsheet. (5) Dana countered that precisely this kind of rich causal narrative is what the outside view disciplines: forecasters with detailed stories about why now is different have a consistently worse track record than those who anchor on base rates, and Marcus himself trusts reference classes everywhere else -- life expectancy, insurance, actuarial tables -- so his resistance here is selective. (6) Marcus finished with a statistical point: maybe empires simply don't have a characteristic lifespan at all. If the historical lifetimes look roughly like a flat hazard rate -- each polity at any age having roughly the same annual chance of losing preeminence, with no aging or senescence -- then an empire's current age carries no information about how much longer it will last, so the base rate Dana started from was a misreading of the statistics, not a finding. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is basically right, basically wrong, or right for the wrong reasons, and explain why. Then tell me who comes out ahead overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing about the relation between the reference class problem and the hazard-rate point.
