I spent thirty-five years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, and I am now working through the philosophy of mathematics in retirement. Something is nagging at me about the debate over actual infinities. Cantor's diagonal argument proves that the reals form a strictly larger infinity than the naturals, and the Hilbert hotel thought experiment shows that a fully occupied infinite hotel can always make room for more guests by shifting everyone up a floor. Between them, it seems to me, mathematics has demonstrated that actual infinities are not just coherent but unavoidable — the hierarchy of infinite cardinals is as settled as anything in mathematics. That would seem to make finitism, which holds that actual infinities cannot exist, no more defensible than denying the infinitude of the primes. Yet I keep encountering serious philosophers, and apparently some mathematicians, who defend finitism or at least take it seriously. So my question is: given Cantor and Hilbert, why hasn't finitism simply been refuted, and are the finitists being as irrational as I suspect? I should say that my instinct, honed by a career of measuring finite quantities, is that the finitists are onto something about the physical world, but I cannot see how they escape the mathematics.
