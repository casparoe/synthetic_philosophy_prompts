At a faculty reading group on mental causation, a colleague defended the causal exclusion argument and then, when we pushed back, extended it in a way I cannot refute but also cannot accept. His argument, reconstructed as faithfully as I can:

(1) The physical is causally closed: every physical event that has a sufficient cause has a sufficient physical cause.
(2) A decision to raise one's arm is a mental event, and it is not identical to any neural event — multiple realization bars the type-identity, and a decision, unlike a firing, has content and can be rational or irrational.
(3) The arm's rising is a physical event.
(4) So if the decision causes the rising, the rising has two sufficient causes, a complete physical one and a distinct mental one, and neither is individually needed: the neural cause alone would have produced it.
(5) Overdetermination is tolerable only when it is rare and engineered, as with two assassins or redundant braking systems; it cannot be routine in every voluntary action.
(6) Therefore decisions do not cause bodily movements; mental events are epiphenomenal.

Then the extension that has me stuck:

(7) But (6) applies to itself. My concluding just now that mental events are epiphenomenal — taking in the premises, finding the exclusion step persuasive — was itself a mental event.
(8) If (6) is true, those considerations played no causal role in producing my belief; the belief was fixed by neurochemistry and would have been fixed the same way whatever the premises had said.
(9) A belief that would have been held the same way regardless of its grounds is not justified by those grounds.
(10) So if the argument is sound, no one is ever justified by an argument — including this one. The reasoning that convinces us of epiphenomenalism is, by its own lights, not reasoning.

I cannot accept (10), yet each premise looked unassailable in the room. My suspicion is that the trouble lies in (2): my colleague quietly assumes that non-identity plus closure leaves overdetermination as the only option, and he will reply that this simply is the exclusion argument, not an extra premise smuggled in. A second suspicion is that (9) conflates the causes of a belief with its justifiers, but I cannot see how to prize them apart without claiming that justification floats entirely free of causal processes in the head, which seems to hand him the victory. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs.
