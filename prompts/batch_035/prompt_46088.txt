Longtime lurker here — been reading Overcoming Bias and the rationalist-sphere since about 2009, so I've had 'costly signalling' drilled into me more times than I can count. Last week at our meetup we got into a row about why firms spend millions on adverts that literally convey nothing about the product — no specs, no price, just vibes and a logo — and someone ended the whole arguement by announcing that 'the cost is the message': the money burned is itself the proof of quality, since only a company expecting repeat business can afford to waste that much. Thing is, I keep hearing the same family of one-liners doing what look like very different jobs: 'honest signals have to hurt', 'you can't fake a peacock's tail', 'cheap talk carries no information', even 'if it doesn't cost anything it doesn't mean anything'. I have a suspicion these aren't all the same claim — some sound trivially true in a game-theory textbook way, some sound like Zahavi's handicap principle proper (costs deter low-quality mimics who can't afford them), and some sound frankly false (there seem to be honest signals that are cheap, and costly lies definitely exist — con men spend fortunes on looking credible). Could you lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, as a list I can bring back to the meetup? For each reading, say whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or just wrong, and — this is the part I actually care about — say which of the readings would genuinely support the 'wasted ad spend proves quality' move and which would leave it hanging. Also flag which version the people repeating these lines probably have in their heads. I'd rather have the full menu with verdicts than you picking a favourite reading and arguing for it, since I don't yet trust my ear for which reading was doing the work on Tuesday.
