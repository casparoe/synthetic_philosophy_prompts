I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training — my exposure to the field is limited to some popular essays and a documentary I watched over a holiday break — so please bear with me if I misuse a term of art. Last month my book club read Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, and around the same time I found myself reading about Hannah Arendt's reports on the Eichmann trial: her claim that Eichmann was not a monster but a careerist who had simply stopped thinking, a man of slogans and protocol rather than demonic ideology. The two things collided in my head and now I can't unsee it. Stevens the butler looks, on its face, like a literary cousin of Arendt's Eichmann: the professional vocabulary of dignity, greatness, and service to one's employer invoked as a substitute for judgment; the pride in executing duties flawlessly while treating their ends as none of his business; the retreat into the work itself at the decisive moments, such as serving drinks at Lord Darlington's crucial conference while his father is dying upstairs, or smoothing over the dismissal of the two Jewish maids because it would be presumptuous for a butler to question his employer's decisions.

Could you give me a proper reading of the novel through Arendt's idea — one that takes both the book and the concept seriously? Concretely, I'd like to know which scenes genuinely dramatize what she meant by thoughtlessness — the inability to pause and look at what one is doing from anyone else's standpoint, including one's own future one — and which scenes resist that reading. Stevens narrates the entire novel in retrospect and at least half-recognizes what he did to Miss Kenton and to himself, whereas the striking thing about Arendt's Eichmann was that he seemed incapable of that kind of reckoning even in the dock. Is Stevens's interiority a counterexample to the banality thesis, or is his elaborate machinery of self-deception — the nostalgia for a lost golden age of servants and masters, the framing of cowardice as dignity, the near-miss conversations with Miss Kenton — precisely the mechanism Arendt described, seen from the inside rather than the outside? And does it matter morally that Stevens's complicity is indirect (the maids, the appeasement he facilitated) while Eichmann's was the machinery of deportation itself? My book club is split between "he's just a sad, repressed old man, not a study in complicity" and "the novel is exactly about how ordinary people launder their participation in terrible things through professionalism," and I'd like a reading rigorous enough to say which side is closer to right, where the analogy genuinely breaks down, and what the novel shows us about the phenomenon that Arendt's courtroom observation, from the outside, could not.
