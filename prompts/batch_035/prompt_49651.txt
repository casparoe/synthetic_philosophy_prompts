there's an analogy that gets dropped into basically every heritability thread I read, usually to end the argument: asking whether a trait like height or IQ is mostly genetic or mostly environmental is like asking whether a rectangle's area is due more to its length or its width. genes and environment combine multiplicatively, so the question is malformed, and heritability numbers are noise -- or at least they tell you nothing about what would happen if you actually changed the environment. I bought this for years and honestly still repeat it at parties. but lately it's been bugging me that animal breeders use the additive heritability estimate (narrow-sense h²) to predict how much the next generation gains from a given selection differential, and it apparently works fine, which is a prediction about what happens when you change something. so the rectangle seems to prove too much. audit the analogy for me: what is even supposed to map onto what (trait variance to area, genes to length, environment to width?), where does the mapping break (genes and environment aren't independent orthogonal axes, they're correlated, environments vary while the rectangle's sides are fixed... though on the other hand a rectangle's area is nothing but an interaction, there's no independent contribution from either side, so is it secretly a good model of pure GxE?), and whether the conclusion it's used for -- that high heritability says nothing about malleability -- survives once you take the breeder's equation stuff seriously. if there's a better analogy in the neighborhood I'd want that too. please just write continuous prose, no headings or bullets or anything, and no citations, I'm not going to go check them.
