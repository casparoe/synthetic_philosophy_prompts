I teach an upper-level social epistemology seminar and I'm writing the take-home final. I want one essay question on the Condorcet jury theorem that can't be answered by reciting the theorem and its two premises. The math is not what I want to test — anyone in the class can state that if voters are each a bit better than a coin flip and their votes are independent, majority rule becomes arbitrarily reliable as the panel grows. What I want the question to turn on is the fact that the premises are doing all the work and they quietly pull against each other.

Here's the scenario I want baked into the question: a funding agency uses a panel of 101 reviewers to decide whether a grant proposal is scientifically sound. Each reviewer, considered on her own, is 51% likely to reach the correct verdict, so competence is satisfied — barely. But all 101 read the same proposal, and a single flaw in it (say a misleading methods section) tilts everyones judgment in the same direction at once. So the question should force them to confront: what happens to the independence premise when the "signals" voters receive are correlated through a common source, whether the theorem can be rescued by re-individuating the voters (e.g., counting distinct methodologies or independent replications as the voters rather than individual readers), and whether that rescue is a genuine fix or just relabeling the problem. And I want a part where they answer the cynical student in my 11am section who says the theorem is useless because once evidence is shared, sincere voters are stuck at 50/50 and no amount of them helps.

Give me the question itself (90 minutes to write), a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points worth 4 marks each (total 20), plus a one-line note per point on how students typically lose it.
