I'm an undergraduate taking an upper-level metaphysics course on causation, and I've been assigned to lead next week's discussion section on causation by omission. Please help me prepare a decision tree that will sort my classmates into positions on whether absences can be causes.

The starting point for the tree should be a concrete case: a gardener who was hired to water my plants goes on holiday without doing so, the plants die, and we're inclined to say the gardener killed them by not watering them. The tree should take someone from that starting commitment to a definite position, with each internal node phrased as a question about a case or a reaction that my classmates can answer without knowing any of the position labels — so nothing like "do you accept the causal powers view of absences?" but rather questions like "did something happen in the room during the week the plants went unwatered, and if so, was it an event?"

The tree must branch on at least the following issues, though you can add others you think belong: (1) whether the gardener's not-watering is a genuine instance of causation, a fake or surrogate instance (some weaker relation that merely mimics causation), or nothing causation-like at all; (2) whether the causal work is done by an event (some positive happening the gardener performed instead, or a state of the gardener) or by an absence itself; (3) the proliferation problem — I also didn't water the plants, and neither did my neighbor, the Prime Minister, or Genghis Khan, so the tree needs a node asking whether all of us killed the plants, and how the view being sorted into handles saying yes (or avoids saying it); (4) whether absences that cause must be absences of action by agents with the capacity and perhaps the obligation to act, or whether inanimate omissions can cause in the same way — I'd like at least one node to use a non-agent case, such as a pacemaker that fails to deliver a shock, to test this; (5) where, if anywhere, the omission is located — whether an absence needs a time and place to be a relatum of causation, and what the answer implies for views that demand spatial relations between cause and effect.

Please include at least two cases designed to pull apart positions that look similar: one contrasting an absent agent who had a duty with an equally absent agent who had none, and one contrasting an omission with a double prevention, so the tree can check whether someone treats these as the same phenomenon or different ones.

For the leaves: where a position on absences has a recognizable name in the literature, give it that name; where the combination of answers corresponds to no view anyone actually defends, label the leaf "unnamed" and add one sentence on its most awkward commitment (or, if you think it's incoherent rather than merely unexplored, say so). Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate — I'd rather tell my classmates honestly that the instrument runs out of discriminating power than pretend it doesn't.

Aim for roughly seven to nine leaves and keep every node answerable in a sentence by someone with no background in the literature, since most of my classmates are taking this course as an elective.
