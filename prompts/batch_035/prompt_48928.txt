You are a philosopher of education with practical experience designing professional training curricula. I run curriculum development at a flight training academy, and I need a decision procedure for classifying the competency statements we inherit from regulators and safety boards — anything from "state the crosswind limitations for this aircraft" to "execute an instrument approach" to "know when to abort a takeoff" — as knowing-that, knowing-how, or something the distinction doesn't cleanly capture. The classification matters practically: it determines whether we teach an objective in the classroom, in the simulator, or through supervised line experience, and whether regulation permits a written test as the assessment. Before writing the procedure, think carefully about where the philosophical debate actually bites for this use: the intellectualist claim that knowing-how is just a species of knowing-that, the cases of expertise that resist articulation (the senior pilot who cannot say how she knows an approach is going wrong but knows), and objectives that seem to change category as trainees gain experience, with facts absorbed into fluent performance. Then write the procedure itself: the questions to ask of a competency statement in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops and hands the decision to a curriculum committee because the question is one of values rather than classification, and which steps are honest judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Finish by running it on three statements: "state the fuel requirements for a diversion," "manage a slow cabin decompression," and "demonstrate sound aeronautical decision-making." If it classifies the third as something other than a teachable objective — which I suspect it will, and which would be inconvenient for us — let it, and tell me what to do about it.
