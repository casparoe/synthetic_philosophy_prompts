My college roommate and I took a decision theory seminar together years ago, and we've kept up a long-running game of trading hypotheticals. She's given firm verdicts on several of the classics, and I've been trying to figure out what general rule she's implicitly running before our next meeting. So far: on Newcomb's problem, with opaque boxes and a predictor who is right about 99% of the time, she takes only the sealed box — and when we pushed her by lowering the predictor's accuracy to 70%, she still one-boxed without hesitation. On Parfit's hitchhiker — you're dying in the desert, a driver will rescue you only if he predicts you'll pay him the reward afterward, you promise, he rescues you, and now you're standing safely in town deciding whether to hand over the money — she says she'd pay. But her stated reasons were maddeningly mixed: she said something to the effect that if she were the sort of person who'd skip out, she'd be dead in the desert right now, and also, more casually, that a promise is a promise. When we offered the variant where she could take a pill in the desert that would irrevocably compel her to pay later, she said she'd obviously take it, but she got visibly annoyed when I asked whether she'd take a pill compelling her to pay a driver she'd already been rescued by.

Next week I want to present her with two new cases, and I'd like to go in with a prediction. In the first, a gene is known to cause both a fondness for smoking and lung disease, but smoking itself has no effect on the disease; she has never been tested for the gene, nobody is predicting or observing her behavior, and she's deciding whether to light up. In the second, a being flipped a fair coin long ago, telling itself that if the coin landed tails it would give her a million dollars, and if it landed heads it would ask her for a hundred dollars today; the coin landed heads, the being has now shown up, no one else will ever know what she does, and the offer will never be repeated. So: what will she say about each case, and how confident should I be? More importantly, what are the live candidates for the rule generating her track record — for instance, a unified theory on which she chooses as if her decision were the output of a computation that also determined the prediction, versus something less unified, like evidential habits in cases involving predictors plus an independent commitment to keeping promises? Say what each candidate rule predicts for the two new cases, tell me which reading of her earlier answers you find most plausible and why, and describe the further case — one we haven't yet tried on her — that would best separate the candidates from each other.
