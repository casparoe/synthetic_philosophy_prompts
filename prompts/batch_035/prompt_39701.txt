My closest friend and I spent most of last weekend arguing about whether an individual act can be wrong when its harm only materializes collectively, and I came away unable to pin down the principle she is actually applying. Her verdicts on the cases we managed to get through:

1. A person living an otherwise low-impact life taking one extra transatlantic flight purely for convenience: not wrong.
2. A person who flies long-haul every month and eats meat at every meal over decades, with affordable alternatives available and acknowledged: wrong — though she insisted that no single flight or meal within that life is wrong; the wrongness attaches to the whole pattern.
3. One person eating a single chicken sandwich when an equally available and equally liked plant-based option exists: not wrong.
4. A consumer's purchase that happens to be the one that triggers a new production run, say the ten-thousandth sale that prompts the factory to expand: wrong.
5. A citizen voting for the clearly worse candidate purely out of spite, in a national election her vote is all but certain not to decide: not wrong, though she thinks voting well is admirable.
6. An executive who could costlessly refuse to sign one routine directive whose individual effect on a collectively harmful process is nil, but signs it anyway: wrong — she said his position gives him responsibilities the rest of us do not have.

She has now posed three further cases, partly to test me, and I genuinely do not know what she will say:

(a) An ordinary engineer, one of ten whose signatures are jointly required on a routine directive contributing marginally to a collectively harmful process. Hers is the last needed; the other nine have signed; refusing would change nothing, since a deputy will sign the next morning, but the refusal would cost her nothing either. She is not an executive and holds no special authority.
(b) A person who eats meat only on weekends, every weekend, for decades, with easy alternatives available and the collective harm of the practice fully acknowledged.
(c) A spite vote cast in a mayoral election in a town of three thousand voters, where her vote has roughly a one-in-fifteen-hundred chance of being decisive, for a candidate who would plausibly make the town materially worse off.

Please predict her verdict on each of (a), (b), and (c); identify the candidate principles that fit her existing record about equally well (for instance a pure difference-making principle, a personal-pattern principle, a role-and-responsibility principle, and some form of expected-consequence principle that must somehow survive her verdict on the national spite vote); say which principle you would bet on and why; and, if her record turns out to be genuinely inconsistent rather than merely underdetermined, tell me that plainly instead of rescuing it. Finally, describe the single further case that would best separate the two most plausible principles, and state what each would predict about it.
