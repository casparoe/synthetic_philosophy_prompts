Every year in my decision theory seminar I run the dollar auction, two students bid each other past a dollar, everybody laughs, and we all agree that was irrational. This year I tried actually writing down my verdicts on escalation-ish cases and I can't find the principle behind them. Here's the list.

Bidding 95 cents when I'm already in at 90 (second place still pays): locally the right move — you lose 90 for sure or bid and probably lose less — and I'd do it, yet I also think the whole spiral is a paradigm case of irrationality. Staying in a PhD program I despise because I'm eight years in: irrational, sunk costs are sunk, no debate. Cortés scuttling his own ships: smart, even though it destroys options, because it forecloses retreat for everyone at once and the enemy can see that. Announcing I'll wage a ruinous war on anyone who attacks my ally, then getting attacked and waging the war even though it's now pure loss: I genuinely think I should follow through. But that last one bugs me — the war itself is a sunk cost being honored for sunk-cost reasons, so how is it different from the PhD, other than scale?

My tentative principle is something like: sunk costs count when they're paid in public, where others have watched you pay and will condition their future behavior on whether you keep paying. That handles deterrence, the ships, maybe firms finishing money-losing projects to protect a rep for finishing things. But it cuts both ways against me: it implies continuing the dollar auction IS rational once you're in it with the whole class watching (you'll be known as a quitter), and that feels flat wrong. And it can't touch the case I keep circling back to, which is that I think I should finish the dreadful 900-page novel I'm 700 pages into, and literally nobody knows I'm reading it.

So: is there one principle that saves all or most of these verdicts? Where exactly does the publicity clause break, and what's the least bad thing to give up — the deterrence verdict, the auction verdict, or the novel? Also tell me what your principle says about a case I haven't listed: a company sticking with a doomed product line because the team's morale, and their willingness to start hard projects at all, depends on believing that projects here get finished. I suspect that one is the real stress test but I can't tell which side it stresses.
