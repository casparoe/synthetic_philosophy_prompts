Been in a Kant reading group for about two years now and the argument over how the Formula of Universal Law test is actually supposed to work has gone nowhere, and I'm starting to suspect the problem isn't anyone's arguments. Sketch of the sides: the 'logical contradiction' people say a universalized maxim of false promising fails because if everyone lies when convenient, the institution of promising dissolves and the maxim can't even be carried out — the contradiction lives in the system of practices, which means the test needs an empirical premise about what practices are for. The Korsgaard-style practical contradiction crowd says no, the problem is that in a world where everyone reasons as you do, your act becomes impossible or pointless in itself — rational agency breaks, you don't need to go out and check facts about social institutions. And then there's a third group (Herman-ish) who claim the test was never meant as a standalone decision procedure at all, it just screens maxims for further scrutiny by the other formulas, so fighting about what kind of contradiction it generates is a category mistake.

What gets me is that the positions havn't budged in decades — O'Neill was sorting this out back in the 70s and 80s and the maps people draw today look basically the same. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement isn't really about Kant exegesis, it's about what a moral test owes you: whether it has to be self-contained, squeezing content out of the bare form of a maxim, or whether it's allowed to lean on background facts about purposes and needs — in which case what's actually being litigated, by proxy, is Hegel's old charge that the CI is an empty formalism. But maybe I'm wrong and it's a genuine fight about the text, or maybe it's two projects under one name: one group building a decision procedure, the other describing the structure of practical reason, and they only look like opponents.

Diagnose it for me. Verbal, substantive, or ships passing in the night? Use what each side actually claims, not a survey of the literature, and tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, a test case where the readings come apart cleanly (the suicide maxim and the indifference-to-others maxim are the usual suspects), whatever — or whether the honest answer is that this one just doesn't move, and why.
