I do research-adjacent work for a small institute that funds work on AI welfare, and we have room in the next funding cycle for roughly four researchers for three years, with a mix of philosophy and ML-interpretability backgrounds. The question we've committed to is whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer, and if so, what that would depend on. Our eventual deliverable is a policy-facing assessment that labs could actually use when deciding whether and how to monitor training runs, so the work has to be much more fine-grained than the usual "sentience is hard, be careful" gesture.

Rather than a survey, I want a defended prioritization of the open questions. Candidates we've been kicking around include, among others: whether reward prediction error is a better candidate for valence than reward itself, given that the TD-error/dopamine analogy cuts both ways; whether the fact that reward is an optimization target rather than a state of the agent undermines any straightforward identification of reward with experienced valence; whether model-based agents that simulate future trajectories during planning could suffer in the simulations, or whether simulated states are categorically different; whether offline RL agents that replay stored transitions re-experience anything; and whether there is a meaningful difference in moral risk between dense shaped rewards and sparse, punishing signals under non-stationary environments.

For each question you'd prioritize, I'd like the reasoning, not just the placement: what makes it upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers versus people who can run interpretability studies on actual trained agents, and what empirical findings over the next few years would move it up or down your list. Please also be explicit about the criteria you're using -- importance, tractability, neglectedness, how much downstream policy depends on it -- and where you'd accept trade-offs between them. Finally, tell me which of these questions you'd deliberately deprioritize despite their popularity, and why their answers wouldn't change much in practice.
