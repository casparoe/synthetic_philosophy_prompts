Write a satirical services brochure, roughly 650 words, from an invented firm — call it something like "Meridian Honor Solutions" or invent your own name — that sells accelerated moral revolutions to beleaguered clients. The target of the satire is the Appiah thesis that moral revolutions (the end of dueling, foot-binding, the Atlantic slave trade) are driven less by new arguments than by shifts in honor: the arguments had typically been on the table for decades or centuries, and what actually collapsed was the practice's ability to confer standing within the relevant honor group.

The humor must come from getting the thesis right, not caricaturing it. In particular:

1. The firm's core product is the honor flip, not persuasion. Somewhere in the brochure there should be a deadpan acknowledgment that arguments are stocked in bulk, have been available since time immemorial, and are thrown in free with every engagement — the scarce commodity is the flip.
2. The pricing model should track the fact that honor is positional and reference-group-relative: the firm only needs to flip enough of the relevant peer group, not the whole population, and the brochure can be breezily frank about this ("why pay for eight million minds when four hundred pairs of eyes will do?").
3. A tiered service offering: a full flip, a downgrade ("the practice survives but now reads as low-status"), and a legacy tier for practices whose honor has already collapsed but which persist anyway — with the firm declining all warranty for the residue, since a practice can outlive its honor.
4. At least one section must quietly mark the distinction between honor and dignity — e.g., a fine-print clause stating that dignity is not sold here, has never been sold here, and cannot be installed by this or any vendor.
5. A testimonial sidebar with invented clients or their descendants in period-appropriate voices (a pledge-society organizer, a naval officer tired of courts-martial, a committee of weary abolitionists) — no real quotations, no real names, just plausible period voices whose complaints track the actual historical mechanisms.
6. An FAQ in which the question "Couldn't we simply win the argument?" is answered with corporate cheer and total sincerity, and a question about whether the flip can be reversed is deflected in a way that uneasily reminds the reader that honor is contingent in both directions.

Constraints on execution: the piece should read as a real corporate brochure — section headings, numbered clauses, a tagline, an asterisk somewhere that resolves into something ominous or revealing. Keep the irony deadpan throughout; the firm believes completely in its product and in nothing else. A reader who knows the moral-revolutions literature should conclude that the author understands exactly what Appiah claims (arguments chronologically prior and inert; honor as the hinge; the reference group doing the work; the revolution not requiring universal persuasion) and should find the satire fair to the thesis rather than smug about it — the uneasiest thing in the piece should be how plausible the firm sounds, since its business model is just the mechanism described with the normativity stripped out.
