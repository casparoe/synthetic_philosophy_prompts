I've been down the rabbit hole of the biological function literature for like six moths now — it started because of an argument in a LessWrong-adjacent discord about whether it's meaningful to say an AI system "has the function of" predicting text, and it escalated from there — and I've become convinced the etiological people and the causal-role people are at least 40% talking past each other. Quick background in case you need it: one camp (the Wright/Millikan/Neander lineage) says the heart's function is to pump blood *because pumping is what hearts were selected for*, and this is supposed to explain why a diseased heart that's doing a terrible job still has the function of pumping — selection history, not current performance, fixes function, and that's also supposed to ground the normativity of saying the heart is *supposed* to pump. The other camp (Cummins and friends) says function talk is about what a part contributes to some capacity of the containing system, so if you're explaining how the organism circulates blood, the heart's function falls out of that explanatory analysis, and dragging evolutionary history into it is optional or confused. The frustrating thing is that each side has a clean counterexample to the other: etiological functions can float free of what anything currently does (what about a trait in the very first generation of a lineage, before selection has had any generations to act on it?), and causal-role functions multiply like rabbits — the classic line of attack is that on a naive causal-role account it becomes a "function" of clouds to rain, since that contributes to the water cycle, and the account can't cleanly say a heart is *malfunctioning* rather than just doing something it was never selected for.

Here's what I want you to do: write the joint statement that a committed etiological theorist and a committed causal-role theorist could actually sign after a serious adversarial collaboration — I'm imagining two philosophers of biology who've been sniping at each other in journals for a decade and finally sat down with a mediator. Give me (1) a numbered section of claims both accept, including about the phenomena that need explaining at all (why function language is useful in physiology and medicine, malfunction ascriptions, the apparent normativity of function talk); (2) a disagreements table where each row states the disagreement in wording its own side would endorse — really try to make each formulation neither vacuous nor slanted; (3) the cruxes — sub-questions where settling one would actually move at least one side — and for each crux, a concrete case or argumentative test both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. To seed it, at least one crux should involve this scenario I thought up (patch it if it's flawed): a population of bacteria engineered in a lab with a synthetic gene circuit that lets them digest a novel plastic, released into the ocean, where the circuit contributes to cell maintenance for a while and then, after some generations of selection acting on it, becomes fixed in the population. Was plastic-digestion the circuit's *function* back before selection ever got a grip on it — in the release generation? You can see how the two camps will want to answer differently and, more importantly, what each answer costs them, so make sure the document records those costs instead of sweeping them under the rug.

One more thing. Everyone who's read the sequences knows the move where you declare a two-sided debate "merely verbal" and dissolve it into disguised queries about definitions — but the etiological side will insist that normativity is a substantive residue the causal-role analysis just can't capture, and the causal-role side will insist the etiological appeal to history does no explanatory work the causal-role analysis doesn't already do. So if you conclude that a chunk of this dispute really is purely verbal — both sides keeping the word "function" with a subscript and nothing substantive left over — say exactly which chunk and defend the claim that it's verbal, rather than just asserting it. Formatting: numbered agreed-claims section, then a markdown table for the disagreements, then cruxes each with the test and both predictions. Keep the content dense but don't assume I know more than a motivated amateur — gloss any jargon in a line, and don't include citations or reading lists, just the ideas. And please be scrupulously fair to both camps; I've noticed almost everyone who writes about this has a horse in the race and it defintely shows.
