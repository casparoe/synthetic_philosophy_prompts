My forecasting group has a long-running argument I want you to settle. One member is deep into decline discourse: he says America today is late Rome, point for point — military stretched thin at the frontiers, cheap grain and entertainment for the masses, a currency that's slowly being debased, elites hoarding wealth while the middle hollows out, newcomers crossing the borders in numbers the existing population struggles to absorb. From this he concludes the U.S. has maybe a few decades left as a great power, since Rome's slide from overstretch to collapse ran a couple of centuries and, by his reading, we're already in the late innings. He backs it up with that old essay that circulates online claiming every empire in history lasted about 250 years, so if the analogy fits at all, we're basically just reading the scoreboard.

Here's what bugs me, and I want you to take both halves seriously. First, audit the analogy itself: give me the correspondence table (what plays Rome, the barbarians, the debased coinage, the Praetorian Guard, and so on), then where the mapping breaks — stuff like nuclear weapons changing the cost of great-power war, reserve-currency status working differently than Roman coinage did, and the question of whether hegemony in a globalized economy is even the same kind of thing as rule over a contiguous empire. Then tell me which disanalogies actually matter for a forecast and which ones are just color. Walk me through how the choice of reference class moves the forecast, and say whether his conclusion survives once you stop letting the single vivid case do all the work. If the honest answer is that the Rome comparison adds nothing beyond what a proper reference class already gives you, say that plainly.
