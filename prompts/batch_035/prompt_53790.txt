For about a year I have been recording my gut verdicts on cases in which someone may or may not know something about their own current condition, and I would like to know whether a single principle lies behind them. Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

1. A sommelier is given a wine that is just barely corked. Blind, he calls it: off, reject it. Asked a minute later whether he had known it was corked or merely guessed, he says honestly that he could not tell which. My verdict: he knew the wine was corked; he did not know that he knew it.

2. A mathematician has a proof she has checked twice. Verdict: she knows the theorem, she knows that she knows it, and I would say the same at every further level up, though I accept I cannot inspect them all.

3. A patient lies in a room cooled very slowly. Early on, I am confident he knows he is cold. Somewhere in the middle, I am confident he knows he is cold but not that he knows he knows he is cold. I cannot locate the point at which the first confidence lapses, and that worries me.

4. A sound engineer hears a hum sitting right at the threshold of audibility. Verdict: no one, not even he, is in a position to know that the hum is audible, though it is audible; had it been comfortably audible, he would have known. Knowledge here seems to require a margin.

5. A man with blindsight reliably reaches for the X on the screen while sincerely reporting that he has no idea whether anything is there. Verdict: he does not know the X is there. This is the verdict I would least like to surrender, and I cannot fully square it with case 1.

6. A woman is definitely and comfortably cold, but, having been raised to think that claiming knowledge is arrogant, she sincerely says she does not know she is cold. Verdict: she knows she is cold, and knows that she knows it, whatever she says.

A friend who has read a good deal of epistemology tells me that all of this is really about the KK principle — that if one knows, one is in a position to know that one knows — and that since I deny KK in case 1, I should deny it everywhere. Another says the operative notion is luminosity: that for certain conditions one is in a position to know one is in them whenever one is in them. Neither story fits all six.

So: is there one principle, or a small set of principles, that captures the pattern? If so, please state it precisely, tie each clause to the case that motivates it, and tell me what it predicts for cases I have not listed — for instance, a man who is just barely angry, or a dreamer who is dreaming that she is dreaming. And please be honest about which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot save, since I suspect that at least one of them has to go.
