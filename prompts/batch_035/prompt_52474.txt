I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about discounting the future, based on my gut verdicts on cases, and I want to know if there's a principle behind them or whether some of them just have to go.

Counting the welfare of people born in 2200 as worth less than ours in a climate cost-benefit model, purely because it comes later: wrong. Me choosing a mediocre trip this year over a much better one in three years because I want it now: not wrong, it's my life, my business. A government draining a national endowment to benefit current voters because the present generation feels more vivid to them: wrong. Putting off the dentist until a small filling becomes a root canal: dumb of me, but not a moral failing, and I don't think I'm doing anything wrong by caring less about future-me. Someone who donates to help people alive today instead of to an intervention they're confident helps many more people a century from now: fine, honestly maybe even admirable, and I cannot square this with my first verdict. My own pain at 80 counting for less than my pain at 35 in my own planning: I waffle on this one.

So the candidate principle I keep half-forming is something like: discounting just for lateness is legitimate when a single person allocates within their own life, and illegitimate whenever it compares the welfare of different people. Does that survive the charity case, or does that verdict have to go? Does it even survive the endowment case, since that's also present people being weighed against later people — but across a boundary my vacation case doesn't cross? And what does the principle say about two cases I haven't decided: whether the welfare of someone in a coma who wakes up in ten years counts fully for the hospital deciding about a cheap fix now versus an expensive one later, and whether a health ministry that can save 100 lives now or 150 lives spread over the next fifty years is allowed to discount. Please state the principle (or a small set of candidates) as precisely as you can, tell me which clause each verdict motivates, and be honest about which of my verdicts the cleanest version forces me to drop. If it helps, I'm sympathetic to the old idea that pure time preference is ethically indefensible, but apparently only when it's not my own time.
