I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and my department has hosted a year-long reading group pitting two of our postdocs against each other: one a committed illusionist in the style of Frankish and Dennett, who holds that phenomenal consciousness as standardly conceived involves properties that could not survive serious science, and that the appearance of such properties is itself something to be explained by cognitive science; the other a realist about phenomenal properties, who agrees that consciousness is natural but insists that the first-person facts resist the illusionist's deflation and that something genuinely qualitative is left over after all the functional and discriminatory facts are in. After a year of arguing, they have agreed to write up their residual disagreement in the form of an adversarial-collaboration joint statement, and they have asked me to prepare a draft they can both work from. Please write that draft for me.

The document should contain the following four sections:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These should be substantive, not platitudes: for example, claims both could sign about the existence and reliability of first-person reports as data, about what change blindness and inattentional blindness show about introspective access, about the failure of purely behavioristic or purely neural vocabulary to capture what subjects report, about the distinction between access consciousness and the supposed phenomenal residue, and about which parts of the dispute are empirical versus conceptual.

2. A table of disagreements, with each entry phrased so that its own side would accept the wording. The disagreements should be stated at the level of exactness the two camps have reached after a year: not vague statements that one side believes in qualia and the other does not, but precise formulations, for instance about whether the properties introspection seems to present are merely non-instantiated or actually incoherent; whether the illusionist's explanatory project requires the misrepresentation in question to be non-phenomenal in character, and whether such a misrepresentation is possible; and whether the realist's residue is a theoretical posit or something given prior to theory.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect at least one crux concerns whether a representation can misrepresent its own content as having intrinsic, private, ineffable character without itself having any qualitative character, and another concerns whether the phenomenal-neighborhood vocabulary that survives in cognitive science, in phrases like what it is like, can be paraphrased away without remainder. Identify the cruxes you take actually to be load-bearing, not every point of divergence.

4. For each crux, a case, experiment, or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it, together with each side's prediction about the outcome, recorded in advance. These may include empirical cases both camps already cite, such as blindsight, the delayed awareness of one's own decisions in readiness-potential paradigms, or studies of introspective limits; conceptual test cases both take seriously, such as whether a system could report its own internal states in full discriminative detail while being wrong about their character in the way the illusionist claims we are; or agreed methodological standards for what would count as a successful paraphrase of phenomenal vocabulary. For each, state what the illusionist predicts, what the realist predicts, and what outcome would count against each side's own view.

If, in the course of drafting, you conclude that some portion of their dispute is verbal, or that some disagreement dissolves once the two uses of a key term are disambiguated, say which portion and why. Conversely, do not manufacture agreement where none exists: the realist should not be made to sign anything that concedes that the phenomenal properties in question are illusory, and the illusionist should not be made to sign anything that concedes that there is a further fact for science to explain beyond the functional and representational facts. The measure of success is that each postdoc, reading the section describing their own view and its predictions, would say that it is put fairly.
