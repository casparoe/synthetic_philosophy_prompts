The question:

Q4. Our hospital network runs two clinics, Eastside and Riverside. Looking at 30-day survival for patients who got drug A versus drug B: within each clinic, A patients survived at a higher rate than B patients. But when the clinics are pooled, B patients survived at a higher rate than A patients.
(a) What has to be true of the data for this to be possible? Is it enough that one clinic is bigger than the other?
(b) Is what's going on here confounding? Does the reversal by itself tell you anything about how patients ended up on A versus B?
(c) A new patient who will be treated at Eastside asks which drug she should take. Which table — clinic-by-clinic or pooled — answers her question? Does your answer depend on facts about how patients came to be assigned to clinics or to drugs?

The student's answer:

(a) "The pooled survival rate for a drug is a weighted average of its clinic rates, with the weights given by clinic size. If the clinics are the same size, the pooled rate is just the midpoint of the two clinic rates. So for A to lose overall while winning in both clinics, you need the sicker clinic to hand out mostly A and the healthier clinic to hand out mostly B — clinic size by itself does nothing. Example with made-up numbers, 200 patients per clinic:
Eastside (sicker patients): A: 54/180 survive (30%), B: 4/20 (20%). Riverside (healthier patients): A: 18/20 (90%), B: 153/180 (85%).
A wins in both clinics, but if you add up the columns, B wins overall, 157/200 versus 72/200."

(b) "Yes, this is textbook confounding: the clinic — really the patient mix, severity, and so on — is tied both to which drug you got and to survival, so it's a confounder, and once you control for it the comparison flips back to the right direction. The general principle is that whenever conditioning on a variable reverses an association, that variable was a confounder and the disaggregated table is the causally meaningful one. Simpson discovered the phenomenon in 1951 and it's been called Simpson's paradox ever since."

(c) "She should take A. In her clinic, A beats B, and the pooled table only favors B because B was mostly given in the healthy clinic, which has nothing to do with the drugs themselves. The rule from part (b) applies here: when the pooled and disaggregated tables disagree, the disaggregated one is the one to trust, since it conditions on more information, and conditioning is always at least as informative as not conditioning. So no, the answer doesn't depend on how assignment worked — the stratified table wins in every case like this."
