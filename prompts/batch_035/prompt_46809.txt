hey so I'm a science/ideas journalist, I write mostly longform for a general audience, and I've got a draft (well, draft-ish notes) for a piece on akrasia and hyperbolic discounting that I want feedback on before I show my editor. the working title is "Your future self is a stranger with your credit card." please answer in American English!

the gist: the standard story I keep seeing in pop coverage is that akrasia = weakness of will = you're just bad, or the slightly nicer version, your brain is two systems and the impulsive one wins. that framing feels stale to me and also kind of wrong. here's the angle I want to take instead, and I'd love to know if it holds up philosophically and scientifically.

my pitch: if hyperbolic discounting is real (which I take it the behavioral econ literature says it is, pigeons and humans both discount delayed rewards in a curve where preference reverses as the reward gets closer), then akrasia isn't a failure of one weak will, it's more like a coordination failure among a bunch of temporally extended agents who happen to share a body. the me who decides at 9pm to run tomorrow and the me who wakes up at 7am have different interests, and each of them is individually kind of rational given their discount curves. so akrasia looks less like a moral failure and more like... a failure to form an institution? the piece I want to run with is that personal rules ("I never drink on weeknights," "I always run tuesdays") are literally institutions the self builds to solve its own internal collective action problem. like, the person is a little society and willpower is a constitution. which, now that I type it out, sounds either brilliant or like I've been reading too much economics.

specific things I'd love comments on:

1. is the analogy between the intrapersonal case and actual collective action problems (like overfishing or climate) doing real work or is it just vibes? like, in real commons problems the agents can at least in principle negotiate. can time-slices of a person negotiate in any meaningful sense, or is that where the analogy breaks? I gesture at precommitment devices (Ulysses and the sirens, apps that lock you out of stuff) as the "institution" but a skeptical reader will say that's just the old two-systems story with a flag on it.

2. accuracy check: am I getting hyperbolic discounting right? my understanding is that with hyperbolic (as opposed to exponential) discounting you get preference reversals — the classic example being preferring two cookies in 21 days over one in 20 days, but preferring one cookie now over two tomorrow. is that right? also is it fair to say the exponential discounter doesn't have this problem because their preferences stay stable? and I want to claim that hyperbolic discounting is arguably "rational" in some evolutionary sense, is that defensible or should I cut it.

3. there's a point where I want to say that on this picture, the classic philosophical treatments of akrasia — Socrates saying you can't knowingly do what you know is worse, Aristotle distinguishing being overcome from being impulsive, Davidson's tweak — are all arguing about a single-agent model that maybe just doesn't fit, and the two-agent/multi-agent framing dissolves the puzzle rather than solving it. is that fair to those guys or am I strawmanning for the sake of a punchy paragraph? be honest, I can take it.

4. the last section is the weakest: I claim that if akrasia is a coordination problem, then self-help advice should look less like "build willpower" (which on my framing is like telling a village to individually be less greedy) and more like institutional design — constitutional rules, bright lines, precommitment, maybe even "checks and balances" on your future self. does the essay need a serious objection here? the obvious one I can think of is that rules made by one time-slice can be tyrannical toward the others, and rigid personal constitutions seem like they can shade into something disordered (rigidity, scrupulosity, eating disorder energy). should I face that head on?

mostly I want to know: (a) is the core idea actually interesting or is it a dressed-up version of stuff everyone's already said, (b) where would a philosopher or an actual behavioral economist wince, and (c) if you had to kill one section, which one. tear it apart, I've got two weeks before my editor sees it. also flag anywhere my framing smuggles in a disputed empirical claim as settled, that's the kind of thing that gets me angry letters.
