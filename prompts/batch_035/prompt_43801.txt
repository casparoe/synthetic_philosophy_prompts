I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and in two months I'm giving the keynote at the annual meeting of a professional society whose members are actually building pieces of the relevant technology: large-scale neural interfacing, nondestructive brain mapping, and early whole-brain emulation work. The room will be mostly computational neuroscientists and systems engineers who treat substrate-independence as an engineering premise rather than a philosophical thesis; a substantial contingent of signed-up cryonicists who think of themselves as future patients and are touchy about anything that sounds like betrayal of the biological original; a couple of philosophers of mind who know the personal-identity literature cold; and one hospital ethicist on the program committee.

My keynote defends a position I haven't seen anyone in this debate quite hold, which is exactly why I'm nervous about the question period. Nearly everyone treats gradual replacement — swapping neurons for functionally equivalent silicon, one small cluster at a time, with the subject awake and reporting throughout — as the peacemaker between the camp that says uploading is death-plus-a-copy and the camp that says it is survival. The standard thought experiment concludes that either consciousness would fade so gradually that the subject could never notice, which seems absurd, or nothing of significance is lost at all. I want to argue that this shows less than either camp hopes. It shows, at most, that the subject's own testimony becomes worthless as evidence while the swap proceeds, and so the interesting questions migrate outward to the bystanders — toward what I want to call custody. During a replacement schedule that could run for years, someone must decide when the biological original loses standing to alter the plan; whether journal entries written mid-process belong to the patient or to the platform; whether a spouse's promises transfer automatically or require renewed consent; and who is entitled to halt or reverse the procedure. On my view, whether gradual uploading preserves a person is settled less by metaphysics than by whether these decisions are made rightly — survival, for this procedure, is partly procedural.

Please prepare my prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they could do, each phrased the way that segment of the room would phrase it — the em researchers in engineering terms, the cryonicists as advocates for a patient, the philosophers through the classic thought experiments. For each objection, provide a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front to buy credibility, and identify the question I am hoping nobody asks. Please write the prep sheet in American English.
