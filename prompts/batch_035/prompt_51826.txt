So this happened on our hiking trip last weekend and it's basically all I've thought about since. Quick setup: me and four friends were doing a ridge trail, weather was turning, and about two thirds up one of us, Priya, said "I think we should turn back." Nobody said anything for a few seconds, then everyone kind of shrugged and started walking down. On the drive home my two friends got into it about what actually happened at that moment on the trail. Maya (who's also in my collective action seminar with me) said "we decided to turn back" and meant it literally — the group decided, as a group. Dev said that's just a manner of speaking: what happened is that five individuals each separately decided to turn around, roughly simultaneously, and "we decided" is only a summary of those five private decisions. He pointed out that nobody pooled their minds — there was no discussion, no vote, no explicit agreement, so there's no extra group-level fact hovering over the five heads.

Maya came back with the obligations point, which is where it got interesting. She said even if there was no explicit agreement, once everyone stayed silent and turned, we became jointly committed to descending together, and that generates actual obligations: if Dev had suddenly said "actually I'm going on alone, see you at the car," he wouldn't just be doing something dangerous — he'd be letting us down, and we'd be entitled to demand an account from him, in a way we wouldn't be entitled to demand an account from a solo hiker we happened to meet on the trail who chose the summit. Dev thought this was crazy. He said you can't create binding obligations by not talking — on Maya's story he got locked into a contract by standing quietly in the rain, which would mean every awkward silence where you fail to object is an agreement. He also said the feeling of being "let down" can be explained without any group-level commitment: it's just each individual expecting a certain pattern and being annoyed when it breaks, like being annoyed when the bus is late.

Then Maya tried to draw a line: it's not any silence, it's a silence in a context where objecting was cheap and everyone knew that everyone was relying on everyone else also turning — she said that's totally different from, like, an audience member who claps because everyone else clapped even though she hated the show, where refusing is socially costly. And Dev's last move was that even if you grant some coordinated expectation, the right story is just interlocking individual intentions: Priya intended to turn and wanted us to turn partly through her saying so, we each intended to turn partly because we heard her and saw each other turning, and that loop of mutual awareness does all the work — no mysterious "we" subject needed on top. Maya said that loop explains the coordination fine but not the normativity: the interlocking-intentions story has no room for the thought "you can't just quit on us," which she claimed is exactly the thought we'd all have if Dev had bolted for the summit.
