I'm a final-year undergraduate taking a seminar on contemporary normative ethics, and we have just finished a unit on Scanlon. For my next paper I have to set out my considered position on contractualism and the aggregation problem, and I would like a consistency check before I commit anything to print. Here is roughly where I have landed.

(1) I accept Scanlon's contractualist test for wrongness: an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any principle for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject.

(2) I accept the individualist restriction, which is what draws me to the view in the first place: when we ask whether a principle could be reasonably rejected, we consider each person's complaint on its own, and a complaint never gains strength from the fact that others have similar complaints. Reasons for rejection are never summed across persons.

(3) In a plain rescue case — five people will die unless rescued at one site, one person will die at another, the prospective losses are the same for everyone, and I can reach either site but not both — I hold that I must save the five. The numbers count, and Taurek was wrong to flip his coin.

(4) I hold the same when the stakes are probabilistic: if I must choose between saving one person from certain death and saving a hundred people each from a one per cent risk of death (two expected deaths, but nobody on the hundred-person side faces anything approaching certain loss), I must save the hundred. This seems obvious to me.

(5) I think Scanlon is right about his World Cup case in What We Owe to Each Other: Jones is caught in the transmitter room of a television station with electrical equipment crushing his arm, receiving extremely painful shocks, and the only way to rescue him at once is to turn off the transmitter for fifteen minutes, interrupting a match that millions are watching and that will not be over for an hour. I hold that we must rescue him immediately, and that the viewers' disappointment, however widely it is spread, is not the kind of thing that could reasonably be invoked to make him wait.

(6) I hold the mirror-image verdict as well: it would be wrong to impose a day of mild headache on each of a million people in order to save one stranger from death — say, the only process that can manufacture a life-saving drug in time predictably leaves everyone in the region with a mild headache for a day. Small burdens borne by many must not be added up against a grave burden borne by one.

Before the seminar tears this apart, I would like to know whether it hangs together. Which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension, and which only look to be? Please rank any real tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally serious, and for each one tell me exactly what would have to give and what the revision would cost me elsewhere. If one of my verdicts can in fact be delivered by Scanlon's own machinery without any summing across persons — that is, if an apparent tension dissolves on inspection — please say so and show me the mechanism rather than assuming it. I honestly do not know where the problem is, if there is one.
