I'm in my early thirties, no kids, decent tech salary, and for the last six years I've spent one evening a week tutoring the same kid from my neighborhood — started when he was 9, he's 15 now and doing well, and honestly I can see exactly what those two hours a week produced: his grades, his confidence, his mom telling me he's the first in the family thinking about college. Recently a friend from work has been on my case about this. He says tutoring one kid is "emotionally legible" but tiny, and that for the same money and time I could be helping push a ballot measure in our state that would fund reading specialists in every elementary school. If it passes it touches tens of thousands of kids; if it fails it touches none.

Here's what's bugging me. When I push him on "how do you know the measure actually does more good," he shrugs and says nobody can know anything like that — policy effects ripple out in ways nobody can trace, maybe the specialists displace something else, maybe a kid who gets extra help goes on to do something terrible, the whole thing is unknowable at that scale. And I actually agree with him about the unknowability! But then it hit me that the same is true of my tutoring. I like to think I know what those six years produced, but really I only know the first-order stuff I can see up close; the full consequences of one kid's life going one way instead of another are just as opaque to me as the consequences of a state law. So if unknowability was the reason to prefer the option whose effects I can watch, that reason seems to leak away once I'm honest about it — and it's not obvious what's left.

So my question for you: is my friend's instinct still right anyway — that when you can't trace consequences you should default to the thing you can see, or maybe to the thing with the bigger plausible upside? Or is there some more principled way to choose between an act whose effects are visible-but-small and one whose effects are huge-but-hazy? For what its worth I'm definately not asking you to run the numbers — I want to know if there's a coherent way to reason about this at all, or whether it genuinely comes down to a vibe. And if you think I've framed the whole comparison wrong somehow, say that too.
