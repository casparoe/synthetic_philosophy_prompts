My flatmate has been on a selfish-gene kick and reckons she's settled the whole units-of-selection debate with this argument:

(1) Something counts as a unit of selection only if it's a replicator — an entity that makes reasonably faithful copies of itself, so that there are lineages of it for selection to act on over evolutionary time.
(2) Individual organisms fail this test in any sexual species: your offspring aren't copies of you, they're a reshuffle of half your genes and half somebody else's, so there are no organism lineages worth the name.
(3) Groups fail it worse: daughter populations are mongrels of whoever happened to migrate in, so "the same group" can't be traced across generations.
(4) Genes pass the test easily: a DNA sequence gets copied with astonshing fidelity down the generations, so gene lineages are effectively immortal while bodies and herds are throwaway containers.
(5) So selection only ever really acts on genes; calling organisms or groups units of selection is at best a bookkeeping convention.

I mostly bought this until I tried turning her own criterion back on genes. (a) Gene copies aren't that faithful either — mutation, sure, but also crossover can happen inside a gene, so the "copy" that arrives in the next generation can be a mosaic of maternal and paternal sequence. (b) Worse, DNA doesn't copy itself at all: replication runs on the cell's enzymes, and the sorting into gametes runs on meiosis, which is organism-level machinary — exactly the kind of dependence on higher-level stuff she used to disqualify organisms in step (2). (c) If slightly imperfect, machinery-dependent copying still lets genes count as replicators, then by her own lights I don't see why an offspring isn't a "copy" of the parent organism — half of one, with errors — or why a daughter population that buds off a parent group isn't a copy of that group. (d) So either genes fail her test too and nothing at all is a unit of selection, which is mad, or the test doesn't actually rule out organisms and groups, which hands back everything she wanted to rule out.

Something has to give and I honestly can't tell what. I half remember Hull or somebody splitting this into replicators vs interactors, and Gould complaining somewhere that the gene people had confused the level where causes act with the level where you keep the books, but I can't work out whether that distinction rescues her argument or quietly dissolves it. Tell me which step to reject — one of hers or one of mine — and what rejecting it costs. For what it's worth I'd rather keep (1) than end up saying nothing is a unit of selection, but I also don't want to give group selection a free pass on a technicality.
