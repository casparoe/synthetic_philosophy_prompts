I am a novelist finishing a book in which a retired actuary, who has spent her career pricing catastrophes, confronts a hospital administrator with a trade: consent to one instance of fifty years of unrelievable torture, or allow that a number of people too vast to write out in decimal notation — Knuth's triple-up-arrow territory — each receive a single dust speck in the eye on an otherwise unremarkable afternoon. In the afterword I intend to take a side, and I want to take the side the actuary rejects: that the trade is not merely objectionable but malformed, and that nothing is actually being weighed against the torture, because the specks-side of the ledger has no total.

The argument I want made on my behalf runs through a claim I can only gesture at from inside my craft: that a life has the structure of a narrative rather than a sum, and that trivial harms are narratively inert — they leave no trace in the subject's life-story, are not remembered, do not alter what the life was. I want to argue that only harms with narrative purchase can enter moral arithmetic, that adding inert events across persons is not adding magnitudes but counting tokens of a kind that has no magnitude, and that this is why the question "how bad are all those specks?" has no answer rather than a very large one. My professional experience is meant to be evidence here, not decoration: a novelist who inserts a speck into a character's day has not written a different character, and I believe this reflects something about what harms are, not just about what fiction can represent.

Please construct this argument properly, which means confronting the two rejoinders I cannot get past on my own. First, the slippery one: if only narratively salient harms count across persons, what happens to the ordinary cross-person tradeoffs we all accept — one broken leg versus a million stubbed toes, the tiny per-person risks that justify vaccine mandates, epidemiology as a whole? I need to know whether my principle draws a line or merely retreats from every application, and whether a defensible threshold can be found that is not arbitrary. Second, the devastating one: does narrative inertness not exclude the unremembered sufferings of the anesthetized, of infants, of those with dementia, from the moral ledger altogether — a conclusion I refuse to accept? I want the argument in its strongest form, told honestly where it must bend or break, and I want to know if what survives is a genuine restriction on aggregation or just my intuition dressed in narratological language.
