The idea I keep circling: in IRL you assume an agent's behavior is roughly optimal for some reward function and then infer the reward from the behavior. Stevens gives you about three decades of a near-perfect behavioral record -- flawless service to Lord Darlington, every errand run, every feeling suppressed. But that same behavior seems compatible with at least two very different reward functions: one where dignity-in-service really is his terminal value, and one where what he actually values is something like a life with Miss Kenton, but his "training" (the honor code he absorbed from his father and from the culture of service) has effectively reward-hacked him into a policy that looks maximally dignified. And since he's an unreliable narrator, we never get ground truth about his preferences either -- just behavior plus rationalization, which is exactly the kind of data an IRL agent would recieve if you only observed the human.
