My friend and I got into a huge fight about money pumps last night and I want you to settle it in essay form. Setup: someone's preferences cycle — they'd rather have a nice dinner than a movie, rather see a movie than stay home, but when you catch them tired on a wednesday they'd honestly rather stay home than go to the fancy dinner. Suppose also that each time they'll pay a dollar to swap for the option they currently prefer. Some shady character can walk them around the loop and drain their wallet forever. My friend says this is THE argument that preferences have to be transitive: being pumpable just is what being irrational means, so either your preferences were transitive all along or you're running some rational patch on top of a broken machine. I said the pump quietly assumes the person decides one trade at a time and never notices the loop — they'd see that the fourth offer brings them back to where they started, minus money, and refuse, without their underlying preferences getting any more transitive. He replied that this noticing is itself the rationality we were after, that we've just located transitivity at a meta level, which felt sneaky but I couldnt quite say why.

Write an essay on whether money pump arguments actually show that intransitive preferences are irrational, or only that a certain kind of unreflective agent is exploitable, and whether those two things come apart. Cover the standard escape routes — binding yourself to a plan in advance, planning around your own future choices, refusing trades that you recognize as part of a pattern — and for each say whether it rescues the agent or just concedes my friend's point in different words. You also have to actually take a side at the end: does the money pump argument work as an argument for transitivity, does it establish something weaker, or does it fail outright? Don't cop out with "depends what you mean by rational" unless you genuinely defend that line.
