I'm a senior software engineer (no formal philosophy background; everything I know about this comes from engineering blogs, a couple of decision-theory adjacent books, and a lot of postmortems), and in two weeks I'm giving a talk at our company's internal engineering conference. My thesis: when we estimate our own projects, we should treat the outside view -- what happened to similar projects in the reference class, including all the ones that failed -- as a prior that only a specific kind of inside information can override, and we should be honest that most "but our case is different" arguments don't qualify as overrides. To make this actionable I've built a small framework I call the "defeater test": an inside-view claim only licenses moving off the reference-class base rate if it names a causal mechanism that was *absent* in the reference-class cases (not merely present in ours with more intensity), and it has to survive the observation that members of the reference class usually believed they had such a mechanism too. I also want to argue that "epistemic modesty" here is about calibration, not timidity: deference to the base rate is a claim about where your information lives, not a reason to stop doing technical diligence. My intended audience objection-landscape is complicated, so I want a prep sheet.

The audience: roughly 150 engineers from across the org, but the people who will actually ask questions are a specific cast. There's a cluster of staff/principal engineers who are deeply invested in bottom-up analysis -- they think estimation failures are failures of rigor, and that averaging over other people's projects is what you do when you haven't done the work. There's a director who swears by our team velocity metrics and planning poker, and who will hear my talk as an attack on the tooling she built. There's one former quant who left finance for engineering and thinks reference-class reasoning is obvious and beneath discussion; his objections will be about my framework's sloppiness rather than its conclusion. And there's a VP of platform who can name three teams inside this company that beat their base-rate estimates badly, and who believes that if you always defer to the distribution you can never rationally greenlight anything exceptional.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from *this* audience, in the order they'll probably arrive and flagged by how much damage each could do, each phrased the way that particular person would put it (the staff engineer's version of "the outside view is an excuse for not thinking" is not the VP's version of it). For each, give me a two-minute reply that stays inside my framework where possible, and a candid note on where the reply is soft -- especially for the VP's exceptionalism objection and for the former quant's likely point that my defeater test, applied consistently, might never permit *any* inside override, which would make my framework unfalsifiable within a single project's lifetime. Include what I should concede up front (I suspect the velocity-metrics director deserves a real concession, not a dodge) and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks, which for me is probably whether I followed my own framework on the project I'm currently leading. Close the prep sheet with your honest assessment of which single reply has to land for the talk to succeed, and what to do if it doesn't. Keep everything in the vocabulary of working engineers -- no philosophical jargon unless you define it in terms I'd actually use on stage.
