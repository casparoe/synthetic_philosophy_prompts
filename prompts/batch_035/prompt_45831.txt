I am a reliability engineer with no philosophy training but a long-standing curiosity about it. My working vocabulary is the one I use for root-cause analysis of system failures: fault trees and their logic gates, FMEA with its severity–occurrence–detection ratings, common-cause analysis, latent or dormant conditions that sit undetected until they line up with a demand, defence in depth, single points of failure, the distinction between a component fault and a functional failure, and the 'five whys' as a practical heuristic rather than an article of faith. I have read enough to know that philosophers have an entire literature on causation by omission — the gardener who fails to water the plants, the bodyguard who fails to take the bullet, the doctor who fails to give the antidote — and that they disagree about whether absences can be causes at all, and if so how.

Restate the main positions in that debate in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report. The positions I want translated, at minimum, are these: the counterfactual-dependence view, on which the gardener's not watering counts as a cause because the plants would have lived had he watered; the production view, on which causation requires a physical process connecting cause and effect, so that absences, having no location and carrying no conserved quantities like energy or momentum, cannot strictly cause anything; Mackie's INUS-style analysis, on which a cause is an insufficient but necessary part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition; Hall's distinction between causation as dependence and causation as production, and his claim that ordinary causal talk equivocates between them; and the trouble cases involving double prevention, where one event causes a failure precisely by preventing something that would have prevented it. Where a position has no natural home in my vocabulary, say so plainly rather than forcing a home for it.

Then tell me what didn't survive the translation. Specifically: which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto things I already do — for instance, whether fault-tree OR-gates already treat omissions as full citizens of causal explanation, or whether the standard practice of pruning the infinitely many candidate omissions down to those relevant to a safety function is my field's implicit answer to the philosophers' 'problem of proliferating absences'; which parts had to be distorted to fit, and how; and which parts have no expression at all in my vocabulary. Finally, I want your view on where the residue points: is there something the philosophers are arguing about that fault trees, latent conditions and FMEA simply cannot say — and if so, is that because my vocabulary is missing something, or because my field quietly replaces the metaphysical question with a practical one (what to fix before the next demand) that dissolves the philosophical worry? I have a suspicion that engineers tolerate absences as causes precisely because our aim is prevention rather than attribution, and that the philosophical squeamishness is downstream of an interest in what actually happened, not what to do next — tell me whether that suspicion survives contact with the debate.

Please begin your response with the residue verdict itself — one or two sentences stating, up front, what did and did not survive the translation and where the residue points — and then give the reasoning and the translations, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
