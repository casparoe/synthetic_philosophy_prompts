Every time I write a piece about longtermism or the effective altruist crowd, I hit the same wall: population ethics. Everyone tells me the total view leads to Parfit's repugnant conclusion, everyone tells me the alternatives all blow up somewhere else, and after 40 years nobody's moved. So instead of the usual tour of total vs average views, I want a number: what's your actual credence that there exists a population axiology — a consistent way of ranking worlds by the wellbeing in them — that is both formally coherent and doesn't violate core intuitions about how lives and populations compare?

Decompose it for me. Something like: the probability that Arrhenius-style impossibility theorems really do block every escape route, given that the "adequacy conditions" they encode are themselves just intuitions formalized; the probability that one of those conditions is mistaken and should just be dropped — and if so which one (accepting some version of the repugnant conclusion? avoiding the sadistic conclusion? the idea that additional worthwhile lives add value at all); and the probability the question is ill-posed from the start, i.e. that "better than" simply isn't a total order over populations the way it is over outcomes for a fixed set of people. Give each term a number and one line of justification, combine them, and then tell me which term the final credence is most sensitive to — and what argument or result would move it the most. I'd also take a straight answer on whether I should tell my editor this beat is dead.
