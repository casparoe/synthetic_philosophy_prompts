I'm a retired instrument fitter with no academic background whatsoever, but I've spent the better part of twenty years reading history on my own — I started with the Egyptians, drifted into the question of how we know what we know about dates at all, and ended up spending more evenings than I care to admit on how the conventional timeline was actually assembled. I belong to a regional "hidden history" society — some seventy members, meeting monthly in the back room of a library — whose whole character is affection for the unfashionable account: we've hosted talks on the Sea Peoples, on the difficulties of the Bronze Age collapse, on Velikovsky (twice, both warmly received), and on whether the Sphinx is older than the Egyptologists allow. I love the club precisely because it is not a university common room.

Next season's headline slot has been offered to a visiting speaker who promotes the New Chronology — the school associated with Anatoly Fomenko, which holds, roughly, that recorded history has been stretched by a millennium or more, that whole dynasties are duplicates of one another misfiled centuries apart, and that virtually everything before about AD 800 is construction or forgery. The committee has asked me to give a short talk before they finalise the invitation, arguing that the society should decline — not because the claim is heterodox, which would betray everything the club is, but because there is a principled difference between a heterodox claim that still plays by the rules of evidence and one that is built so that it cannot lose, and the New Chronology falls on the wrong side of that line.

My own argument, worked out on my own over the years, has three planks. First, a claim earns a hearing by saying in advance what would count against it; as far as I can tell, the New Chronology absorbs every anomaly — every cross-check that disagrees is dismissed as part of the same grand fabrication, and every cross-check that can be re-read is claimed as support. Second, the theory owes us an account of the forgery it posits that is at least as economical as the history it replaces: I want to ask how and why scribes, astronomers and coin-cutters across three continents coordinated a single false timeline for centuries before anyone kept a central record. Third, the physical cross-checks: I expect the speaker's answer to carbon dating to be that the calibration curves were themselves fixed by the conventional timeline, so the method only ever returns the dates it was fed; my sense is that this badly misdescribes how calibration actually works, but I couldn't defend the details under questioning, so I intend to lean on the convergence of independent methods — tree rings, ice cores, annals, eclipses — rather than on any single one.

What I need is a question-period prep sheet, and the objections must be the ones this particular room will raise, phrased in this room's vocabulary, not a seminar's. Please give me: the seven or eight objections I will actually face, ordered by how much damage each would do to the vote that follows my talk, each phrased roughly the way a member of this society would put it; a reply to each that I can deliver in about two minutes, in plain language, with no footnotes and no appeal to credentials I don't have; and an honest note on where each reply is soft or where I ought simply to concede. I already know I should concede up front that consensus has been wrong before, that amateurs have humiliated professors before, and that genuine puzzles in the conventional timeline are what gave Morozov and his successors their opening — tell me what else belongs in the concessions. And I'd like the one question I'm hoping nobody asks: my suspicion is that it's the one about Schliemann — the businessman the professors mocked, who went and dug up Troy — because I've never worked out an answer to it that doesn't cut my own throat. If I'm wrong about that, tell me what the real killer question is.

Finally, please give the whole thing a bottom line in fixed form: end the prep sheet with a final line of exactly the form \make_or_break{...}, containing no more than twenty-five words, naming the single reply on the night that has to land if the vote is to go my way.
