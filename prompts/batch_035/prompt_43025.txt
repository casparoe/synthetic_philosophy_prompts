Last night I had a long and surprisingly heated argument with my closest friend, a palliative care nurse, about the numerical pain scales her hospital runs on, and it has left me unable to stop thinking about what those scales implicitly claim about the nature of unpleasant experience. Here is the practice as she described it. Patients are asked to rate their pain from 0 to 10. That single number, gathered in seconds, drives real decisions: analgesic dosing, escalation to a pain service, discharge timing. The same scale is applied to experiences as heterogeneous as a third-degree burn, a kidney stone, phantom limb pain, the deep ache of spinal metastases, and the distress that her field, tellingly, still files under total pain, which includes grief and spiritual suffering. Training material she was given as a student endorsed something close to the claim that pain is whatever the patient says it is, whenever the patient says it exists, and that the reported number is to be accepted as authoritative regardless of the patient's demeanor or vital signs. Beyond the bedside, the numbers accumulate: clinical trials aggregate these ratings into group means and mean changes, and those means figure in the evidence base for approving and reimbursing drugs. In effect, a burn's 7, a bereavement's 7, and a phantom limb's 7 are treated as the same quantity of the same thing, responsive to the same interventions, and comparable across patients by averaging.

My friend's side of the argument was that this is all a useful fiction that everyone quietly understands: the number is a communicative shorthand, not a measurement of anything real, since her migraines and her post-surgical patients' incision pain are not two amounts of one stuff but different kinds of experiences. My side was that something can't be a mere fiction and still be the variable on which randomized trials, drug approval, and dosing protocols are run. We never resolved it, so let me put the question to you.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of unpleasant experience that this practice presupposes. Does it require the view that all these experiences share a single common property, a common currency of felt badness, which the number expresses on something like a cardinal scale? Or does it require less, for instance only ordinal comparability within a patient and comparability of differences across patients? Be careful to distinguish what the practice strictly needs from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: would a hedonist who posits a single quale of unpleasantness, an ordinalist about subjective states, a functionalist who identifies pain with a role rather than a feeling, and a theorist who treats the numbers as nothing but conventions for triggering care protocols all be able to license the same bedside behavior, or do some of them diverge somewhere concrete in the pipeline? Notice also the tension inside the practice between two commitments: the quantitative aggregation presupposes an objective, shared dimension of unpleasantness, while the deference to self-report presupposes that each patient is the sole authority on that very dimension. Can both presuppositions be true at once, or does the authority claim quietly undermine the measurement claim?

I would also like you to address the mirror image of what philosophers of well-being call the heterogeneity problem for hedonism, since my friend's complaint was exactly that there seems to be nothing the scratch of an itch, the agony of a burn, and the ache of grief have in common, and that therefore there is nothing for the number to measure. If she is right that these experiences do not share a common felt property, what exactly has the pain scale been tracking all these years, and why does it work as well as it does? Would a practice built on her view have to change, and how, for instance by adopting multidimensional instruments that separately rate intensity, character, and affective distress, or by abandoning numbers in favor of qualitative descriptors and function-based triage of what the pain prevents the patient from doing? Finally, tell me whether the inconsistency I think I see is real or merely apparent: is a hospital that treats pain as a single measurable quantity in its trial data while treating it as irreducibly plural in its humanities seminars actually committed to contradicting itself, or are these two parts of the practice compatible under some one coherent theory you can state?
