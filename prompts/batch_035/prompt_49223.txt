I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I've been invited to run a participatory session at a weekend science festival for curious general audiences — about 25 adults, 75 minutes, in a large open room with movable tables. The theme I've chosen is the question Douglas Hofstadter stages in the two linked dialogues of Gödel, Escher, Bach — the Prelude and the Ant Fugue — where Achilles and the Tortoise meet an Anteater who is on friendly terms with the very ant colony he feeds on, a colony he calls Aunt Hillary. The Anteater's claim, roughly, is that the colony can think and even converse, though not a single ant can; individual ants are just twitchy bugs, but their organized twitches at a larger scale constitute a mind. I want my audience to run into this problem with their own bodies before I name it.

Design the session for me. Specifically:

1. A colony-level task that participants, playing ants under strict local-information rules — no talking, no gesturing, no global vantage point; each ant may exchange simple tokens only with ants in immediate physical contact — must accomplish, even though no individual can hold the solution. My rough idea is a pheromone-token mechanic in which the colony must select among three feeding sites of different quality and then commit to one, with positive feedback and some kind of quorum rule for the commitment, and with ants dying and rotating out continuously so the colony's membership churns even while its decision holds. But I'm open to a better structure if you have one; if you keep mine, specify the token colors, the site-quality values, the quorum threshold, and how the ants' turnover is staged.

2. Two special roles that dramatize the levels-of-description problem. A Queen, who arrives wanting to command the colony and is allowed only one narrow, essentially reproductive function — and who should discover mid-game that command is impossible from her position, since real ant queens don't rule anything. And an Anteater, played by a volunteer, who removes ants from the trails at intervals — and whose predation, counterintuitively, helps the colony's pattern stay clean, the way the Anteater in Hofstadter's dialogue describes his eating of distressed ants as a kind of surgery. Tell me how to brief each role privately, and when in the debrief to reveal each role's true function.

3. A second, harder round in which the distinction Hofstadter draws between a signal — a token or small cluster with a fixed, local meaning — and a symbol — a larger pattern whose meaning depends on the state of the whole colony — becomes visible in the gameplay itself. For instance, the same token having different effects depending on where in the larger pattern it appears. The round should end with the colony having done something that no ant could be said to have done, and with the participants able to see, from a spectator's replay or photograph, that the pattern was the thing that mattered.

4. A debrief that maps specific moments from the session onto the philosophical questions: where, if anywhere, was the decision located? What stayed the same while the ants turned over? Is talk of Aunt Hillary a metaphor or a literal claim, and what would make it literal? And how does any of this bear on brains and on the self — given that the standard moral of Hofstadter's story is that what a person is, is a pattern at the colony level of some very fast ants called neurons. The debrief should also say frankly what my participants are entitled to conclude and what they are not, since a staged game is not an evolved colony.

5. Predicted failure modes. Some participants will try to talk. A faction may try to impose a plan by huddle or majority vote before the game starts, or simply march as a bloc to one site and call it pheromone logic. For each predictable failure, tell me whether to forbid it, ignore it, or exploit it as a teaching moment, and what it shows — I suspect the top-down-control temptation is itself the best evidence for how unintuitive colony-level description is.

6. An honest paragraph on what the game cannot reproduce: the mechanics are designed by me, with a target outcome I already have in mind, whereas a real colony's intelligence was not designed to be legible to anyone; and my participants know from the start that they are enacting a point I have chosen. Name what I must supply in words that the rules cannot, and where in the session I should say it.

Materials should be cheap and quotidian — index cards, colored chips, tape, a stopwatch. Assume no prior reading, and arrange things so that the phrases emergence and levels of description are not spoken until the debrief. Please give me the full run sheet: setup, timings, rules as they would be read aloud to participants, the private briefing cards for the Queen and the Anteater, the debrief outline, and the postmortem of failure modes.
