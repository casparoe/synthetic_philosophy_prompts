I've been reading about Nāgārjuna's tetralemma (the catuṣkoṭi: exists, doesn't exist, both, neither) and I keep seeing the take that he was basically a dialetheist before it was cool, i.e. that "both exists and doesn't exist" shows he accepted true contradictions the way Graham Priest does. But something feels off about that to me. Priest treats a contradiction as a genuine claim that is true (at the limits of thought, etc.), whereas a lot of Madhyamaka commentary reads the four-cornered negation less as an assertion and more as a way of dissolving the whole question — the point being that none of the four options is assertible, including the contradictory one, since even emptiness is empty. So my question: compare the tetralemma with Priest-style dialetheism. Where does the analogy actually hold up, and where does it break down? In particular I'd like to know whether the "both" and "neither" corners of the tetralemma work like dialetheia at all, or whether they're doing something closer to refusing to play the game of assertion in the first place — and if it's the latter, does that make Nāgārjuna closer to some kind of quietist than a paraconsistent logician? Sorry if this is a bit rambly, I havent quite gotten these distinctions straight in my head yet.
