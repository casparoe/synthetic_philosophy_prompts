I'm a high school senior, and I've gotten obsessed with the question of whether you can rationally choose an experience that will change your values — the kind of thing philosophers apparently call transformative experiences. To figure out what I actually think, I've gone through cases and recorded my gut verdicts, and now I want to know what principle, if any, explains them.

My verdicts: choosing a college where I expect to acquire a passion for a subject I currently find dull — rational, people do this all the time. A friend deciding whether to audition for a music conservatory, which would reshape her whole identity around music — also rational, and I even think her private teacher is in a position to advise her well. Starting a medication for depression that will probably change her personality — fine. A deaf couple deciding to give their hearing child a cochlear implant the child can't yet consent to — fine. An eighteen-year-old deciding whether to have a baby based on her current values — not fine, or at least not really a rational decision in the way the others are. Taking a one-time pill that permanently rewires you to love hard work — not fine. Taking a one-time pill that permanently makes you content with a small, idle life — definitely not fine. Joining the military out of high school — fine, even though it's at least as identity-changing as having a baby, which is one of the cases I can't shake.

I've tried out a principle of my own: a transformative choice is rational only if people who have already had the experience can tell you enough about it for you to compare it with your current life. But this saves the conservatory and baby verdicts and seems to condemn the medication one, which I want to keep. So maybe the real distinction is between changes that make you better able to pursue what you already care about and changes that replace what you care about — except I can't tell which side the cochlear implant falls on, or whether "contentment with a small life" is worse on that test than depression medication.

Please structure your answer as follows: first, a section called "Candidate principles," stating each in a single sentence and naming the case verdict that motivates it; second, a section called "The best principle, stated precisely," of roughly 150 words, including how it classifies the cochlear implant case; third, a section called "Two new cases," of about 100 words each, where you invent two transformative choices I haven't mentioned and give the principle's verdicts; and finally a section called "Verdicts to give up," of about 100 words, stating plainly which of my gut verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and why I should let them go rather than complicate the principle further.
