I teach philosophy at a mid-sized state school, and because I made the mistake of saying "game theory" out loud in one meeting, I'm now on the Faculty Senate's Committee on Academic Standards. Here's our situation, and I'd like your help with the actual document we have to produce.

Over about the last fifteen years our institutional GPA has crept from roughly 2.9 to 3.4 while every measure of learning we have (exit exams in the gen-ed courses, standardized tests where students take them, hours studied on the national surveys) has stayed flat or drifted down. Everyone knows the grade inflation is happening and basically nobody can stop it, and the mechanism is not exactly mysterious. Each department sets its own grading norms. Students pick majors partly on expected GPA. Departments that grade harder lose majors, and lost majors mean lost faculty lines, which means losing your officemate. Faculty who hold the line get hammered on evals, which now matter for retention. Adjuncts whose contract renewal depends on enrollment numbers have essentially zero room to be the one tough grader. So every actor is doing the locally rational thing and the aggregate result is that our grades mean less every year.

Our committee has to recommend something to the Senate by spring. The live options are (a) a mandated common distribution -- no more than, say, 20% A's in any section of any course, enforced centrally, or (b) give up on grades entirely and instead print the course median next to each grade on the transcript so employers can do the discounting themselves. One guy on the committee keeps saying this is a coordination problem and you can't legislate your way out of a coordination problem, which as a philosopher I find both probably right and suspiciously convenient, since it's also the position that requires him to do nothing.

So: act as an expert in collective action and institutional design, someone who knows the races-to-the-bottom / multipolar-trap literature cold but isn't a zealot about it -- these framings get overapplied, and I'd rather find out ours doesn't fit than get a document that assumes it does. Before you write anything, do a quick brainstorm and pressure-test the setup: is grade inflation really a prisoner's-dilemma-style race to the bottom, or is it something else wearing that costume -- a preference change driven by the eval system, a principal-agent problem between faculty and adminstration, a positional good where only relative GPA matters? Then draft the memo I'll circulate to the committee. It should recommend one of the two options or argue for something better if you see it, lay out the reasoning in plain language my non-philosopher colleagues will actually follow, give the "can't legislate your way out" objection its strongest form rather than a strawman, and include a short section on what would have to be true for our unilateral policy to backfire on our own students. Under 800 words, and no name-dropping -- this committee is not impressed by citations, they want the actual argument.
