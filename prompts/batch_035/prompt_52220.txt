I'm a final-year psychiatry resident with a philosophy undergrad degree, and I've become a fairly committed partisan of the looping-effects picture of psychiatric classification — the Hacking-style view that the categories in our manuals are interactive rather than indifferent kinds. On this picture, a DSM category doesn't passively describe a condition sitting still; the people classified can know how they're classified, care about it, and adjust, and the classification loops back through patients, families, advocacy organizations, insurers, and clinicians' expectations, altering the very phenomena it purports to track. The historical cases are what convinced me: nineteenth-century fugue, the late-century explosion and then retraction of multiple personality, even the way certain diagnoses seem to arrive in a culture and then travel. My working conclusion is that diagnostic categories are partly constitutive of the conditions they name, not just descriptions of them, and that the dream of a psychiatry classifying people the way mineralogy classifies rocks is confused at the root.

What I want is a serious map of what would count against this — not a rehearsal of why it's clever, but the defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Some things I've been chewing on and can't resolve myself: How much stability would have to show up, and at what grain, before the view is in trouble — if a construct like schizophrenia holds roughly constant in prevalence and structure across fifty years, radically different cultures, and successive manual revisions, does that count against looping, or is stability exactly what a settled loop looks like? What if the loops turn out to operate only on self-report, help-seeking, and diagnostic fashion, leaving the underlying condition untouched — does the view survive that narrowing, or does it collapse into a much weaker and less interesting claim about labeling behavior? Is there a principled way to distinguish the truly interactive cases from the ones where the classification tracks something indifferent — chromosomally-defined syndromes seem to sit near the indifferent end, and I don't know whether the view has anything principled to say about that gradient, or only a smell test. And I have a real worry about insulation: the view seems able to explain any change as looping and any stability as a loop in equilibrium, which makes it hard to see what observation would embarrass it.

A few more things I'd like you to take seriously. Someone in a journal club once claimed that Hacking himself later sounded a retreat on how much classification actually changes people — that he thought he'd been too free with the looping talk — though I've never tracked down where he says it, so treat it as hearsay. If true, does the founder's own hedging count against the view, or is that just ordinary caution? Also, don't spare me the question of what the view predicts that a rival — say, a straightforward biomedical realist who thinks our categories are latching onto natural disease entities and will eventually be refined into them — doesn't; I'd like to know what the two pictures actually disagree about observationally, if anything. And since I'd like to end with something concrete: name the single finding about a currently contested diagnosis (autism prevalence, ADHD, pediatric bipolar — your pick) that should move me most, and in which direction. I'm comfortable being told the view is well insulated and that this is defensible for a thesis of this kind, or being told it's well insulated and that this is fatal to it — but I want the reasoning, not the verdict by fiat.
