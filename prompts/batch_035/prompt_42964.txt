I host a small philosophy podcast and next week's guest is a hardcore utilitarian who thinks supererogation is a myth. Her line is basically that once you rank acts by the good they do, the whole idea of acts that are praiseworthy to do but not wrong to skip just collapses — either doing the better act is required, or the acts are morally on par and the rest is taste. Draft me eight to ten interview questions that actually press on this instead of letting her restate it. Requirements:

- At least one question built around the classic case of a soldier throwing himself on a grenade to save his squad. Does her view really entail that whoever can do it at least cost is obligated to? And if she bites that bullet, I want a follow-up about whether it's still supererogation in any recognizable sense once it's just the demanded optimum.
- At least one question on the standard picture of supererogation — optional, beyond duty, praiseworthy when done, no blame when omitted — and whether she can keep the praiseworthiness part while dumping the optionality part.
- One question on the mirror-image category, suberogation: acts that are boorish or uncalled-for without being wrong (a mild slight you had every right to commit, so to speak). If she accepts that asymmetry-free utilitarian ranking of acts, suberogation should be just as unreal as supererogation — is she really willing to say that?
- One question pressing a thought a friend of mine had that I can't get out of my head: a "moral savings account" idea, where heroic acts build up credit that an agent can later cash in to justify doing less than her fair share without being blamed. If utilitarians can't make sense of that banking intuition (and it seems like they can't, since on her view the heroic act was owed anyway), maybe that's evidence something's gone wrong somewhere — either with the banking intuition or with her ranking.

For each main question, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give (she's fond of saying the felt difference between the obligatory and the heroic is just a fact about our psychology, not about duty). No generic stuff about how she got into philosophy or her new book or whatever, I realy want this one to hurt.
