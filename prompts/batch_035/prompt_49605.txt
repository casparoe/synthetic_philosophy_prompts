My coworker Dana and I have been arguing for like eight months now and it's getting embarrassing. It started with a postmortem: we had an outage because a null check was missing from a payments service. I wrote "the missing null check caused the outage" in the doc and Dana flat out refused to sign it. Her position: absences can't cause anything, because an absence isn't a thing, it's just a place where a thing could've been. Nothing doesn't *do* stuff. My position: sorry, but that null check not being there is exactly what took us down, and our entire industry runs on this way of talking — "the missing timeout caused the cascade," "the cron didn't fire so the cache went stale." If all those sentences are false or meaningless I'd like to know before I write another postmortem.

I've read around enough to know philosophers fight about this too (something like "causion by omission"), and I gather the two camps map roughly onto us: people who say omissions genuinely cause things (and that this happens absurdly often — like, the absence of a meteor is "causing" my desk to stay undamaged right now), and people who say omissions only count as causes relative to some norm or expectation — the gardener who didn't water the plants versus the Queen of England who also didn't water them, where only one feels like a cause and the difference is that the gardener was *supposed to*. Dana is obviously in that second camp.

We tried writing down what we actually agree on and it went nowhere, so I want the thing we should have produced instead. Draft the joint statement Dana and I could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want:

1. The claims we both accept — about omissions, counterfactuals, whatever — phrased so neither of us would wince at signing.
2. Our exact disagreements, in wording that each of us would accept as a fair statement of our own view.
3. The cruxes — sub-questions where settling one would actually move one of us, not just restate the disagreement in fancier words.
4. For each crux, the argument or test case both of us agree bears on it, with each of our predictions written down in advance.

A few things we keep tripping on that the statement has to actually handle rather than paper over:

- The case I think is mine: an engineer explicitly disabled the failover last week (an action) versus the failover was never built (an omission). The outage looks the same either way and my gut says the causal talk should be symmetric there. Dana says they aren't symmetric and the asymmetry matters — I don't fully follow her why yet.
- The case Dana thinks is hers: our postmortems say things like "the pager went unanswered," which only makes sense because a specific human was on call. If I get to say the outage was caused by every person on earth not being on call, then everything is caused by everything absent, and causal talk becomes useless for figuring out what to fix.
- A wrinkle neither of us has a name for: the deploy *was* configured to run migrations, the config file existed but was empty. Is an empty file an omission or an action? We both keep flip-flopping on it, so the statement should either resolve it or explain why it's genuinely hard.
- I want an honest verdict on whether any chunk of our dispute is verbal — like, would we both be fine if my sense of "caused" just got a different word and Dana kept the plain one? If part of it is only that, say which part and why, because if the whole thing is verbal I'd honestly rather find out than win.

Don't be diplomatic to the point of mush — if a section is only signable because it leans on some distinction, name the distinction. And at the end, tell me where you think your own draft is most likely to fail: which section is most likely slanted without me noticing, and what Dana or I would probably say on a careful read that would change how you'd have written it. Also flag if you can see a resolution of one of the cruxes that would make one of us fold, because at this point I mostly want us to stop having this argument in code review.
