I'm preparing a paper on the unilateralist's curse — the problem, introduced by Nick Bostrom and colleagues, that when many actors each have the option to take some action that is individually tempting but collectively harmful, the action is very likely to be taken even when every individual actor would judge it shouldn't be. Their proposed remedy is a principle of conformity: if you are considering an action and others who have considered the same action have abstained, you should weight that as evidence against your own judgment. I've drafted the core argument of the paper below, and I would appreciate comments on whether it works, where it's weakest, and whether the final move is a genuine contribution or a dodge.

The draft's central claim is that the curse is misdiagnosed as a purely epistemic problem, and that this misdiagnosis affects the remedy. The curse mechanism, as I reconstruct it, presupposes what I call the "shared-target model": all N agents want the same outcome, each receives a noisy signal about whether the action is good for that outcome, and each is somewhat better than chance at reading the signal. Under that model, the case for conformity is essentially statistical — if each agent is right more often than wrong, then the set of agents who have considered and abstained from the action constitutes a poll whose aggregate verdict is more reliable than any one member's, so a lone actor overriding the poll is disproportionately likely to be the one agent whose signal went wrong. The curse, on this reconstruction, is the jury theorem run in reverse: the same independence and modest competence that make majority rule accurate make unanimity-to-act catastrophic, because a single false positive among N agents suffices.

My objection is that the shared-target model rarely holds in the cases people actually invoke the curse for — gain-of-function research, unilateral climate intervention, frontier AI deployment, announcing controversial empirical results. In those cases, agents disagree not because their signals about a common target are noisy, but because they have different values, priorities, and risk tolerances. The maverick who deploys the system while everyone else abstains is not, in the typical case, the agent whose noisy reading of a shared target is wrong; he is the agent whose genuine, considered values endorse what the others' genuine, considered values forbid. For such agents, the principle of conformity is not an epistemic correction. It is a demand that the minority defer to the majority's values, dressed up as deference to the majority's evidence. Calling this a "curse" smuggles in the assumption that all divergent judgments are errors relative to some privileged common target, and that assumption is doing all the work while remaining unargued.

I then consider the obvious reply: for catastrophically asymmetric risks, we do have a shared target — survival — and disagreement about how much risk to run for how much gain can be modeled as noise around it. Here I concede, but only conditionally, and this is the move I most want scrutinized. I propose that the curse survives my objection only when two conditions hold jointly: (a) the harm in question is of a kind all parties actually disvalue (not merely disvalue relative to a tradeoff), and (b) the disagreement is about the probability of that harm rather than the price worth paying to avoid it. Condition (a) rules out cases where the "harm" is a value-laden description — "normalizing a dangerous idea," "undermining a social institution" — that some agents may reasonably welcome. Condition (b) keeps the machinery epistemic: disagreement about probabilities under shared utilities is plausibly noise; disagreement about utilities is not. My worry, which I flag in the draft, is that this conditional reformulation may be so restrictive that it exempts nearly all of the philosophically interesting cases, leaving the principle of conformity governing only situations where everyone already agreed the action was terrible.

Specific comments I'm hoping for: (1) Is the noise-versus-values distinction I'm relying on tenable, or does it collapse under the observation that "disagreement about probabilities" is itself often value-laden, since what counts as a reasonable prior reflects priors about what matters? (2) Is the reverse jury-theorem framing an asset or a gimmick — does it add anything beyond what the original informal statement of the curse already establishes? (3) Does the two-condition concession in the last paragraph save the argument or give away the game, in the sense that a defender of conformity can simply insist that the catastrophic cases are the only ones the principle was ever meant for? (4) Finally, and candidly: is any of this a genuine advance on the existing discussion, or am I re-deriving the familiar distinction between factual and moral disagreement and attaching it to a new example? I'd rather be told now. Comments on structure and emphasis are welcome too, but I'm primarily interested in the soundness of the argument and whether the closing concession undermines it.
