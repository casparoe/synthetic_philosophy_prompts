I'm a PhD student in computational psychiatry, working on reinforcement-learning and active-inference models of affective states. The vocabulary I actually live in is value functions, policy selection, reward-prediction errors, expected free energy, precision weighting, and hierarchical generative models; I also know the nociception literature reasonably well from a rotation in a pain lab (the spinothalamic system, the sensory-discriminative role of primary somatosensory cortex, the affective-motivational roles of the anterior cingulate and insula). My philosophy, by contrast, is patchy. I've picked up secondhand that there's a debate in philosophy of mind about pain asymbolia — the condition, most often reported after damage to the anterior cingulate region, in which patients correctly identify a stimulus as painful, point to where it hurts, even withdraw reflexively, but report that they don't mind it at all, and show none of the fear, distress, or threat-avoidance that normally accompanies pain — and that this condition has become a load-bearing case in arguments about whether the badness of pain is inseparable from what pain feels like. But I know that literature only through friends' summaries and a couple of conference talks, and I want it restated properly, in terms I can evaluate.

Please take the role of a philosopher of mind who is also genuinely fluent in RL and active inference — not someone who sprinkles in the jargon. Before writing the final piece, sketch a short plan of how you'll organize it. Then proceed in three stages. First, translate the main philosophical positions on pain and its badness into my vocabulary, one at a time: evaluativism, the view that the experience of pain represents the body's condition as bad or damaged (associated with Michael Tye); imperativism, the view that pain is essentially a bodily command to protect or stop, defended by Colin Klein; the dual-component or sensory-affective picture that my own field half-invented; and the thesis I find most puzzling — that pain is bad intrinsically, because of its feel, such that it would remain bad even for an agent who placed no disvalue on it and had no further use for avoiding it. For each, say what translates into value terms, policies, cost terms in an objective function, or precision-weighted nociceptive prediction errors, and be explicit about where you're tempted to smuggle in my framework's assumptions and present that as translation — that move is exactly what I want flagged.

Second, treat asymbolia as a lesion experiment and tell me what it corresponds to in my terms: the ablation of a value term? The uncoupling of the nociceptive channel from policy selection while perception stays intact? The loss of a precision weighting rather than a signal? Or something that has no RL description at all? Third, give me the residue report, which is what I actually care about: which of the philosophical claims survive translation cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. Conclude with a verdict on where the residue lives — whether it marks a blindness in my framework (which defines "bad" as whatever the agent minimizes, and so may simply be unable to pose the question) or a looseness in the philosophical claims (which may be trading on "bad" in a sense that dissolves under pressure). I'd especially like your honest view on whether the intrinsic-badness thesis, once posed in my vocabulary, turns out to be substantive, trivial, or incoherent — and don't soften the answer to spare the philosophers.
