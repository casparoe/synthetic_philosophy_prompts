I am a retired geophysicist who has spent the last few years reading philosophy of language after a career spent arguing about which rocks count as what, and I would like the Kripke–Putnam picture of natural kind terms stress-tested against an episode I remember well: the International Astronomical Union's 2006 redefinition of "planet." The salient facts, as I understand them, are these. Pluto had been the ninth planet since Tombaugh's discovery in 1930. The 2005 discovery of Eris, an object at least as large as Pluto, forced a choice between a solar system with ten-plus planets and a definition that excluded Pluto. The IAU's General Assembly in Prague adopted, by a show of hands among the few hundred members present, a resolution requiring that a planet orbit the Sun, be round under its own gravity, and have cleared its orbital neighbourhood; Pluto failed the last criterion and became a "dwarf planet," to the lasting annoyance of schoolchildren and of a number of astronomers who thought the procedure was mishandled. I should note that this was not the field's first such episode: Ceres was listed as a planet for roughly half a century after its discovery before being demoted to asteroid.

Please analyze the episode through the lens of rigid designation, semantic externalism, and the necessary a posteriori. I want the mapping done carefully and in both directions — the theory illuminating the case, the case testing the theory — and I want it allowed to be silent where it is silent rather than stretched.

Specifically: (1) On the causal theory of reference, what was the initial baptism of "planet," what does the chain of transmission look like as Uranus and Ceres were added and Ceres subtracted, and what exactly does the 2006 vote occupy in that picture — a fresh baptism, a change of reference, or the discovery of what the term had meant all along? (2) Is "planet," on this evidence, a natural kind term in Kripke's and Putnam's sense? If it is, then whether Pluto is a planet was an empirical question with a determinate answer that science rather than a ballot should have settled, and the vote either uncovered the essence or legislated over it; if it is not, what sort of term is it, and where does the externalist machinery misfire? Relatedly, is "Pluto stopped being a planet in 2006" even the right description — did anything about Pluto change, or only a word? (3) Does the aftermath — textbooks rewritten, museum labels replaced, the laity deferring to the experts and mostly forgetting the controversy — vindicate the division of linguistic labor, or does the fact that the question was settled by vote at all expose a gap, since the theory says nothing about whether the boundary of a natural kind is out there to be found or drawn by us?

Finally, since my own discipline cannot agree on what a continent is, tell me frankly whether "planet" is closer to "water," whose essence chemistry eventually discovered, or to "continent," where arguably nothing waits to be discovered — and what follows from your answer for the popular complaint that the IAU didn't learn anything about Pluto but merely changed the subject. I would like a verdict, in the end, on whether this episode confirms, strains, or merely decorates the Kripke–Putnam picture.
