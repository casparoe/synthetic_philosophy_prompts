My philosophy club friend has this argument he won't drop and I've been stuck on it all week:

(1) Something can only be bad FOR you at some time when it's happening to you -- there has to be a moment where YOU are worse off, and if you don't exist there's no you there to be worse off.
(2) When you're dead, you don't exist, ever again.
(3) So death is never bad for the person who dies.
(4) But the same reasoning works for ANY gap of nonexistence. Say tomorrow I get put into a coma for 200 years and then wake up perfectly fine and live out the rest of my life -- the argument says those 200 lost years were never bad for me, since there was no time at which I was around being harmed.
(5) So if a wizard offered me 10,000 extra years of healthy life but with a 50-year gap of nonexistence in the middle, refusing him would cost me nothing.

He's actually fine with all of that, he leans in and says "gaps where nothing happens to you can't be bad for you, that's the whole point." But that seems crazy?? If there was a button I could press to painlessly skip ahead 200 years, obviously I wouldn't press it, and not because I'm scared during the skip -- I'm losing the years themselves. When I try to say death is bad because you lose the life you would've had, he just repeats that the "loss" never happens at any time so it can't be bad at any time, and honestly I can't find the moment where the trick happens. My gut says step (1) is the problem but it sounds so reasonable when I read it out loud. Which step do I give up, and what do I end up paying for giving it up?
