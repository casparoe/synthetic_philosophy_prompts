In my philosophy of probability course we have just finished a stretch on the principle of indifference, and I have reached a point of genuine confusion that I hope you can untangle for me. I understand the setup of Bertrand's paradox itself reasonably well: if you ask for the probability that a randomly chosen chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, several natural ways of applying indifference — treating the midpoint as uniformly distributed over the disk, treating the direction as uniformly distributed from a fixed point on the circumference, treating the endpoints as uniformly distributed along the boundary — deliver different answers, which looks like a straightforward reductio of the principle. What I am struggling with is the dialectic that follows. My professor's suggestion was that the real lesson is that indifference only makes sense relative to a specification of the random experiment, so that the Bertrand question is ill-posed until you say which physical procedure generates the chord. But we also encountered the claim, associated with Jaynes, that the paradox can actually be dissolved by demanding invariance: the answer should not change under rotations, translations, and dilations of the circle, and imposing this requirement supposedly selects a unique measure rather than merely exposing the question as ambiguous. Then there is the wine and water paradox, where all we know about some mixture is that the ratio of wine to water lies in a fixed interval, and applying indifference to the ratio rather than to its reciprocal yields contradictory probabilities. Here it is far less clear that any symmetry consideration breaks the tie, because there is no obvious group of transformations that privileges the ratio over its reciprocal.

What I would like you to explain, in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a letter — is the following. Second, does the wine and water paradox show that the invariance strategy only succeeds where the geometry smuggles in a privileged group of transformations, and if so, what should a defender of indifference as a constraint of rationality concede in the face of that?
