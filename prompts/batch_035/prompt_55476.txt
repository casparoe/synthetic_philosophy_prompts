I'm a third-year student in a philosophy of law seminar, and I also run a weekly board-game night for a mixed group — mostly engineering students, a couple of law majors, one linguist, about ten of us. This term I promised them a session on Nomic, Peter Suber's self-amending game, because it plays well at the table and it's secretly the most legal philosophy any of them will do this semester. I have forty-five minutes and a whiteboard, and no one in the room has any background in jurisprudence. Please don't explain Nomic to me; I want a plan for the explanation. The components I can see needing to fit together: the basic engine (players propose changes to the rules of the very game that authorizes those changes), the starter ruleset's split between immutable and mutable rules with a transmutation procedure for crossing that line, the question of whether a rule can validly amend the rule that defines what counts as a valid amendment, and the classic self-repeal puzzle — a constitutional article that purports to repeal itself, so that if it's valid it abolishes itself and if it's invalid it remains in force. I'd also like to work in a dispute from our own table: we play with a house rule that every proposed rule change must cite the rule that authorizes it, and last month someone proposed a rule reading, in effect, "this rule, and any rule citing it, can never be repealed" — half the table thought it was a clever lock, the other half thought it was meaningless noise. I don't know how to place that scenario in the sequence, or even whether it's the same phenomenon as the self-repeal case or a different one.

So the deliverable I need is: the order in which the pieces must come and what depends on what (I suspect the mutable/immutable distinction has to be felt as a game-mechanical fact before it becomes a philosophical problem, but I'm not sure where the self-repeal paradox should sit relative to it); the one carrying example or rule proposal that the whole session should be built around and what it must have and lack; the misconceptions the group will predictably bring or form — my guesses are "self-reference automatically means paradox, like Russell's set," "Nomic is just Calvinball, anything goes, there's no actual structure," and "the immutability split means the immutable rules are metaphysically fixed rather than fixed only by another rule" — with the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; one question I can ask at the end to check whether the idea has landed; and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud (I'm inclined to forbid Gödel analogies entirely, since the group will reach for them within thirty seconds).

One more request on how you frame it: as you lay out the plan, please keep separate what is settled among people who have actually worked on this — for instance, whether the self-amendment problem is a formal contradiction or a problem about legal interpretation and authorization — from what is genuinely contested, such as whether self-amendment ultimately requires an implied background authority beyond the rules themselves, or whether the self-repeal puzzle has any solution that scholars broadly accept rather than several live rival ones. If the sequencing itself depends on taking a side in one of those disputes, say so explicitly rather than baking the choice in silently.
