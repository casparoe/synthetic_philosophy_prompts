I have been reading a semi-popular book on the history of probability, and its chapter on the classical interpretation closes with the following summary of Bertrand's paradox:

"Take a circle with an equilateral triangle inscribed in it, draw a chord of the circle at random, and ask for the probability that the chord is longer than a side of the triangle. The principle of indifference — the classical rule that you should spread probability evenly when you have no reason to favor one possibility over another — seems to license three equally natural constructions: fix the chord's midpoint uniformly over the disk, fix its distance from the center uniformly along a radius, or fix one endpoint uniformly on the circumference. These yield 1/4, 1/2, and 1/3 respectively, and there are no other natural choices to consider. Since one and the same principle gives contradictory answers to one and the same question, the principle is simply inconsistent, and the paradox amounts to a refutation of classical probability: there is no such thing as an uninformative distribution, and every probability assignment must in the end be a subjective judgment. The principle was accordingly abandoned, surviving only as the physicist's rule of maximum entropy — which, as Jaynes showed in the 1970s, actually recovers the correct answer to the chord problem: demanding invariance under translations and rotations uniquely singles out 1/2, a resolution now generally accepted."

I confess I find the refutation claim compelling — three inconsistent answers from one principle does look fatal — but a colleague who works in Bayesian statistics tells me the indifference idea is alive and well in her field, so the book cannot be right that it was abandoned. I would be grateful if you would go through the book's paragraph claim by claim. Which claims would a philosopher of probability or an objective Bayesian accept as stated? Where does the paragraph overstate a defensible point, conflate two things that should be kept apart, or trade on a term used loosely? And where is it simply wrong? Most importantly, does the book's overall moral — that indifference is refuted and that probability is therefore irreducibly subjective — survive once the summary is corrected? I would also appreciate your view on a tension I feel in the paragraph itself: the book seems to hold both that probability assignments must ultimately be subjective and that Jaynes settled the chord question objectively, and I cannot tell whether these are compatible positions or whether the book is trying to have it both ways.
