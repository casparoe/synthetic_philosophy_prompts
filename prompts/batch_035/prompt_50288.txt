I have no academic background — I left school at seventeen and spent my career as an insurance claims adjuster — but for the past year and a half my brother-in-law (a retired high school mathematics teacher) and I have been arguing about the two-envelope problem, first over family dinners and later by email, and we have decided that before we say another word to each other about it, we want a proper joint statement: the document a neutral draftsman would write if both of us had to sign every line we accept. I am asking you to be that draftsman.

The problem as we pose it: you are shown two sealed envelopes and told only that one contains twice as much money as the other. You pick one at random and are permitted to look inside — suppose you find $40 — and are then offered a one-time swap for the other envelope. My brother-in-law, whom I will call the switcher, reasons that the other envelope holds $20 or $80, that you have no reason to favor either possibility, and that the average of losing $20 and gaining $40 is a gain of $10, so you should always swap, whatever you find. I will call myself the sticker: I reason that the envelopes are symmetric, that nothing since they were filled has told me anything, and that if swapping were genuinely favorable then swapping back would be favorable too, which he and I both find absurd. Two things to keep the document honest: neither of us doubts expected value as the right tool for ordinary gambling questions, and neither of us claims the problem is deep because of the money — we each would concede ten dollars to settle it.

So that the document is about our actual quarrel and not the textbook version, here is what we have actually said to each other. He charges that my symmetry argument ignores the observation: once I have seen the $40, he says, I am no longer choosing between two faceless envelopes, and the fact that I would have made the same argument before opening cuts no ice. I charge that his letter for the amount in my envelope is doing illicit double duty — in the two halves of his averaging it names two different sums, and if he denies this he should tell me whether his argument still works when the envelope stays sealed; he says it does, since the expected contents of the other envelope are one and a quarter times mine however I came by mine, and I have never managed to make him see why that frightens me. We also got stuck on whether it matters that the total money in the world is finite — he thinks my finitude point is a red herring, I think it is the only reason his averaging ever looks innocent — and on a variant our nephew raised: does anything change if the referee who fills the envelopes is allowed to watch my choice and choose the amounts afterward? Finally, we half-agreed to settle it by simulation, and never did: a computer fills the envelopes a million times, the smaller amount being $10 or $20 decided by a fair coin and the larger always double, with the whole procedure known to both of us; each of us writes down, in advance, what we predict about the long-run average payoff of always staying, always switching, and switching only when a particular amount is seen.

Please draft the joint statement we should be able to sign. I want: (1) a numbered section of claims both of us accept, about the setup, about symmetry, and about what expected value may and may not be used to conclude here; (2) a disagreements table in which each row states one remaining disagreement in wording that the named side would accept as a fair statement of its own view — nothing slanted, nothing so vague that we could both sign it; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us — including, if you think it belongs there, what the simulation would and would not show; and (4) for each crux, the argument, case, or simulation result both of us would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If any part of our dispute strikes you as merely verbal — for instance, a dispute about what a symbol in an expected-value calculation refers to, rather than about what to do — say which part and why, but do not declare the whole thing verbal unless you truly believe neither of us should change behavior in any version of the setup.

Format: numbered agreed claims first, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes with predictions. The very last line must be exactly a crux statement in the form \crux{...}, containing either a short phrase of under ten words naming the deepest genuine crux between us, or, if you conclude our dispute is entirely verbal, \crux{verbal only}.
