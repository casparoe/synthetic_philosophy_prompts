In my work as a journalist covering science and ideas, I rely constantly on generational labels: I write that Millennials delayed homeownership, that Boomers dominate the electorate, that Gen Z worries about climate in ways their parents did not. Editors and readers treat these sentences as ordinary explanatory claims, and so do I. But an editor recently asked me, in passing, what a "generation" actually is — whether the people born in, say, 1981 and 1996 have anything in common beyond the dates, and if the dates alone do the work, why we need the label at all. I realized I had no answer, and that my profession's entire vocabulary of cohort analysis might be resting on assumptions nobody has examined. So: what does this practice presuppose about the nature of social kinds? Does writing that "Gen Z is anxious about AI" commit me to the view that generations are real entities with shared, causally produced traits — something like a shared formative period that stamps a cohort the way a culture stamps its members — or can the practice get by with something weaker, where the birth-year bin is just a convenient cut and the label is shorthand for a statistical tendency I could equally well have described with plain dates? Is the practice even internally consistent, given that we treat "Millennial" as picking out a kind while mocking astrology, which also sorts people by birth dates into types with predicted personalities? I'd like to know which claims my reporting strictly requires the generational ontology for, which would survive if generations turned out to be arbitrary bins, and what my copy would have to look like if I stopped presuming the kind exists at all.
