I'm on the grant allocation committee of a mid-sized foundation funding global health work, and we've got a fight brewing about how to choose between two portfolios for our next funding cycle. Portfolio A is concentrated: by far the highest expected lives saved per dollar, but a real chance the whole thing collapses if the partner government in the main country falls. Portfolio B spreads smaller grants across many countries — lower expected impact, but almost no chance of a total wipeout. One faction says we should just be straight expected value maximizers: being risk averse here means trading away other people's lives for our own peace of mind, which is basically immoral. The other faction keeps going on about risk-weighted expected utility — the idea that you weight outcomes by some concave function, so bad outcomes count extra, and that this can be fully rational rather than a cowardice or a bias in need of correction. Their point is that plain EU maximization forces you to treat a certain outcome and a gamble with the same mean as equally good, and its not obvious we should accept that constraint when the downside falls entirely on third parties. I need to circulate a short memo before Thursday's meeting recommending which framework we should actually use for this decision and for future ones. Please give a real recommendation, not a split-the-difference cop out, and include an honest treatment of the strongest point on whichever side loses — the losing faction will definately read it closely.
