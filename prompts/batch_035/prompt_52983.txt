I've been on a bit of an Arendt kick and I keep coming back to her essay on the Pentagon Papers (the one later collected in Crises of the Republic, usually called Lying in Politics). Here's my problem: everybody quotes it, but I've never actually seen anyone point her machinery at the episode she was writing about and see whether it does real analytical work there, instead of just describing it in her own vocabulary. That's what I want you to do.

Salient facts, so we're on the same page: in 1967 Robert McNamara, then Secretary of Defense, quietly commissioned a classified historical study of US decision-making on Vietnam from 1945 to 1967 — thousands of pages, mostly documents. Daniel Ellsberg, who had helped produce it, leaked it to the press in 1971; the Nixon administration went to court to stop publication and lost at the Supreme Court. The contents showed that successive administrations had been told by their own people, in internal documents, that the war was going worse than claimed, that progress figures given to the public were inflated, and so on — while publicly saying the opposite, and in many cases coming to half-believe their own public version.

So here's the assignment. Take her conceptual apparatus from that essay — the distinction between the old-fashioned liar who knows the truth and the modern organized lying of image-makers and PR; her claim that what was really disturbing about the Pentagon Papers was not deception of the public but self-deception, the problem-solvers who first talked themselves into a picture of reality they'd constructed; her thought that lying is a kind of action, that the liar is a world-builder and not just a falsifier; and her worry that facts destroyed by organized lying can't simply be fished back out of the memory hole the way a lost object is found again. Map it carefully. Who exactly is the liar here — McNamara? the presidents? the bureaucracy as a whole? Who is the dupe, and is the public ever actually the target, or mostly an afterthought? What is it that gets lied about — facts, opinions, predictions about the future, which are different beasts on her account? Where does ordinary deception end and self-deception begin in this material? And where does Ellsberg fit — is he just the truth coming back up, or is his leaking itself an act of the same world-changing kind she attributes to liars, which her framework maybe didn't budget for?

Then, honestly, tell me where the lens breaks. I have three suspicions but push back if you think I'm wrong. (a) She plays up the self-deception angle, but the Papers contain plenty of flat-out lying to Congress and the public — does her emphasis misdescribe what actually happened, or is she making a subtler point I'm flattening? (b) Her picture of organized lying seems built for states and bureaucratic machinery; a lone individual committing civil disobedience by leaking doesn't obviously have a slot in it. Does that matter, or is that me misreading her as offering a typology when she's offering something else? (c) She wrote before the aftermath played out — the plumbers, the burglary, Watergate, the resignation. On one hand that looks like her thesis confirmed, lying breeding more lying until it hits the wall of reality. On the other hand maybe it just shows ordinary political crime and cover-up, which needed no fancy theory. I'd like a verdict on whether the episode confirms her, or exposes a case she improvised around — and I'd rather have "her framework is silent on X" than have you force it to speak.
