I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long essay on the digger wasp Sphex, whose famously mechanical routine — dragging prey to the burrow entrance, leaving it, going inside to inspect, and then, if an experimenter moves the prey even slightly while she's inside, dragging it back and repeating the whole inspection indefinitely — has made her the mascot of "sphexishness": behavior that looks intelligent but turns out to be rigidly pre-programmed. My working thesis is that sphexishness is the wrong litmus test for free will, and I'd like you to argue for it on my behalf. The idea: the property that matters is not flexibility in the execution of a routine but what I've been calling "meta-flexibility" — the capacity to revise the very criteria by which one notices that things are going wrong. An animal or machine that can compensate for a moved cricket is still running a fixed loop, just a wider one; what would distinguish genuinely free behavior is the ability to step outside the loop and ask whether the loop itself is the problem. Please argue for this position rather than surveying the options, and in doing so take a definite stand on two points: whether the wasp's re-inspection loop differs from human habit only in degree or in kind, and whether meta-flexibility actually escapes the trap or merely moves it up a level. On the second point, confront the strongest objection head-on: that for any flexibility criterion, however high up, one can imagine a "meta-sphex" that revises its criteria mechanically, so the regress either never terminates or stops at an arbitrary rung, and the free-will question is left where it started. If you think the objection defeats the thesis, say so plainly and argue that instead — but commit to whichever answer you think the reasons favor, and don't end by handing the decision back to me.
