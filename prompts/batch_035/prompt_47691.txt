So I'm a science/ideas writer and I've somehow ended up writing a feature (long overdue, my editor keeps emailing me about it lol) about why anyone should trust math. Like, mathematicians keep telling us certain things about numbers and sets, and philosophers keep saying wait, HOW do you know that? And every time I interview someone about this I get dragged back to this one old paper — Benacerraf, early 70s, "Mathematical Truth" — which basically said we're stuck between two demands. One demand is that math statements have to be about something for them to be true or false like normal statements, and if they're about numbers, then numbers are these weird abstract things sitting outside space and time. The other demand is that we should be able to say how we know about whatever our statements are about — and nobody has a story for how a brain made of meat gets in touch with a number that isn't anywhere.

Here's what's bugging me though: this paper is FIFTY years old and the debate feels exactly where it was when I started reading about it. The realists keep redefining what "access" means (some of them just say we don't need access, it's a priori, or that our theories merely have to be reliable without us knowing how), and the anti-realists keep saying that's cheating, and then someone chimes in with the point that we have the exact same problem with theoretical posits in physics, so math isn't special — the "companion in guilt" move — and everyone nods or rolls their eyes depending on their team, and nothing moves.

So for the piece I need a diagnosis more than a scorecard. What KIND of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a hidden disagreement about what reference to abstract objects would even consist of? Is it two questions being run together — what makes math true versus what makes our math beliefs justified? Is one side just failing to notice that their demand already assumes the other side is wrong? My half-baked hunch is that the two demands in the original paper aren't really two — that the "how do we know" demand was never neutral, it was built from a causal picture of knowledge that had no room for abstracta from the start, so the whole dilemma is kind of rigged. But I might be screwing that up, and I want an honest take on whether that hunch holds up, what each camp would say to it, and whether anything — a distinction, a better theory of reference, whatever — could actually get this thing unstuck, or whether it's just a hard problem where smart people should be expected to disagree forever. No citations please, I have a folder full of those already, I need the actual structure of the fight.
