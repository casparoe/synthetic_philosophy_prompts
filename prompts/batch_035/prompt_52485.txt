My political philosophy seminar has spent two months on luck egalitarianism, and the debate has become comically static: every position is on the table, everyone knows every counterexample, and nobody has budged. I'd like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, rather than another round of adjudication.

Here is the state of play as we've mapped it. The luck egalitarian core idea is that inequalities are unjust when they trace to brute luck (the genetic lottery, childhood circumstances, disasters) but not when they trace to choices the person could have made otherwise. Dworkin's version splits luck into brute and option luck, uses the hypothetical insurance auction to convert brute luck into choices behind a veil of ignorance, and tests equality with an envy test. Cohen then hammered the taste cases: if someone develops a taste for expensive wine, or has preferences whose satisfaction is costly for structural reasons, does the luck egalitarian have to tell them to bear the cost themselves — which seems harsh — or subsidize them — which seems to collapse the responsibility distinction the theory was built around? Dworkin replies that the crucial question is identification: whether the person endorses the taste as theirs or experiences it as an alien affliction. Cohen finds this too subjective, noting people can endorse afflictions and disavow their own choices. Meanwhile the relational egalitarian critics, following Anderson and Scheffler, claim the whole project is mistaken at the root: the point of equality is not a pattern of distribution keyed to responsibility but standing in relations of non-domination and non-stigmatization — and a theory that lets the imprudent perish in their choice, or that hands out compensation with pity attached, betrays that point regardless of how cleanly it draws the luck/choice line. The luck egalitarians in the room respond by producing increasingly refined patches: thresholds of responsibility, a distinction between preferences whose formation was under one's control and those that weren't, and so on — each of which the critics call ad hoc without proposing a worked-out alternative that handles the distributive cases luck egalitarianism handles.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the expensive-tastes dispute is really a proxy for a deeper, rarely-stated disagreement about what responsibility requires — namely whether responsibility for a preference demands that one could have controlled the process by which it formed, which would generate a regress that leaves almost nothing attributable to choice, and would quietly gut the theory. And the luck/relational dispute might be two groups answering different questions under one heading: one about the right pattern of holdings, the other about the right shape of social relations, so that each side's "counterexamples" simply miss the other's target. But I'm not confident, since the two camps do seem to genuinely disagree about concrete cases (should the person whose religious observance raises their cost of living get extra resources?).

So: is this a substantive normative disagreement, a verbal one, or two research programs with different questions and overlapping vocabulary? Where do the sides actually part company — on facts, on standards for what counts as a counterexample, on bedrock intuitions about responsibility and desert, or on the regress question about preference formation? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here: a conceptual distinction, a set of cases with shared verdicts, a formal criterion for the luck/choice line, a change of question, or nothing at all — and if nothing, why has the debate continued for four decades anyway?
