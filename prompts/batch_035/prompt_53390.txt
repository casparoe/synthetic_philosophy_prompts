Something's been bugging me about karma and I want to get it straight. I've been reading about the problem of moral luck (Nagel and Williams type stuff) and it struck me that karma seems to just dissolve it. If your circumstances at birth — what country, what family, what health, what talents — are all supposed to be the result of your own past actions, including in previous lives, then nothing about anyone's starting point is luck. You earned it. Same for what befalls you later, good and bad. So within a karma framework there's no such thing as circumstantial or constitutive moral luck, right?

But that seems to lead somewhere pretty dark, and that's really my question. If everything that happens to a person is the fruit of their own past deeds, then famine relief, disability accomodations, foreign aid, all of it is an attempt to interfere with a cosmic bookkeeping system — handing people goods they didn't earn and shielding them from outcomes they did. Doesn't karma, taken seriously as a theory of moral desert, make humanitarian intervention not just futile but actually unjust, roughly like paying off someone elses debt for them when the creditor had every right to collect? I'm aware that Buddhist and Hindu communities historically did and do tons of charity, so the tradition clearly disagrees somewhere, but I can't find where the argument slips. Where exactly does it go wrong? Or is the honest answer that the doctrine and the practice just sit in tension?

And a second thing, if karma dissolves moral luck by pushing everything back onto past lives, it looks like all the luck gets concentrated into the very first life, whenever that was, which has no prior karma to explain why it started out one way rather than another. Is that regognized as a problem anywhere in the classical treatments, or is it just kind of swept aside?

One request: I know karma means different things in Buddhist, Jain, and various Hindu framings, and probably within each of those too — please seperate for me what's actually common ground from what's contested, and don't present one school's reading as if it were THE doctrine. I'd rather come away knowing the shape of the disagreement than with a tidy answer.
