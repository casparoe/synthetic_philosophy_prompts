My book club has just finished Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, and I've been arguing about it with two of the members ever since, without making much progress, so I'm hoping you can help me think it through properly. The novel is narrated by Stevens, an English butler looking back over decades of service to Lord Darlington, and my fight with my friends is about whether Stevens's famous talk of "dignity" and "greatness" represents what he actually wants out of life or whether it's something that was drilled into him by his father, his class position, and his employer's convenience. One friend says his choices -- staying loyal to a man who turns out to have been a dupe of the Nazis, refusing Miss Kenton, pouring himself into the polishing of silver at the exact moments his life is passing him by -- are all his own preferences, freely made, and the tragedy is just that he chose badly. The other says nobody could genuinely prefer that life, and that what he calls his creed is really a bias in favor of his masters' interests that he has absorbed and then renamed. I honestly can't decide between them, and the more I think about it, the more the whole question seems to turn on something I can't put my finger on: what is the actual difference between a preference that is authentically yours and a bias you've been handed?

Here's why I think the novel is a good place to work this out. Take the scenes near the end -- Stevens sitting on the pier at Weymouth, telling a stranger that the "remains" of his day are all he has left, and then almost immediately cheering himself up by resolving to master the art of banter, which he had spent the whole trip treating as a humiliating chore. Is that resilience or self-deception? And what about Miss Kenton? She spends the book hinting at what she wants from him, he deflects, and she marries someone else -- and by the end she tells him she sometimes thinks she made herself unhappy for no reason, though she also says (and I found this maddening) that she has come to love her husband. Was her marriage a preference or a settling? It seems to matter morally how we answer: one of my friends is ready to condemn Lord Darlington's whole social world for hijacking these people's wants, and the other thinks that's condescending, that it treats two grown adults as if they couldn't have wanted what they wanted.

What I'd like from you is a reading of the novel through this exact question -- the preference-versus-bias one -- rather than the general "dignity and regret" reading everyone gives it. Some specific things I want addressed: is there a test the novel itself suggests for telling a corrupted want from a real one (Stevens is given several chances to break character -- the moment he cries in front of others, his reading of the romance novel, the near-misses with Miss Kenton -- do those moments reveal his real preferences, or are the deflections the real him?); whether the ending, where he decides to practice banter for his new American employer, reads as him finally developing a genuine small preference or as the bias machinery adapting to survive; and whether Ishiguro, as far as you can tell from the book itself, takes a side between my two friends, or deliberately builds the story so that no honest reader can sort Stevens's choices into "his" and "not his." I'd also like you to say where the novel resists being read this way at all -- is there something in Stevens that this whole framing misses, treating him as a case study rather than a person? I'm writing up some discussion questions for our next meeting, so the more concrete to actual scenes and turning points, the better. Please don't give me a reading list or send me off to other books; I want your actual thinking about this novel and this question.
