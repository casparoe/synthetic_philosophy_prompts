My friend has firm, and I suspect systematically held, views about when it is permissible to believe something without adequate evidence, and I have started keeping a record of her verdicts so I can work out what rule she is implicitly applying. Her rulings so far, each on the question whether the believer may hold the belief despite insufficient evidence:

1. A woman's religious faith: she believes in God although the evidence seems to her inconclusive, and the question matters more to her life than any other. — Permitted.
2. A buyer who believes a used-car salesman's assurance that a particular car is sound, having no independent evidence, when a mechanic could check it for a modest fee. — Not permitted.
3. A tennis player who believes she will make a decisive shot at match point where the objective odds are roughly even, in circumstances where the confidence itself steadies her arm. — Permitted.
4. A doctor who believes, without evidence, that an experimental treatment works, and conveys that confidence to an anxious patient. — Not permitted.
5. Someone who believes her partner is faithful although she has no way to verify it, in a situation where sustained suspicion would corrode the relationship. — Permitted.
6. A person who does not find theism at all attractive but believes in God anyway, purely to avoid hell. — Not permitted.

Next month three further cases will come up in our conversations, and I would like predictions in advance:

A. An entrepreneur who believes, against the known base rates, that her startup will succeed, where the belief partly makes itself true by sustaining her through years of work; the venture is momentous for her, but she could decline to found the company at all, and much of the money at risk belongs to friends.
B. A teacher who believes, against the student's whole record so far, that a failing student has unusual talent, where the belief is to some degree self-fulfilling because of how it changes her expectations of him, and the main person affected is the student.
C. A scientist who believes her favored hypothesis is true before the experiments are done, because the belief motivates the difficult work of testing it, while being careful to let the data overrule it later.

For each of A, B, and C, predict whether she will permit the belief or not. Then reconstruct the rule she appears to be running, identify the other rules that fit her six verdicts just as well, and say which of A, B, and C would best separate those rival rules. Finally, propose one further case, not among these, that would decisively tell the rules apart. Please note in advance: she has never read Clifford or James, so derive the rule from her verdicts rather than from any label — though if her rule turns out to resemble one of their positions, I would like to know that as well.
