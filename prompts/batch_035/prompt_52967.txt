You're a professional metaphysician who also writes literary fiction, and I need the philosopher half to do real work inside the story. Write a short story, roughly 1200 words, about a medical illustrator at a radiology clinic who spends her days stitching MRI slices into 3D reconstructions. On the side she's been running a longitudinal project — her father, photographed and interviewed every Sunday for eleven years — and after a stroke shifts his personality hard, she starts seeing him the way the software sees a body: a stack of temporal parts, strictly earlier slices and strictly later slices, with nothing that is wholly present in any single frame. Her partner thinks this is a grief-avoidance trick and keeps insisting the man before the stroke and the man after are literally the same one, and that change only makes sense if something persists through it.

Brainstorm the structure before writing. The metaphysics has to carry the plot, not decorate it: the temporary intrinsics problem should be enacted, not explained (how could one single thing be both loquacious and taciturn at the same time without contradiction — the perdurantist's answer being that those are properties of distinct stages, not of one whole), and if you can work in the special relativity angle — that there's no privileged present for anything to be wholly present in anyway — do it through her work or her father's old physics obsession, not a lecture. Nobody gets to deliver a lecture. The two positions get expressed in how they photograph, argue, and grieve.

Please don't end it with a tidy scene where one of them concedes. The story can lean perdurantist or endurantist, but it should lean honestly — and I want to be able to tell, reading it, which way it leans and why.
