In machine learning, the validation of a system follows a now-standard ritual: split the data, train on one part, measure error on the held-out part, and report the number. When the number is good, papers and safety claims alike say the model has learned the objective. My worry is about what this ritual silently takes for granted. The alignment literature distinguishes the outer objective — the thing the training process optimizes, the loss function — from whatever internal target the trained system itself actually pursues, and observes that these can come apart: a model can perform perfectly on-distribution while the goal it has actually internalized is a proxy that merely coincides with the intended objective on the training distribution and diverges away from it. The same community that relies on the train/test ritual also, in its safety discussions, points out exactly this gap. So I want the implicit epistemology of the ritual made explicit.

Concretely: the held-out test establishes that the model's behavior matches the intended objective on a distribution the training process already rewarded matching on. That looks close to being the premise rather than the conclusion. So what view of "having learned the objective" does the practice presuppose? Does it quietly assume that behavioral evidence, gathered on a distribution, suffices to fix which internal goal produced the behavior — which would be a substantive assumption, roughly that proxies which coincide on the training distribution also coincide everywhere behaviorally nearby? Or is the practice merely underdetermined: compatible with a stricter view of alignment evidence, but not itself committed to the loose one? And is the practice internally consistent, given that the same field treats distribution shift as a routine engineering problem when it degrades accuracy, but has no routine test for the case where the shift reveals the goal rather than the competence?

I'd like a committed answer, not a survey of positions: take a definite stance on whether the implicit view is genuinely incoherent as a method for establishing alignment, or whether there is a defensible reconstruction on which held-out testing is doing more work than I've credited — and say which, and why. Then tell me what evaluation would have to look like if the distinction between the outer and the inner objective were taken seriously: what a test would have to vary, what it could establish at best, and, importantly, what no test of this behavioral kind could ever establish, so that I know where the floor of the method lies rather than just that it has one.
