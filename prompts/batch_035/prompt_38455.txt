I work as a rates derivatives quant, and my day-to-day vocabulary is the standard no-arbitrage toolkit: liquidation values, static replication, dynamic replication and the Greeks, arbitrage bounds, mark-to-market, model risk, and coherent risk measures as the cone of losses bounded by what can be replicated. I have no formal philosophy training, but I've been reading about the foundations of Bayesianism on my commute and keep bumping into Dutch book arguments, and I suspect they are basically my home turf dressed up in different words: the claim that degrees of belief violating the probability axioms expose you to a guaranteed-loss portfolio sounds like it should just be an arbitrage argument. So here is what I want. Restate the classic Dutch book argument for probabilism in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can -- treating the agent's announced credences as quoted prices, the bookie as a counterparty, the Dutch book as a portfolio that costs nothing or less than nothing to enter and pays off negative in every state, and so on. Then do the same for two extensions: the diachronic argument that one ought to update by conditionalization on pain of a book constructed across time, and the arguments involving bets on logically or mathematically equivalent propositions, where coherence seems to require recognizing equivalences that no finite agent can actually compute.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which parts of these arguments map cleanly onto static or dynamic arbitrage in finance, which parts only translate with distortion, and which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? Some specific pressure points I want addressed: in my world an arbitrage argument presupposes prices that exist in a market and are binding on someone, whereas a degree of belief is not obviously a two-sided quote anyone is obligated to fill -- the argument seems to need an assumption that the agent will accept any bet at (or better than) the odds implied by the credence, and I want to know whether that assumption is doing real work or is just a harmless idealization. Second, conditionalization: a dynamic replication argument in finance makes money only along an actual realized path and only relative to a specific hedging schedule, whereas the diachronic Dutch book is supposed to lose in every state; I want to know whether that disanalogy is the source of the standard philosophical complaint that the update argument proves less than it advertises, or whether the complaint dissolves once the translation is done properly. Finally, the equivalence-clause cases look to me like model risk -- trading against an agent with a wrong or incomplete model of which positions are actually identical -- and I want to know whether the philosophers' version of this issue contains anything beyond that.

Close by answering the question I can't answer myself: where there is residue, is it because my vocabulary is missing something the philosophers need, or because their disagreement is less substantive than it looks? I don't want the translation to smuggle in my framework's assumptions and call the result the original argument, and I'd rather you flag every place you had to do that than produce a clean-looking correspondence that isn't one.
