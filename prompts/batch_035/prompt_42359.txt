I'm giving a paper next month at a workshop on political institutions, and I want a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis, roughly: everyone treats the surviving constitutional monarchies (UK, Scandinavia, Japan) as either harmless decoration or embarrassing path dependence, but there's a stronger case available, and it's republican in spirit. The argument I want to defend: an elected head of state creates a second site of partisan contestation — whoever wins the presidency arrives with a mandate, a constituency, and every incentive to spend the office's symbolic capital on their own side, which is exactly the kind of domination the neo-Roman republicans worry about. A hereditary monarch, precisely because nobody earned the job and nobody can win it in future, takes that whole function off the competitive board: a hereditary head of state is a domination-reducing device, not just the dignified window dressing Bagehot talked about. I've been using an analogy to independent central banks — nobody thinks we should elect the central bank governor, because we want that office neutral, and I want to say monarchy does something analogous for the symbolic head of state. I know the analogy has holes (a central banker has a job, a monarch is a symbol), so flag for me whether it's an asset or a liability in the room.

The audience: about twenty political theorists, mostly deliberative democrats who think legitimacy has to come from reasons every citizen could in principle accept, plus two neo-Roman republicans who know Pettit's work on non-domination backwards, plus a couple of comparative politics people who will want evidence and counterexamples — I suspect they're already rehearsing points about Weimar and about de Gaulle's Fifth Republic presidency. No royalists, no constitutional lawyers, nobody with sentimental attachments to crowns. So: give me the objections in roughly the order they'll arrive, phrased the way each group would actually put it, with a two-minute reply to each and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front — my guess is the sheer arbitrariness of hereditary succession is where the deliberative democrats get me. And include the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks; I think I know which one it is, but I want to see wether you land on it too.
