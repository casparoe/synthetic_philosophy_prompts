so I write this little ethics-ish blog — audience is regular curious people, not philosophy majors — and I want to build one of those "which position on lying do you actually hold?" quizzes. Everybody says lying is wrong, but they mean wildly different things by it, and I want the quiz to sort readers by what they'd actually do in concrete cases, not by which label sounds nice to them.

Can you write me 8–10 questions, each a specific scenario with 2–3 answer choices, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions? These positions have to show up as leaves:

1. The Augustinian view: lying is always wrong because it corrupts the God-given point of speech. Augustine defined a lie as asserting against your own mind with intent to deceive, and he even counted jokes and made-up stories as lies (mild ones, but still lies) — so I definately want a campfire-story or poker-bluff style question where people have to decide whether playful falsehood even counts as lying.
2. Kant-style absolutism, which also says never lie, but grounds it in a duty owed to other people rather than in God. I suspect these two give the same answers on every behavioral case and only split on the grounding, so please include at least one question that could seperate them — like who exactly is wronged by a lie the victim never finds out about, or whether lying would still be wrong in itself if God didn't exist.
3. The casuist line where you can never assert what you believe false, but you can mislead with strictly true statements (evasive answers, technically-true dodges). At least one case should offer a misleading-truth option — the classic murderer-at-the-door asking where your friend is hiding works well there, with choices like a flat lie, a technically true dodge, or refusing to answer.
4. A pro tanto / can-be-outweighed view where lying is normally wrong but saving a life tips it.
5. A plain consequentialist ranking of lies by harm.

Other cases I'd like in there: a kid asking about Santa, a dying patient asking if they'll get better, covering for a surprise party, and at least one case where the only moves available are silence, a misleading truth, or an outright lie. Please flag honestly any pair of leaves the quiz can't really pull apart instead of papering over it. Also tell me whether the scoring key should have a catch-all output for people whose answers are all over the place (yes to lying to the murderer, no to the surprise party), or whether that's just "inconsistent, congrats, you're human."

Hard rule: no question can just be "do you think lying is wrong?" — every single one has to be a spot where the positions genuinely come apart. Keep it fun but real, and use American spelling.
