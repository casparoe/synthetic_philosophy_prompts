I'm a retired schoolteacher who does local history as a hobby, and I keep running into a pattern I can't find a name for. A community cherishes some custom as ancient — a clan tartan, a village festival "dating back centuries," a national dish "unchanged for generations" — and then someone digs into the archives and shows it was largely put together in the nineteenth or early twentieth century, often by an antiquarian, a tourism board, or a regime that wanted a usable past. Here's the part I'm interested in: the revelation causes a brief scandal, and then, instead of the custom dissolving, it just... absorbs the revelation. A few decades later the same community recites the fact of the invention itself as a charming piece of trivia, and practices the custom more self-consciously than before, precisely because everyone now knows it's constructed. I have the vague sense that the invention side of this has a name in the literature, but I'm not asking about that. I'm asking about the aftermath: the apparent immunity of these practices to exposure of their origins, and even the way the exposure gets folded back into the practice as a new layer of lore. Does that resilience have an established concept attached to it, and if so, what exactly does the concept cover? I'd also like to know where its edges are — for instance, whether it extends to cases like wedding customs or recipes that people know perfectly well are recent and never pretended otherwise, and whether it helps explain why some exposed traditions do collapse while others don't. If there's no established concept, I'd be grateful for a careful definition of the thing itself and a proposed name, so long as it's clearly distinguished from the nearest neighbors like plain nostalgia and from the standard observation that a practice's origin shouldn't settle its value.
