So a friend of mine works at an AI lab and keeps telling me about this scheme they're trying out for checking answers that no human could produce or verify on their own. The setup: take a hard question, give it to two AI systems, one is assigned to argue yes and the other no, they go back and forth seeing each other's points and attacking them, and then a human judge — someone who couldn't answer the original question and can't check the underlying evidence — reads the transcript and picks a winner. Whatever the winner was arguing for is what you go with. The pitch is that a single confident answer can be bluffing, but a bluff can't survive cross-examination, because the opposing model gets paid (in whatever sense) to find the holes. Honesty becomes the stable strategy.

My question is what this whole practice takes for granted, philosophically. It seems to be betting on something like "catching a lie is cheaper than telling one" — that for any false claim, the other side can produce a gotcha that lands with the judge using less ability than it took to make the claim. Is that a real epistemological commitment, or just hope? It sounds related to the idea that verifying things is easier than generating them, but that can't be true in general — some claims are exactly as hard to check as to produce. So is the practice implicitly restricting itself to some subclass of questions, and if so does it even know it's doing that?

Second thing that bugs me: the judge. The judge can't answer the question and can't verify the conclusion, but is supposed to be able to rank the two arguments. Is that even coherent — what does it presuppose about the relation between judging an argument and judging its conclusion? Is there some general reason to think argument-quality is more accessible to a weaker reasoner than answer-correctness is, or is that just the thing the whole method quietly needs?

Third: they're both wrong. If neither side knows the answer and both are confabulating, the debate just crowns the more convincing confabulator. So the setup seems to presuppose that the question, once you've split it into yes vs no, has a true side waiting to be found. What does it presuppose about questions where the dichotomy itself is the problem?

And the format is obviously lifted from adversary legal procedure — prosecution vs defense, jury. So I wonder if it's importing that system's presuppositions wholesale: that truth is best elicited by two skilled partisans each trying to win, that clash reveals more than neutral inquiry does. Courtrooms run on something like that faith, and people have complained about it forever. If the debate setup is just court with models, what exactly is it presupposing about truth — and would it have to look structurally different if you instead presupposed that truth is what survives impartial examination rather than partisan contest?

Sorry, that's a lot of threads. Basically what I want is: reconstruct the implicit epistemology of this setup — what has to be true about truth, arguments, and human judgment for the thing to work at all. Tell me which of those commitments are load-bearing versus incidental, where the practice is underdetermined (i.e., several quite different views of justification would license the same setup), and if you swapped in a rival epistemology — say one where what persuades a judge tracks rhetorical polish and the judge's priors more than truth — what would have to change in the setup for it to still be worth running at all?
