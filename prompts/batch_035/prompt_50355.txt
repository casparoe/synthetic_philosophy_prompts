I'm a science and ideas columnist and I'm sketching a piece on epistemic injustice after reading a pop-philosophy book that came across my desk. The author sums the whole thing up roughly like this: Miranda Fricker showed that prejudice can wrong people in their capacity as knowers, and there are two flavors -- testimonial injustice, where a listener discounts what someone says because of who they are (a woman not being believed about her own experience, say), and hermeneutical injustice, where a group lacks the shared concepts to make sense of what's happening to it, like before society had a term for sexual harassment. Both come from prejudice in the background culture, so the upshot is that every credibility deficit suffered by a marginalized group is itself an injustice, and the remedy is to compensate by believing members of those groups by default. Is this a fair run-down or is the book smoothing over stuff Fricker is actually careful about? I'd love to go claim by claim -- what she'd sign off on, where the author merges things she keeps distinct (I have a vague memory that she has some notion of credibility deficits that are NOT injustices, maybe where the hearer isn't prejudiced at all or the deficit tracks something else, but I couldn't reconstruct it), and whether the believe-them-by-default conclusion follows from anything defensible in the earlier parts or is just the author extrapolating. My editor will want to quote the book so I want to know which bits of it I should push back on before I do.
