So the essay's core, quickly. As I understand it, Harsanyi showed back in 1955 that if each individual satisfies the von Neumann–Morgenstern expected utility axioms, and the social ordering is Pareto-respecting and also satisfies expected-utility rationality, then social welfare has to come out as a weighted sum of individual utilities. Which looks like a derivation of utiltarianism (apologies, typing fast) from rationality assumptions alone. There's also a second version he pushed, the impartial observer one: put yourself behind a veil where you're equally likely to be anyone in society, and rational choice under risk says maximize the average of everyone's utility. Rawls hated this and argued for maximin instead, partly on the grounds that you shouldn't assign probabilities to being particular people.

My proposed contribution, and the thing I want scrutinized most, is an analogy to measurement in physics. When I average temperatures from two thermocouples, the average is meaningless unless both are on the same scale with the same zero point — Celsius vs. Kelvin vs. Fahrenheit completely changes what a "sum" of temperatures even means, and only differences and ratios within one scale are safe. Now, vNM utility for a single person is only defined up to a positive affine transformation, and crucially that transformation is separate for each person, with an arbitrary zero per person. So when the theorem drops everyone's utilities into one big expectation, it seems to quietly presuppose that the utilities are already on a common scale — the theorem doesn't derive interpersonal comparability, it assumes it and then derives the summation form. I want to claim the deep content of the theorem is: given commensurability, aggregation must be linear. The commensurability itself is smuggled in.

Fourth, does my objection hit the two versions of the theorem differently? My instinct is the impartial-observer version is more exposed, since the veil does the commensurating by fiat, but I haven't thought it through.
