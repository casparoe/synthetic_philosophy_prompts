I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and by accident of volunteering I now sit on the board of trustees of our regional history museum. We're planning a permanent exhibit on the Holodomor, the famine in Soviet Ukraine in 1932–33, and the exhibits committee must decide what the main wall panel calls it. The local Ukrainian-American community, some of them descendants of survivors, has asked in writing that the panel read "genocide." Two trustees object. One says the scholarly dispute about whether the famine meets the legal definition is unresolved and a museum shouldn't take sides in it. The other worries about precedent: we plan future exhibits on other mass atrocities, and she thinks "genocide" functions as a prize — the label confers a unique moral status, and the fights over who gets it are themselves political acts we'd be joining rather than adjudicating. My own reading, as far as I can follow it, is that the real fault line is definitional. Lemkin, who coined the word, thought in terms of destroying groups as such, national groups included; the 1948 Convention narrowed it to intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group, and excluded political and economic ones. The Holodomor sits right on that line: if Stalin's aim was to break Ukrainians as a nation, it is genocide; if it was to crush peasants as a class and extract grain whatever the human cost, it is not, however monstrous. Whether that distinction tracks anything morally real, or is a lawyer's artifact, seems to be the question underneath the label fight — but I may be missing part of the picture.

Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate to the exhibits committee before our next meeting. Recommend exactly one of three courses — use "genocide" unqualified; use it with explicit qualification, presenting the definitional dispute on the panel itself; or avoid the term — and defend that recommendation with reasons the trustees can act on, including an honest treatment of the strongest argument behind each rejected option. Don't simply restate the Convention's text; engage the question of what the label is for, morally and institutionally, and why that favors one course. Please write in American English, keep it to something readable in one sitting, and end the memo with a single line in the form "Recommendation: <unqualified / qualified / avoid>" followed by one sentence of justification.
