I'm a journalist who covers science and the environment for a general-interest outlet, and I keep tripping over this one corner of the animal ethics debate that nobody in mainstream conservation wants to touch. The standard line is obvious: habitat destruction is a tragedy, fewer wild animals is bad, protect the forest. But there's this small-ish crowd — mostly people who come out of effective altruism and worry about what they call wild animal suffering — who point out it might not be so simple. If the average wild animal life is net negative (think of the frog that lays thousands of eggs and all but two of them die within days), then shrinking wild populations could mean less total suffering, not more. On that view, clearing a forest might spare a huge number of animals from lives not worth living by never letting them exist. Honestly I find this creepy and also can't quite refute it, which is an awkward place for a journalist to be.

So give me your credence on a specific version of the question: when humans destroy a mature wild ecosystem — say they clear a forest for farmland, or drain a wetland and leave it as degraded scrub — does that reduce net wild animal suffering over the following century, relative to leaving the habitat alone? I want it decomposed. The terms I can see are at least these: (1) whether the average wild animal life is really net negative, which turns on r-selection, infant mortality, and whether positive experiences can offset suffering; (2) whether the destroyed habitat actually ends up holding fewer animals than before — this is less obvious than it sounds, since cropland is crawling with rodents and insects, and pasture is basically a livestock farm, so "destruction" sometimes just swaps one set of animals for a different (possibly larger, possibly worse-off) set; (3) whether preventing bad lives from ever existing counts as reducing suffering in the first place — the non-existence problem, where the animal that never exists isn't obviously better off, and different answers to that one flip the whole conclusion; and (4) whatever else you think belongs in there, maybe something about knock-on effects like eratic population booms in whatever species do survive the disturbance.

Put a rough probability on each term with one line of justification, combine them, and give me a final number. Then tell me which term you're least confident in and what kind of field evidence would actually move it. And be honest with me about whether the question is even coherent enough to have a truth value — my editor thinks it's philosophy-magazine filler, and I half suspect the real story is that the answer depends entirely on how you frame the non-existence part, in which case I'd rather write that story.
