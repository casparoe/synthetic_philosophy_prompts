Second opinion please. The question was:

"Money is often given as a paradigm of a socially constructed kind. (a) Explain the difference between a kind's being causally dependent on social practices and its being socially constituted by them. (b) What does Searle's notion of a constitutive rule add here? (c) Does it follow from money's being socially constructed that a sufficiently coordinated group could change it at will? Defend your answer."

The student's answer:

"Money is socially constructed because whether something is money depends on us and not on its physical properties. A twenty dollar bill is just paper (or cloth); what makes it money is that we all treat it as money, unlike water, whose being water is fixed by chemistry. But this is not mere causal influence. The International Astronomical Union's practices causally influenced what counts as a planet when they demoted Pluto, yet planets are not socially constructed, since Pluto's properties do not depend on us at all. With money the dependence is constitutive: in Searle's terms, the rule 'this counts as money' makes the thing money, rather than regulating a kind of thing that already existed. Constitutive rules are everywhere once you look: traffic laws constitute driving in exactly the way the rules of chess constitute chess. Searle also describes the looping effect, whereby classifying people changes their behavior, which is why constructed kinds stay unstable and never quite settle. Since money exists only by collective acceptance, its causal powers are entirely our beliefs: a bill's purchasing power is nothing over and above everyone believing it has purchasing power. Still, it doesn't follow that money can be changed at will, since getting everyone to accept a replacement is a hard coordination problem. So constructed doesn't mean arbitrary, but it does mean that if we all stopped playing the game money would vanish overnight, which shows it was never fully real in the first place, only real-for-us."
