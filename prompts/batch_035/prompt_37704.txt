I'm running a film-and-philosophy night and want to do Whiplash through the knowing-how vs. knowing-that debate. Ryle's line was that knowing how to do something can't just be knowing a bunch of facts, or you'd need a further skill to apply the facts, and so on down an infinite regress — the ability is the primitive. The intellectualists (Stanley and Williamson are the big names) answer that knowledge-how is really knowledge-that, just entertained under a practical mode of presentation. Whiplash looks like gift-wrapped Rylean material: Fletcher basically never teaches Andrew anything propositional, he just terrorizes him until the ability shows up on its own, and the family dinner scene literally sets Andrew's nameless, unrankable skill against a table of cousins whose achievements (division three football, Model U.N.) can be recited as credentials. But here's my worry: maybe the film is secretly intellectualist, because the one thing Fletcher does deploy is stories — the Charlie Parker cymbal anecdote he tells the band is pure proposition, and it visibly does something to Andrew. So read the film through the debate both ways: does Fletcher's classroom show that skill can't be transmitted propositionally, or that it can be, just cruelly? And what about the final scene, where Andrew improvises — if knowing-how were knowledge-that, is there anything Andrew could literally tell me that would let me do what he does up there, and does the film's answer to that cut either way? Also be honest about where the film resists the reading: it's arguably not about the epistemology of skill at all but about whether the skill is worth having, and I don't want to strain it. Point me to 2-3 scenes to build the night around. I want a reading that takes the Ryle vs. Stanley-and-Williamson fight seriously, not just "practice makes perfect."
