Longtime lurker here (LW, decision theory threads, the works), and I thought I had the two-envelope paradox fully filed away, but recent discussions have made me realize I only understand the boring 60% of it. Help me out.

The version I internalized: "Let X be the amount in your envelope. The other one has either 2X or X/2 with equal probability, so switching has EV 1.25X, so switch — but by symmetry that can't be right." The standard fix, as I understand it, is that the argument illicitly treats X like a fixed constant when it's actually different random variables in the two branches — the X in "2X" ranges over the smaller amounts and the X in "X/2" ranges over the larger ones, and averaging them as if they were the same value is where the funny business happens. Fine. Symmetry, no free lunch, no EV from switching, done. Or so I thought.

But here's where I keep falling off the wagon:

1. People claim that if you actually OPEN your envelope and observe a concrete amount, the situation genuinely changes — conditional on what you see, switching can be +EV for some observed values and -EV for others, and these exactly cancel ex ante, which is supposedly how this squares with the symmetry argument. What's the actual mechanics here? Like, the observed amount is evidence about whether you're holding the smaller or larger envelope (via Bayes on whatever generated the amounts), so a small observed number favors "you've got the small one, switch" and a big one favors staying? What property of the prior distribution over amounts determines where the flip point is, and are there priors for which observing never helps?

2. Worse: apparently there exist distributions where EVERY possible observed amount favors switching. How is that not a literal money pump / a decision theory disaster rather than a cute curiosity? I gather this only happens with heavy-tailed or unbounded priors where the expectation never terminates — is that right? Is the "you must always switch" conclusion then legitimate, or is it another case of conditional probabilities being individually fine while the joint thing is broken?

3. And then the infinite-expectation variants (Broome-style, where switching seems to give infinite expected gain, or at least gains that outrun any bound) where people say the standard resolution just doesn't apply. Is the right takeaway there "expected utility maximization is broken," or is it "comparing infinite/undefined expectations is the broken part, EU itself is fine once you stop doing that"? And is this the same underlying disease as the St. Petersburg / Pasadena nonsense — divergent expectations that let you reorder terms to get whatever answer you want — or a genuinely separate failure mode? I.e., if you personally had to patch your decision theory for exactly one of St Petersburg or Broome-style two-envelope, would the patch be the same patch?

Please walk me through the actual math-y mechanics of the conditional-on-observed-value picture rather than just re-restating the symmetry point — I've read the "you're double-counting / X isn't a constant" resolution like fifty times and I'm specifically trying to understand where the clean story stops applying and what takes over in the weird cases. Intuition pumps welcome, formalism welcome, but connect them.
