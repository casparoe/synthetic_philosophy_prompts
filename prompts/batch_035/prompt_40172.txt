I'm a second-year history undergrad taking an environmental history survey, and something about how our textbook (and basically every "big history" course I've come across) handles non-human causes has been bugging me. The book is totally happy crediting germs, climate, and volcanoes with causing events: the eruptions of 536 plus the Justinianic plague get blamed for ending antiquity's long economic boom, the 1815 Tambora eruption is credited with the "year without a summer" and everything downstream of it (crop failures, famine, food riots, even the wet gloomy vacation during which Frankenstein got written), the Little Ice Age is invoked as a cause of the seventeenth-century "general crisis" of wars and revolts, and diseases in the Columbian Exchange are said to have done most of the actual conquering of the Americas. Non-human things are basically allowed to be historical actors, and nobody blinks.

But the moment anyone tries to explain a standing pattern with the environment -- why Europe industrialized first, why some regions ended up rich and others poor -- the same textbook turns cold and starts warning about "environmental determinism" as if it were a discredited sin. So germs and volcanoes can flip events one way or another, but climate and geography can't explain persistent differences between places. Is that a principled distinction or just mood? My instinct is that there's an implicit theory of historical causation doing the work here, something like "non-human factors count as legitimate triggers of events but not as background conditions that shape what the actors are like in the first place." But I can't tell if that's actually coherent, and I'm not even sure the textbook authors themselves believe it, since they also say things like the Little Ice Age mattered *because* it interacted with agrarian economies that were structured in specific ways, which starts to sound like it's explaining standing differences after all.

So here's what I'd like: reconstruct the implicit theory of causation / historical explanation that this practice of selective crediting presupposes, if there really is one. Which rival views does it quietly rule out -- is it ruling out determinism as such, or just determinism about certain kinds of explananda (trajectories vs. events)? Is the distinction maybe just about robustness, i.e. a human-made institution can be reorganized in response to a volcano but not to a standing climate, or something about the counterfactuals involved? And importantly, tell me whether the practice actually *needs* any of this -- maybe it's compatible with several different theories of historical causation and the asymmetry is doing no real work, in which case, what would the textbooks have to look like if they dropped it and treated triggering and standing explanations even-handedly? My TA said something about "structure vs. conjuncture" when I asked her, but I don't think that dissolves the puzzle. Also feel free to push back on my framing if the textbooks are just right and there's no tension at all.
