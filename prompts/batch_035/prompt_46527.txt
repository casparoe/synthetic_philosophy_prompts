A guy in my forecasting group (we do calibration exercises and argue about odds in a group chat) has taken to ending basically every dispute with de Finetti's line from the preface to his Theory of Probability: "PROBABILITY DOES NOT EXIST." He deploys it whenever anyone says anything like "the true probability that the incumbent wins is 60%" — his claim being that there's no fact of the matter to calibrate against, so the whole idea of scoring our forecasts against reality is confused. Last week it came up over whether a bridge player's claim that a finesse has a 50% chance is a fact about the deck or a fact about their ignorance. He also has a habit of dismissing talk of chances in physics with the same line, which honestly seems like a different move.

What I'd like is a menu of the distinct precise claims that one sentence could actually be expressing, from the near-trivial to the genuinely radical, with a verdict on each — which readings are defensible, which are false or at least fighting words, and which one de Finetti himself plausibly meant (I gather from the preface that he thought of it as a denial that probability is some objectively existing thing like phlogiston, and that he was oddly insistent it didn't matter whether the underlying processes were deterministic or not — which seems relevant). And crucially: which of the readings, if any, would actually license what my friend is doing with it? It's one thing to say probabilities are degrees of belief, another to say there are no objective single-case chances at all, and a further thing to say that talk of "the true probability of the election outcome" is category-confused. I want to be able to tell him which version he needs for his argument and whether he'd want to accept its consequences (e.g., for what it even means for one forecaster to be "better" than another, or for chances in quantum mechanics).

Please lay the readings out as a list I can literally paste into the group chat, ordered from weakest to strongest, and for each one say: does it support using the line to shut down "true probability" talk, and does it support using it against chances in physics? If the readings come apart on those two uses, flag that, since he seems to think the one sentence does both jobs.
