In my philosophy of biology seminar, a student who works on optimality modelling and a student who came to the field through Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique have spent an entire term arguing past each other. The first insists that proposing and testing adaptive hypotheses is simply how evolutionary biology makes progress; the second insists that the field's default explanatory posture is Panglossian, mistaking byproducts, constraints, and frozen accidents for adaptations, and that the resulting stories are too cheap to count as science. Rather than adjudicate between them, I want to force the discipline of a joint product. Draft the joint statement the two of them should be able to sign after a serious adversarial collaboration.

The document should contain, in order: (1) a numbered list of claims both would accept -- about the fact and power of natural selection, the reality of developmental and architectural constraints, the role of drift, and the underdetermination of any single historical explanation; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated in wording its own side would endorse -- I expect these to include at least the expected proportion of phenotypic traits that are byproducts rather than adaptations, whether adaptive hypotheses can be tested at all without a specified space of alternatives, whether constraint-based and historical explanations have predictive content or are only narratives told after the fact, and whether adaptationism is best understood as a substantive empirical thesis about the world or merely as a research heuristic that has proven fruitful; (3) the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party -- with, for each, a test, measurement, or line of argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction about the outcome recorded in advance, so that neither can claim afterwards that the result meant something else; and (4) an application of the whole framework to one concrete case, which they should take to be human music: by their own agreed criteria, what would it take to establish that music is an adaptation, a spandrel of language and auditory pattern-processing, or something shaped by sexual selection, and what does each predict the evidence would show if the relevant studies could actually be run?

Finally, I want them to confront a suspicion I have raised in seminar without resolution: that part of their dispute is verbal, because the very individuation of traits is at stake. A byproduct is only a byproduct relative to some prior decision about what counts as the unit to be explained, and it is far from obvious that the two students carve the organism into traits the same way -- the adaptationist's "trait" may already be a selection-circumscribed object, while the Gouldian's may be a structural or developmental one. Ask them to say whether this is a genuine methodological disagreement, a hidden verbal one, or a substantive disagreement about the ontology of the organism, and, if verbal, exactly which of the disagreements in their table should be dissolved by fixing terminology rather than settled by evidence. Please keep the tone of a formal signed document: no advocacy, no scoring of points, and nothing in the agreed-claims section that either party could plausibly deny.
