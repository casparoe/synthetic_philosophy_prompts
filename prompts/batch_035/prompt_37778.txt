Me and my little brother had this rule forever: whenever there was one last slice of cake, whoever cut it let the other pick first. No parents involved, it just enforced itself — the kid with the knife makes it even because he knows he's getting last pick. I got into an argument with my friend Dara, who took a philosophy class last year, about taxes of all things, and she kept going back to this "veil of ignorance" thing from Rawls — imagine you could shape the whole basic structure of society (taxes, schools, healthcare, all of it) but you had to do it behind a veil where you didn't know if you'd end up rich or poor, healthy or disabled, what talents you'd have, or even what you'd want out of life. The moment she said it I thought, that's literally our cake rule blown up to society. Cut the social pie without knowing your slice, and you'll cut it fair.

But then she said the veil is supposed to justify this thing called the difference principle — inequalities are only allowed if they work out to the advantage of the worst off — and that's where I started to feel like the cake picture was doing work she wasn't admitting to. With actual cake, the whole point is you cut *evenly*; you'd never say "an uneven slice is fine as long as the smaller one is still better than the smaller slice would've been under a different cut." Yet that's apparently what parties behind the veil pick, because uneven slices can grow the whole cake. Also our cake thing only worked because there were exactly two of us and we watched each other cut — behind the veil it sounds like everyone at once, somehow agreeing? And Dara's roommate chimed in that if you really don't know which slice you get, you'd just gamble on the average, the way you'd take a lottery where every ticket is drawn equally, which is apparently a whole different conclusion other people drew from nearly the same setup.

So that's what I want: lay out the correspondence table — what plays the knife, the picking, the last slice, the two kids — then tell me where the analogy breaks down and which of the breaks actually matter for the conclusion versus which are just noise (I don't care that real cake isn't education). I especially want to know whether the difference principle survives once the cake picture is gone — is it something you can argue to directly, or was the "cut the pie blind and you'll play it safe" intuition the only thing carrying it? And if the analogy is a bad fit, is there a better everyday one, or does fairness at that scale just not have a homely version?
