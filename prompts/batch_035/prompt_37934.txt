I've been defending a version of negative utilitarianism in a reading group and someone just ran an argument past me that I think proves far too much. I want help diagnosing it.

The view, first, so we're on the same page. Call it *lexical threshold negative utilitarianism*. Below a certain intensity of suffering, hedonic goods and bads commensurate in the ordinary utilitarian way: mild pains can be outweighed, compensated for, and traded off against pleasures, and more of either side shifts the balance in the usual manner. But suffering at or above a certain threshold is different in kind: it is lexically prior to all pleasure, such that no amount of happiness, however vast or prolonged, can outweigh or compensate for even a moment of it. The motivation is the familiar Popperian thought that no quantity of additional joy in the universe could make up for a single instance of torture-level agony, combined with a reluctance to accept the full negative utilitarian package, on which even the mildest headache can outweigh paradise and a painless extinction of all life would be an improvement.

Here is the argument against my view:

1. The intensity of a pain is a continuous quantity: for any two pains that differ in intensity, there are pains of every intermediate intensity, and we can construct pairs of pains whose intensities differ by an arbitrarily small amount.
2. The disvalue of a pain is a continuous function of its intensity, at least in the neighborhood of the threshold: pains that differ only infinitesimally in intensity differ only infinitesimally in how bad they are.
3. Let T be the threshold intensity. By my view, any pain of intensity T or above cannot be outweighed by any amount of pleasure, while any pain of intensity just below T can be outweighed by some finite amount of pleasure — call the amount needed for a given sub-threshold pain its "covering amount."
4. Take a pain p⁻ just below T and a pain p⁺ just above T. By (1), their intensities can be made arbitrarily close; by (2), their disvalues differ arbitrarily little.
5. If p⁻ has a finite covering amount F, and p⁺ is only negligibly worse than p⁻, then p⁺ has a covering amount only negligibly greater than F — still finite. But by (3), p⁺ has no covering amount at all.
6. So (1), (2), and (3) are jointly inconsistent. Since (1) is not in serious dispute, either (2) is false or my threshold view (3) is false.
7. And if I drop the threshold, I face a dilemma: I can push the threshold to zero, making every pain, down to the pinprick, lexically prior to all pleasure (plain negative utilitarianism, with its unpalatable corollaries), or abandon lexical priority entirely and accept that enough ice cream can outweigh a torture (ordinary hedonic utilitarianism). Either way, the moderate view I actually hold is refuted.

The reason I say it proves too much: step (5) looks like a pure sorites-style tolerance principle, and the same pattern of reasoning would seem to demolish every sharp threshold imposed on a continuous quantity — the age of majority, the clinical cutoff for a diagnosis, the depth that separates a pond from a lake. A person one second before her eighteenth birthday and one second after differ arbitrarily little, yet differ radically in legal standing, and I do not conclude that legal thresholds are incoherent. If I accept thresholds elsewhere, what entitles me to reject this argument at (2) rather than at (5)? Or is value special among quantities — the one domain where a discontinuity in the underlying intensity really does forbid a discontinuity in how it may be weighed?

I have also wondered whether the argument equivocates on what lexical priority claims. If "no amount of pleasure outweighs T-level pain" means T-pain has some enormous or infinite magnitude on a single shared scale, then continuity of disvalue is at risk and the argument bites. But if it means instead that the two sides are simply not commensurable — that the value ordering is incomplete across the threshold, so that comparisons of amount are simply undefined there rather than decided in favor of the pain — then perhaps no continuous function was ever in play. I don't know whether that escape is coherent, whether it leaves me with anything that still deserves the name "utilitarianism," or how it aggregates when a choice forces a comparison anyway.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If your answer is (2), tell me whether the discontinuity it buys me is principled or ad hoc, given that I concede continuity everywhere below the threshold. If your answer is (5), tell me exactly how the disanalogy with legal and clinical thresholds is supposed to go. And if you think the incomparability reading rescues me, explain what the view then says when a decision must be made between allowing a T-level pain and forgoing an arbitrarily large pleasure — since "undefined" is not an answer an ethical theory is permitted to give in practice. I would most like to keep (3); I am prepared to be told that wanting to keep it is the problem.
