A friend in my Foucault reading group has taken to calling the platform economy a digital panopticon. Her thought: on social media we are permanently visible, and as in Bentham's prison and Foucault's reading of it, we can never confirm when we are actually being watched, so we internalize the gaze and discipline ourselves — docile subjects, no punishment required. She uses the analogy to conclude that the real product of surveillance capitalism is the self-monitoring soul, and that this is where its power over us mainly lies. Audit the analogy. First lay out, in prose, the full correspondence: what plays the role of the tower, the unseen inspector, the inmate, the norm being enforced, and the institution the prison stood in for. Then say where the mapping breaks — for instance, whether anyone is watching in the relevant sense, whether the visibility is unwanted or actively courted, whether the norm at stake is docility or something more like engagement, and whether observation still needs to be unverifiable now that it is effectively total — and separate the disanalogies that matter for her conclusion from the incidental ones. Then tell me whether her conclusion survives the audit, or whether a different piece of Foucault's own toolkit fits the case better: his insistence that power is productive rather than merely repressive, or his later account of governmentality, on which power operates through our freedom and choices rather than through imposed visibility. Continuous prose only, please — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a long letter.
