I'm in a grad seminar on aggregation in ethics, and we just spent two weeks on whether the numbers count when you can rescue some people but not others. I've read Taurek's 1977 paper (the David case, the coin flip) and a pile of responses to it, but honestly the response literature feels like it's talking past each other because nobody ever states what they're demanding of a principle here. Before I read any more of it, I want the spec first. So: write the desiderata — what any adequate answer to the numbers problem has to get right, which cases it must handle, which intuitions it either vindicates or explains away, which distinctions it can't collapse. Obvious things to include would be the coin flip fairness intuition (that it's not obviously a wrong to give each person an equal chance even when the groups are unequal in size), the separateness of persons, the need to say something about unequal losses (five people each facing the loss of one year of life vs. one person facing the loss of fifty — do the five still win, and if so why is it now the numbers rather than the losses counting), and whether the principle extends from rescue cases to charity allocation or has to be quarantined there.

But also stress-test the weirder requirements, because I suspect the real action is there. Like: does the principle have to be invariant between rescuing groups and rescuing individuals one at a time (I save one drowning swimmer, and while I'm doing that, one more shows up, and then another — at what point, if any, am I doing something wrong if I keep going back for single people? my seminar calls this the bridge worry but I don't know if it has a real name)? And does it have to give consistent verdicts when the same people are re-described — five strangers vs. five members of one family who each claim to count separately vs. a "group" that's really one person losing five distinct things? And should the principle cover the reverse-Taurek cases where almost everyone's intuition flips, e.g. a thousand people each facing the loss of a fingernail vs. one person facing paralysis? Give the case behind each requirement, say which ones you think are genuine desiderata and which are just prejudices of the aggregative or anti-aggregative camps, and flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied.

Then, briefly, score the main contenders against your own list — Taurek's equal-chances view, straightforward utilitarian aggregation, and a threshold view where numbers start mattering only past some cutoff — but keep that part short, the requirements are what I care about. And don't hand me a menu: commit to which desiderata are non-negotiable, take a definite stand on whether the set is jointly satisfiable, and defend it, even if you end up biting a bullet. If you think a requirement that's commonly treated as sacred (like the separateness of persons) should actually be dropped, say so and say why — that's the sort of thing I want to argue about in my response paper.
