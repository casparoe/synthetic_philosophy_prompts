I grew up in a mid-sized river town whose population has fallen by a third since I was a child. I left for a decade and came back last year, when the council hired me to direct the municipal museum — partly because I wanted to come home, partly because my mother needed someone nearby. The town's 250th founding anniversary is eighteen months out, and in three weeks I present the interpretive plan for the anniversary exhibition to the town heritage committee. It is the most consequential thing I will do in this job, and preparing it has sent me back to a text I first read as an undergraduate: Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, on the uses and disadvantages of history for life.

My volunteers have spent a decade assembling exactly the exhibition that convention expects — the founding families' ledgers, portraits, tools, and letters, indexed with a devotion I can only admire. That is what Nietzsche calls antiquarian history: history as preserving and revering, addressed to those who want to keep and honor the familiar. My plan inverts it. I will argue that the anniversary should be organized monumentally — history in the service of acting and striving — staging the founding as evidence of what a few hundred ordinary, frightened people can build, deliberately addressed to the twenty-somethings who are leaving: selective by design, stripped of the glass-case apparatus, unapologetic about being an incitement rather than a record. I also want a subordinate critical strand — the founding was entangled in land speculation, and the telling should concede it — but as a subordinate movement, not the center of gravity. Two places in my own position I already know are soft. Nietzsche concedes that monumental history shades into free invention where the record is thin, and he warns that great-individual history becomes the consolation of the mediocre, who want to feel connected to something great as an excuse for their own smallness. I am fairly sure the professor knows that second passage by heart.

The committee, seated across a folding table next to a photocopier:

- The two longest-serving volunteer researchers, who between them have indexed every parish record and gravestone in the district. One of them trained me in the archive. For them the anniversary is the capstone of a lifetime of accumulation, and they want the full documentary record on the walls. Their objection will be grief wearing the clothes of scholarship.
- The council's economic-development officer, who wants the anniversary aspirational, has already put the phrase our founding story in a draft tourism brochure, and would happily adopt my plan as his own. My problem is that what he wants and what I want look uncomfortably alike from the outside, and I have not yet found the sentence that distinguishes monumental history in Nietzsche's sense from a founding myth for marketing.
- A retired professor of social history who moved back to town, and who spent his career puncturing exactly the kind of commemorative myth I am proposing to build — the invented-tradition school of historiography is his native vocabulary, and he will be the best questioner in the room.

Prepare my Q&A sheet. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will actually arrive, each phrased the way that particular committee member would put it, in that person's own vocabulary. For each, give a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft, and an honest verdict on whether the objection should change the plan or merely be survived. Tell me the one I am hoping nobody asks. And I am asking you to commit, not to curate: if the monumental core of the plan is indefensible before this committee — or defensible only in substantially altered form — say so, and give me the altered form. Do not end by telling me that antiquarian and monumental history each have their place and leave the weighting to me; commit to the weighting and defend it. Where you depend on a reading of Nietzsche that scholars dispute, flag it, but still take a side.
