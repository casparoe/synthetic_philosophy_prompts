In my political philosophy course we just finished the part of Plato's Republic where Glaucon tells the ring of Gyges story, and I've been trying to work out my own response to his challenge by listing my gut verdicts on modern versions of the ring: cases where I'm as certain as I can be that I'd never be caught, so the only thing that could keep me honest is whatever part of justice is valuable for its own sake rather than for its reputation. My verdicts: keeping the $260 in a wallet dropped by a tourist who has already flown home and can never be traced — not fine, and I expect it would nag at me for years. Taking sole credit for a colleague's idea in a meeting she skipped, when she never reads the minutes — not fine. Cheating on a curved exam by a method that leaves no trace, where my extra points lower everyone else's grades by some undetectably small amount — not fine, though I can't pretend the harm is what's moving me, since it's essentially nil. Downloading a film from a large studio when I'm sure I would never have paid for it — fine, honestly, and I don't lose sleep over it. Breaking a promise made to a friend who has since died, in circumstances where no one else ever knew the promise existed — this is the one I keep going back and forth on. I notice the third and fourth verdicts sit oddly together: the exam cheating and the download both involve near-zero harm, guaranteed undetectability, and a rule I'm simply choosing to break, yet I judge them differently. My tentative principle, which I take to be roughly what Glaucon is pushing us toward, is that in ring situations one may do whatever is both undetectable and harmless — and it can't explain why the download feels fine and the exam doesn't.

Could you help me find a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures these verdicts? Please state each principle precisely, tie each of its clauses to the case that motivates it, and say what it predicts for cases I haven't listed — for example, whether it's permissible to tell a dying friend that her unpublished novel is brilliant when she will never submit it anywhere and no one will ever read it, or to quietly break a promise to a spouse in a way she will never discover. And be honest about which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate: I'd rather overturn one of my gut calls than bolt on epicycles to save all of them, so I'd like to know which verdict has to go and why, if any does.

Finally, since I plan to build a paper around whichever principle we settle on, please indicate how confident you are in each of your central claims, and single out the point you are least sure of.
