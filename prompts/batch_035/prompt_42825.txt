I help run a small online discussion group about free will and the brain, and I want to build a "where do you actually stand on free will now that you know about Libet?" quiz for our members. Most of them have heard of the readiness potential showing up in the brain a few hundred milliseconds before people say they first feel the urge to move, and of Libet's suggestion that we can still veto an action in the last moment, but they have never had to say what they think follows from any of it. I have also read that later work questions whether the readiness potential is really the beginning of a decision at all, rather than just background activity that the decision happens to tip over — I would like the quiz to force people to take a stand on that too.

Please write eight to ten questions, each a concrete case rather than a request for a label — something like a pianist whose fingers start moving before she reports deciding anything, or a sniper who feels the urge and stops himself just before pulling the trigger. Two or three answer options each, in plain wording that a reader could choose between without knowing any position's name. One question should turn on whether the early brain activity is the decision itself or merely a precondition of it; one should turn on whether the last-moment veto deserves to be called free will; at least one should be about cases where acting on autopilot turns out better than deliberating.

Then give a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the main positions: the view that conscious will is an illusion; the view that the timing results show nothing because the early activity is not a decision; the compatibilist view that the experiments are beside the point because free will was never about the timing of urges; the libertarian or dualist view that consciousness initiates action in a way electrodes cannot see; and the veto view that our freedom lies mainly in stopping ourselves. Finally, please flag any two of these positions the quiz cannot genuinely separate, and note any pattern of answers that no real position occupies — I would rather know where my quiz fails than pretend it sorts everyone neatly.
