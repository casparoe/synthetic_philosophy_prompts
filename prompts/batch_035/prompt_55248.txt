Everyone learns the Galileo affair as science versus the church, but the philosophically interesting part is that Cardinal Bellarmine's position around 1615 — that the Copernican model was fine as a predictive device but couldn't be asserted as literally true — was, for its moment, a fairly respectable underdetermination claim: the astronomical data available then genuinely didn't decide between Copernicus, Tycho Brahe's hybrid system (planets circle the sun, sun circles the earth), and a patched-up Ptolemy. So try analyzing the whole episode as a case study in underdetermination of theory by evidence, not as a story about dogma. I want a careful mapping: what the evidence base actually was circa 1615 (naked-eye planetary positions, the failure to observe stellar parallax, the telescopic stuff — the phases of Venus, which killed pure Ptolemy but left Tycho standing), which theories were genuinely in play, and what Bellarmine got right if you take transient underdetermination seriously. Then the breakdown: Galileo's tides argument, which he treated as his clincher and which was simply wrong, and the eventual empirical resolution via stellar aberration in 1728 and actual parallax measurements in the 1830s. Does the fact that it took two hundred years to get decisive evidence vindicate the underdetermination claim, or does it show that underdetermination was never a serious obstacle because the non-empirical virtues — simplicity, harmony, explanatory unification — had already tilted the field? And run it both directions: what does the episode teach about underdetermination itself? Is this a case where the philosophical point was correct but got weaponized for political and theological ends, and does the standard distinction between transient and permanent underdetermination actually do any work here, or is it just a way of saying wait and see? I'd also like you to be honest about where underdetermination talk misdescribes the episode — alot of the dispute was about scriptural hermeneutics and Galileo's own tactical blunders, and I want to know whether the underdetermination lens explains anything about how it actually ended. Don't just tell me science eventually won so the problem was solved.
