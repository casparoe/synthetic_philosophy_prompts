I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar — fourteen students, one 90-minute session — and I want them to leave with multiple realizability as something they have felt rather than recited. Design an in-class activity built on the following scenario, which you should extend and sharpen rather than merely restate: each team receives a sealed substrate dossier describing an alien or engineered system — a creature with a radically decentralised nervous system, a market of trading algorithms, a silicon artefact whose internal cycle runs a thousand times slower than a human's, a slime mould that reorganises itself chemically in response to damage — and the teams' task is to award or withhold a single certificate: this system is in pain, or in a state functionally equivalent to pain. The certificates are where the action should be. To award one, a team must first write down which causal role they are treating pain as, and you should engineer the systems so that the same role shows up at different levels of grain and at wildly different clock speeds — forcing the question of whether "the same functional organisation" is a fact about the system or an artefact of how coarsely the team chose to describe it. That last twist is what I care about most: the standard textbook examples vary only the hardware, and I want them to discover that the harder question is what individuates the roles.

Give me rules, materials, a timeline, a scoring scheme that needs no real money, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the view — including where the game puts pressure on functionalism from the chauvinism and liberalism direction, and where it quietly begs the question against the type-identity theorist. Teams must be able to challenge one another's certificates, and that challenge round should be where the real philosophy happens, not an afterthought.

Two further requests. First, commit: make the design decisions and defend them rather than laying out options and leaving the choice to me — in particular, decide whether the game should be rigged so that functionalist verdicts tend to win, or left genuinely open, and tell me which you chose and what it costs. Second, include an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce — which part of the multiple realizability debate no classroom game with dossiers and certificates can deliver — and what I must supply in the debrief to make up the gap. Finish by predicting at least two things that will go wrong the first time I run it, and how I should handle each.

Write the whole thing in British English.
