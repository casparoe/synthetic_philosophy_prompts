so, background: I'm a novelist, mostly realist stuff — small towns, families, the kind of book where nothing explodes but everyone is quietly ruined by their own choices. I'm four chapters into a new novel set in a fishing town that's overfishing itself to death. Not because anyone is stupid or evil — every single captain knows the stocks are collapsing, and every single captain goes out anyway, because if he ties up his boat the guy in the next slip just takes the quota. I had it plotted as a classic tragedy, everyone's fatal flaws doing them in, the whole engine of the book. Then a friend who reads a lot of rationalist blogs sent me an essay — Meditations on Moloch, Scott Alexander, I think 2014 — and I felt the floor shift a little. Multipolar traps, races to the bottom, the idea that when enough agents compete, whatever sacrifices values for speed and power wins, and everyone ends up worse off than if they'd coordinated, and no villain is required. That's my whole novel. Except, apparently, my novel is not about what I thought it was about.

Here's what I want. Take the Moloch idea in full — multipolar traps, the race to the bottom, why coordination is the escape hatch but a fragile one — and restate it in the vocabulary I actually think in: character, agency, stakes, antagonist, plot necessity, tragic flaw, the difference between a tragedy and a mere disaster, the question of whether anyone in a story gets to be innocent. Be systematic about it, like a real translation and not vibes. Moloch-as-antagonist: what is lost when you give a faceless dynamic the antagonist slot, does it need intent it doesn't have, can a reader fear something with no interiority? Tragic flaw: my captains don't err, each individual choice is rational — is that still hamartia or is the concept doing work it can't do here? Tragedy itself: tragedy arguably needs the doom to be in some sense fitting or meaningful, and my characters' doom is just... an equilibrium. Does tragedy smuggle that in, and what breaks when you take it away?

Then the residue report, and honestly I care about this part most: which parts of the Moloch idea translate cleanly into my vocabulary, which parts only translate if you distort them (say which way the distortion goes, and who pays for it), and which parts have no expression at all in the vocabulary of narrative craft. And then a verdict: is the residue there because narrative craft is missing something, or because the Moloch talk is less clear than it looks, or — this is the possibility that actually scares me as a novelist — because a story about a faceless dynamic is not a story, and the act of making it one is already a lie? Because if the tragic form requires me to distribute the blame among my characters, and the whole point of a multipolar trap is that blame doesn't distribute like that, then maybe I shouldn't be writing this book at all, and I'd rather know now.

One more thing: as you go, tell me how confident you are in the translation claims, mark anything where you're basically guessing or leaning on a contested reading of tragedy or of the Moloch argument, and single out the one point in the whole thing you're least sure of. I don't need you to be right about Aristotle, I need to know where you're on thin ice so I know which of my chapters to rewrite first.
