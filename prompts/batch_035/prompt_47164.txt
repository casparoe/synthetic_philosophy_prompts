I'm taking a course on the philosophy of social science, and my term paper is on whether something like preference inference is epistemically sound -- basically the idea behind inverse reinforcement learning: that human preferences can be recovered from human behavior, because behavior is (approximately) optimal for those preferences, so we can work backwards. I've come out roughly in favor: I think the fact that we do this informally all the time -- inferring that someone must prefer quiet restaurants because they keep choosing them -- suggests the formal version is just tidying up something we already do, and the approximate optimality assumption is a modeling assumption like any other, not a wild empirical bet.

What I need is a serious map of what would count against my view. Not just the obvious stuff like "humans are irrational sometimes" -- I want to know which kinds of observed irrationality actually damage the inference (does akrasia break it, or just noise it can average out?), what formal results in the IRL literature or economics count as bad news (I gather there are results about how multiple reward functions can explain the same behavior, and I want to know how lethal those actually are vs. something the framework can absorb), and whether there are cases where the whole method gives an answer that's not just imprecise but confidently wrong. And honestly: is my position one of those views that's been qualified so many times ("approximate optimality", "bounded rationality", "preferences given the agent's beliefs and biases") that nothing could ever refute it? If so, tell me whether that's fatal or whether this kind of flexibility is fine for a modeling framework, the way no single anomaly refutes expected utility theory. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed defender of the view would concede vs. wave off.

One more thing: please keep settled stuff clearly separated from genuinely contested stuff. If the economists and the ML people and the philosophers of action actually disagree about, say, whether preferences must be defined relative to beliefs, I want to know that's a live fight and not have you hand me one side as the consensus. I'd rather my paper be honest and hedged than clean and wrong.
