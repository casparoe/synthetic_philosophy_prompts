I am a general relativist by training and profession: my working vocabulary is causal structure — timelike curves and their ordering, global time functions, Cauchy surfaces, global hyperbolicity, stable causality, chronology violation, and the singularity theorems. My acquaintance with philosophy is secondhand and mostly filtered through popularizations, so please pitch everything to that background.

I would like you to restate Gödel's philosophical argument for the ideality of time — the one he built around his rotating-dust solution of the field equations, which we know as the Gödel universe (1949) and which he also presented in the essay he contributed to the Schilpp volume honoring Einstein — in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. I already understand the mathematical pieces: his spacetime is homogeneous, geodesically complete and singularity-free, simply connected, and admits closed timelike curves through every point; consequently it admits no global time function. What I do not see is how he got from such facts to the conclusion that time does not objectively lapse — that the passage of time is, in his word, ideal.

So please construct the translation dictionary entry by entry, and tell me for each entry whether it is faithful, whether it smuggles in a premise, and whether anything on the philosophical side has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Two candidate entries I have guessed at: his "objective lapse of time" would become something like the existence of a foliation by Cauchy surfaces that is intrinsically distinguished, not merely relative to a chosen timelike congruence or observer; and his "journey into the past" is just a future-directed closed timelike curve that returns to the neighborhood of one's own past worldline. I suspect the first entry already begs a question, since whether chronology violation is physically permissible — or whether only globally hyperbolic spacetimes count as reasonable — is precisely the point at issue between me and Gödel, and I want you to flag that rather than bury it in the dictionary.

Then give me the residue report. As I read him, two steps look untranslatable. The first is the modal step: that if in some solution permitted by the field equations time cannot be objective, then time is not objective in our world either, since whether time lapses should not depend on how matter happens to be arranged. That looks like a substantive philosophical premise about the comparability of solutions, not a theorem about causal structure. The second is his appeal to the felt quality of temporal passage, which he treats as the one datum a theory of time must save; nothing in my vocabulary can express that at all. Please tell me whether this diagnosis is right, and whether the residue reflects a limitation of the causal-structure vocabulary or a lack of clarity in Gödel's argument itself. More pointedly: is the conclusion "time is ideal" anything over and above "there exist solutions of the field equations that admit no global time function"? If not, why have so many physicists and philosophers written as though Gödel had established something stronger? And if you think the chronology protection program is relevant, say whether it functions in my vocabulary as an answer to Gödel or as a refusal to answer the question he asked.
