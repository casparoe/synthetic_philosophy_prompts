I run the philosophy club at my school, and for our next session I have been put in charge of a fifteen-minute activity on how objects persist through time. Rather than explaining endurantism and perdurantism up front, I want to hand out a quiz that sorts people into a position without them knowing any of the vocabulary, and only then reveal what the labels are and whether anyone actually ended up where they thought they would.

Please write me such a quiz, with the following requirements.

First, the diagnostic questions: I would like eight to ten, each presenting a concrete case rather than an abstract question, and each answerable without knowing which theory it leads to. Some cases I would particularly like you to include: the bent poker that used to be straight (whether something has one shape simpliciter or different shapes at different times); the statue and the lump of clay that share every atom while both existing (are they one thing or two, and does the answer depend on whether we are counting at a moment or over a lifetime?); the person who existed as an embryo and, if all goes well, will exist as a very old adult (are you literally the same entity as that embryo, or is it more accurate to say you are one stage of a longer entity?); and at least one case you devise yourself involving counting — a question where the rival theories give different numerical answers about how many objects are in the room, or how many people have sat in a particular chair over the past decade.

Second, the positions to be sorted: the leaves of the tree should include at minimum endurantism (wholly present at each moment), the worm theory (four-dimensional objects with temporal parts), the stage theory (we are momentary stages related to other stages by a counterpart-like relation), and whatever deflationary or eliminativist option you think belongs there — plus, if you can find room for it, a branch that combines endurantism with presentism, provided you phrase the questions so that this combination actually gets reached by some answer pattern rather than being impossible to land on.

Third, the structure: please build the tree from the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, and then locate the named positions in that logical space, rather than just listing theories with one question each. I have been told this matters because I want to see where the logical space has combinations nobody defends — and for each unoccupied combination, a sentence on whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. Please also flag honestly any two leaves your questions cannot genuinely separate, rather than pretending the quiz is sharper than it is.

Finally, please write everything in British English, keep the tone serious and precise enough that a teacher could read it without wincing, and add a short scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves, since I will be tallying results by hand in a noisy classroom and need it to be usable at a glance.
