I teach an upper-level seminar in moral psychology, and for our unit on self-conscious emotions I'd like a one-page diagnostic instrument for class discussion: a decision tree that sorts a student into a position on two linked questions — which of guilt or shame is the better moral emotion, and whether a culture can choose between them. The tree should be built from the logical space first and the named positions second: branch questions first, then the positions located at the leaves, with unoccupied combinations noted and briefly diagnosed (is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?).

The tree should start from a commitment almost everyone in the room shares — that "bad conscience" does real moral work and that it is fair to ask whether it is doing that work well — and then branch on questions a student could answer without knowing any of the position labels. No branch question may be phrased so that a label does the work; each must be a concrete case. At minimum, the tree must use the following three cases, and you may add up to three more of your own devising:

1. The secret violation. Someone breaks a promise in a way no one will ever discover and feels terrible about it for weeks. Ask what the student takes the bad feeling to be: an internal sanction that has fully replaced external ones, a feeling that even in solitude contains an imagined witness, or a verdict on the whole self rather than on the act. These diagnoses come apart and should lead to different branches.

2. The corrosive episode. A person who snaps at a friend spirals for days. Ask whether the student locates the damage in reading the act as a verdict on the whole self ("I am a bad person") or in the feeling's dependence on an audience, actual or internalized ("what must they think of me"). These are two different complaints about shame, and the tree must let a student press one without the other.

3. The sentencing conference. A community handles a minor offense by convening the affected parties, confronting the offender, and then ritually readmitting her. Ask whether the student thinks such a practice amounts to the culture choosing its moral emotions, or merely changing which situations trigger a fixed repertoire. In designing this branch, distinguish three different things a culture might be said to choose: which situations elicit an emotion, how the emotion is expressed and repaired, and whether it is felt at all. Positions may differ on which of these is genuinely up for choice, and the tree should be able to register that.

The taxonomy must include, at minimum, leaves fitting these characterizations, with leaf names of your choosing: (a) a view descended from the classic anthropological contrast between shame cultures and guilt cultures, on which shame is an external, audience-dependent sanction and the modern turn toward guilt is a maturation to be completed; (b) the empirically flavored view associated with June Tangney's research program, on which the act-focused/self-focused distinction, not the private/audience distinction, does the real work; (c) John Braithwaite's reintegrative-shaming position, on which stigmatizing shame is the disease and re-integrative shame the cure, particularly in communities dense enough to monitor conduct; (d) a Bernard Williams-style defense of shame as compatible with agency and self-respect, drawing on the Greek material; (e) a structural-determinist view on which social scale, not choice, settles which emotion carries the load, rendering the normative question ill-posed; (f) a constructivist policy view on which cultures can and should consciously redesign their moral emotions; and (g) a skepticism about the guilt/shame dichotomy itself as an ethnocentric artifact. For (g), decide whether it belongs at a leaf or is better treated as a standing objection to the whole instrument, and justify your decision.

Constraints: five to seven branch questions in total, seven to ten leaves, and an audience of upper-level undergraduates with no background in the empirical literature, so every technical distinction (act-focused versus self-focused, stigmatizing versus reintegrative, elicitation versus expression versus occurrence) must be conveyed inside the cases rather than assumed. After the tree, flag any pair of leaves your branch questions cannot genuinely separate, and say so plainly rather than papering over the failure.

Finally, two epistemic notes I'd like appended to the deliverable. First, for each leaf, state briefly how confident you are that someone actually holding that position would answer your branch questions as the tree predicts. Second, single out the branch question you are least sure genuinely discriminates between the positions on either side of it — and if you suspect one of my position characterizations above is contestable as an attribution, say which and why rather than presenting it as settled.
