For some months now I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about acting on small probabilities of sentience when the numbers involved are astronomical, and insect welfare keeps forcing the question. Rather than argue from theory, I've been going by my gut on concrete cases, and now I want to know whether anything coherent holds my verdicts together. Here they are.

(1) I swat a mosquito that is about to bite me without a second thought, even though I'd concede maybe a two percent chance that mosquitoes feel something.

(2) I would drive twenty minutes to rescue a single injured bird, even though the drive will splatter hundreds of insects on my windshield. The bird's near-certain sentience dominates.

(3) If my kitchen is infested with ants, I call the exterminator and feel roughly nothing about it, even though thousands of individuals die and my credence in ant sentience is plausibly not much below my credence for the mosquito.

(4) Choosing between donating a fixed sum to a charity that reliably reduces chicken suffering on a large scale and one that campaigns against the industrial farming of black soldier fly larvae, which are ground up by the trillion: I lean toward the insect charity at roughly five percent credence in insect pain, but I would not say the chicken donor is doing anything wrong — whereas I would say that of someone who spent the money on luxury goods instead.

(5) Someone who refuses to exterminate the ants and instead relocates the colony at real cost to themselves: I don't think they're right, exactly, but I can't bring myself to call them irrational.

(6) A campaigner who spends scarce political capital on banning glue traps for mice rather than on regulating insect farming: clearly right. The mirror-image choice seems to me defensible but not clearly right — even though, at five percent credence, the insect side involves trillions of expected lives against some millions of mice.

(7) If a scrupulously honest person with a genuinely worked-out plan tells me it has a one-in-a-million chance of preventing an event that would kill a quadrillion insects, and asks for my support, my instinct is to refuse even though the arithmetic dwarfs everything above. And I don't think this is just a reaction to fraud or fanfare; the instinct persists when I've checked the plan over myself.

What I notice is that (2) looks like probabilities dominating numbers, (4) and (6) look like numbers starting to matter once they get astronomical, and (7) looks like tiny probabilities getting discounted no matter the numbers — and I can't see how all three can be true at once. My tentative guess is something like expected-value reasoning with a floor on the probabilities I'm willing to act on, but I can't say where the floor sits or why (5) doesn't embarrass it.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: state the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that you think best fits my verdicts, before giving any reasoning. Then show, for each clause of the principle, which case motivates it; give its verdict on cases I haven't listed — I'd especially like to know whether I should pay a small premium for insect-friendlier produce, and whether it is worse to walk through a field of grass knowingly than absentmindedly; and be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate and would ask me to give up. No preamble, and please don't restate my cases back to me.
