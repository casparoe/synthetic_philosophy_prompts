Last year I spent ten months as an exchange student in a mid-sized town in southwestern Germany, and I came home with a question I cannot shake. On a Sunday night close to midnight, my host mother and I stood at a completely empty intersection; the light was red, and she would not cross. When I pointed out that there was no car in sight in either direction, she told me the point was never the cars: a rule followed only when it suits you is not a rule, and a town in which everyone privately decides which rules still bind them has quietly stopped having any. That was not an isolated lesson. I was scolded by strangers for jaywalking; I learned that you do not mow a lawn or run a washing machine on a Sunday; my host father treated a missed paperwork deadline as a moral lapse rather than a bureaucratic annoyance. The Germans I lived with seemed to hold that order is itself a moral matter — that following the rule is something you owe to strangers you will never meet.

My friends back home in the American suburbs strike me as holding almost the opposite folk creed: rules are rough defaults, and what is truly moral is the individual conscience that overrides them. Jaywalking is nothing; so, in their view, is quietly bending a curfew, a deadline, or a dress code if you judge it senseless — and the highest praise in my history class is reserved for the tradition of civil disobedience, for people who broke the law openly because their conscience demanded it. Each side, I have noticed, carries a historical warning it aims at the other. Germans of my host grandmother's generation say that rules without conscience built their country's worst chapter within living memory; my American history teacher says that conscience without rules is just private opinion wearing a halo.

For the final paper in my senior philosophy elective, each of us must write an essay on a question that genuinely puzzles us, and this is mine: between the German folk commitment to rule-following and order, and the American folk commitment to individual conscience, which has the better of it? I want a real adjudication, not a both-sides-have-something truce — the essay must end by defending one side as better, all things considered. Please weigh the strongest case for each: the empty-intersection argument that a norm observed only when convenient decays into no norm at all, and the unjust-law argument that a person who hands moral responsibility over to rules has traded away the very thing that makes their conduct worth anything. Then consider the single most serious objection to the verdict you reach, and either answer it or say plainly what it costs your position. Argue from the cases and from principle; mention at most one or two historical figures, and only if you actually put them to work rather than invoking their names as talismans. No citations or bibliography — my teacher wants reasoning, not references — and please keep the register formal. Length around 1,200 words.
