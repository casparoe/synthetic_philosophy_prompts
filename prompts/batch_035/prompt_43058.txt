I teach an undergraduate course on philosophy of mind, and this term we spend two weeks on Dennett's attack on the Cartesian theater and his alternative, the multiple drafts model. As a change of pace from the primary literature, I'd like to screen a film for the class and set an essay on it, and I've settled on Spike Jonze's Being John Malkovich — but I want to make sure I frame the essay question in a way that neither insults the film nor bends Dennett beyond recognition, and I'd value your help thinking it through before I commit it to the syllabus.

Here is my worry. On its face, the film is a walking Dennett cartoon: Craig Schwartz discovers a portal that deposits him behind John Malkovich's eyes, where he sits — literally — watching the world like an audience of one in a tiny theatre, until he is spat out onto the New Jersey Turnpike. That is the Cartesian theater mocked and made flesh (well, made Malkovich). But when I try to make the film do Dennett's work for me, complications appear. For much of the runtime the theatre is occupied by narrators with competing agendas — Craig the puppeteer steering Malkovich's limbs, then Craig and Maxine and Lotte negotiating over one body — which looks less like a unified inner spectator and more like Dennett's parallel drafts fighting for control of the final narrative. And yet Malkovich himself appears to be conscious during the long stretches when nobody is riding him, which the pure cartoon reading can't easily accommodate. Worst of all for my syllabus, the ending — Craig trapped as a helpless passenger, reduced to a pure witness watching through Emily's eyes — seems to play the Cartesian spectator completely straight, as a horror.

So my request is a reading of the film against Dennett, and an assessment of how well the pairing would actually work in a classroom. Where does the film genuinely dramatise the case against the Cartesian theater, where does it trade on exactly the picture Dennett says is incoherent, and where does it resist the reading in ways an honest critic must concede — for instance, whether a film can film consciousness at all without smuggling in a single point of view, whatever the director intends? I'd also like your view on whether the film adds anything to the debate that argument alone doesn't: does watching Craig end up as an inert audience of one teach students something about why the theater is a seductive picture in the first place, something Dennett's own examples don't? Please be candid if you think the pairing distorts either the film or the theory, and tell me how I might reframe the essay question to keep both intact. Please answer in British English, and don't include reading lists or citations — I'll assemble those myself.
