I am a novelist drafting my third book, a secular transposition of the Grand Inquisitor episode from Dostoevsky's Brothers Karamazov, and I have reached the point where I must decide what to keep. In my version the action moves to a near-future technology company that administers a welfare-and-guidance platform: guaranteed subsistence, algorithmic life recommendations, and a community of users who have quietly stopped making major decisions for themselves. My Inquisitor is a founder who has spent thirty years building this system and is convinced that the mass of humanity never wanted freedom at all, only bread and certainty. Late in the book he delivers a long monologue to a dissident engineer — my silent Christ figure, who answers him, as in the original, only with a gesture rather than an argument.

Dostoevsky's Inquisitor builds his case on three legs, derived from the three temptations in the wilderness: miracle, mystery, and authority — the show of supernatural power that compels belief, the withholding of reasons that makes belief voluntary, and the consolidation of rule that relieves human beings of the burden of freedom. My problem is that a secular novel cannot carry all three at equal weight. Miracles are hard to stage in a boardroom; authority translates almost too easily and risks flattening the book into a standard techno-dystopia; mystery is the subtlest of the three and the one I understand least well.

So I am asking for an engineering-style assessment of the argument rather than a literary appreciation. Please rank the three legs — miracle, mystery, authority — along two separate dimensions. First, by how load-bearing each one is in the Inquisitor's case as Dostoevsky actually constructed it: if a leg were removed, how much of the argument's power over the reader survives? Second, by how much force each retains after translation into my platform setting, where miracle becomes demonstrable infallibility of the system's recommendations, mystery becomes the opacity of the algorithm to its users, and authority becomes the users' voluntary surrender of choice. Where the two rankings diverge, tell me what the divergence reveals — presumably that some of the Inquisitor's appeal is theological machinery that does not survive secularization, while some part is a permanent fact about human beings and consolation. Then, given both rankings and the divergences, tell me which single leg my founder's monologue should foreground and which one I can cut entirely without the scene collapsing, together with the trade-off I am accepting if I cut it.

Please be opinionated rather than balanced; I would rather disagree with a definite ranking than receive three coequal verdicts. And end your reply with a final line in exactly this form, so I can find it while shuffling through my notes:

Verdict: [foreground] / [cut] — [no more than twelve words on the trade-off]
