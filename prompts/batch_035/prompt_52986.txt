I'm a novelist working on a book where one of the main characters is a psychologist from around 1900 who gets, let's say, improbably frozen and revived in the late 1960s, and the middle section is basically him reacting to the science of his own future. I've made him a thinly veiled William James — same wandering attention, same nervous breakdown in his twenties that he clawed his way out of by deciding to believe in free will for a year, same obsession with consciousness as a stream rather than a chain of beads. The set piece I'm planning is him encountering split-brain patients: people whose corpus callosum was cut to stop their seizures, who can name an object flashed to the right visual field but can only point at it with the left hand when it's flashed to the left, and who calmly confabulate an explanation whenever their right hemisphere makes the left hand do something. So here's my question: what do you think James would actually make of this? Would he say there are literally two streams of consciousness in one skull, or two centers that add up to one person anyway, or would he — being the pragmatist he became later in life — refuse to treat it as a deep question and just ask what difference the answer would make in the conduct of the patient's life?

The reason I ask instead of just making it up is that the character needs internal tension to be worth writing, and I can see the tension but not how it resolves. James was neck deep in psychical research and in cases of dissociation and multiple personality — he took seriously the idea of subliminal selves and consciousness outside the ordinary margins — so part of me thinks he'd feel vindicated: see, consciousness is not the simple unitary thing the associationists assumed, it can fracture along lines we didn't know were there. But part of me thinks he'd be disturbed in a specific way: the patients don't feel split. They report being one person. And James was so committed to taking experience at its word, to describing how things actually feel from the inside, that maybe for him the first-person report of unity would trump any pointing experiment you could run in a lab. There's also the question of his view of the self as basically a bundle of habits and associations held together by attention — does surgically cutting the connection between hemispheres count as interrupting the unity of consciousness itself, or just one of the many associative pathways, in which case the self shrugs it off the way it shrugs off a severed telephone line?

If you could lay out the most Jamesian position, with the real tensions left inside it rather than smoothed over, that would help me write him honestly instead of as a mouthpiece for whatever I already believe. I'd especially like to know which way you think the cuts both ways: does his pragmatism pull him toward saying the question of how many consciousnesses are in the room is empty unless it changes how we treat the patient, or does his radical empiricism pull him toward saying the experimental facts settle it and the patient's serene self-report is itself just the left hemisphere talking? And if it helps sharpen the contrast, feel free to say where some other psychologist of that era — a Wundt type, someone more laboratory-minded — would react differently, since I've been toying with giving my character a rival.
