I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about how much the unknowable knock-on effects of my actions should matter morally, so heres me listing my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and hoping they add up to something. Donating some money each year to a well-studied effective charity: feels obligatory, and the fact that I can't trace the full ripple effects (population effects, second-order incentives, whatever) doesn't feel like it lets me off the hook at all. Becoming a doctor vs. going into finance and donating the surplus: both fine, neither required, even though the finance path plausibly does more good if the donations are well-chosen. Voting in a national election: my single vote is almost certain to make no difference, but I think I should vote anyway, and I'd quietly judge someone who couldn't be bothered. Eating meat: wrong, because of the agregate of many such choices, even though my individual consumption makes no difference to any particular animal. Having a child: obviously permissible, zero hesitation, even though it's the single largest unpredictable causal intervention most people ever make — a whole new person whose effects ramify for generations. Working at an AI lab when you genuinly can't tell whether you'd make things safer or accelerate something bad: permissible, but I'd want the person to have thought hard about it first, and I'm honestly not sure what the thinking hard is supposed to accomplish given the uncertainty is irresolvable. Donating to a political movement in another country because you calculated it's your highest-leverage option: feels off, kind of presumptuous, though I can't articulate why given that I endorse the ordinary charity donation.

So — is there a principle that captures all that? The obvious candidate, something like 'act on the expected value you can reasonably estimate, and cluelessness about distant effects doesn't undermine the near effects you can see', gets the donation cases right, but I can't see how it handles the child case without implausible gymnastics. And the meat/voting pair feels unstable in its own way: both are negligible-individual-effect-big-aggregate cases, yet I feel the force of one much more than the other. Give me your best candidate principle (or two competing ones if that's more honest), say precisely which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and why I should give those up rather than complicate the principle, and tell me what it commits me to on a case I haven't listed — say, whether to take a very well-paying job at a tobacco company with the plan of donating everything above subsistance. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence — which principle wins and which of my verdicts dies — and then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my cases back at me.
