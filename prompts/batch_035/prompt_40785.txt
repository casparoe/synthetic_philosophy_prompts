I'm trying to get my head straight about the Kelly criterion, and I keep running into what looks like a flat contradiction that I suspect is really a subtle distinction. On the one hand, the Kelly bettor ends up richer than anyone betting a different fraction with probability approaching one, as the number of repeated favorable bets grows — asymptotically, Kelly dominates almost surely. On the other hand, expected wealth at every finite horizon is maximized by a bettor who risks the whole bankroll on every positive-edge bet, and the Kelly fraction only maximizes the expectation of the logarithm of wealth. So "almost surely richer in the long run" and "lower expected wealth at every finite horizon" are somehow both true of the Kelly bettor. Please explain, at the gut level, how this coexistence is possible — what the small set of astronomical-payoff paths is doing to the mean, and why the law of large numbers doesn't wash it out the way I intuitively expect. Then, connectedly: critics in the spirit of Samuelson charge that maximizing expected log wealth is not a theorem about how to bet but merely expected utility theory with a logarithmic utility function assumed rather than derived, and that the asymptotic-dominance argument is irrelevant to anyone facing finitely many bets. Defenders reply that the time-average growth rate, unlike the ensemble average, is the thing an actual non-cloned bettor actually experiences, so the logarithm is forced on us by the structure of multiplicative dynamics rather than by taste. Explain what work this ergodicity argument is and is not doing — in particular, whether it genuinely rivals expected utility maximization or just redescribes a particular utility function, and what the popularity of fractional Kelly (betting, say, half the Kelly fraction) suggests about which side has the better of the dispute.
