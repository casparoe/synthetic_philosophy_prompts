I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, though I've become fascinated by game theory puzzles. In the centipede game, backward induction says the first player should grab the small pot at the very first move, while real experimental subjects almost always pass for a while first. A standard reaction to this gap is epistemic: the paradox arises only because the textbook assumes rationality is common knowledge, so once you allow even a tiny doubt about the opponent's rationality, backward induction no longer forces taking at the first node. On this view, the "paradox" is just an artifact of an overly strong assumption, and the paradox dissolves once the assumption is relaxed.

Please argue against this reaction. In particular, address the following concern of mine: if moving from "rationality is certainly common knowledge" to "it is almost certainly common knowledge" flips the prescribed play from "take at the first node" to something resembling what real players actually do, that seems less like dissolving the paradox and more like revealing that the theory's prescriptions are discontinuous in its own assumptions. In my line of work, that pattern is familiar: a piece of software that behaves correctly only when its inputs are exactly well-formed, and degrades unpredictably the moment they are slightly off, is usually considered fragile, not vindicated by noting that the malformed case was outside its specification. My worry is that the common-knowledge fix works the same way: it hides backward induction's brittleness behind an idealization rather than repairing it. Please make the case that this is right — that the fix relocates the paradox from "people don't play the equilibrium" to "the equilibrium's derivation collapses under arbitrarily small perturbations of its premises" — and that the latter is a genuine, unresolved problem rather than a technicality.
