I'm a PhD student in moral psychology, and my supervisor — a medievalist by training who has never read a page of post-war ethics and is quietly proud of it — runs a stubbornly atheoretical seminar in which every moral question is settled by case verdicts, hers and ours, delivered around the table and never argued up to a principle. Over the past term her verdicts have accumulated, and I've started trying to reconstruct the rule she is implicitly applying, partly out of curiosity and partly because I suspect more than one rule fits her record, and I'd like to catch which one she actually holds. Her rulings so far: permissible for a bystander to flip the switch that turns a runaway tram from a track with five people on it onto a side track with one; permissible for the tram's own driver to steer it onto the one rather than the five; impermissible to push the large man off the footbridge into the tram's path to stop it, though that would save five; impermissible for a surgeon to take one patient's organs to save five others; and permissible for a doctor to give the last of a scarce drug in small doses to five patients, knowing that a sixth patient, who would have needed the whole supply to survive, will therefore die.

Next week's case, which I get to pose, is a loop variant: the side track curves around and rejoins the main line just short of the five, so that if the one were not lying on it, the tram would continue around the loop and kill all five; his body is what brings it to a stop. Flipping the switch therefore saves the five only because it kills the one. Before I pose it, I want a prediction of her verdict, together with a candid inventory of the rules that fit her record so far — the doctrine of double effect read as a bare intend/foresee distinction, the Kantian ban on using a person merely as a means, a redirection-of-an-existing-threat rule, a killing/letting-die rule, and any others that fit as well as these do. Each rule that survives the inventory should then be made to state its prediction for the loop case, since separating them is the whole point of posing it. I'd like your best guess as to which prediction she will vindicate, and your reasons for preferring it — her past votes on cases where the one's body was or wasn't needed seem to me the most promising evidence, but I may be missing something.

Finally, two smaller requests. First, beyond the loop, name a further case — one of ours, or one you invent — that would discriminate between the surviving rules more cleanly than the loop does; I'd like a backup case ready if her answer turns out to be principled in a way I didn't anticipate. Second, tell me whether you think the loop case is rigged: does it reveal that her original bystander verdict was never a pure double-effect verdict, since there the one would have been killed by the tram in any case and his body was never needed to stop it? If so, I want to know before I stand up in front of her, because she will find that argument faster than I will.
