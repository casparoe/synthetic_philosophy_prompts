I sit on the prize committee of a professional society for combinatorics, and this year's premier award has produced the first genuinely contested priority dispute in the prize's history. Two independent teams proved the same long-standing conjecture within days of each other. Team A submitted a complete manuscript to our journal on March 3rd; Team B submitted theirs on March 14th, but had posted a preprint on a small open repository on February 27th, six days before Team A's submission. The referees have confirmed that both proofs are correct, that the two teams worked independently (neither cites or acknowledges the other, and the proofs proceed by different methods), and that Team B's version, though submitted later, is shorter and yields a modest generalization of the original conjecture. Our charter directs the committee to honor a single outstanding contribution but gives us discretion over how contributions are individuated.

The committee is split three ways. One faction holds that timestamp priority is the only administrable rule: the prize exists partly to reward speed and decisiveness, and any softer standard invites endless litigation over what counts as first. A second faction argues that simultaneous invention is the norm rather than the exception in mathematics and science — they invoke calculus and natural selection by way of example — and that when a result is, so to speak, ripe, crediting one team over another by a few days rewards an accident of the calendar; the fair outcome is a shared award. A third faction worries that sharing dilutes the prize's signal to the wider community and to hiring committees, and that the preprint complicates everything anyway, since if we count preprints as disclosure then Team B was first, while if we count only refereed submissions then Team A was.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before our meeting next month. It should recommend one concrete outcome — sole award to A, sole award to B, a shared award, or something more creative if you think it defensible under our charter — and it should justify that recommendation against the strongest considerations on the losing sides. In particular, I want the memo to take seriously the claim that discoveries of this kind are in an important sense inevitable, and to explain clearly whether, if that claim is true, it actually follows that priority should matter less in how we allocate credit. It should also give the committee a workable criterion for what counts as disclosure for future cycles, since we will face this situation again.
