I'm in a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and we just finished the unit on Dutch book arguments. I was fully on board with the synchronic version: if your credences violate the probability axioms, a bookie who knows only your credences can offer you a set of bets, each individually fair or favorable by your own lights, whose joint acceptance guarantees a loss, and it's hard to deny that being exploitable in that way is a rational failing. Then the instructor extended the machinery diachronically, first to justify conditionalization by updating, and then to a principle our class attributed to Bas van Fraassen under the name Reflection: your current credence in any proposition A, conditional on your future credence in A being p, must itself be p. When I tried to write the argument up cleanly, it seemed to prove something I can't accept, and I want to know where it goes wrong.

Here is the argument as best I can reconstruct it. (1) The general Dutch book schema: whenever a condition C on credences is violated, there exists a finite set of bets, each of which the agent at the time of accepting it regards as fair or favorable, whose joint acceptance guarantees a net loss; susceptibility to such a book is irrational; so rational credences satisfy C. (2) For C = the probability axioms this is the synchronic argument, which I accept. (3) The same construction works diachronically: if your current conditional credence in A, given that your future self's credence in A will be p, differs from p, then a bookie who knows your current dispositions can sell you bets now and bets later that jointly guarantee a loss — the bets now are fair by your current lights and the bets later are fair by your future lights, since the later bets are evaluated using the very credence of p that figures in the condition. (4) Crucially, the construction makes no assumptions about how the future credence of p is produced: the book goes through exactly the same way whether the future self formed p by careful Bayesian updating or by some process the current agent knows to be corrupt. (5) So rationality requires Reflection without exception: defer now, conditionally, to every future credence you anticipate having, including ones you know in advance to be the output of a corrupt process. (6) But here is the case that bothers me: I am about to be locked into a mandatory weekend sales seminar for a dubious investment scheme, and I know from abundant testimony that virtually everyone who attends ends up credence 0.99 that the investment is sound, while I currently sit at 0.05. (7) Reflection then demands that my current credence in the investment's soundness, conditional on my future credence being 0.99, be 0.99 itself — that I treat the scam-induced credence as authoritative. (8) That is plainly wrong: knowing that a process is corrupt is precisely a reason not to defer to its output. (9) So the Dutch book method proves too much, and if it can't be trusted here, I don't see why the synchronic argument for the axioms should be trusted either.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd like to keep as much as possible: the synchronic argument (2) is the thing I most want to preserve, and I'd also like to hold onto the idea that the diachronic argument for conditionalization is in decent shape. So tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs; be explicit about whether the flaw is a genuine equivocation or invalid step (e.g., whether the argument slides between different things "your future credence being p" can condition on), or whether the argument is valid and the only escape is to restrict Reflection in a way that has to be principled rather than ad hoc. I'd also like you to address the obvious objection that being bookable only matters if you would actually accept the bets — in the seminar we noted that an agent can just decline to bet — and to say whether that objection, if it works against the Reflection case, works equally against the synchronic argument I want to keep. Finally, please keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this: which parts of the Dutch book literature reflect broad agreement, and which rest on disputed assumptions (for instance, about the relationship between credences and betting dispositions, or about whether susceptibility to a book is itself the irrationality or merely a symptom of it). If your diagnosis depends on a reading of Reflection or of the diachronic construction that some philosophers would reject, say so rather than presenting it as the consensus.
