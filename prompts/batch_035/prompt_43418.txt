I want a thought experiment about the bootstrapping problem / easy knowledge, but running in the unusual direction: the bootstrapping reasoning should come out looking fine. The classic cases (checking a reliable gas gauge, trusting a competent witness) are supposed to show you can't argue "the gauge says low, the gauge says low, so the gauge is reliable" — bootstrapping your way to a source's reliability using nothing but the source itself. I want the reverse: a case where the agent does structurally the same thing and we intuitively want to say they're within their epistemic rights, so the anti-bootstrapper has to explain what differs. Constraints: no measuring instruments, clocks, or thermometers, keep it an ordinary realistic setting (workplace, friend group, whatever), one paragraph for the scenario, and the agent must have no independent check on the source — that's the whole point. Don't stipulate that the agent knows the source is reliable, that would be cheating: the case has to generate the intuition, not declare it. Afterwards walk me through it: state the exact bootstrapping argument in the case, say why it seems permissible here, identify which feature of the case is doing the work and what happens to the verdict if you strip that feature out, and then give the best reply from someone like Stewart Cohen or a transmission-failure theorist trying to stop the case from counting. I'm mostly interested in whether permissibility can come from narrowing the conclusion (say, to the source's local, short-run track record rather than reliability in general) or whether that's just bootstrapping with a smaller ambition.
