I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but dispositions came up at work in an unexpected way: we were debating how to characterize a service as "reliable," and someone said that "reliable under load" behaves like what philosophers call a disposition — a conditional claim about what the system would do if triggered, which is complicated by things like circuit breakers that intervene before a crash can occur. That sent me to a popular philosophy book, which summarizes the topic as follows, and I'd like to know how much of it to trust:

'Dispositions such as fragility and solubility were traditionally analyzed as simple conditionals: a glass is fragile just in case, were it struck, it would break. Philosophers refuted this analysis with cases called finks: suppose a guardian watches over the glass and would render it unbreakable at the very instant of a strike. Then the conditional is false — the glass would not break if struck — and since the guardian removes the fragility at the moment of the trigger, the glass does not have the disposition, which shows that no conditional about what would happen can ever capture what a disposition is. Later work introduced masks: a mask is a background condition that prevents the manifestation, such as a carefully packed case that stops a struck glass from breaking. Masks show that whether an object manifests its dispositions depends not on any property of the object itself but on its surroundings, so dispositions turn out to be relational rather than intrinsic, and the entire idea of a disposition as a real property of things collapses: there are only events and regularities among them.'

Could you go through this passage and tell me which parts a philosopher working on this literature would accept, which are simplifications, and which are outright errors? Two things strike me as suspicious given my software analogy, but I don't trust my instincts here. First, the claim that in the fink case the glass lacks the disposition seems backwards: intuitively, the glass is still fragile until the guardian intervenes, and if it weren't, the case wouldn't be a counterexample to anything — so have I misread the book, or has the book misread the philosophy? Second, I can see how my circuit breaker "masks" the service's tendency to crash without the tendency being unreal or relational, so I don't see how masks are supposed to establish that dispositions depend on surroundings rather than on the object. Finally, I'd like to know what, if anything, survives for the conditional analysis after these counterexamples — whether the book is right that it is a total loss, or whether philosophers have repaired it in some way I should look into.
