I'm teaching a philosophy of mind unit built around Dennett's ladder of creature kinds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian, as he presents it in Kinds of Minds — and we'll be pairing it with Daniel Keyes's Flowers for Algernon. Please read the novel through that taxonomy. On the face of it, the plot is a forced march up the ladder: Charlie Gordon begins as at best a Skinnerian creature, learning bakery work through drill and correction, and after the operation he gains the Popperian capacity to test hypotheses offline — reinterpreting his own memories, auditing the scientists' claims, and eventually predicting the flaw in the experiment from the pattern of his own decline. The novel also hands him the characteristic Gregorian move: he appropriates the mind-tools of a culture he was shut out of — books, the researchers' methods, the journal itself. The journal deserves special attention. It opens as barely literate report-writing and becomes the instrument by which a mind audits its own deterioration; it is both the medium of the story and the external tool doing some of the thinking. Is the diary the novel's image of a Gregorian creature recruiting an artifact — and does the fact that we read his decline in his own words change his moral status, or only our evidence of it? Give Algernon a paragraph too: a Darwinian creature whose maze performance is the benchmark for a human's, and who dies, so to speak, in the stead of the hypothesis.

Then say where the reading strains. Dennett's ladder orders cognitive power, but the novel is equally invested in a different axis — warmth, trust, the capacity to be valued — which Nemur denies Charlie before the operation and the bakery coworkers, ambivalently, grant him throughout. Keyes implies the surgery changes Charlie's processing rather than his personhood. Does the taxonomy have anything to say about that, or does it silently assume that more cognitive power is more of everything worth having? Close by taking a position on the ending: at the end Charlie is back to bakery work and basic learning, yet plainly not back to his starting point, since he retains a narrative past. Is that a counterexample to the taxonomy, or simply outside its remit?

Constraints: the students and any assistant working from this will not have the text, so paraphrase scenes and characters rather than quoting wording, and treat the taxonomy as defined rather than the novel as its illustration — where a scene fits only by stretching the definitions, say so plainly. Finish with a short paragraph on what the novel adds to the debate that Dennett's argument alone does not.
