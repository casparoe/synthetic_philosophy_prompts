So I'm a science journalist — I mostly cover cosmology and quantum foundations for a general audience — and I've been poking at a feature on whether physics leaves any room for free will. Last week a physicist friend of mine, who's normally the least metaphysical person I know, walked me into an argument over beers that I can't shake and can't accept, and my editor wants to use it as the spine of the piece, so I need to actually understand where, if anywhere, it breaks. He's a relativity guy, so he starts with the Rietdijk–Putnam thing, the block-universe argument from special relativity, and does the whole Penrose Andromeda routine: two people pass each other on the street at walking pace, and because simultaneity is frame-relative and Andromeda is two and a half million light years out, the "nows" of those two walkers disagree by days about what the aliens over there are up to — for one, the invasion fleet already launched, for the other the decision hasn't even been made yet. Fine so far, I can verify the math myself. But then he adds steps. (1) What's real, for you, includes whatever is simultaneous with your current experience — otherwise you need a preferred frame to say what's real, and relativity forbids that. (2) The walkers' "nows" disagree about Andromeda, so either reality is relative like velocity is, or both slices are equally real. (3) Reality can't be relative like velocity — if it's real that the fleet launched, it's real, full stop. (4) So whatever's real for any observer is real, period. And now he says watch this: for any event in my future — my next birthday, the election results, the heat death — there's some observer, somewhere, moving at some velocity, whose "now" contains it; by (4) each of those events is real right now; and you can chain observers to reach anything whatsoever, so the entire four-dimensional block is real now. So far I'm grudgingly following — that's just the block universe, it's the standard conclusion, lots of serious people sign off on it. But then his kicker: (5) nothing I do next can affect what already exists — you can't change what's real; (6) so my actions affect nothing downstream of them, the fleet comes or doesn't regardless of me, and choice, deliberation, my whole beat as a reporter covering outcomes that people bring about, is theater. He calls this "the block does your worrying for you."

My problem is that the conclusion is absurd — it proves not just a frozen future but that I'm causally inert, which is way more than the block was supposed to buy him — and yet every step sounded okay while he talked. Where does it go wrong, if it does? For what it's worth I'd really rather not give up (3), the bit where reality isn't just relative to observers — relativizing "real" seems to me like giving up on the word meaning anything — so if something has to go I'd want it to be in the middle somewhere. Part of me suspects the trick is in the chaining move in (4), hopping from observer to observer, but I can't articulate what would be wrong with that, if anything. Part of me suspects the trick is in (5), that it quietly changes the meaning of "affect" between the two times he uses it. And part of me worries the whole thing is valid and I just have to live with it. Walk me through which step to reject and what rejecting it costs me — and if there are two respectable places to cut, tell me what difference it makes where you cut.
