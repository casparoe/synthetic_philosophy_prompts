Write a dialogue between two commanders stuck in a variant of the electronic mail game (Rubinstein's "almost common knowledge" thing from epistemic game theory). Quick setup so we're on the same page: attacking the enemy camp only works if both commanders attack at dawn together — going in alone is a disaster, and there's no payoff for holding back alone. Whether the attack is actually worthwhile depends on the state of the world, which neither commander can observe directly. If the state is favorable, commander A's computer automatically pings commander B's, B's auto-replies, A's auto-confirms, and the computers just keep ping-ponging confirmations back and forth — but every message has some small chance of getting lost, and neither side can ever tell whether their last confirmation actually got through. No other communication channel exists.

Here's the variant I want: A's screen shows 6 received confirmations, B's shows 7. In the plain email game the punchline is that, in equilibrium, the two of them never attack no matter how many messages pile up, even though each commander individually is ~99.99% confident the state is favorable and they'd both be better off attacking. So the dialogue is A and B trying to talk each other into attacking anyway.

Both of them should be smart and genuinely engage with each other's points, no strawmen. I especially want them to grapple with why their mutual confidence doesn't just add up — e.g. B realizing that from A's perspective, A has to consider that maybe B only got 5 confirmations, and that B would then worry that A only got 4, and so on down the ladder, even though in this actual run of the game the ladder never actually bottoms out anywhere near the bottom. One of them should at some point hit on the backward-induction-ish logic (whoever could conceivably have zero confirmations won't attack, and that fear infects every level above), and the other should push back on whether that logic really applies when the chain is this long and the loss probability is this tiny. Let them end unresolved or with a reluctant compromise — don't have one of them definitively win.

Don't just have them recite game theory at each other btw — keep it as an actual conversation with some frustration and second-guessing, they're tired and dawn is coming. And maybe one typo somewhere, like they're typing these messages over the same unreliable channel. Length: medium, maybe 600-800 words of dialogue.
