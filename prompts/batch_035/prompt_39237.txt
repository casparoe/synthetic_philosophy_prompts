You are a philosopher of mind with a specialty in the foundations of cognitive science, and I'd like you to audit a set of positions I've written up for a graduate seminar on the computational theory of mind. Please think through the commitments carefully, mapping out the logical dependencies between them before writing, and then give me a severity-ranked account of where the set breaks.

Here is where I stand. (1) I am a computationalist: mental states just are computational states of the brain, and cognition is best explained at the level of computation rather than neurochemistry or bare physics. (2) What makes a physical system a computer of a given kind is its causal organization, so mental states are multiply realizable — a silicon system with the same functional organization as my brain would have beliefs, sensations, and consciousness just as I do. (3) I am nonetheless persuaded by the point that computation is in an important sense observer-relative: physics by itself doesn't single out which computation, if any, a physical system performs — any mapping from physical states to computational states has to be chosen, and nature doesn't choose one for us. (4) I firmly reject pancomputationalism: it is just false that a rock, a wall, or the weather implements every finite-state machine someone might describe it as implementing, and any theory of computation that entails that they do is wrong. (5) I hold that a computer simulation of a storm doesn't make anything wet and a simulation of digestion digests nothing — simulations of most phenomena lack the powers of the things simulated — but I equally hold that a detailed simulation of my brain, run on suitable hardware, would genuinely think, feel, and be conscious. (6) Finally, I think the classical picture of cognition as discrete, rule-governed symbol manipulation over sentence-like representations is dead wrong — the brain is a continuous, noisy, embodied dynamical system — but I see no tension between that and (1).

Tell me which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension and which merely appear to be, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each, say what would have to give and what it would cost me to give it. Identify the single cheapest revision that would clean up the most. Don't lecture me on what each position is; assume I know that. Instead, trace what each commitment commits me to. Please be candid about where your own diagnosis is most likely to go wrong — which step of your reasoning a defender of my package could most plausibly resist — and say what argument or consideration would move you to a different verdict on whether the set is tenable.
