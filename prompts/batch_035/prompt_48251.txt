A colleague of mine has a consistent line on heritability claims, and I want to anticipate where she'll land on some new ones. Her track record so far: she agrees that the roughly 0.8 twin-based heritability of height in present-day Denmark means that individual differences in height among Danes today are largely genetic; she rejects the inference that the average height gap between Danes today and Danes of a century ago must therefore be genetic; she rejects the claim that a trait with heritability 0.8 can be reduced by environmental means by at most 20%, citing phenylketonuria as her counterexample; she agrees that having five fingers is genetically determined while having essentially zero heritability; and she agrees that the heritability of height is genuinely lower in malnourished populations than in well-nourished ones, without either estimate being flawed. Three new cases are coming up for discussion. First: a country equalizes school funding, and the heritability of educational achievement rises — did genes become more important, or did the reform fail? Second: over two generations a trait's population mean shifts dramatically while its heritability stays near 0.8 throughout — does the constant estimate tell us anything about what drove the shift? Third: two villages with the same gene pool but different staple diets show high heritability of adult height within each and a large mean gap between them, known to be dietary — do the within-village estimates carry any information about the between-village gap? Predict her verdicts on all three, reconstruct the rule (or competing rules) implicit in her track record, say which of her earlier rulings each candidate rule struggles to fit, give your best guess with reasons, and propose the single further case that would best discriminate among the surviving rules.
