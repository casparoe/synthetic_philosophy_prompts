You'll probably be able to tell I'm a philosophy professor and not an anthropologist, so bear with me. I'm teaching a seminar this term on ritual and obligation, which means I've been reading a bunch of anthropology and cultural-evolution stuff on the side, and I keep running into a pattern I can't find a name for. Help me figure out whether the cultural evolution people have a term for it, or whether it needs a new one.

The pattern: a cultural practice survives and gets passed on *because* it's costly, and when somebody comes along and removes the cost — usually to be helpful — the practice doesn't spread, it dies. It's as if the cost is doing the transmitting, and the "content" is almost incidental.

Some instances, so you know what I mean. After Vatican II, the Catholic mass was translated into the vernacular partly so ordinary people could participate without the barrier of Latin, and it worked — and parishes with the easy version have been emptying out for decades, while the small crowd keeping the demanding Tridentine rite keeps producing the priests and the families who carry it forward. Handwritten thank-you notes: once email made them effortless, the gratitude didn't migrate to the effortless medium, the practice just evaporated. Fraternity hazing: administrators keep banning it on the theory that it's a pointless cost standing in front of the fellowship, and it keeps coming back, while the groups that swap it for a pizza mixer don't bond. Even in my own shop: the brutal reading-heavy seminars are the ones students evangelize to their friends; nobody has ever recommended a course to me on the grounds that it was streamlined.

Nearest things I know of, none of which quite fit: Veblen goods, where the high price signals wealth to onlookers — but here the cost isn't a status display aimed at outsiders, it seems to be what makes *transmission* work at all. Some signaling theory of religious cost that I've seen referenced (Sosis, Irons?) — that explains why an individual is willing to pay, not why the practice spreads better when it's expensive. And plain survivorship bias — maybe I only notice the costly survivors because the easy versions that died are forgotten? Though I don't think that's it, since the easy versions are sitting right there, visibly failing to get picked up.

So what I'd like:

First, casual prose. (a) Tell me whether this configuration has an established name, and if so whether the name actually covers it or just overlaps; (b) if nothing established fits, coin a term and give a real definition, and be explicit about the edges — what's in, what's out, especially how it differs from costly signaling and from the boring idea that people just value what's effortful.

Then, after the prose, a JSON object summarizing your verdict, with this schema. Fields: "nearest_concepts", an array of objects each with "name", "how_it_fits", and "where_it_diverges"; "verdict", either "established" or "needs_new_term"; "best_fit" (if established) or "coined_term" (if new), the latter as an object with "name" and "one_line_definition"; "boundary_cases", an array of one-sentence cases that stress the edges; "confidence", a number from 0 to 1 plus a one-line reason; and "what_would_change_my_mind", where I want you to actually commit — name the kind of evidence or counterexample that would move you to a different verdict, and say which link in your reasoning is the most likely to be wrong. Don't be vague on that last field, it's the part I care about most.
