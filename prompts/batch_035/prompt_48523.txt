After a career in measurement science — I retired some years ago from a national metrology institute, where my work concerned calibration chains and interlaboratory comparisons — I have begun attending a philosophy of science reading group, and I find myself persistently at odds with one of its members over a single sentence. The group has been discussing what the literature calls epistemic circularity, bootstrapping, and "easy knowledge": situations in which someone arrives at confidence in a source's reliability only by relying on that very source, as in the much-discussed case of a commuter who tracks her elevator's record of safe trips and comes to believe, on that basis alone, that the elevator is reliable. When I proposed that our perceptual faculties might be vindicated in something like the way instruments are — independent channels such as sight, touch, and proprioception cross-checking one another, as instruments in a round-robin comparison each check the others — this member dismissed the suggestion with the remark that you cannot use a source to check the source itself: no ruler measures the ruler, no witness vouches for his own testimony. That sentence seems to me to trade on an ambiguity, and I would like help pulling it apart before I raise the matter again.

From my own field I can testify that things are not so simple as the slogan suggests. Until recently the kilogram was, by international definition, a particular platinum-iridium cylinder: any "measurement" of it could only ever report the measuring instrument's error, never the standard's, and no one took this to be an objection to the practice. Modern realizations of the units through fixed fundamental constants raise a related puzzle, since checking such a standard appears to collapse into checking our implementation of a definition rather than anything independent. And in a comparison ring in which instrument A checks B, B checks C, and C checks A, no instrument checks itself, yet the configuration as a whole appears to lift itself by its own results — which, I gather, is close to what the group means by bootstrapping.

What I would like, then, is a menu of the distinct precise claims the sentence "you cannot use a source to check the source itself" could be expressing. For each reading, please say whether it is a mere platitude, true but doing no work; a substantive and defensible thesis, for instance that no source can furnish non-circular evidence for its own reliability, or that circular support never raises rational confidence, or that reliance on a source without independent prior warrant yields no knowledge at all; or a claim that is simply false, as it would be if it entailed that no belief formed through a source is justified until the source has been validated by independent means. Most important, please indicate which reading my interlocutor needs for his dismissal of my proposal to succeed, since on some readings the commuter and the cross-checking senses are condemned while the calibration ring escapes, and on others all three stand or fall together. And for each reading, say whether it also condemns the metrological practices I describe; if it does not, I would like to know what exactly is supposed to distinguish the commuter's inference from a ring of instruments. Please lay this out as a numbered list I can bring to our next meeting.
