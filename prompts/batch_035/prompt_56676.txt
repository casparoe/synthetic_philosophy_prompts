I write about science and ideas for a living, mostly longform features, and for the past year I've been doing a series where I ask researchers to put odds on things. Not fun stuff like aliens — stuff in their own lane, like whether their favorite theory survives the next round of data. And I keep running into a pattern I don't have words for.

The obvious split I already knew about: they'll give you numbers when there's a base rate ("maybe 60% this replication pans out, similar designs come through about that often"), and they refuse numbers for one-off events, which I gather is the risk-vs-uncertainty thing. Fine. But there's this other behavior nobody I've asked can name. Take a physicist I interviewed. She'd confidently say her theory was more likely to be right than rival theory A, and that A was more likely than rival B. Then I asked whether her theory was more likely than B, and she said — completely matter of fact — that that question didn't have an answer. The two weren't comparable, they were "different kinds of claims." Her phrase, not mine. Not that it was a close call, not that she needed more time: the comparison itself was off the table. When I pushed, she said ranking them would be like asking whether a law is greener than a symphony is loud.

I've collected maybe a dozen of these now. Common features: the comparisons people will make form a patchy partial list — some pairs yes, some pairs a flat no — and the "no" pairs are not the ones they're ignorant about. If anything they'll happily compare things they know little about. The refusals are about the kind of claim involved. And the patchiness does weird structural stuff: one economist ranked tax reform as more likely than the central bank changing course, and the central bank as more likely than a particular war, but then declined war-vs-tax-reform entirely. I checked — he wasn't confused, he just insisted those two sat on differnt scales.

So, questions. Does this configuration have a name? "Knightian uncertainty" got thrown at me and doesn't seem right — these people aren't refusing because there's no data, they're refusing because the comparison itself seems category-crossing to them. Someone else mentioned that probabilities don't have to be numbers at all, some old economics-adjacent philosophy position, but they couldn't recall where it came from. Is that a real position, and does it cover what I'm seeing? And if there's no established term, coin one — but with a definition that actually separates it from (a) ordinary hedging or reluctance to be quoted, (b) risk vs uncertanty, and (c) people just being bad at comparing things, which I refuse to believe is all that's going on. Last thing, and honestly the thing I'm most curious about: if these partial rankings are for real, does anything go wrong formally? Like, can you build a dutch book out of somebody whose comparabilities have holes in them? Apologies if I'm using that term wrong, a poker friend taught it to me.
