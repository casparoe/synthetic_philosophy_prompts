I am a novelist drafting a political retelling of the Odyssey in which the mast scene becomes an allegory of founding, and to prepare I have been reading constitutional theory. Again and again the scholars reach for the same image: entrenching a constitution against the sirens of populist passion is Ulysses binding himself to the mast — the ropes are the entrenched clauses, the crew's standing order not to untie him is judicial review ignoring his later pleas. The image is doing real work for them: it is meant to show that precommitment is not just compatible with freedom but somehow constitutive of it, self-rule exercised across time rather than betrayed by it. Please audit the analogy for me. Set out the correspondence table first — what plays the mast, the ropes, the wax, the crew, the standing order, the sirens, the sea — and then show where the mapping breaks. Two fractures I have noticed but cannot weigh: Ulysses binds only himself, on his own diagnosis of his own weakness, and keeps his ears open because he wants the song while escaping its consequence, whereas a constitution binds majorities who never consented and may not share the founding generation's picture of their own irrationality; and Ulysses shouting to be untied while the crew tightens the ropes looks less like a citizenry under a constitution than like something else, since a bound people can usually amend the ropes themselves. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that do not, and tell me whether the freedom-preserving conclusion survives once the fractures are on the table — or whether the mast was carrying it the whole time. If there is a better analogy from literature or history, I would be glad of it.
