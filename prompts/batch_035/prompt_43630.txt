Last night I argued until well past midnight with a friend about the Republic, and I have been turning over her summary of Glaucon ever since. She read it at university, whereas I have only read about it, and her recollection of Glaucon's challenge in Book II went roughly as follows. Glaucon holds that no one is just by choice: justice arose as a contract among people too weak to do wrong with impunity, and so it is valued purely for its consequences, reputation above all. His ring story is meant to prove the point: Gyges, a shepherd in Lydia, found a ring that made him invisible, used it to seduce the queen, murdered the king, and seized the throne — and Glaucon says that anyone armed with such a ring would behave exactly the same way and would be right to, since no one who could escape detection would remain just. Following Thrasymachus, Glaucon concludes that justice is simply whatever advantages the strong, and that Socrates must show the just man to be happier even when he is believed a criminal and tortured to death for it — a challenge, my friend added, that Plato never really answers, since he ends up talking about the soul instead. I came away suspecting she has flattened something, but I do not know the dialogue well enough to say where. Is her summary fair? Please go through it claim by claim, telling me what a Plato scholar would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a straightforward mistake about the text — and then say whether her closing remark, that Plato dodges the challenge, survives the corrections.
