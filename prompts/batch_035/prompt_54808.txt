I'm a software engineer with about fifteen years in API design and infrastructure work, and I instinctively think about persistence, authority, and change in terms of contracts, versions, and running systems. I've gotten interested in the bioethics debate about advance directives — instructions a competent person records about medical treatment for a future self with dementia — and I'd like you to restate that debate in the vocabulary I actually use. The positions I have in mind:

1. The view, associated with Ronald Dworkin, that the earlier competent self has full authority over the later demented self: autonomy includes the power to legislate for one's own future, so an advance directive binds even when the current patient seems content and would want to go on living. Dworkin distinguishes, roughly, interests in the shape of one's life as a whole from interests in one's moment-to-moment experience, and holds that only the earlier self can judge the former — so it's the earlier self whose judgment should govern.

2. The dissenting view, associated with critics like Rebecca Dresser, that once the earlier and later selves have diverged, what counts is the welfare of the patient who now exists: if the person with dementia is comfortable and engaged, honoring a directive to let them die serves no one who is still around to be served.

3. Intermediate views — for instance, that a directive carries weight only insofar as the current patient can still be said to endorse, however dimly, the values it expressed, or that directives are presumptive rather than decisive.

Please translate each position into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: contracts and their owners, pinned versions, deprecation notices, breaking changes, backward compatibility, the divergence between a running instance and the source of truth it was deployed from, forks, and migrations. Where a philosophical claim has an obvious analogue, say so; where you have to bend one of my terms to make it fit, flag the bend. Then give me the residue report. I'm particularly curious about a few candidate residues: whether the thesis that the demented patient is, or is not, "the same person" as the author of the directive has any counterpart in my vocabulary, or whether forking a project and drifting from its spec simply doesn't capture what's at stake; whether the idea that the earlier self has a special claim on the body — that the running instance executes on hardware the spec author laid down instructions for — is expressible at all in a framework where contracts bind instances, not people; and whether the notion that the later self's momentary experience has moral weight of its own, independent of anyone's prior intent, is like arguing from observed runtime behavior against a declared contract, or is something my vocabulary quietly cannot say.

Finally, tell me whether my working intuition is a good frame or a distortion: my instinct is that this whole debate is just the question of which source of truth wins when a running system has drifted from its declared contract — the spec, the running instance, or some reconciliation procedure. Does that mapping illuminate the debate, or does it smuggle in assumptions (for example, that there is a spec author with standing, or that authority must reside in exactly one place) that the philosophers are actively contesting? Where the residue points to something my vocabulary is missing rather than to something the philosophical claims never managed to make precise, I'd like to know which it is and why.
