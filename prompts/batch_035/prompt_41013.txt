I've been working through the William James / W. K. Clifford exchange in a seminar on the ethics of belief — Clifford's idea that believing on insufficient evidence is always a kind of misconduct, and James's reply that when evidence cannot settle the matter in time, and believing is itself part of what makes the believed thing true, we are entitled to let our passional nature decide. I have my own gut verdicts on a spread of cases, and I can't tell whether they add up to something like James's principle, something like Clifford's with exceptions carved in, or just plain inconsistency. I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures the pattern, and to be candid about which of my verdicts it has to overturn.

My verdicts, roughly in order of how confident I feel: a jury member who becomes convinced of guilt because the prosecutor is eloquent is doing something wrong, full stop. A friend relays a serious accusation about someone I know, and I simply believe it on their word without checking anything — wrong, even though the friend is usually reliable. A graduate student who believes his hypothesis will pan out before there is any evidence, so as to sustain years of grinding work on it — fine, that seems like part of how research has to work. A climber in trouble who must believe he can make a jump, since the belief itself is what steadies him — fine; this is practically James's own case. A dying woman who believes, without evidence, that she will see her dead husband again — fine, though nothing whatever hangs on it, which is awkward for the Jamesian story. An investor who believes a startup will succeed mainly because the founder's conviction is contagious — not fine, though this one makes me uneasy, since venture investing seems to run partly on exactly such beliefs. A student who believes he will pass an exam without evidence, where the confidence marginally improves performance but mostly substitutes for studying — not fine. And here is my problem: that belief is partly self-fulfilling, like the climber's, yet it seems clearly wrong.

My tentative principle, which I cannot get to cover everything: it is wrong to believe on insufficient evidence, except (a) where the evidence cannot be obtained in time and something depends on believing now, and (b) where being wrong would cost no one anything. This saves the climber under (a), the dying woman under (b), and it condemns the jury member, the accusation case, and the investor. The grad student is the one I keep going back and forth on — perhaps belief there isn't really belief but something more like a working commitment, or perhaps he just falls under (a). But the student defeats the principle outright: clause (a) arguably applies to him too, since the exam is coming, the evidence isn't in yet, and his confidence does improve his chances. So either (a) needs a further condition — perhaps that the belief must not crowd out available means of improving the outcome, or that its self-fulfilling effect must not be swamped by what gathering evidence could achieve — or my verdict on the student has to go.

So, three requests. First, state the best principle you can find, precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it, and say plainly whether the result is closer to Clifford, closer to James, or neither. Second, apply it to two cases I haven't given verdicts on: a coach who sincerely believes in a mediocre player, where the belief lifts that player's performance but also leads the coach to start him over a better teammate; and a terminally ill person who believes an unevidenced experimental treatment will work, where the belief sustains her hope but delays her move into palliative care. Third, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot save, and whether I should prefer it to some more complicated, verdict-saving alternative — I would rather hear that one of my gut reactions is wrong than end up with a principle so riddled with exceptions that it explains everything and forbids nothing.
