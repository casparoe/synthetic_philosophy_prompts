I have recently been promoted from senior engineer to head of user research at a mid-sized company that builds scheduling and triage software for hospital clinics, and I have discovered that nearly every product decision here rests on asking people to look inward and report what they find. When a clinic cancels its contract, someone telephones the practice manager and asks why. When we test a redesigned booking screen, we have users think aloud and afterwards tell us what confused them. When leadership wants to know whether clinics would pay for a new bed-management module, we ask them, and their answers go into the deck as findings.

My own background is engineering rather than psychology, but I have read enough lately to be uneasy. There is the well-known line of work associated with Nisbett and Wilson in the 1970s arguing that when people explain their own choices they are often reporting a culturally available theory about what plausibly influenced them rather than anything they actually observed in themselves; there are the split-brain patients who confidently invent explanations for actions initiated by the other hemisphere; and there are the more recent choice-blindness experiments, in which participants notice nothing when their stated preference is secretly swapped for its opposite, then fluently explain and defend a decision they never made. At the same time, I do not want to overcorrect. It seems obvious that a report about experience happening right now — "I cannot read that label" — is different in kind from a retrospective causal claim — "I cancelled because of the price" — and I suspect the first kind is far more trustworthy than the second. I need something that captures that difference rather than a blanket rule that users lie.

Please write a procedure my team can actually run. Specifically:

1. A set of ordered questions or tests a researcher can apply to an individual introspective report during a twenty-minute debrief, with no statistics beyond what a non-specialist can do in their head. The questions should distinguish, at minimum, concurrent reports of experience from retrospective attributions of cause, and low-inference reports (what something felt like) from high-inference ones (why one behaved as one did), and should say what conditions make confabulation more or less likely — for instance the interval between the event and the report, whether the person is being asked for a cause at all rather than a description, and whether the offered cause is a socially flattering or conventional one.

2. For each question, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates with "do not treat this as evidence about why the behaviour occurred; treat it only as data about what this person is prepared to say", and where the question is genuinely one of judgment that no procedure can close off — mark those honestly rather than dressing them up as steps.

3. Then apply the procedure to three matters currently on my desk: (a) our churn interviews, in which eight of eleven cancelling clinics named price as their reason; (b) a round of usability tests in which six of eight users said the new triage screen was confusing, though their task times and error rates both improved against the old screen; and (c) a proposal to build a long-requested feature on the strength of clinic managers' assurances that they would use it weekly.

4. Include at least one case where the procedure's honest output is one I will not enjoy delivering — I will say plainly that our most-quoted finding, the price explanation in the churn interviews, is the sort of retrospective, high-inference, socially conventional self-attribution the procedure weights lowest, and the procedure had better confirm or refute that rather than spare me.

5. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do at all: which kinds of introspective reports it cannot grade, and which questions about users it cannot answer even in principle.

For context on constraints: my team is three researchers and a designer, none with formal training in philosophy of mind or cognitive science; interviews and usability tests happen weekly, so the procedure must be cheap enough to run every time; and whatever we conclude has to be argued before a vice-president who trusts instruments and funnel numbers and will not accept "users might be confabulating" as a reason to discard data she has already quoted in a board meeting.
