For a while now I've been following the case for picking lawmakers by random selection instead of elections. The people who make it almost always rest on one comparison: a legislature chosen by lot would work like a jury. Twelve randomly chosen citizens, the argument goes, are already trusted to decide whether a person spends decades in prison — a weightier call than most lawmaking — and they handle it reasonably well because they're ordinary people with no stake in the verdict, instructed to weigh evidence and deliberate together. So a random sample of citizens, given time, information, and neutral facilitation, should likewise be trusted to make laws, perhaps more trusted than professional politicians, who are screened for exactly the wrong traits.

When I tried to build the correspondence table myself, the mapping only worked if I quietly supplied things juries have that a free-standing legislature would not: a judge who filters the evidence, a charge fixed in advance by someone else, a standard of proof that settles how to weigh what's heard, and an appeal available afterwards. A citizens' assembly writing law would have to be its own judge, its own question-setter, and its own reviewer at once. When I raised this with a friend who supports selection by lot, he replied that the analogy is only meant to show that ordinary people deliberate well when the setting is right — which left me unsure what work the comparison is actually doing.

So: lay out the full mapping — what is supposed to play the role of the judge, the rules of evidence, the fixed charge, the appeal, the unanimity demand, the public courtroom — then mark where it genuinely breaks, distinguishing the breaks that matter for the conclusion from the ones that don't. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion the analogy is used to carry — that a randomly chosen legislature would be at least as reliable as an elected one — survives with the breaks on the table. If it only survives in a weaker form, say which, and whether the jury is even the right comparison, or whether something like an opinion poll estimating a population fits better.
