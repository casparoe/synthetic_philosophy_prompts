My brother-in-law is deep in a bunch of alternative-health rabbit holes and whenever anyone in the family pushes back on something he posts, he doesn't really argue the specific claim — he just says "do your own research," like that ends the discussion. It happend again over the holidays, this time about fluoride in the water, and it struck me that I honestly don't know what the sentence is even claiming. He clearly doesn't mean it literally (he's not running his own toxicology studies), but the people who repeat it also don't seem to mean something trivial like "check a second source." And it's not just him — I've seen it from crypto guys, from history Youtubers, and weirdly also from some totally mainstream self-help types, so it's clearly doing work across a lot of different worldviews.

So what I want is the slogan taken apart the way you'd take apart any one-liner that's quietly doing a lot of epistemological lifting. Lay out the distinct precise claims "do your own research" could be making, as a menu rather than one favored interpretation. For instance: readings where "research" means reading popularizers vs. reading the primary literature yourself vs. actual firsthand investigation; readings where it's a claim about institutional epistemic authority being untrustworthy in general vs. about particular captured institutions; readings where it's a directive to the listener vs. a defense of the speaker's own practice; maybe even a reading where it's not about evidence at all but about who has the right to be an epistemic authority over you. I'm sure there are others I haven't thought of.

For each reading, give a verdict: is it a platitude, is it substantive and actually defensible, is it flatly false — and, the part I most want, if that reading were true, would it actually support what people deploy the slogan for, which is dismissing expert consensus? My suspicion is that some readings are obviously true but inert (sure, don't believe everything you're told), some are strong enough to do the dismissing work but pretty crazy, and the rhetorical move is sliding between the two, but I'd like to see that laid out properly instead of just asserted. If you can finish by saying which reading my brother-in-law most likely means and whether it's the same one doing the work in his arguments, great. No need to dunk on him — he's genuinely a nice guy, I just want to have something better to say next time than "that's not an argument."
