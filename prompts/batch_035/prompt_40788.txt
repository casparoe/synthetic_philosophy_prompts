I'm grading final exams for a graduate seminar on the naturalization of intentionality and would appreciate a point-by-point assessment of one answer. The exam question was:

"Teleosemantic theories propose to explain, in naturalistic terms, how mental states can be about things in the world. Explain what such theories take the content of a mental state to be fixed by, why the appeal to selection history is supposed to solve the problem of misrepresentation (i.e., the difficulty that crude naturalistic accounts seem to leave no room for error), and one significant objection the view faces."

The student wrote:

"Teleosemantics holds that the content of a mental state is fixed by the biological function of the mechanisms that produce and consume it. A trait's function is whatever it does that contributes to the organism's fitness: the heart has the function of pumping blood because pumping contributes to fitness, and by the same token, if the sound of the heartbeat helped doctors detect disease, producing that sound would count as a function of the heart too. As for content, a state of the frog's visual system represents small dark moving things in the vicinity, because the mechanism was selected for firing in their presence. This is what allows for error: a mechanism can fail to perform its function, so the frog that snaps at a passing pellet misrepresents, much as a heart that beats irregularly fails to pump properly. As Fodor showed, this cannot be right: the frog snaps at the pellet only because it looks like a fly, so if content were fixed by selection history, the frog's state would have the disjunctive content fly-or-pellet, which no frog's state could have. And since content is fixed entirely by history, a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of the frog that arose without that history — a 'swampfrog' — would have no contentful states at all, which just shows that teleosemantics collapses into behaviourism, since for such a duplicate only actual behaviour could carry content."

Please assign a grade (letter grade or score out of 100, as you prefer) and provide the specific comments that justify it: identify what the answer gets right, and exactly where and how it goes wrong, point by point, rather than giving a general impression. The comments will be passed on to the student, so please phrase them for the student to read.
