I'm a journalist who has spent the better part of a decade covering democracy and its discontents for a general-interest ideas magazine — mostly reporting from Budapest and Warsaw, with a stretch in Istanbul and more recently on American institutions. Next month I've been invited to give a talk at a university workshop on democratic erosion, and I want to use it to defend a thesis I've gradually drifted toward: that "democratic backsliding" has become a concept doing two jobs at once, and should be split. On my view, the label now covers both illiberal *policymaking* — governments pursuing things their opponents call authoritarian, within a system where voters can still turf them out, as arguably in Poland before 2023 — and structural *entrenchment* — changes to courts, electoral administration, media, and constitutional amendment rules designed to make removal practically impossible, which is what I think we saw in Hungary after 2010 and Turkey after the 2016 coup attempt. I want to argue that only the second deserves the name, that applying it to the first both inflates the concept and hands incumbents a ready-made "partisan smear" defense, and that the 2023 Polish election, in which the entrenched-seeming PiS government actually lost, is exactly the kind of case we misclassify when we run the two together.

The audience is not my readership. The room will be maybe twenty-five comparative politics scholars: a cluster of Hungary and Turkey specialists who regard Orbán and Erdoğan as textbook cases of a well-documented phenomenon, at least one or two people connected to the big cross-national measurement projects who treat backsliding as a continuous, graded variable precisely so they can track it, an Americanist who studies U.S. institutions and will want to know whether my narrower concept leaves the American case outside the category entirely, and possibly a constitutional lawyer interested in what the literature calls abusive constitutionalism. Several of them will have spent their careers resisting the objection — coming from Orbán's own rhetoric and its imitators — that "backsliding" is just a term Western elites apply to elections they dislike, and I suspect some will hear my argument as accidentally arming that objection.

Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet. I'd like the eight or so objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to my case, each phrased the way these particular people would put it — in their vocabulary, invoking the distinctions and the country cases they care about, not in a pundit's vocabulary — with a suggested two-minute reply and, crucially, a note on where the reply is soft. Where an objection has real force, tell me plainly what I should concede versus what I should hold. I'm particularly unsure how to handle the measurement crowd, since my conceptually motivated split could look like a step backward from their graded indices, and I don't want to pick that fight blind. And include the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks.
