I've been teaching the unity of the proposition this week and a student (a sharp one, annoyingly) produced an argument I can't fully dismantel without paying more than I want to. It's basically Bradley's regress dressed up as an argument for eliminativism about structure, and it seems to prove way too much. Here it is as she gave it:

(1) A proposition (or state of affairs, or fact — pick your ontology) with the form aRb is more than a mere list: <loves, Romeo, Juliet> is not the proposition that Romeo loves Juliet unless the constituents are unified into one thing.
(2) Whatever unifies the constituents must relate them, and relating is something only a relation can do.
(3) So a further relation R* must relate R to a and to b. But R* is itself just one more constituent, and it too needs relating to what it relates — and so on without end.
(4) A regress is vicious when the task it's supposed to complete is never completed; unification is a task that either gets done at some level or never gets done at all.
(5) So nothing is ever unified: no propositions, no states of affairs, no facts.
(6) But every truth is true in virtue of something, and that something would have to be a unified entity. So there are no truths.
(7) Therefore eliminativism about structure across the board — no complexes, no facts, no truthmakers. Bradley himself swallowed something like this and went monist; she goes the other way and just denies structure outright.

And then she points out, gleefully, that if she's right, her own argument isn't unified either, so it can't be sound — and she says that's fine by her, since nothing else is either, and self-refutation is only an objection to an argument if arguments exist.

Now, I don't buy the conclusion, obviously. I can see a couple of places to push. The move from (2) to (3) smells like it treats the *holding* of R as one more item that itself needs relating, when maybe R's holding just is the relating. And (4)'s "task" framing seems to assume unification is something one constituent does *to* the others rather than a status of the whole. But whenever I reject (3) by saying R relates a and b in its own right, she asks whether that's a brute fact — and brute unity at the level of R looks no better than brute unity at the level of <aRb>; I've just moved the spot where I refuse to answer. And if I reject (4) and call the regress benign, she asks benign in what sense, since each new relation adds a new constituent that's as unordered as the original list was.

So: which step do I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep (1) — I really don't want to say a proposition just is a list — and I'd prefer to keep (6), since truthmaker-style talk is load-bearing in my other work. Walk me through what the standard escape routes (R's holding being internal to R rather than a further constituent, facts with exemplification as a non-relational tie, positionalism about relations, Bradley's own monist route) each have to pay, and whether any of them can answer her "you just moved the brute fact" jab without merely denying that explanation has to bottom out somewhere.

Two things about how I want the answer. First, don't be mealy-mouthed: commit to a step, even though I know reasonable people land on different ones. Second, say explicitly what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would move you to indict a different step, and where in your own diagnosis you think you're most likely to have gone wrong. And after the prose, give me a JSON object with exactly these fields: "verdict_step" (the number of the step you reject, as a string), "diagnosis" (one sentence naming the flaw if there is one, or "none" if the argument is valid and it's just a question of cost), "self_undermining_response" (one sentence on what to say about her closing jab), "escape_route_ranking" (an array of objects, each with fields "route" and "cost", ranked from least to most expensive), "keep_premise_1" (true or false, whether your diagnosis lets me keep premise 1), and "would_change_mind" (the single consideration most likely to flip your verdict). Valid JSON, keys in quotes, no trailing commas.
