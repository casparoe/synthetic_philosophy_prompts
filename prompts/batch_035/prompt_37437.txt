In my reporting on the history of science I keep running into a pattern for which I don't have a name. When the same idea is reached independently by two people, the one better placed — better connected, at the more prestigious institution, publishing where more people will see it — ends up with the credit, and the other is remembered, if at all, as having merely anticipated the winner. Stranger still, the runner-up's work is then retroactively judged to have lacked something essential: sufficient rigor, the right timing, a receptive audience. The Darwin–Wallace case is the canonical instance, but I see the same shape in the calculus priority dispute and in twentieth-century mathematics. I know of Stigler's law of eponymy and of the general literature on multiple independent discovery, but neither seems to capture the retrospective rewriting of the rival into a mere precursor. Is there an established term in the sociology or historiography of science for this consolidation of credit after a contested priority claim? If so, how does it differ from the Matthew effect and from an ordinary priority dispute? If not, please coin a term, define it, and give boundary cases that separate genuine differences between the two contributions from differences invented after the fact. Please also say what would change your mind: what would count as evidence that a suitable term already exists, or that your boundary has been drawn in the wrong place.
