I am a high school senior, and for my school's philosophy elective we have been asked to write a position paper on free will: not a survey of the literature, but an honest statement of what we actually believe, followed by our own defense of it. Our teacher warned us that the most instructive failures on this assignment tend to be internal ones — a set of views that each sound plausible on their own but cannot all be held together. Before I commit myself to defending anything, I would like to know whether my set of commitments is even defensible. Here is where I currently stand:

(1) The free will worth wanting is compatible with determinism. What matters is that I act on desires and values I endorse on reflection, and that my actions are responsive to reasons. Being an ultimate originator of myself — an uncaused cause somewhere back in the chain — is not something anyone has, and I doubt we would even want it if we understood what we were asking for.

(2) Frankfurt-style cases succeed: if Jones decides entirely on his own to do something, while a counterfactual device would have blocked any other decision had he been about to make one, Jones is still responsible. What he actually did came from him, so responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise.

(3) At the same time, when I deliberate — choosing between universities, for instance — I take myself to genuinely have several options open, and I do not think this sense is a mistake. Something real is captured by seeing the future as open in that moment, even though I accept determinism in the abstract.

(4) Manipulation cases strike me as very hard to answer. If neuroscientists covertly implanted someone's values and he then deliberated flawlessly from them, he seems not responsible for what he does; and I cannot find a principled difference between that case and ordinary character formation through heredity and environment, since in both the values one deliberates from are fixed by factors outside one's control. If there is no relevant difference, it follows that no one is responsible.

(5) The luck objection seems to me decisive against libertarian free will: if a choice could go either way with my reasons, character, and the entire past held fixed, then which way it goes is luck, not control, and luck is not a source of anything worth wanting.

(6) On blame and punishment I find myself holding two things at once: people who act from endorsed, reasons-responsive values genuinely deserve blame in a basic, non-instrumental sense; yet punishment by the state is justified only by forward-looking considerations — deterrence, protection, rehabilitation — and never by the claim that suffering is intrinsically deserved.

So here is my request: which of these commitments are in genuine tension, which merely appear to be, and what would I have to give up to restore consistency? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and for each one tell me what the cheapest repair would be and what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the list. Finally, if several of the tensions turn out to share a common root, I would like to know which single revision would clean up the most. I would rather have a careful audit of my own position than a general lecture on the free will debate.
