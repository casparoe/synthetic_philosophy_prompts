I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and this semester I'm TAing our department's upper-level seminar on philosophy of the special sciences. Each TA gets to run one 90-minute section of their own design, and I want to spend mine on reduction and emergence — specifically I want the students to actually feel why "in principle derivable" and "actually usable as an explanation or prediction" come apart, instead of just reading Nagel-style reduction and critiques of it and nodding along. The section has 16 students, mostly philosophy majors with a few cog sci people mixed in, no real math background beyond what they remember from high school, and the room is a no-tech seminar room, so no laptops — it all has to run on handouts, a whiteboard, maybe tokens or candy.

My half-formed idea is some kind of bureau simulation: a subset of the students receive the complete micro-state of a toy world plus it's exact dynamical rules, the other students play the special scientists working on that world, and the special scientists submit questions they need answered. Then the micro-people discover that having literally all the information at the bottom level doesn't let them answer anyone's questions in time, while the special scientists figure out shortcut regularities of their own. I don't want to have to program anything — no computers, remember — so I'm imagining a printed grid, a cellular-automaton-ish world small enough to advance by hand a few ticks per minute, and queries like "will this thing still exist at t=50" or "which of these two regions wins." Could you design the full activity for me: rules, materials, the exact kind of queries each team gets, a scoring scheme that doesn't involve real money (section participation points or candy is fine), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the standard moving parts (bridge laws, multiple realizability if it comes up, weak vs strong emergence) without me lecturing for more than ten or fifteen minutes at the end.

Two more things. First, tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I've never run a simulation-based section before and I assume the micro-team will start improvising macro-level shortcuts, which might actually be pedagogically great or might be chaos, I genuinely can't tell. Second, be frank about which part of the debate a hand-run simulation simply cannot reproduce — my hunch is it can't show anything about strong emergence, since whatever toy world we use is fully computable by construction, so the underivability claim has to be supplied by me after the fact, but tell me if I'm wrong or if there's a cleverer way to set it up so that limitation itself becomes a teaching moment.
