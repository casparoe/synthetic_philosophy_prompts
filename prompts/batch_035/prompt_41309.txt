My roommate is pre-law but takes philosophy way too seriously, and he's got this argument he now deploys at dinner about once a week. It's about future people and I can't find where it slips. Here it is as he gives it:

(1) To wrong someone, you have to make them worse off than they would've been if you'd acted differently. If nobody ends up worse off, nobody's been wronged — you can't owe something to someone who isn't worse off in any way.

(2) Making someone worse off than they would've been requires an alternative in which that very person exists and does better. If in the alternative they never exist at all, there's no version of them doing better.

(3) Anything that changes the timing of a conception changes which particular person results from it — different sperm, different egg, different kid.

(4) Nearly everything we do changes the timing of some conception somewhere downstream. If I take the bus instead of walking, some couple a hundred years from now meets at a slightly different moment, and the kid conceived that night is a different kid than the one who'd have been conceived a week later. This stuff propagates.

(5) So for nearly any act, the future people whose lives my act shapes would simply never have existed had I acted otherwise.

(6) By (1) and (2), I can't have wronged any of them — whatever I did, they're not worse off than they "would've been," since there is no would've-been for them.

(7) So it's basically impossible to wrong future people at all. A government choosing between a policy that leaves the people of 2200 with great lives and one that leaves them lives just barely worth living does nothing wrong to anyone by picking the worse one — the people who'd have had the great lives never exist to be wronged, and the people who do exist can say "better barely worth living then never born," which is true.

He also runs it closer to home: a couple who could conceive now or in a month, who know conceiving now means a child with a painful condition and conceiving in a month means a child without it, and who conceive now anyway. Nobody wronged, he says — the child with the condition owes its existence to the choice, and the child without it was never anyone.

The conclusion seems crazy to me, but every step looks fine on it's own. Well, actually, here's what bugs me most. Step (4) says nearly everything we do shifts some conception somewhere. But my roommate runs (6) as if this insulates every act, and I don't think it does. If I shove someone off a bridge, my shoving probably also shifts some distant conception, but the guy I shoved is still worse off than he would've been — the alternative where he's better off exists just fine, and he's in it. So (6) can't be licensing what he thinks it licenses. But when I push back he just says fine, the bridge case is a wronging of the guy on the bridge, but the future is all identity-dependent anyway, so (7) still stands for anything long-run. And honestly, for the long-run cases — depletion, climate, the 2200 policy — I can't see that he's wrong, and I hate the conclusion.

So: where does this go wrong, if it does? Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep (1) — if we drop it, it starts to look like people who never exist can be wronged, which seems to license way too much (do I have to worry about the welfare of the infinite possible children I could've had with everyone I've ever met?). But tell me straight if (1) is what has to go, and what the least bad way of dropping it looks like. Also, does my bridge-case point actually do any work, or is he right that it leaves (7) untouched?
