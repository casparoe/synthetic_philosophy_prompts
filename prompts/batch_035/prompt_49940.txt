I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and the situation is that my department's one-year dissertation fellowship has an internal deadline in six weeks, my prospectus meeting is right after that, and my advisor has made it clear that she will not sign off on a prospectus on higher-order theories of consciousness unless I can show her the project is more than a re-run of the standard misrepresentation debate. Her view is that the objection tradition going back to Dretske — the problem of the rock, and the family of cases where a higher-order state points at a first-order state that isn't there, or misdescribes one that is — sunk the research program, and that I should move to something else. I don't want to move, and I have a half-formed idea for why the program is salvageable, but I can't get it into shape on my own. My suspicion is this: when defenders of higher-order theories answer misrepresentation objections by analogy with ordinary perceptual error — a higher-order thought can be wrong about the red thing in the same way a perception can be wrong about the red thing — they are treating the higher-order relation as a single thing that either obtains or misfires. But I think there are really two distinct failure modes being run together: the higher-order state can fail by pointing at nothing (vacancy), and it can point at the right first-order state while getting its properties wrong (mismatch). My hunch is that vacancy and mismatch have different fates once one takes seriously the causal story about how higher-order states get their contents — roughly, that vacancy is either impossible or harmless because higher-order states are generated by the first-order states they target, while mismatch is real but entirely benign, just ordinary error about properties rather than error about occurrence. If that's right, then some of the classic objections (the rock-type cases) dissolve, others (the cases where the higher-order state targets a distinct but neighboring first-order state) get sharpened rather than answered, and the whole debate gets reorganized rather than settled. What I can't yet do is say precisely what question my project answers, or defend the split against the obvious worry that it's ad hoc.

What I need from you is a two-page fellowship proposal that I can adapt and submit. Specifically: a precise statement of the question, something determinate enough that a non-specialist on the fellowship committee can tell whether the project succeeded; an explanation of why the existing higher-order positions — the thought-first versions, the perception-first versions, and the self-representational hybrids — leave the question open, stated in terms of the positions themselves rather than by recounting the literature; the method, broken down month by month over one academic year, being honest that this is a philosophical project with no empirical component; what would count as the project failing (I want genuine failure conditions, not the polite kind); and a candid assessment of whether this is too small for a year or too big, with your recommendation for how to scope it if it's too big. Please don't include a bibliography or citations — I'll add references myself afterward, and invented ones would be worse than none. Refer to authors and positions by name where it helps, but the proposal shouldn't depend on any specific text.
