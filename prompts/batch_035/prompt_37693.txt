i'm a software engineer, zero philosophy background, but last year during a really slow sprint i fell down the embedded agency rabbit hole and now i can't stop thinking about it. the idea as i get it: we usually picture an "agent" as something sitting safely outside the world it acts on, choosing actions from a distance, like my code talking to a database over the network. but real agents (humans, animals, eventually whatever we build) are inside the world they're modeling. they're made of the same stuff as the thing they're trying to predict, their compute is a physical chunk of the environment, and they can't fully model themselves because the model would have to include itself -- the map of the map of the map, forever.

anyway i keep having the same argument with a coworker of mine, dan, who thinks the whole framing is just a software problem we'll engineer our way out of. his take: sure, a naive agent has self-reference issues, but you just factor it out -- sandbox the self-model, put it in a different module than the thing doing the modeling, keep the introspection coarse on purpose. we do abstraction layers all day every day and they work fine. he says i'm basically complaining about a layering violation.

my counter is that the modular split doesn't remove the boundary, it just moves it somewhere, and every place you move it is still a place where the world doesn't actually have a line. but honestly he lands some hits (a thermostat doesn't need to model its own transistor physics, it works fine) and i want to figure out what the real disagreement is instead of just vibing at each other.

so: write me a dialogue between the two positions. set it up as an argument between two characters -- call them whatever you want, but one is "the architect," who thinks the agent/environment boundary is an engineering choice you get to draw wherever is convenient and the convenience is the whole point, and the other is someone who's basically made of the same swamp as its environment and thinks the boundary is something you're stuck with, only ever approximate, and the approximating is where all the interesting failure modes live.
