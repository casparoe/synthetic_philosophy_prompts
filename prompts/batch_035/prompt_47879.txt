I teach an upper-level seminar titled "Induction, Compression, and Complexity" that runs through Kolmogorov complexity, the invariance theorem, and Solomonoff induction before ending with the philosophical question of what, if anything, this machinery does for Occam's razor. For the three-hour final, I want one long essay question worth 25 marks that cannot be answered by reciting definitions. The question I have in mind concerns whether Solomonoff induction *vindicates* Occam's razor or merely *presupposes* it. I want the question worded so that a student who reflexively writes "the universal prior assigns exponentially decaying probability to longer programs, which is just Occam's razor, QED" gets little credit, while a student who engages with the following tensions gets room to shine: (i) the invariance theorem only secures machine-independence up to an additive constant, and since that constant can be arbitrarily large, it can encode any finite amount of "preferred" behavior — including the entire future of any observation sequence a student could care about — so in what sense is the simplicity bias non-arbitrary?; (ii) the predictor is incomputable, so its putative justificatory role for an Occam's razor that actual scientists and learners use is questionable; (iii) Solomonoff's convergence result (the total expected squared prediction error is bounded and the posterior concentrates on the true computable environment asymptotically) might be the best reply available — the bias washes out in the limit, and its price is bounded — but the bound and the pre-asymptotic behavior still depend on the chosen universal machine, so the reply may only relocate the worry rather than answer it; and (iv) there is a "no free lunch" style point lurking: perhaps no formalism could justify a simplicity bias on entirely neutral grounds, in which case Solomonoff's achievement is to make the bias explicit, precise, and asymptotically harmless rather than to justify it — a distinction the question should force students to take a stance on.

Please write the exam question itself (one substantial paragraph, addressed to the students, with clear instructions about what a strong answer must do), a model answer of roughly 600 words that takes a definite position while acknowledging the strongest opposing consideration, and a marking scheme. For the marking scheme, break the 25 marks into five or six must-hit points with individual mark values summing correctly; for each, give a one-line description of what earns the marks and a one-line note on the way students typically fumble it. Separately, include a short section explaining what separates an A answer from a B answer on this question — I want the difference to be about specific dialectical moves (e.g., noticing that the convergence theorem's error bound is only up to a constant that itself depends on the machine, or distinguishing a *vindication* of Occam's razor from a *precisification* of it), not about polish or completeness. Please also note one or two common errors that should cost marks outright, such as claiming the invariance theorem makes the prior fully machine-independent, or conflating the uncomputability of Kolmogorov complexity with the uncomputability of the Solomonoff prior in a way that muddles which one is at issue in the justificatory argument.
