A friend of mine who works on reinforcement learning spent most of last evening trying to convince me that inverse reinforcement learning — and, more generally, any attempt to infer what someone wants from what they do — is empty as an empirical enterprise. We argued for hours, and although I left convinced he must be wrong somewhere, I could not say where. Here is his argument as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Inferring preferences from behavior requires a linking assumption connecting the two: that people tend to act optimally, or nearly so, with respect to what they want.
(2) A linking assumption earns its keep only if it can fail — only if some possible patterns of behavior would be inconsistent with it.
(3) For any finite record of behavior, however strange, there exists a preference assignment relative to which every observed choice is optimal: if nothing else, assign high value to precisely the actions chosen and low value to everything else that was available.
(4) So for any behavior there is some way of satisfying the linking assumption; hence the assumption never rules anything out.
(5) An assumption that never rules anything out is evidentially inert: no possible observation could ever count against the hypothesis that a person has a given preference, so observing behavior can never raise or lower the credibility of any hypothesis about preferences.
(6) Therefore what an IRL system — or an economist reading preferences off consumer choices — outputs is fixed entirely by the assumptions built in at the start; the data contribute nothing. Preference inference is a ritual for restating one's priors in the past tense.

His conclusion strikes me as too strong, and yet each step survived when I pressed him. I did try one objection: that in practice we don't allow arbitrary preference assignments, but only some restricted, reasonably simple class, and that within such a class plenty of behaviors cannot be rationalized. He answered that in that case whatever constraint exists comes from the restriction I chose, not from the data — I have merely moved my prior into the hypothesis class, which concedes his point. I could not find a reply to that.

I have my suspicions about the move from (3) to (4), and again from (4) to (5), but I cannot articulate what slips. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked unassailable, and what rejecting it costs. If more than one step could plausibly go, say which rejection is cheapest and what the alternatives pay. And please address his reply to my objection directly: if the constraint on inference comes from a restricted hypothesis class rather than from the data, has he shown that the data are doing no work — or has he traded one distinction (between assumption and evidence) for another that does not hold? For what it is worth, I would like to keep (1) and the general idea that choice is evidence of preference; I am prepared to give up a good deal else to preserve them.
