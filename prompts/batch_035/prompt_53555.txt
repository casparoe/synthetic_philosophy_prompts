I've been trying to figure out what I actually believe about precommitment -- when my past self should be allowed to bind my present self -- going by gut reactions to cases. Ulysses ordering his crew to keep him lashed to the mast no matter what he yells: should stay bound, even if the sirens turn out to be a mediocre cover band. A woman's advance directive refusing tube feeding once she has advanced dementia, written back when she was sharp: should be honored, even tho the demented her is content and wants to live. A problem gambler who puts himself on a casino's self-exclusion list and months later begs to be let back in: the casino should turn him away. A guy who asks a friend to hide his gun during a depressive spell and later demands it back: the friend should refuse. But then: a 25-year-old signs an irrevocable deed giving her inheritance to a commune, and at 50, a different person entirely, she regrets it -- I think she should be able to get out. And a constitution whose amendment clause entrenches itself against ever being amended, forever: illegitimate, even though I'm fine with that same constitution demanding supermajorities twice over. My tentative principle is that precommitment binds only when the later change of mind comes from akrasia or impairment rather than reflective growth, but I can't tell if that explains the constitution one or just paper over it. Test it, repair it or replace it, tell me which of my verdicts has to go, and tell me what you'd predict for the case I keep flip-flopping on: a directive refusing a treatment written on the assumption it would mean months bedridden with tubes, when by the time it's needed the treatment has become routine and mild. Does the past self still bind when the future it imagined turns out to be the wrong one?
