The essay's topic: variations on a theme as the crux of creativity, and what I'm calling the knob-twiddling model of it. For context if it's been a while: the idea, which Hofstadter pushed in various places in the 80s and 90s, is that creative acts aren't mysterious leaps. Instead you take a concept (a tune, a recipe, a proof strategy) and mentally represent it as a bundle of dimensions — knobs — like the knobs on a synth. Take the US anthem: knobs for tempo, instrumentation, key, the performer's gender, the degree of solemnity. Duke Ellington's recording of it is one point in that knob-space. Creativity, on this picture, is the capacity to slide around that space in a fluid way, to imagine where you'd land before you travel — and crucially, Hofstadter thought this imaginative sliding, not the execution, is where creativity lives. Most people can play the anthem in 7/8 if you tell them to; hearing it in your head that way first, unprompted, is the creative act. He also stressed that the number of conceivable variations explodes combinatorially and that taste, some temperature-like control over how wild the twiddling gets, is what keeps it from being noise.

Draft: "Twiddling What Isn't There"

The knob model is usually presented as a theory of how we vary what's already given: the knobs are sitting there in the representation — tempo, key, instrumentation — and creativity is turning them. This gets the emphasis backwards. The hard part of creativity is not turning knobs; it's noticing that a knob exists at all. Bach could invert a melody because inversion was a recognized dimension of his musical culture. But when Beethoven spends the first movement of the Fifth doing almost nothing with a motif except deciding, over and over, how few notes suffice for it to count as the motif at all, the dimension being twiddled — how much of the theme counts as the theme — was not a knob anyone handed him. It's one his contemporaries could operate only after he'd built it. Call these second-order knobs: dimensions whose invention is itself the creative act, and which thereafter look like ordinary knobs to everyone downstream.

This reframes the whole model. First-order twiddling explains Ellington but not the string quartets; it explains a good mashup but not the first mashup. The standard reply is that new knobs come from analogies to old ones — you get the "how much counts as the theme" knob by slipping in from "how much of a signature counts as signing," or whatever. But that reply quietly assumes the very thing it should explain: that analogy itself can be seen as knob-twiddling, which Hofstadter sometimes says and sometimes doesn't. If analogy is knob-twiddling, then the model isn't a model of creativity, it's a model of one idiom of it, and the idiom only became available at some historical point. If it isn't, then the model has a hole exactly where the crux was supposed to be.

I then argue that this suggests a picture of art history as a slow accumulation of knobs, most of which are first-order for later generations. The avant-garde are the people manufacturing knobs at cost; everyone else twiddles cheaply on the margin. Whether a given work is creative, then, is not a property of the work or even the mind that made it, but of where it sits relative to the current knob-set — which is why the same piece can be creative in 1913 and inert in 2024.
