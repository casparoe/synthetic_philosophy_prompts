I do forecasting for a logistics company, and my team has been having the same argument for two years about when to trust the outside view. One side treats reference-class forecasting as basically mandatory: when we're estimating a new warehouse rollout, we should look at how similar rollouts actually went and average, and any story about why this one is different needs to be penalized hard, because that story is exactly what every failed project told itself. The other side says the outside view is a illusion of discipline: reference classes are infinitely divisible -- is our rollout like 'warehouse rollouts,' 'rollouts in our region,' 'rollouts run by this particular ops VP'? -- so choosing one is itself an inside-view judgment dressed up as humility, and once you concede that, the outside view collapses into just thinking carefully about the case at hand with extra steps. Two years, nobody has moved, and I'm not even sure either side thinks the other is wrong about facts. My working theory is it's a proxy fight about which error is worse -- being overconfidently wrong or being deferentially wrong -- and I gather philosophers have a version of the same fight under the banner of epistemic modesty. Diagnose it for me: verbal dispute about what counts as a reference class, real disagreement about priors and how much structure the world has, or two groups optimizing against different failure modes? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says in our arguments. And then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis -- a distinction, a procedure, something we could actually test against our back-catalog of completed projects -- or whether this impasse is just the permanent shape of the disagreement.
