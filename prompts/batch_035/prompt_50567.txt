I'm putting together a series of writing exercises for a philosophy-of-science reading group, and our longest-running fight needs settling on paper. For over a year, one member — a theorist who works on string compactifications and takes Richard Dawid's framework of non-empirical theory assessment seriously — has been at odds with another, a committed empiricist in roughly the Popperian mold, who insists that a theory which makes no testable predictions at accessible energies has no more claim on our belief than its unfalsifiable rivals. The Dawidian's key exhibits are the three arguments from Dawid's *String Theory and the Scientific Method* (2013): the no-alternatives argument (that string theorists' failure, despite ability and motivation, to find a rival unifying framework at the same level of coherence raises the probability that no such rival exists), the argument from unexpected explanatory coherence (that string theory's unanticipated internal unifications, like the convergence of its different formulations or the packing of gravity into a quantum framework, would be a surprising coincidence were the theory on the wrong track), and the meta-inductive argument (that the scientific community's informal trust in analogous structural features has historically tracked truth in cases that were later empirically vindicated). The empiricist's key exhibits are the underdetermination of theory by non-empirical evidence, the long history of beautiful, coherent, unified theories that died on contact with data, and the worry that assessments of "no alternatives" are hopelessly contaminated by the sociology and training of the community doing the assessing.

Rather than another shouting match, I'd like you to write the joint statement these two should have produced after a serious adversarial collaboration on non-empirical theory confirmation in fundamental physics. Please give me: the claims both could sign — about what string theory has achieved mathematically, about what counts as evidence, about the limits of naive falsificationism in a field where direct tests are forever out of reach, and about the role of underdetermination; then the precise statements of their remaining disagreement, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording (i.e., the Dawidian must recognize their view in the pro-non-empirical-confirmation formulation, and vice versa); then the cruxes — I suspect one is whether scientists' failure to find alternatives is evidence about the space of possible theories or only about the space of theories that this community is trained to see, and another is what exactly the meta-inductive track record of past physics licenses — and for each crux at least one argument, historical case, or future scenario both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If any part of their dispute strikes you as verbal rather than substantive (for instance, whether "confirmation" and "rationally elevated credence" name the same thing), say which part and why.

Format the document as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, with the cruxes and predictions folded into the table or listed immediately after it. Keep the whole thing under 800 words — tight but complete — and write it in American English.
