I spent thirty-five years doing experimental work in physical chemistry, and since retiring I have been reading metaphysics — most recently Lewis's On the Plurality of Worlds and the transcripts of Kripke's Naming and Necessity. The dispute between counterpart theory and transworld identity puzzles me in a way most other disputes I have encountered do not. As I understand it, the disagreement is not about how many worlds there are but about whether the person who wins the 1968 election in the nearest world where it happens is Humphrey himself, or only someone resembling him. On one side is the complaint that counterpart theory makes Humphrey's own possibilities into the achievements of somebody else — he doesn't get to win, only his double does. On the other side is Lewis's argument that transworld identity would be arbitrary or indeterminate: nothing in the qualitative facts about a distant world fixes which of its inhabitants is Humphrey rather than merely his look-alike, since cross-world identity cannot literally be a relation of similarity, yet nothing else seems to pin it down either. Each argument looks decisive to me when I read it, which makes me suspect they are not really answering one another. So: what kind of disagreement is this? A verbal one about the phrase "could have been," a substantive one about haecceitism and what makes de re modal claims true, or a choice between two notational systems that validate the same arguments and differ only in what the formulas are meant to say? My tentative hypothesis, formed by a lifetime of theories being told apart by data, is that no observation or proof could decide it, because both sides are describing the same underlying structure with different bookkeeping. If that is right, explain why the disagreement nonetheless refuses to die; if it is wrong, tell me what would count as progress here, and whether anything since the 1980s — when the arguments seem to have simply run out — actually constitutes any.
