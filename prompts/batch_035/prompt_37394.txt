For several years now I have been reading about referendums and ballot initiatives — the Swiss system above all, the 2016 Brexit vote, and California's proposition system — and I have settled on a set of views about when citizens should decide things directly. Before I defend any of this in public, I would like to know whether the views are consistent with one another, and I would rather be told now than in an argument. Here is the set:

1. On any question of fundamental direction for the country — who governs us, what we owe the state, what the state owes us — the people should decide directly, because ordinary voters have nothing personal to gain from the answer, whereas representatives always do: careers, party advantage, donors. A voter's judgment is therefore cleaner than a politician's.
2. The 2016 Brexit referendum was the right way to settle the question of EU membership, and the 52–48 result settled it. I was impatient with Members of Parliament afterwards asking what Leave actually means: the people were asked a question, they answered it, and treating the answer as an opening bid was a refusal to accept it.
3. Switzerland is the best working model of democracy I know of. Voting several times a year on everything from taxes to immigration, and requiring constitutional changes to carry both a majority of voters and a majority of cantons, strikes me as the mature version of what Britain attempted once.
4. I disliked the outcome, but I accept the 2009 Swiss referendum that banned the construction of new minarets as legitimate: the people were asked, the people answered, and if you only accept referendum results you happen to like, direct democracy means nothing. I would say the same about any Swiss result I disliked.
5. California's proposition system, by contrast, looks broken to me. Laws are drafted by interest groups, put on the ballot by paid signature gatherers, and sold with saturation advertising; voters are then asked to decide technical tax and budget measures they have never studied, and the results are locked in so that the legislature cannot amend them afterwards.
6. When I try to state my underlying standard, it is this: questions of ends belong to the people, and questions of means belong to experts and representatives. That is why membership of a supranational body was rightly a referendum question and a tax formula is not.
7. Finally, I believe one of the main jobs of a constitution is to keep certain questions off the ballot altogether: no nationwide vote should ever be able to restrict a minority's religion or language, however large the majority in favour.

Some of these, I suspect, clash; others, I suspect, only look as if they clash — for instance (2) and (6) seem to point in different directions on how much detail voters can be trusted with, and (1) and (5) on whether voters can be manipulated at all — but I cannot tell which is which, and I do not want to smuggle in a repair I have not stated openly. Please go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and for each real tension what the cheapest repair would cost me elsewhere. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and be specific about which commitment would have to give. I have no prior view about which of the seven is the weak one, so do not spare any of them; if the set cannot be saved as it stands, say so plainly.
