I'm a teaching assistant grading a philosophy of science exam for second-year undergraduates, and I'd like a second opinion on one script. Please assign a grade (our scale is A to F, where a B signifies a solid grasp with some errors) and justify it with specific, point-by-point comments: for each passage in the answer, indicate what it gets right, what it gets wrong, and what a grader might be tempted to let slide but shouldn't. Please be explicit about which mistakes are substantive errors of understanding and which are merely loose wording.

The exam question was:

Shortly before an operation, a surgeon tells a patient: "There's about a 30% chance of a serious complication." Explain how (a) the frequency interpretation, (b) the subjective interpretation, and (c) the propensity interpretation each make sense of this claim. For each, state what would make the claim true or well-grounded, and identify one serious difficulty the interpretation faces with this kind of single-case assertion.

The student's answer was:

Under the frequency interpretation, probability is relative frequency in the long run. The surgeon's claim means that in a long series of similar operations, complications would occur in about 30% of them. The interpretation faces an obvious problem with the single case: this patient will either suffer a complication or not, so there is no frequency for her. The frequentist can respond that the relevant series is hypothetical — an endless sequence of patients just like this one — and by the law of large numbers the relative frequency in that sequence converges to the true probability. This is also why the frequentist is not really troubled by the reference class problem: the reference class is simply all patients resembling this one, and the long-run frequency settles what the 30% means.

Under the subjective interpretation, the 30% is the surgeon's degree of belief that a complication will occur. Since the patient's case is unique, there is no objective fact for the surgeon to be tracking, so all that can be demanded of the claim is that the surgeon's overall beliefs be coherent. This means that if a second surgeon, with the same training and looking at the same evidence, says 60%, the first surgeon's 30% is no less correct: subjectivism holds that rational people with the same evidence can permanently disagree, and it has no resources for saying who is closer to the truth, since for a single event there is no truth of the matter to be closer to.

The propensity interpretation was introduced, notably by Popper, precisely to handle single cases. Probability is a disposition of the chance set-up: this patient, with this physiology and this operation, has a 0.3 disposition to develop a complication. This makes the surgeon's claim straightforwardly true or false of this individual, which neither of the other interpretations achieves, and it faces no further difficulty here: the only standing objection to propensities is their vagueness, which is a metaphysical quibble rather than a real problem for this kind of claim.
