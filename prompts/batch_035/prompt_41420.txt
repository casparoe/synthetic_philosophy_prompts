I'm a student in an ancient philosophy course, and this week we covered Aristotle's discussion of justice in Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics, including his treatment of exchange, reciprocal justice, and the role of money, alongside the passages in Book I of the Politics where he criticizes the kind of wealth-getting that aims at unlimited accumulation. My professor explained that, for Aristotle, a just exchange requires an equality between the things traded, and that money exists to serve as the common measure that makes this equality possible — his illustration being a house being exchanged for some definite number of beds or shoes. I've been turning this over since the seminar and I can't make it come out right.

Here is my worry. If every just exchange requires exact equality of value on both sides, then it seems to follow that exchange cannot make either party better off: you always surrender precisely as much value as you receive. So Aristotle appears to have shown that commerce is at best a zero-sum redistribution of pre-existing value, and that any merchant who walks away with a profit has, by Aristotle's own criterion, violated justice. That would make him a committed zero-sum thinker about trade. But then I'm puzzled by two things. First, my professor described this very passage as one of the earliest serious analyses of exchange, and I don't see what there is to analyze if trade is just an equal swap. Second, later thinkers like the scholastics took Aristotle's authority seriously when they developed the idea of a just price, which again suggests they thought he had said something substantive about fair terms of trade rather than having simply ruled out gain. A classmate tried to convince me that the equality Aristotle has in mind is something about proportions between persons and their contributions rather than a claim about gains, but I didn't find her explanation persuasive, since surely the shoemaker trades with the builder in the first place because he'd rather have the house than his shoes.

So my question is whether Aristotle really held the zero-sum view of exchange that I've described, and whether he thought any profit from trade is inherently unjust — and if not, where exactly my reading of the equality requirement goes wrong. Please begin your response with your bottom-line answer in the first sentence, stating plainly whether my zero-sum interpretation is correct, and then lay out your reasoning. Skip any preamble and don't restate my question back to me.
