My oldest friend has become the de facto referee for consent disputes in our group, and she is remarkably consistent, in romantic contexts and medical ones alike. We have started writing down her rulings because we suspect she is running a single principle she has never articulated. So far: a one-night stand where the man said he was divorced when he was still married -- she ruled the consent valid; a partner who claimed to be on contraception and wasn't -- invalid; a man who wildly exaggerated his income and job title before a casual encounter -- valid, on the grounds that his bank account is not what the consent was *about*; a surgeon who described a one-in-twenty complication as vanishingly rare -- invalid; a partner who said "sleep with me or I'm leaving you" -- invalid; a payday loan at an outrageous rate accepted purely out of desperation, with no threats made -- valid, since "a hard bargain is still a bargain"; a woman with early dementia who is lucid most mornings consenting to her own care plan each morning -- valid; and a patient in an acute manic episode agreeing to an experimental procedure -- invalid.

The new case came up last week and I want to walk into Friday's dinner with a prediction. Our friend Dana, recently divorced, slept with a man who told her, at length and convincingly, that he was falling in love with her and could imagine a future with her. He has since admitted to others that he said it only because he was fairly sure she would refuse otherwise. Dana says she certainly wouldn't have slept with him had she known his feelings weren't real, but she doesn't feel violated the way she imagines the contraception case victim does, and she's unsure what to call what happened to her.

What I'd like from you: predict the ruling my friend will hand down, and more importantly, reconstruct the principle (or principles) she seems to be implicitly applying across the whole track record. Her rulings look compatible with at least two different views -- one on which deception voids consent only when it concerns the nature, risks, or physical consequences of what is agreed to, and one on which it voids consent whenever it targets facts that would matter to a reasonable decision about whether to agree. Spell out how each candidate principle handles her earlier rulings, including the ones it handles awkwardly, say which reading of her is most plausible and why, and describe the further case -- real or invented -- that would most cleanly force her to reveal which principle she is actually committed to.
