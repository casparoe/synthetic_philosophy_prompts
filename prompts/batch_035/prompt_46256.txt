I have been working through a popular introduction to twentieth-century analytic philosophy, and its chapter on Donald Davidson closes with a compressed account of radical interpretation that I would like to have audited, since I do not know the literature well enough to do it myself.

The chapter's summary runs roughly as follows. Davidson imagines a field linguist confronting a people with whom she shares no dictionary, no interpreter, and no common history, and asks how she could come to understand their language from scratch. His answer runs through what he called the principle of charity: since the linguist has no independent access to what the speakers believe or desire, she must proceed by assuming, as a defeasible working hypothesis, that they are mostly right about their immediate environment and mostly rational, and then assign their words whatever meanings would make what they say come out largely true by her own lights. Because interpretation can gain a foothold only on these terms, the book credits Davidson with three conclusions: first, that the method demonstrates that most of what any speaker believes is true, since a speaker whose beliefs were largely false could not be interpreted at all; second, that external-world skepticism is therefore not merely unanswered but incoherent; and third, that reports of incommensurable conceptual schemes must be confused, since anyone we can recognize as speaking a language must, once properly interpreted, turn out to agree with us on most substantive matters, whatever their rituals and cosmologies appear to say on the surface. The chapter then closes by crediting Davidson with having shown that deep cross-cultural disagreement is, at bottom, always a mirage.

The chapter reads as authoritative, but something in it strikes me as off, and I cannot tell where. Please go through the summary claim by claim and sort it into three groups: what a careful Davidsonian would accept as stated; what are common simplifications that hold at the level of exposition but would need qualification in a scholarly setting (I have in mind, for instance, whether radical interpretation is meant as a description of what actual translators do); and what Davidson explicitly denied or would have denied — I would particularly like to know whether the status assigned to charity is right, since I have encountered the suggestion elsewhere that it is meant as a constraint on the very possibility of interpretation rather than as an assumption of the ordinary kind. Finally, take up the closing claim on its own: does the thesis that deep cross-cultural disagreement is always a mirage follow from the accurate portions of the summary by themselves, or was it carried by one of the distortions? For my own part, I am inclined to accept the anti-relativist upshot and to suspect that the anti-skeptical one has been strengthened somewhere along the way; I would like to know whether that division of my instincts is sound.
