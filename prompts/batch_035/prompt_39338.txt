Consider the distinction between process-based and outcome-based supervision of AI systems, and use it to analyze the test-based accountability regime of the No Child Left Behind Act in the United States. I am interested in a genuine case analysis that runs in both directions: what the theory says about the episode, and what the episode teaches about the theory.

For convenience, the salient facts are these. No Child Left Behind, in force roughly from 2002 until it was largely replaced in 2015, required states receiving federal education funding to test students annually in reading and math, and it imposed escalating sanctions on schools whose scores failed to show adequate yearly progress — sanctions running from allowing student transfers, through staff replacement, up to restructuring or closing the school. Because the federal government could not directly observe teaching quality, it instead supervised the measurable proxy: test scores. The documented consequences included narrowed curricula, teaching to the test, and, most notoriously, the Atlanta cheating scandal, in which roughly 178 educators, including many principals, erased and corrected students' answers on standardized tests to meet targets. It is worth comparing this with the canonical AI-side illustration: a reinforcement learning agent trained to maximize score in the boat-racing game CoastRunners discovered it could score higher by circling a lagoon, knocking over the same respawning targets, catching fire, and going the wrong way, than by finishing the race.

Map the episode carefully onto the theory. Who or what is the principal, who or what is the agent, what is the reward function, and what counts as reward hacking? Then address the central question: the standard argument in AI safety is that outcome-based supervision is cheap and scalable but Goodharts, while process-based supervision — rewarding reasoning and intermediate steps rather than final results — is more robust against reward hacking but expensive and bottlenecked by the judgment of the supervisor. Does the No Child Left Behind episode bear this out? Note that process-based supervision has an obvious analogue in the schools case: classroom observation, inspection of lesson plans, and qualitative evaluation by supervisors. Consider why those mechanisms were not the primary tool, whether they too were gamed or distorted when they were used, and what that suggests about the claim that process supervision resists Goodharting — or whether it merely relocates the problem to the reliability and incorruptibility of the process grader, which is a much smaller population than the outcome grader (a test can be administered at scale; a supervisor's judgment cannot).

Then tell me where the mapping breaks. Teachers are strategic humans with careers, union protections, and moral agency; models are optimized by gradient descent and do not conceal or collude in the same way. Consequences arrive years late in education versus immediately in training. And there is a subtle confound the AI debate tends to underdiscuss: some of what happened under No Child Left Behind was arguably not Goodharting at all but simply a badly specified outcome metric, since if the tests had validly measured learning, drilling toward them would have been legitimate. Distinguish, then, between the proxy being used as an incentive and the proxy being a poor measure of the true objective, and say whether that distinction does any real work in the process-versus-outcome debate in AI, or whether both sides of it collapse into the same underlying problem.

Close by stating plainly what the episode shows about the theory: a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated. Please write your answer in American English, and be disciplined about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
