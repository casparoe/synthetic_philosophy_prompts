I am a sixth-year PhD student finishing a dissertation on Madhyamaka philosophy, and in three weeks I present my central chapter to my department's weekly metaphysics workshop. I could use help preparing for the question period.

The thesis of the chapter is roughly this: Nāgārjuna's tetralemma — the systematic rejection of all four answers to the question of whether a phenomenon exists, does not exist, both exists and does not exist, or neither exists nor does not exist — is not a piece of deliberate illogic or mystical paradox-mongering. It is a diagnostic device. All four options share a hidden presupposition: that things must have an intrinsic nature (svabhāva), some way of being what they are from their own side. Once that presupposition is exposed, the four options are seen to fail together, not pairwise, and emptiness (śūnyatā) is simply the denial of the presupposition rather than a fifth metaphysical thesis. I further want to claim that this makes Madhyamaka importantly different from the global anti-realism my audience will already have on its shelf, and that the doctrine of two truths — conventional and ultimate — is not an ad hoc escape hatch but the working out of how ordinary truth survives the denial of intrinsic natures.

The audience will be about fifteen analytic philosophers with no background in Buddhist thought. I know them well enough to describe them: two metaphysicians who work on grounding and essence, one of whom is quite serious about essences being out there in the world; a logician who teaches non-classical logic and is on record as thinking that if the catuṣkoṭi says anything clear at all, it says that some contradictions are true, and that this is a feature rather than a bug; a metaethicist who has spent her career on deflationary, quietist, and expressivist programs and will suspect I have reinvented something she already owns under a different name; a historian of early modern philosophy who is allergic to anachronism and will want to know whether I am interpreting Nāgārjuna or conscripting him; and a handful of graduate students who will take their cues from whichever faculty member sounds most confident.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet. List the objections in rough order of how likely they are to come up weighted by how much damage they would do if answered badly, and phrase each one the way this particular audience would actually put it — in their vocabulary, with their default examples, not the way a Buddhist studies scholar would put it. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in two or three minutes, a note on what I should concede up front rather than fight, and a note on where the reply is soft. Please make sure the set includes the self-refutation worry about whether emptiness applies to itself, the logician's attempt to recruit me for dialetheism, and the deflationist's claim that there is nothing here she doesn't already have. The philological and doctrinal-history questions — which commentator's reading I follow, how to translate a particular Sanskrit term — I can handle myself, so leave those out. And include the one objection I am privately hoping nobody asks, with your honest assessment of whether the hope is realistic.
