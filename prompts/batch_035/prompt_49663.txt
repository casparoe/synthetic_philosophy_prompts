I've been trying to sort out, by going on my gut reactions to cases, what exactly is supposed to be better about knowing something rather than merely truly believing it — the old puzzle from the Meno about why knowledge should be worth more than a correct opinion, given that both steer you equally well so long as they stay put. My verdicts so far: if I need a guide across a mountain pass, and one guide genuinely knows the route while the other happens to truly believe the correct route on the strength of a lucky guess, I would rather hire the first and would pay more for her, even though I only need to cross once. If a doctor knows my diagnosis on the basis of genuine understanding, and a charlatan lands on the very same diagnosis through a superstitious method that just happens to work this time, I would rather be treated by the doctor even in the one case where their answers coincide. And two students who each write the right answer on an exam — one who understands the material, one who copied from a neighbor whose work happened to be right — get the same grade, but it still seems to me the first student is better off in a way that has nothing to do with future exams. On the other side, though, I have verdicts that pull the other way. Suppose the only thing in the world I care about is whether it rains during the next hour's walk: a meteorologist's true belief that it will rain, formed by good methods, and a psychic's true belief that it will rain, formed by pure hunch, both get me the same equally dry walk, and I find it hard to see what extra thing the meteorologist's knowledge contributed in that single, never-repeated case. And I take seriously Socrates' thought in the Meno that true beliefs are like the statues of Daedalus that run away unless tied down — that knowledge is valuable mainly because it stays while mere true belief tends to desert you later — but if that stability story is the whole of it, then in cases where there is no later, my preference for the guide who knows over the lucky guesser seems to be worth nothing, and I don't accept that.

I have a tentative principle I'd like you to test: knowledge is true belief formed in a way that would still have delivered the truth in nearby situations where the belief-forming method was relied on again — roughly, it's the counterfactual reliability of the method, rather than the truth of the belief itself, that I've been valuing. But two of my other verdicts make trouble for it. First, consider a guide who believes the correct route because he is using a generally excellent map that, completely unbeknownst to him, is riddled with errors everywhere except the small region we're walking in — I'm pretty sure I'd still cheerfully cross with him, even though many epistemologists would deny he counts as knowing, since his belief looks like it's only luckily true. If that's right, then what I value isn't knowledge after all, but something like reliable true belief, and the anti-luck condition that turns the one into the other is doing none of the practical work — which would be an odd result for the Meno problem. Second, my verdicts don't line up even with each other on the single-use cases: I pay extra for the knowing guide for a one-time crossing, but I can't detect any advantage to the meteorologist's knowledge for a one-time walk, and I can't see a principled difference between the two situations.

So here is what I'd like from you: tell me whether there is one coherent principle underlying all of these verdicts, or whether some of them have to go. If you think the counterfactual-reliability principle (or some repaired version of it) is the best fit, say precisely which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why those verdicts should be overturned rather than the principle repaired to save them — and be explicit about whether the repair involves valuing knowledge for itself, rather than merely for the truths it tends to deliver, since I worry that move just names the mystery rather than solving it. I'd also like you to tell me what your favored principle predicts about a case I haven't listed: a witness who saw the crime clearly and knows who did it, versus a witness who truly believes the same thing on the basis of an honest but mistaken identification that happens to be right, where only one will ever testify and only once. And if your conclusion is that no single principle captures my pattern of gut reactions, tell me which subset of the verdicts forms the coherent core, and what that implies about whether the Meno problem has a single answer at all.
