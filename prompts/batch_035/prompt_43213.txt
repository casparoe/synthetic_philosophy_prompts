I'm a junior in high school and a member of our school's philosophy club, where we have a tradition that each term one member circulates a short position paper stating their current views on a chosen topic so the rest of the club can try to poke holes in it. This term is my turn, and my topic is whether consciousness could be present in the basic furniture of the physical world, and what follows for ethics if it is. Before I send my paper out, I want a rigorous consistency check, so I'd like you to take on the role of a professional philosopher who works in both philosophy of mind and ethics. Please think carefully and work through my views systematically — first tracing what each of them commits me to, then auditing the set as a whole — rather than just reacting to whichever view sounds strangest.

Here is my position as it stands: (1) I believe panpsychism is probably true — the fundamental constituents of physics, whatever they turn out to be, carry some primitive proto-experiential character — because I cannot see how consciousness could emerge from matter that is wholly non-experiential at the bottom; (2) I am a hedonistic consequentialist, meaning I think the right act is the one that produces the best overall balance of wellbeing over suffering, with every instance of suffering counting equally no matter whose it is; (3) I believe suffering requires a subject — that something can have unpleasant experience only if there is a unified, persisting point of view, involving something like memory and preference, that the experience belongs to, so raw felt quality alone is not enough for moral status; (4) when I am uncertain whether something is sentient, I treat its interests as counting in proportion to my confidence — if I am ten percent confident something can suffer, its suffering counts ten percent as much; and (5) I am confident that consciousness is substrate-independent, so that a sufficiently detailed computer simulation of a human brain would be genuinely conscious and a full moral patient, no matter what hardware it runs on. For calibration, my case verdicts: I am sure an ordinary thermostat has zero moral status, but I would feel real hesitation about, say, destroying an insect colony.

For context, my friends at lunch have already told me that view (1) is bizarre, and I don't need more of that — assume for the purposes of the audit that each of these views is individually defensible and that people really hold them. What I want to know is whether the package is jointly tenable. Which pairs of my views are in genuine tension and how badly, which pairs only look like they conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and, for each genuine tension, what the cheapest fix would be and what that fix would cost me elsewhere in the set? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything as equally problematic, and if you think one of my views is doing hidden work that I'm not acknowledging, identify it — though I'd prefer repairs to amputations, so tell me what the least costly revision would be before anything more drastic.
