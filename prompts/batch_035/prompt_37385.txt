In a philosophy of mind seminar we covered the color phi phenomenon: two stationary dots of different colors, flashed in quick succession at different positions on a screen, are experienced as a single dot moving smoothly and changing color partway along its path — even though nothing was ever presented in that middle position, and observers report seeing the color change before the second dot appears. My teaching assistant explains this by analogy to an Orwellian ministry of information: the brain first registers two discrete flashes, then retroactively rewrites the record, so what I remember having experienced is a fiction backdated into my stream. He leans on this to argue that conscious experience is never in real time at all — it is a post-hoc narrative, like a news report quietly edited after publication. Please audit the analogy. Lay out what maps onto what: what corresponds to the original record, to the act of rewriting it, to the ministry doing the rewriting, and to the readers of the falsified history. Then identify where the mapping breaks — in particular, whether there is anything in the brain playing the role of the pre-editing "true account" that an Orwellian rewrite presupposes, or whether that requirement is exactly what the analogy smuggles in. Consider the rival Stalinesque picture, on which the intermediate motion is fabricated before presentation rather than corrected afterward, and whether the two pictures could ever be empirically distinguished. Finally, assess Dennett's moral: that the dispute presupposes a place and time at which "the actual experience" is fixed, and that the brain contains no such place. Does my TA's conclusion — experience as after-the-fact narrative — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If the Orwellian frame fails, what would a more honest analogy for postdiction look like?
