Over the holidays I got into an argument with my cousin, who's doing a philosophy degree, about Max Black's spheres — the thought experiment where the whole universe contains exactly two qualitatively identical spheres, nothing else, symmetrically placed, so that no fact about that world could ever tell you which one is "this sphere" and which is "that one." She says this is a live problem for the identity of indiscernibles, and I could not disagree more. Leibniz's law is a theorem of first-order logic: if x = y, then x and y share all their properties. And I don't see why it doesn't go the other way too — if two things share every property, then surely they're one thing. I even wrote it out with symbols and it looked fine to me. On that reading Black's universe is just incoherent, like a married bachelor, so Max Black basically committed an error you'd catch in a first logic course.

So here's my question: if the two-sphere world is a contradiction in terms, why has this "counterexample" been taken seriously in philosophy for seventy-plus years? Why doesn't the standard story just say the world can't be coherently described and move on — is there some technicality about how the properties are quantified that I'm botching, or are philosophers using "identity" in some looser sense than the logicians' =? My cousin kept saying something about "qualitative" versus "non-qualitative" properties but honestly it sounded like moving the goalposts. If there's something I'm genuinely missing about the logic here I'd like to know, because right now my plan is to message her that her whole field can't do basic logic, and I suspect that won't go over well.
