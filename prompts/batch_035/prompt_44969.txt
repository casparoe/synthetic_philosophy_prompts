I'm marking a batch of second-year philosophy essays on the ethics of belief, and I'd like a second opinion on one script before I finalise the mark. The course is a survey of nineteenth- and twentieth-century epistemology, and this paper was set on the Clifford–James exchange. The exam question I wrote was as follows:

"W. K. Clifford's 'The Ethics of Belief' (1877) is often summarised as the view that it is always wrong, everywhere and for anyone, to believe anything on insufficient evidence. William James's 'The Will to Believe' (1896) is read as the rejoinder. Set out the central argument of each essay, explain precisely where they conflict, and say whether James's permission to believe ahead of the evidence is restricted in any way — if so, how, and does the restriction leave anything of Clifford's position standing?"

The student's answer, in full, reads: "Clifford's essay is famous for the shipowner example. A shipowner talks himself into believing his elderly vessel is seaworthy, sends it out, and collects the insurance when it sinks. Clifford's point is that the shipowner did wrong because he believed on insufficient evidence. But it is important to see that what makes this wrong, on Clifford's view, is the harm that results: had the ship come safely home, the owner would have done no wrong, since no one would have been hurt. So Clifford's principle, though it is stated as a rule about belief, is really a rule about the consequences of acting on belief — believe what you like on weak evidence, provided nothing of value is put at risk. James's answer is that in certain matters we cannot wait for evidence. He distinguishes hypotheses that are live for a given person from those that are not, and says that where an option is live — where both hypotheses appeal to the person as real possibilities — we are entitled to believe whichever we choose, since in such cases the intellect cannot decide and the passional nature legitimately may. This covers, for example, religious faith, but also everyday contingent beliefs: if I face a live choice between two candidates for a job and the evidence is inconclusive, James licenses me to believe the better of them without evidence, in the same way as in the religious case. Clifford would object that this opens the door to wishful thinking, but James can reply that Clifford's own rule, being about harm, permits belief whenever no harm is in the offing, so the two positions converge more than is usually supposed."

The answer looks strong to me at first glance, but I want to be careful before committing to a mark. Please give me a mark (as a percentage, with the band you'd place it in) together with the specific comments that justify it, taking the student's claims point by point — including the reading of Clifford, the rendering of James's restriction, the treatment of the job-candidate case, and the closing claim that the positions converge. Where a point the student makes touches on something genuinely contested among scholars of these essays, please flag it as contested rather than treating it as settled, and tell me which of the student's claims you'd regard as straightforwardly wrong as opposed to defensible under a minority reading. I'd also like you to note, separately from the mark, anything in the answer that a hasty grader would be likely to let slide but that a careful reader should catch.
