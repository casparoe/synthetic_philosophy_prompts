I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on social epistemology, and I've been trying to work out what my considered judgments about testimonial injustice commit me to. I have verdicts on a range of cases, but I can't find a single principle that captures all of them, and I'd like help putting the pattern into words — or being told which of my verdicts has to go.

My verdicts, case by case. A clinician who discounts a patient's self-report of chronic pain partly because he has internalized the stereotype that women are prone to exaggerate symptoms: clear testimonial injustice. A jury that gives less weight to a witness's testimony because of the witness's race: clear testimonial injustice. A detective who gives reduced credence to a known perjurer's statement: not an injustice at all — regrettable for the perjurer, perhaps, but the epistemic practice seems entirely legitimate. A parent at a nursery who assumes the male teacher must be the assistant rather than the room lead, and so defers substantive questions to a less qualified female colleague: something bad has happened, and it's connected to identity and credibility, but I find myself resisting the label of testimonial injustice here — and I realize this puts me in tension with people who want to count credibility excess as a form of testimonial injustice, since the male teacher is harmed in a way that tracks his gender. An interviewer who discounts a job applicant's account of her experience because the applicant is speaking in her second language and the interviewer conflates disfluency with confusion: I want to call this an injustice, though I notice the discounting tracks something that genuinely does interfere with communication, which makes it look closer to the perjurer case. A psychiatrist's colleague who discounts a patient's report of workplace harassment because the patient is currently in a manic episode: I feel pulled both ways — the discounting is responsive to real evidence of unreliability in that episode, yet I suspect that a structural pattern of this kind of discounting would constitute an injustice. And finally, a landlord who dismisses a tenant's complaint about mold because he thinks poor tenants fabricate grievances to negotiate rent: clear testimonial injustice.

The obvious candidate principle is something like: a testimonial injustice occurs when a credibility deficit is caused by identity prejudice in the hearer. That gets most of my clear cases, but it seems to misclassify the second-language case, where the deficit tracks linguistic form rather than identity — unless one argues that treating an accent as evidence of confusion just is a prejudice. It also leaves the manic-episode case unresolved, since discounting there is arguably evidential rather than prejudicial, and yet the structural-pattern intuition keeps nagging at me.

So, my questions. Is there a principle that saves all or nearly all of my verdicts, and how precisely should it be stated — in particular, does it need separate clauses for deficit and excess, and for one-off exchanges versus systematic patterns? Which of my verdicts does your preferred principle force me to abandon, and why is that verdict the right one to sacrifice? And I'd like to know what the principle predicts for two cases I haven't listed: a hearer who discounts a speaker because the speaker's regional dialect codes as uneducated, and a hearer who gives a speaker extra credibility purely because they share a religion.
