I'm a software engineer with about fifteen years in databases and distributed systems, and no formal philosophy background. A philosopher friend at dinner told me there are serious people -- dialetheists -- who hold that some contradictions are true: not that we mistakenly believe them, but that reality, or at least semantics, actually contains them; and that paraconsistent logicians build formal systems in which a contradiction doesn't entail everything, rejecting the classical principle they call explosion (from a contradiction, anything follows). My honest first reaction was that we in systems deal with exactly this daily, and I want to know whether that reaction is right or just shallow.

Restate the dialetheist position and the paraconsistent program in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can. The materials I have in mind: SQL's NULL and its three-valued logic, where NULL = NULL is neither true nor false and equality stops behaving like equality; IEEE 754's NaN, which is unordered, does not equal itself, and propagates through arithmetic without crashing the computation; the C standard's undefined behavior, where a violation of the program's own assumptions isn't quarantined but licenses the compiler to do literally anything -- a dynamic that looks explosion-like; eventually consistent stores that keep answering reads while replicas disagree, and whose answers I treat as less trustworthy rather than discarding; defensive code that catches an anomalous state, logs it, and keeps serving; a git repository in a conflicted merge state that we resolve rather than treat as fatal.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. Which parts of the philosophers' view map cleanly onto these engineering notions, which had to be distorted to fit (is paraconsistency really fault isolation, for instance, or does that analogy quietly change what inference means?), and which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? The sticking point for me is this: in my world an inconsistent state is always an artifact of representation -- a stale cache, a partial write, a bug -- and the system's job is to detect it, contain it, or eventually repair it; nothing in my vocabulary lets the inconsistency itself be a fact about the world rather than about the state of my records. Is the dialetheist's claim that some contradictions are true precisely the thing my vocabulary cannot express, or is it a confusion my vocabulary dissolves? And where the philosophers' central examples are things like the liar sentence -- a sentence that says of itself that it is false -- I suspect something different is going on than with two disagreeing replicas, because the liar's inconsistency arises from the expressive resources of the language itself rather than from partial failure, and no amount of replication or logging produces it. Is that suspicion part of the residue worth taking seriously, or is it me smuggling my framework's assumptions in and calling it translation? I'd like an honest verdict on whether my instinct -- that this is just graceful degradation of a knowledge base with known-bad inputs -- is correct, or whether it misses the entire point.
