Our book club has been rowing about a novel we just finished, and I got volunteered to bring the dispute here because I'm the only one who refused to pick a side. Quick background so the argument makes sense: it's a slim mid-century novel where the protagonist keeps feeding pigeons on her windowsill, and one of the central things that happens is her younger sister drowns off-page in chapter two. Half the club read the pigeons as a stand-in for the dead sister -- grief that can't be acknowledged directly. Then someone dug up a late-career interview where the author was asked about exactly this and laughed it off, saying she just liked pigeons and people read too much into things. There's also a bit of trivia from an edition history our most pedantic member found: the manuscript apparently had the sister's ghost appear in the final chapter, and the editor cut it for length, with the author going along grumpily. That's the fuel, and the actual fire went like this.

(1) Maya said the interview settles it: a book means what its author meant by it, the author says she didn't mean the pigeons that way, so the grief reading is just wrong, however comforting it is. (2) Dev said that's a fantasy about writing -- once a text is published it's out there, and if a pattern is genuinely structured into the text, readers can find meanings the author never planned; that's just what literature does. (3) Maya said without the author as a check, anything counts as a reading and criticism collapses into astrology -- the only possible check on interpretation is what an actual person could have meant. (4) Dev then played the editor card: on Maya's rule, the published novel means neither what the author meant (she wanted the ghost in) nor what the editor meant (he just wanted it shorter, and almost certainly never meant anything about the pigeons) -- so if neither of the actual intentional agents intended the final text, whose meaning is Maya even tracking? (5) Maya backed off to what she called a compromise: what matters is what an informed reader would reasonably take the author to have meant, and the grief reading passes that test easily -- the author might have forgotten, or been being modest, or genuinely not noticed what her own habits had put on the page -- so she can keep her reading without opening the astrology door. (6) Dev said that compromise is really just his position wearing a hat: all the work is being done by the text and shared conventions, and "intent" is now just a label for whatever survives that process. Then he lobbed one more thing in: apparently in that same interview the author was read a critic's paragraph on the pigeons and said she wished she'd thought of that -- does her blessing, given hours after the fact, change what the book meant before she gave it?

They went three rounds after that and nobody moved an inch. I mostly want a proper referee's report: go through the six moves and say who's right about which specific point (Maya's later moves aren't the same as her first one, and I suspect Dev's editor argument is stronger than his opening one), where each of them is being unfair or talking past the other, and what, if anything, both of them are missing about what "the meaning of a book" might even be. Don't feel you have to split it 50/50 to be diplomatic -- I'd just like to be able to walk back into book club with something coherent.
