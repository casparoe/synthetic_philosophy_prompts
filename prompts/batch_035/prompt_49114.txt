Next month I defend my dissertation's central chapter — a new-ish version of relationism about spacetime — in front of a philosophy of physics seminar whose composition I know well: a former general relativist who thinks Einstein's hole argument settled the whole debate against relationism in 1915; two philosophers who consider themselves orthodox substantivalists in the tradition of the modern Newtonians, fond of saying that only a substantivalist can explain why the water in Newton's bucket climbs the sides; and a cluster of graduate students whose framework for the whole topic is the distinction between absolute objects and dynamical fields, and who will treat any position as naive until it is stated in those terms. My thesis is that everyone has misread the bucket argument: it shows, correctly, that rotational motion is not relative to nearby bodies, but it is silent about ontology — what it actually establishes is a claim about the structure of the laws, namely that the total material history of the universe fixes motion facts, and Newton illicitly promoted a fact about boundary conditions into a fact about the existence of a container. On this view neither Berkeley's fallback to relations to bodies nor Mach's appeal to distant masses is the right response; the right response is that Newton proved the wrong thing for the right reasons. I want a preparation sheet for the question period. First, lay out the objections this specific audience will actually raise, in roughly the order and phrasing they will use — the hole argument as the relativist will put it, the "why does the water climb?" challenge as the substantivalists will sharpen it, and whatever the gauge-minded students will say about my talk of "laws" doing the work a manifold does for them — each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is thin. Second, rank the objections by how much damage they would do if handled badly, and tell me which one I should concede parts of in advance rather than fight. Third, tell me where my position is genuinely soft: I suspect my law-structure story struggles with the rotating-bucket-in-an-otherwise-empty-universe thought experiment, and with whether I can still say general relativity is friendly to my view when its solution space quantifies over manifold points, but I'd rather know the real weak points than defend the ones I've already rehearsed. And include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
