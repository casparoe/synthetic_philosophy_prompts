Our semantics workshop has spent two terms on the linguistics of slurs and has reached a state of total, well-documented equilibrium. On one side are the people who put the offense in the lexicon: the slur has an offensive meaning — a pejorative component in its semantic value, or a presupposition it triggers, or a conventional prohibition on its use — so that even the most carefully quoted, mentioned, or intention-free token carries the taint. On the other side are the people who put the offense in use: the word has ordinary truth-conditional content, and the offense is a matter of pragmatic exploitation, speaker attitudes, or the norms of the speech situation. Each side has its stock cases — unoffended in-group uses, slurs embedded under negation and conditionals, the asymmetry between slurs and their neutral counterparts in extensionally identical sentences — and each side has absorbed the other's cases without moving. Every argument was on the table by the end of the first month; the rest has been elaboration.

My working hypothesis is that the dispute is verbal: it is really about where the semantics/pragmatics border sits, or even about what a semantic theory is for, and the two camps would accept the same full description of slur behavior while still fighting over which compartment each fact goes in. But I can't rule out that there is a real hidden question — perhaps about the metaphysics of conventional meaning, or about whether group offense can be a truth-conditional matter at all — that is doing all the work under a syntactic-sounding label. Diagnose the disagreement: verbal, substantive-but-hidden, or two different projects sharing one word. Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says about the hard cases, and tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a sharper test case, a formal result, an agreed reclassification of the question, or nothing, and why.
