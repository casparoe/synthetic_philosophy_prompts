I work in software, not law or philosophy, but something about American advertising regulation puzzles me. The FTC does not judge an ad sentence by sentence: it evaluates the "net impression," the overall takeaway a reasonable viewer carries away, so a commercial can count as deceptive even when every claim in it is literally true. That must be why advertisers bother with fine print and rapid-voice disclaimers that contradict the main message — everyone involved seems to treat the conveyed impression, not the literal text, as the real message. Yet the same legal system reads contracts almost the opposite way: when a written agreement is unambiguous, courts enforce it as written, and a party who argues that both sides understood something different generally loses. A friend of mine who studied linguistics keeps telling me that what a speaker conveys without saying — the inference she deliberately invites — is as much a part of the message as the words themselves, and that a speaker can even mean something she would deny meaning. What theory of meaning does each of these practices presuppose: the net-impression test in advertising, the fine-print game, and literalism in contract interpretation? Do they add up to one coherent story about what speakers mean, or is the law just running different theories in different rooms? And if the courts adopted my friend's view across the board, what would actually have to change — in how contracts get drafted, and in what advertisers think the fine print is accomplishing?
