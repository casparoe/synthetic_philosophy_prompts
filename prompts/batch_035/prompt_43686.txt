I've been reading a popular book on the ethics of war, and its chapter on combatants summarizes the traditional just-war position and the revisionist attack on it as follows:

"The traditional theory, given its classic statement by Walzer, splits the morality of war into two independent questions: whether a war is just (jus ad bellum) and whether it is fought justly (jus in bello). Its central convention is the moral equality of combatants: soldiers on opposite sides are moral equals, so whatever the justice of the cause, a combatant who obeys the rules of war does no wrong by fighting. Soldiers lose their immunity from attack because they forfeit it: having chosen to become instruments of their state's policy, they have done something that licenses being killed. Civilians, who have made no such choice, keep their immunity, which is why the laws of war are symmetric for soldiers and asymmetric for everyone else. Revisionists such as McMahan then demolished this convention by observing that fighting in an unjust war is a wrongful contribution to a wrong no matter how cleanly one fights, and that the traditional view is therefore committed to the absurd claim that a volunteer in a war of aggression is the moral equal of a conscript resisting invasion. Since McMahan's argument is decisive, most philosophers of war now reject moral equality."

Something struck me as off, though I can't locate it precisely. The author says soldiers forfeit their rights because they chose to do something wrong, yet he also treats the convention as covering conscripts, who don't choose; I can't tell whether both claims can be true, or whether the book is blending two different rationales. And the closing sentence about what most philosophers now believe sounds stronger than anything the chapter actually argued for.

Could you audit the summary part by part? Tell me which claims a defender of the traditional Walzerian view would accept as a fair statement of the position, which are conflations or overstatements a Walzerian would want to correct (for instance, whether the summary slides between losing a right because one poses a threat and losing it because one has done wrong), and whether the final inference — that the traditional view has been demolished and is now generally rejected — follows from the parts of the summary that are accurate. Where the summary is subtly wrong, I'd like to know what the correct statement would look like, since I may need to explain this to my seminar.
