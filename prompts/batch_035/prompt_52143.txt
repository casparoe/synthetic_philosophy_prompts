I am a novelist, and for the past two years I have been at work on a book set in a seventeenth-century plague village — which may explain how I came to host, at my own dinner table, the argument I am about to describe, between my friend M., a historian of the early modern Baltic trade who has spent her career in archives, and my editor R., who is fond of continental theory. I ought to be writing my acknowledgements; instead I keep replaying their exchange, and I would like a careful verdict on it.

M. opened by insisting that what she does and what I do differ in kind, not degree. The historian, she said, is bound by evidence in a way the novelist is not: she cannot invent a letter that survives nowhere, cannot put words in a mouth on no warrant. And since Ranke, the ambition of her discipline has been to show the past as it actually was — a discoverable order of fact, not a literary effect. Whatever else is true, that ambition marks a boundary the novelist never crosses in the other direction.

R. answered that this understates how much of the writing of history is not governed by evidence at all. Out of the enormous mass of surviving traces, the historian selects — and selects, inevitably, with an eye to the same demands of shapeliness, proportion, and closure that govern the novelist. He invoked Hayden White's observation that one and the same set of events can be emplotted as tragedy, comedy, romance, or satire, with nothing in the documents themselves dictating which; the emplotment is added by the historian, not found. So the difference between our trades is one of degree, dressed up as a difference in kind — and a rather self-flattering costume at that.

M. replied that the hard constraints remain decisive. A narrative in which Napoleon wins at Waterloo is not a bold alternative emplotment; it is simply false, and no theory of tropes rescues it. The interpretive freedom R. describes operates only within bounds the novelist never faces, and whatever selection occurs is selection from — and answerable to — a stubborn remainder of fact that pushes back.

R. conceded the bounds but denied they settle anything important. Across the great middle region of what plausibly happened — which is most of any book — the historian's choices of emphasis, causation, and where to stop the story are as unforced as mine; a difference that holds only at the edges is a difference of degree masquerading as one of kind. He added that even the humble chronicle, the bare list of dated events, smuggles significance in through what it includes, so there is no innocent level at which the past is simply given to us.

M.'s last word was an analogy. A mapmaker's choices of scale, projection, and colour are likewise unforced, yet cartography is not thereby a species of landscape painting; a discipline can be interpretive at every step and still constrained at every step by a world it did not make. R. began to answer — something about how a map, unlike the past, can be checked by walking the territory — but the restaurant was closing, and I never heard the end of it.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is sound, and where, if anywhere, it goes wrong. Then tell me who is more right overall — and, since I suspect they are both missing something, whether there is a position that takes in what is true on each side without collapsing into either.
