My closest friend from graduate school is a medievalist, and this past weekend we had one of those arguments that runs from dinner to two in the morning — hers on revisionist historiography of the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution, mine on why I think she has talked herself into something she can't live with. She laid out a case that sounds airtight to me step by step, and I have not been able to find the fault in it, though I am confident there is one. It runs as follows. (1) A period label is a legitimate historical category only if it corresponds to something in the past that constrains how the label can be drawn — a discontinuity that is there to be found, so that some ways of carving are better than others. (2) But the "Middle Ages" was invented by Renaissance humanists who needed a dark foil for their own self-image; the "Renaissance" was consolidated into a period by a nineteenth-century Swiss historian and then elaborated for decades; and the "Scientific Revolution" was assembled into a single episode largely by twentieth-century historians, some of whom later helped dissolve the very picture they had inherited — arguing there was, strictly, no such thing. (3) The boundaries of each label shift with each generation of scholarship — where the Renaissance begins, whether it exists at all outside Italy, whether the twelfth century's intellectual revival makes "medieval" the wrong word for it — and where the boundaries sit tracks the interests of the historians, not anything that happened. (4) What is true of these three labels is true of periodization as such: no period carves itself; someone, later, always draws the line. (5) Therefore no historical period corresponds to anything real in the past; periods are projections of historians onto the material, and the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution are not things that happened but things we made up.

My difficulty is that step (5) does not stop at the three contested periods. By parity of reasoning it eliminates every historical unit whatsoever — the Roman Empire, the French Revolution, the 1840s, even event-sized ones like the Norman Conquest — since all of these likewise have boundaries drawn by later observers, contested edges, and lives that shift with historiographical fashion. I expected her to flinch there, and she did not: she says fine, the past is a seamless flow, every unit we teach is a convenient fiction, and honesty consists in admitting it. I tried the obvious reply — that species and mountains also have observer-relative, shifting boundaries and she presumably does not deny there are mountains — and she parried it in a way I could not answer: mountains and species, she said, are not taught in twelve-week blocks as though the joints were obvious, and nobody rearranges the mountains when a new department is hired. She then produced a wrinkle that I find genuinely destabilizing: the labels, once coined, get into the past's causal stream — reformers and revolutionaries measured themselves against a "middle age" they had been told they were escaping, and the people we now call medieval are in part people reacting to stories later ages told about them. That, she says, does not rescue my realism; it confirms her point, since it shows the category doing all the work is the category, not any joint in the past. She also asked me to name one fact about, say, the year 1480 that constrains where "medieval" ends, and the best I could offer was the spread of printing, which she noted arrives half a century earlier and changes nothing immediately — so by my own lights I could not say whether 1480 is late medieval, proto-Renaissance, or neither.

Tell me which premise to give up and what giving it up costs. If some step equivocates, name the step and the two meanings precisely; I suspect the word "invented" is doing two different jobs somewhere between (2) and (5), but every time I try to pin it down it slips. If the argument is valid and her bullet is really available, tell me whether the bullet can be softened into something livable — a view on which periods are real but constituted by our practices, real for us in a principled way rather than an anything-goes way — or whether that is just the original mistake with better manners. For what it is worth, she would rather surrender (1) than (4); she is happy to call periods real but stipulative, and I cannot see what work "real" is then doing. I would rather keep (1) and attack (4), but I cannot yet say what distinguishes a boundary drawn around something from a boundary drawn on nothing. Be honest about which of us is smuggling.
