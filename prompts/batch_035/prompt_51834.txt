My philosophy of science reading group has been arguing about natural kinds all year and it's gotten a bit stale, so we're doing the adversarial collaboration thing. One camp is basically a realist: they think the sciences really do carve nature at its joints, that chemical elements and, say, gold are paradigms of kinds picked out by the world's structure rather than our conventions. The other camp is a pluralist/conventionalist type: they keep pointing at cases where the joints look suspiciously convenient -- our kind boundaries tracking our projects, our sampling, our ways of carving things up, not nature dictating one true taxonomy back to us. Nobody's moved an inch in months.

Write the joint statement the two of them should have produced by now. Specifically I want:

1. A numbered section of claims both sides can actually sign. Not vacuous stuff like "kinds are used in induction" -- I mean claims with some bite, e.g. about what the jade/jadeite/nephrite case does and doesn't show, or about what happens to a kind like lithium when you discover its isotopes and have to decide whether the kind got refined or replaced. Phrase these so that neither side would feel they'd conceded something they haven't.

2. A table of the exact residual disagreements, with each row worded so the side holding that view would accept the phrasing of their own position (my realist swears the pluralists keep strawmming him as believing there's exactly one correct taxonomy of everything, and my pluralist says the realists caricature her as thinking kinds are arbitrary labels -- the wording has to thread that needle).

3. The cruxes. We suspect at least one of them is this: whether the existence of scientifically successful but mutually incompatible classifications (monophyletic clades vs. ecological/functional groupings in biology, the way "fish" excluding tetrapods embarrasses folk taxonomy while clades don't map onto anything laypeople care about) shows there's no fact of the matter about the joints, or just that there are joints at multiple grains. If you can find other cruxes, like whether the realist needs her kinds to support induction or only explanation, please include them.

4. For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or bit of evidence that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Psychiatric categories (is major depressive disorder a natural kind?) are allowed as one of these if you think it's a good test case, but don't lean entirely on psychiatry -- it's kind of the lazy example at this point.

5. If you conclude that some chunk of their dispute is basically verbal -- e.g. they agree on all the science and are just fighting over the word "real" -- say which chunk and why, so we can stop wasting Thursday evenings on it.

Keep the tone of a document two tired academics would sign, not a manifesto. Also, one of them (the realist) is on record saying that if it turned out that the class of things we call "whales" had been stable for 200 years of biology and then got quietly redrawn for reasons that were partly sociological, he'd have to weaken his view -- feel free to hold him to that.
