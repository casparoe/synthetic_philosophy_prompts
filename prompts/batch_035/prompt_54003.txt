I'm writing a short essay-ish piece for a philosophy of math reading group, on the Wigner theme. The idea I want feedback on is a deflationary take: I want to argue that the "unreasonable effectiveness" is mostly a survivorship illusion. The short version of my notes: we only remember the math that worked. Epicycles were pretty effective for centuries before being discarded; vortex theories of gravity had some math attached; there were loads of attempts to mathematize heredity before genetics that went nowhere, and nobody writes essays about those. So when we look around at physics and see law-like equations everywhere, we're doing something like standing in a museum of successful compressions and concluding that compression is the natural state of things. The fitting is done after the fact, and iteratively — the key was filed to fit the lock, so to speak, over many rounds of filing. On this telling, what looks like pre-established harmony between mind and world is just the residue of a selection process that kept the schemes with predictive grip and quietly buried the rest.

Where it gets trickier, and where I'm honestly not sure, is the part of Wigner's essay about getting more out than we put in — cases where the same piece of math, developed for one purpose, turns out to fit somewhere totally different. His examples are things like the same functions showing up both in quantum mechanics and in contexts far from where they were invented. My proposed answer in the essay is that cross-domain reuse isn't magic either: the same math recurs because the domains share deep structural constraints (things like locality, or the fact that lots of quantities live in three-dimensional space and dilute over surfaces, which pushes you toward inverse-square-type laws), so it's less that math is mysteriously general and more that nature is repeatedly presenting us with variations on a small number of shapes. But I'm not confident this response carries the whole weight — maybe it just re-locates the mystery from "why is math so effective" to "why is nature so repetitive," and a critic could say I've answered the easy half of Wigner and changed the subject on the hard half.

What I'd like from you: comments on whether the deflationary move actually works, and especially on whether the "nature is repetitive, not math magical" step is legitimate or a sleight of hand. If you think the argument fails somewhere, I want you to point at the specific step where you'd expect it to break, and tell me what observation or consideration would change your verdict — like, is there some kind of case (a type of mathematical success, or a type of failed-selection counterexample) that, if shown to me, should make me abandon the deflationary line? Please don't just cheer it on, I'd rather hear the strongest version of the case against me before the group tears into it.
