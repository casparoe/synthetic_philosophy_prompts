I write novels, and because my books get translated, I have spent more hours than I should admit arguing about what a translator actually has to understand — and about whether the machine systems now doing a passable job of it understand anything at all. Hofstadter's old complaint, that these systems translate surfaces rather than thoughts, is one I half believe. The problem is that when I test it against my own reactions to particular translation cases, my gut verdicts don't line up neatly behind it, and I suspect they may not line up behind anything. What I'd like from you is a committed effort to find the principle, if one exists, that captures these verdicts — and I mean committed: take a definite position, defend it, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. Please don't survey the options and leave the choosing to me.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

First, the obvious one. A machine system renders an English idiom about dice in the middle of a card-playing scene literally, so that my character is suddenly rolling actual dice. No understanding — trivially.

Second, the same system, in the same book, renders an obscure regional expression my narrator's grandmother uses perfectly — idiom for idiom, tone and all. I happen to know it does this because somewhere in its training data that expression sits aligned with a fluent equivalent. My verdict is still: no understanding. It matched a string; it didn't know the grandmother was dead and the narrator was remembering her fondly.

Third, a human translator whose prose I find frankly clunky notices that a flat weather description in chapter nine repeats, word for word, a phrase the narrator's late wife used in chapter two, and renders it with a faint, deliberate echo. Understanding — full stop — despite a mediocre product.

Fourth, a devoted fan produces a fluent translation of my newest chapter by machine and then corrects only the names and the worst slips. The result reads well; readers love it. My verdict is that there is no understanding anywhere in the pipeline, and I would rather have the clunky translator from the third case. Notice these two verdicts together: the quality of the output and my sense of understanding are moving in opposite directions, and I don't fully know what to do about that.

Fifth, the round trip. I feed a paragraph of my own prose through the machine from English into Finnish and back again, and it comes home nearly unchanged, the jokes intact. A translator friend of mine claims that surviving the round trip proves comprehension, since noise would otherwise have crept in. I say it proves only that the mapping is smooth along the well-traveled routes, and nothing about what, if anything, the system grasped along the way.

Sixth, a poet I know who doesn't read the source language produced, working from cribs and a bilingual dictionary and much labor, a constrained translation of a short poem of mine — the alliteration and the acrostic both carried over. I want to say that is understanding, secondhand but real, even though she never accessed the meaning directly.

Seventh, the one I keep going back and forth on. By sheer luck of an ambiguity in the target language, the machine produces a pun in a sentence of mine that contained no pun, and the accidental pun happens to sharpen the chapter's central image. My editor calls it the best sentence in the translation. I want to say the machine understood nothing. But I once had a human translator who reached her finest rendering — one that preserved a pun of mine exactly — through a misremembered meaning that happened to land. Same accident, same product, and I want to credit her with understanding while denying it to the machine. I cannot tell whether that instinct is hypocrisy or a clue.

The candidate principle I keep circling is something like this: understanding isn't in the output but in the route — a translator understands a sentence when her rendering is produced because she grasped why it had to be that sentence rather than another. That acquits the third case and convicts the second. But I can't get it to handle the seventh without adding epicycles about what the translator would have done had the sentence been slightly different — counterfactuals about dispositions rather than about what actually happened — and once the principle is about dispositions, I worry the machine may start qualifying too.

So here is what I'm asking for. State the best single principle you can defend, precisely enough that it delivers a verdict on each of the seven cases, and say which clause is doing the work in each. If you think no one principle can hold all my verdicts, say which verdicts have to go and why those rather than others — but still commit to the resulting principle; I am not looking for a taxonomy of candidate views. Then apply it to a case I haven't listed: a system trained on my entire back catalogue, including drafts and letters, that renders a new chapter's private callback correctly, in a way no ordinary translator would have caught. Does your principle give the verdict I would want it to, and if not, is that a problem for the principle or for my wanting? And at the end, tell me honestly where your principle is ugliest — the place where you've saved my verdict at the cost of a clause that looks bolted on, or overturned a verdict I'll miss. I would rather know the weak joint than have a principle presented as more seamless than it is.
