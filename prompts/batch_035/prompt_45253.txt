I'm a fourth-year PhD student working on formal epistemology, and this term I'm co-designing the final for our department's graduate survey on rational choice and Bayesian reasoning. The cohort has already sat exams that reward recitation, so I want at least one question that punishes students who merely know the standard talking points. I'd like the question to be about the diachronic Dutch book argument for conditionalization — specifically the Teller–Lewis version and its descendants — but I want it framed so that a student cannot pass by simply re-deriving the book against an agent who updates by some rule other than conditionalization. The crux I want the question to target is the epistemic asymmetry between synchronic and diachronic arguments: in the synchronic case, the bookie needs no information the agent lacks, whereas the diachronic bookie must wait to see which proposition the agent learns and then offer the second bet, which suggests the agent's losses depend on the bookie's possessing knowledge the agent couldn't have had. A strong answer should say whether this asymmetry actually weakens the argument, and if so, how much.

I'd also like the question to include some seeding material so that students are pushed beyond the literature they've memorized. In particular, I want to pose them a variant that occurred to me while drafting: suppose we describe an agent not by its current credences alone but by its credences together with a preannounced updating policy (e.g., "I will conditionalize on members of some partition, and on any partition element I will conform to whatever function of my prior and that element I declared in advance"), and we then say the agent is irrational only if a bookie can construct a sequence of bets such that the agent's *policy-relative ex ante* balance is negative no matter what it learns. Students should evaluate whether this reformulation dissolves the epistemic asymmetry objection, whether it quietly smuggles in a stronger commitment than the synchronic argument licenses, and whether it collapses into something closer to an expected-utility or epistemic-accuracy justification — in which case the question is whether the Dutch book apparatus is doing any real work or is just heuristic dressing.

Please write the exam question as it would actually appear (suitable for a 90-minute answer, 25 marks), a model answer of roughly 700–900 words, and a marking scheme. For the marking scheme, I'd like five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, and for each point a one-line note on the most common way candidates get it wrong — e.g., conflating a sure loss with a merely possible one, or assuming that because the bookie must know what was learned, the argument fails outright. Please also indicate, either in the marking scheme or after it, one specific move that would distinguish an A answer from a B answer, so my co-examiners and I grade consistently when we split the scripts between us. You can assume the students have encountered the synchronic Dutch book theorem via Ramsey and de Finetti, the Teller–Lewis diachronic argument, and at least one paper pressing the "betting isn't believing" objection, but you should not expect them to know anything beyond that, and the question should be self-contained enough that a bright student who missed a few sessions could still attempt it.
