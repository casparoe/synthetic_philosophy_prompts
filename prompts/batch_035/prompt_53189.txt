I have been reading the rationalist blogs and their comment threads for the better part of fifteen years, and somewhere in that time I picked up the habit of watching my own cognition at work. There is a pattern I have noticed often enough that I want a name for it, and I cannot find one. Let me describe it and ask whether philosophy or psychology has already isolated it; if not, I want you to carve it and coin a term properly, and I will not be satisfied with a bare label.

The pattern, as plainly as I can put it: a capacity that runs well without words is degraded by an act of putting it into words — not by distraction, and not because the words are inaccurate, but by the articulation itself, aimed at the capacity while it is in use.

Instances, from different corners of life:

1. Motor. An experienced skier asked to narrate each movement as she makes it becomes clumsy within a run. Nothing about the slope or the skier changed; the asking did.
2. Memory. I once read that witnesses asked to describe a face they had glimpsed do worse at later recognizing that face than witnesses who were never asked to describe it. The description was not wrong; it displaced something.
3. Language. A native speaker asked to state the rule for where a pronoun may go in a sentence will pause, produce a wavering formulation, and then start to stumble over sentences she previously produced without effort.
4. Social. Someone whose conversation is naturally easy, asked mid-evening to enumerate what she does that makes it work, goes stiff for the rest of the evening — and not merely from embarrassment, since the stiffness outlasts it.

I know the old story of the centipede who, asked how it coordinates its hundred legs, is thereby rendered unable to walk; I have seen it retold on forums, and I have heard people gesture at the general area with "paralysis by analysis." But I do not think any of these names the thing, because each seems to pick out one instance-type, and the memory case in particular is not about attention to a motor routine — or is it? That is what I cannot tell. Nor do I want the stock System 1 / System 2 talk: plenty of slow, verbal interventions improve a wordless capacity (a coach saying "keep your weight forward" at the right moment; a mnemonic that sharpens a memory rather than flattening it), so the phenomenon cannot simply be that the deliberate system touched it. Something more specific is going on.

What I am asking for:

- The nearest existing concepts, and for each, exactly where it fails to cover the pattern. I expect candidates from several literatures — philosophy (I am aware Ryle drew a line between knowing-how and knowing-that; the thing I am pointing at looks like a cost of converting the first into the second while the first is in use, but tell me whether that framing is right rather than taking it from me), skill-acquisition psychology, and the eyewitness-memory literature, if the case in (2) corresponds to something known there.
- Your own carving, if nothing established covers it: state the common factor across the four cases. Is it (a) attention directed at the components of a routine that normally runs whole; (b) verbalization as such; (c) any explicit representation of a capacity, verbal or not; or (d) the direction of fit — the words being generated from the capacity rather than the capacity being guided by the words? These carve differently; choose.
- Boundary cases that would test your carving, with your honest predictions. For instance: does your account imply that an expert narrating someone else's performance is unaffected? That rhythmic counting aloud, which sometimes helps, must fail? That the memory case turns out, on inspection, to run on a different mechanism from the motor cases — in which case my pattern is two patterns wearing one coat, and I would want to know that too.

Finally, a request about your own confidence. Tell me what would change your mind: if you find there is a single phenomenon here, name the observation or disanalogy that would most plausibly split it in two or force a different carving; if you find there is none, say what would have to be true of the boundary cases for you to reverse that. And wherever your identification of the nearest existing concept rests on recollection of a literature rather than on reasoning you can show me, flag it as such — I intend to use this term, if it survives, in further arguments, and I want to know which parts of your answer are load-bearing on memory.
