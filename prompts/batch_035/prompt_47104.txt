I've been reading a popular introduction to ethics written by a science journalist (the sort of book that spends a chapter on each big theory), and its chapter on the critique of utilitarianism summarises Bernard Williams's integrity objection as follows:

Bernard Williams, working with J. J. C. Smart, mounted the classic attack on utilitarianism in the 1970s. The heart of it is the integrity objection. Utilitarianism, Williams argued, is committed to negative responsibility: you are just as responsible for what you fail to prevent as for what you actively bring about, so no distinction between doing and allowing survives. That means your life is never really yours — you must always be prepared to drop your deepest projects the moment the general welfare requires it, and a theory that makes integrity impossible cannot be a theory of how human beings should live. Williams illustrated this with his case of Jim and the Indians, where his point was that Jim obviously must refuse to shoot, something no utilitarian calculation could deliver. The same idea is behind his famous phrase 'one thought too many': the utilitarian who rescues his drowning wife has to pause first to check that rescuing her, rather than a stranger, maximises expected utility, and that pause is precisely the extra thought that betrays a lack of love. Together these arguments refuted utilitarianism, and Williams concluded that we should replace it with a deontological ethics built on agent-centred constraints.
