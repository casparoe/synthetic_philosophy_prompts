I'm a philosophy PhD student, and a friend who works in computer vision has invited me to give the fifty-minute talk at her lab's weekly reading group on Molyneux's problem — the newly sighted man, the cube and the sphere, and which of them he could come to know by sight alone. The audience is engineers and doctoral students who know cameras, calibration, and sensor fusion inside out and have had no philosophy since school. Don't explain Molyneux's problem to me; plan how I should explain it to them.

One worry, and you should let it shape the plan: I suspect this audience will hear the question as trivially answered in the affirmative, because in their world matching a radar return to a camera frame is just calibration — something you do once, given a known correlation between the two signals. But calibration presupposes that the correlation was established somewhere, by someone, over time — and that presupposition is precisely what is at issue for Molyneux's man. I want the plan to make that visible, but I don't know where in the sequence it belongs or how to do it without a detour through what empiricist and rationalist even mean here.

Organize the plan under these headings, in this order:

Sequence — what has to be established before what, and why that order; I imagine something has to secure what knowing the cube by touch amounts to before the question is even well-posed for them, but I could be wrong (about 300 words).

The carrying example — whether plain cube-and-sphere should carry the session or some variant (perhaps something that blocks the shortcut of solving it by detecting image features like corners), and what the chosen case must have and must lack (about 150 words).

Misconceptions — the errors this audience specifically will bring or form, where each will strike in the sequence, and what defuses each at that point. My predictions: that sensor fusion settles it; that the modern cases of patients given sight late in life after congenital cataracts answer it empirically, one way or the messy other; that it is secretly a question about cortical plasticity; that the old reports of the newly sighted seeing only a blur decide it; and that Locke's confident negative answer, and Molyneux's own, settles anything. If you can predict others specific to people who build perception systems, add them (about 350 words).

The check — one question I can put to the group at the end, and what a good answer to it sounds like against a weak one. I want the distinction between getting it right on the very first trial and being able to learn the matching quickly to be the spine of the talk, so the check should test that — though tell me honestly if that distinction cannot bear that weight (about 100 words).

Left out — what I should openly decline to adjudicate in fifty minutes (I assume the empiricism-versus-rationalism scorecard is one such thing), and how to say so in one sentence without seeming to dodge (about 100 words).
