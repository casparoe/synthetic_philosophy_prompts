I'm taking a philosophy of biology course, and the standing debate between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function has begun to look strangely motionless to me, so I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on which side wins. Here is how the debate looks from my seminar table. The etiological (or selected-effect) account says the function of a trait is the effect of past tokens of that trait that explains why current tokens are there — the heart's function is pumping because pumping is what hearts were selected for. The causal-role account says a function of a part is whatever contribution that part makes to some capacity of the containing system, as revealed by functional decomposition — the heart contributes pumping to the organism's capacity for circulation. My professor points to a familiar set of stalemate cases. Causal-role accounts seem to license absurd verdicts like the heart's function being to make thumping sounds, since that is a real contribution to the doctor's capacity to diagnose you — unless you insist, ad hoc, on which system capacities matter. Etiological accounts seem to deny functions to any trait fixed by drift, even one now doing indispensable work, and to struggle with recent exaptations that have not yet been shaped by selection; Millikan's sperm example looms over all of this, since nearly every sperm fails at fertilization and yet we want to say that is exactly what their function is. Meanwhile the standard escape route is pluralism: both notions are legitimate, they answer different questions, and we should just use different words. My professor suggests the field has largely taken that exit.

My hypothesis, which I want you to evaluate rather than assume: pluralism here is not progress but a polite refusal to answer the question that made the debate matter — malfunction. In medicine we want to say a diseased organ is failing to perform its function, but the causal-role notion seems to make any non-contribution a malfunction (so a healthy postmenopausal ovary is malfunctioning), while the etiological notion makes malfunction attributions hostage to an evolutionary history we often cannot reconstruct, which is acutely embarrassing in psychiatry. So is this whole debate verbal — two explanatory projects (why is this trait here? vs. how does this system work?) fighting over one word — or is there a genuine substantive disagreement about what function attributions commit us to, with the medical cases as the residue pluralism cannot absorb? Could any specific thing count as progress — a decisive case both accounts must treat as data, a formal distinction, a study of how working biologists actually use the word — or is the impasse a sign the question is ill-posed? Please lead with your bottom-line diagnosis in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; do not open with a preamble or restate my question. As you go, indicate how confident you are in each major claim, and explicitly single out the point you are least sure of.
