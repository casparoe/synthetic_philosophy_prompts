I'm a PhD student in political theory, presenting a dissertation chapter next week to a workshop that mixes just war theorists (mostly Walzerians), two international lawyers, and a quantitative IR scholar who coded the Libya intervention — including one senior colleague who defends Kosovo 1999 and another who thinks Iraq 2003 proved humanitarian justifications are always cover for strategic ones. My thesis: the criteria for humanitarian intervention should be codified as a two-prong threshold. The first prong is the familiar one about scale and urgency of harm; the second is my own addition — any intervention authorized on humanitarian grounds must, within five years, submit to a sovereignty-restoration referendum in the target state, monitored by a body no permanent Security Council member can veto. I claim this second prong does the work the vague "right intention" criterion is supposed to do: it would have stopped the Libya slide from civilian protection into regime change, because the interveners would have to stake their own legitimacy on letting the target population pass judgment on them. Prepare me for the question period as a numbered list of exactly five objections, ordered by how much damage they could do, each item exactly two sentences: first the objection in the voice of the particular workshop member most likely to raise it, then my best reply together with the concession I should make where the reply runs thin. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and no preamble or closing remarks — just the five items.
