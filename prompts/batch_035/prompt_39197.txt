My colleague is a die-hard precise Bayesian — for her, a rational agent's credences are always sharp numbers, and anything else is either confusion about your own mind or a refusal to do the work. I've drifted the other way over the last few years: I now think an agent can be fully rational while having only a set of probability functions, with no fact of the matter about which single one is "really" her credence. After arguing past each other for what feels like forever (mostly around Ellsberg-type cases, but also about what evidence does to your views over time), we've agreed to do a proper adversarial collaboration and write it up together. Draft the joint statement we should be able to sign at the end of it.

Here's what I want in the document. First, the claims both of us could put our names to — about the Ellsberg choices themselves (which of them people actually make, and which of the "axiom violations" we'd both agree are violations of something), about what each framework can represent, and about which critiques of the other side are just cheap shots. She should concede that "you must have some sharp number in there, you just can't find it" isn't an argument, and I should concede that imprecise views carry a real burden about sequential decision-making — including the embarassing thing where imprecise theorists themselves recommend flipping a coin to choose between incomparable options. Second, the residual disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side would actually accept — we got burned last time we tried this when she quietly slipped "rational" into a sentence I couldn't sign. Third, the cruxes. I suspect one is whether dilation (your interval getting wider as you learn more) is a bug or a feature, and another is whether the coin-flip move shows that incomparability is unstable under reflection. For each crux, give the case or argument we'd both agree bears on it, with each of our predictions written down in advance — she'll presumably say the two-urn switching behavior is absorbable into sharp probabilities plus risk attitudes, I'll say the people who switch aren't just risk averse, and we should be able to specify beforehand what data would favor which of us.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table, then the cruxes with predictions. And if you think a chunk of our dispute is verbal rather than genuine — maybe we don't even mean the same thing by "credence" — say which part and why. We'd both rather find that out now than in year three. Also, keep it readable for the reading group that's going to referee the collaboration; no more than about 1200 words.
