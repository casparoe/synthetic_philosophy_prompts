I am a novelist by trade, and my work depends on a strange asymmetry I have never fully trusted: I spend my days inventing minds whose self-understanding is systematically mistaken, and my nights occasionally doing the same without my consent. Zhuangzi's butterfly passage has lately begun to feel less like ancient philosophy to me and more like a description of my profession. After a long stretch at the desk I sometimes cannot immediately say whether a scene I remember drafting was drafted or dreamed — and this has made me want to be precise about what, exactly, the butterfly question threatens.

Here is the proposition I want your credence on: that Zhuangzi, upon waking from his dream of being a butterfly, is genuinely unable to know whether he is a man who dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is a man — not merely unable to prove the matter beyond doubt, but lacking knowledge in the sense that would actually matter. Please give me a number between 0 and 1, and decompose it into the sub-questions it rests on. At minimum I would want rough probabilities for each of the following, with a line of justification apiece: (i) that dreaming and waking experience are phenomenologically indistinguishable in the strong way the parity argument needs — my own experience suggests dreams are usually detectable, by the blandness of their detail, their failure of stable memory, their inability to sustain close attention to a page, though perhaps that only shows that unremarkable dreams are detectable, and I do not know how to weight that; (ii) that even granted indistinguishability, knowledge would be absent — that is, whether knowledge here requires the kind of internal warrant Zhuangzi would need to distinguish his two situations from the inside, rather than the kind of reliable connection to the world that waking life affords and dreams do not; (iii) that the passage is even in the business of skepticism, rather than gesturing at something else — the closing remark about the transformation between things, which some readers take as dissolving the man/butterfly distinction rather than expressing doubt on either side of it. Please add whatever further terms you think belong in the decomposition — perhaps something about whether the question is well-posed at all if dreams are not experiences in the ordinary sense, as Norman Malcolm and, differently, Dennett once argued, in which case what it is like to be a butterfly in a dream may have no determinate answer.

Then combine the terms into your final credence, tell me which single term the number is most sensitive to, and identify the evidence or argument that would move it most. I want to know whether my unease is really about the epistemology of dreaming, or about what the text itself is doing — and I would rather have your weak points marked than your conclusion asserted.
