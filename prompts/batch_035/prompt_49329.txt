I have been asked to grade a philosophy essay for the first time in my life, and I would appreciate a careful second opinion on one of the scripts.

By way of background: I spent the better part of four decades in vision science, first working on the primate retina and later on human psychophysics. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy in earnest, and this term I am teaching an evening course on the philosophy of perception in my university's continuing education programme. My students are adults of varying backgrounds, a few with prior philosophy training. I set a take-home midterm essay worth 40% of the course grade, to be marked out of 25. Because I could not resist, I built the question around an argument from my own working life that I do not believe appears in the literature under any name; I have been calling it the argument from equivalence classes. Here is the question as the students received it:

In lecture I offered the following argument. (1) The retinal image is a projection, and projections are many-to-one: indefinitely many arrangements of the world can produce one and the same pattern of light at the eye. A tilted round coin, a properly rotated elliptical disc, a suitably bent wire — each can present the same two-dimensional projection. Call each such set of arrangements an equivalence class. (2) Moreover, the members of such a class need not be arrangements of the world at all: the same pattern of stimulation can be produced by direct stimulation of the retina, with no distal object involved whatsoever. (3) The subject's discrimination is limited by the projection and by the physiology: from the standpoint of the experience itself, the members of an equivalence class cannot be told apart. (4) What is common to every situation in the class is therefore not a relation to any particular object, since the object varies, or is absent, while the stimulation holds constant. (5) So the proper object of awareness — as the traditional sense-datum theorist concluded — is something present in all cases other than the physical object. The laboratory, it seems, rediscovers by measurement what the classic arguments for sense data tried to establish from the armchair.

Question: Does this argument give the disjunctivist anything they must deny? In your answer, explain precisely what the disjunctivist's denial amounts to, and say whether the argument from equivalence classes is closer in structure to the argument from illusion or to the argument from hallucination, and why.

And here, reproduced in full, is the answer submitted by one of my students:

The argument does give the disjunctivist something to deny, though perhaps less than the sense-datum theorist would like.

First, what the disjunctivist denies. The disjunctivist does not deny that a hallucination can be subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical perception; Hinton already conceded that both cases can appear the same from the inside. What is denied is that the two experiences share a common mental kind. In the good case, the mind-independent object is itself a constituent of the experience; in the bad case there is no such object, so the experience must be constituted some other way. This is a rejection of what H. H. Price called the highest common factor conception of experience — the view that veridical and hallucinatory experiences share an underlying mental state, of which the sense-datum is one candidate.

Second, the argument itself. Premises (1) through (3) are physiological facts about the retinal projection and the limits of discrimination, and the disjunctivist has no reason to quarrel with them: the same pattern of light at the eye can arise from a tilted round coin, a rotated elliptical disc, or, as the question notes, direct stimulation of the retina with no object at all. But these premises concern the proximal stimulus, and the proximal stimulus is not a mental kind. That two situations produce the same stimulation shows only that the subject cannot discriminate between them, which the disjunctivist freely grants. The pressure therefore falls on the step from (3) to (5): whether what is common across the equivalence class must be something other than an object-involving perception.

Third, whether the argument is closer to the argument from illusion or to the argument from hallucination. These are best seen as one argument under two names, since in each something appears other than it is and the conclusion is that we are directly aware of something other than the physical object. If there is a difference between them, it lies only in whether an object is present at all. Since illusion is the clearer and more familiar case of this, disjunctivism was developed chiefly as a reply to the argument from illusion, and the disjunctivist holds that in an illusion one is not perceiving the physical object at all, but only an appearance of it.

Fourth, whether the argument succeeds. Here the disjunctivist runs into trouble. Since the disjunctivist denies that veridical and hallucinatory experiences have anything in common at the mental level, the laboratory findings are a threat: if the visual system's responses to two members of an equivalence class are identical right down to discrimination behaviour, then the experiences must be identical as well, and there is a common mental state after all. The psychophysical data therefore favour intentionalism, on which experience represents the world and can misrepresent it, over disjunctivism.

Finally, it should be said that disjunctivism does not deny that hallucinations have phenomenal character, or that they are genuinely perceptual experiences. The bad case is still an experience; it is simply not one with a mind-independent object as a constituent. The disjunctivist's claim concerns what an experience is, not how it feels.

What I would like from you is a mark out of 25 together with the specific comments that justify it. Please go through the answer point by point, identifying each passage that deserves credit and each that should cost marks, and explain for the latter exactly what the mistake is — whether it is a conflation, a misattribution, or an inferential step that does not go through — so that the comments could be returned to the student as actionable feedback rather than a general impression. I would also be grateful for a note on how much weight each error should carry relative to the others, since some passages read fluently and confidently while being, I suspect, subtly off; and I would like your view on whether the passages in the second and fourth sections are consistent with one another, or whether the student concedes something earlier that the later conclusion depends on denying. Finally, please tell me whether the student has genuinely answered the comparison I asked for in the final part of the question, or has found a way to avoid committing to an answer.
