For some years I belonged to a political group that argued constantly, and what strikes me now, looking back, is that we quoted our opponents every single day — their words were all around us — yet nothing ever landed, because we had been taught exactly how to read them: as status moves, as bad faith, as the output of people who wanted us to lose. Elsewhere I have seen communities that simply never encounter disagreement at all. These feel to me like different problems, possibly needing different remedies, and before I read anything scholarly on the matter I want to get clear on what a proper account of them has to accomplish. So please answer in British English, and set out the desiderata for a theory of these two phenomena — call them, for now, the sealed group and the sheltered group — that is, the requirements such a theory must meet. Among other things, I would want it to cover: the cases it must classify correctly (including groups that turn out to be right, and groups whose distrust of outsiders is earned); the distinctions it cannot collapse (being cut off from contrary voices versus being trained to distrust them; a feature of the group as a whole versus a feature of individual members); and the constraints of detectability it faces — I am especially interested in whether the theory can demand a criterion usable by people who do not already agree about which side is mistaken, and whether that demand can be met alongside the demand that the theory tell the harmful cases apart from the benign ones, since if the only test for a harmful case is that the group's beliefs are false, the theory seems to tell me nothing in precisely the situations where I need it. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it; say which requirements you regard as non-negotiable and which are matters of taste; and flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want to see. At the end, take a few lines only to test two rough pictures against the list: that the problem is simply lack of exposure, and that it is a matter of trust rather than of access. Keep the weight on the requirements themselves rather than on the final test.
