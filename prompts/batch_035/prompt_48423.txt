I teach political philosophy, and every few years this puts me in front of a seminar on Weber's Protestant ethic thesis and its critics. Over several rounds of teaching it I have come to suspect that the seemingly diverse objections to that thesis — and to cultural explanations generally — are mostly instances of one recurring argumentative pattern, and I would like to know whether that pattern has been identified, named, and analyzed as such. Let me describe it concretely.

Someone proposes a cultural explanation: an ascetic religious doctrine, spread through particular churches and practices of child-rearing, made certain populations prone to disciplined labor and deferred consumption, and this contributed causally to the emergence of capitalism. A critic then demands to know how the doctrine is supposed to do any causal work. At this point two responses are available, and the critic treats both as concessions. The defender can say that the culture works by shaping individual people — instilling habits, anxieties about salvation, a certain sense of time — in which case the critic replies that the genuine causes are those individual psychological states and their material triggers, and that "the culture" is merely a label for their distribution; nothing cultural remains over and above that to be a cause. Or the defender can insist that culture is a cause in its own right, something like a force operating at the level of the group, in which case the critic charges mysticism: a group mind, an occult entity, Durkheim run amok. Either the explanation dissolves into individual-level causes or it floats free of causes altogether. I have watched this dilemma be run against Weber, against explanations of development that cite trust or "national culture," and against claims that norms of honor explain patterns of violence; the subject matter changes, the pincer does not.

One reason I think the pattern must have a literature is that it has a structural twin I do know: in philosophy of mind, Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument is often read as forcing exactly this choice between reduction and causal idleness for the mental. What I do not know is whether the analog for the social case — culture, or social structure, standing to individual actions as the mental stands to the physical — has its own name, its own canonical statements, and its own standard lines of response, or whether philosophers of social science have simply proceeded as if the two horns exhaust the options. I am aware that Durkheim is the traditional patron of the second horn and methodological individualism of the first, but pointing at the two camps is not the same as stating and evaluating the dilemma itself.

So I have several requests, and a bare label will not satisfy me. First: does this pincer have an established name, and if so, who stated it in its sharpest form? Second: what are the nearest neighbors, and how do they differ from the thing as I have described it — methodological individualism as a doctrine, Durkheim's sui generis social facts, the demand for mechanisms in explanation, and whatever the received view is on whether Kim's argument transfers to social entities? In particular, is the exclusion analogy tight, or do social causes differ from mental ones in ways that blunt the pincer (for instance, because individuals can be causally affected by aggregate facts they each help constitute)? Third: what is the best currently available way out — some notion of causal relevance at the level of populations, contrastive or difference-making explanation, or something else — and what does it cost? And fourth, if the pattern has no settled name, I would like you to coin one, define it precisely, and tell me where its edges are: for example, whether explanations citing institutions face the same dilemma or somehow escape it, and whether the pincer applies only to values and norms or to any population-level fact invoked as a cause.
