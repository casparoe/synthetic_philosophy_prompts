I am drafting a short essay for a bioethics course on the definition of death, and I would like critical feedback on the argument before I revise it. The working title is "Dead Enough: Why Permanence, Not Irreversibility, Should Define Death," and my thesis is that death should be defined as the permanent cessation of circulation and respiration rather than the irreversible loss of whole-brain function. Below are my notes; the finished essay will run about 1,500 words.

1. Historical framing: The 1968 Harvard ad hoc committee redefined death in terms of brain function just as mechanical ventilation made the old bedside tests unreliable and organ transplantation created pressure on the "dead donor rule." My claim: the whole-brain criterion was reverse-engineered from transplant needs, not derived from a coherent account of what death is.

2. The centerpiece of my argument: D. Alan Shewmon's collection of cases in which brain-dead patients were maintained on support for months — including a brain-dead mother whose pregnancy was carried to term and a patient who survived over a decade. If the brain were truly the "integrator" of the organism, these bodies should have disintegrated; instead, wounds healed, homeostasis persisted, and in one case gestation continued. This suggests somatic integration is a decentralized property of tissues, and the whole-brain criterion rests on a false neuroessentialism about organisms.

3. The conceptual pivot: Donation after circulatory death (DCD) protocols already concede my point. In controlled DCD, organs are removed after a few minutes of asystole — commonly two to five — on the grounds that circulation has stopped *permanently* (no one will attempt reversal) rather than *irreversibly* (reversal is impossible). So the actual practice defines death partly in terms of whether anyone intends to resuscitate, which means death as clinically determined is already indexed to context. I argue we should own this rather than hide it.

4. Normative payoff: We should abandon the pretense that brain death is death, adopt a permanent-circulation criterion, and reform organ procurement openly — justifying it by valid consent and the prohibition of harm to a patient, not by the increasingly strained dead donor rule.

5. Bullet-biting: On my view, patients in permanent vegetative states and anencephalic infants are alive, full stop — including the one Jahi McMath's family fought to keep "alive" in California after she was declared brain dead. Whether that life has moral significance is a separate ethical question that a definition of death should not be asked to settle.

Please respond with exactly five numbered comments on these notes — no introduction, no conclusion, and nothing else. Each comment should be a sentence or two and should concern a distinct point, for example: whether my history in note 1 is doing argumentative work or merely rhetoric, whether the Shewmon cases in note 2 actually support a circulatory criterion rather than merely undermining the brain criterion, whether the permanence-versus-irreversibility move in note 3 is a principled definition or an unprincipled concession, whether abandoning the dead donor rule in note 4 opens the door to harvesting organs from patients who are alive-but-irreversibly-losing-consciousness, or whether my bullet-biting in note 5 is consistent with the rest of the argument. I especially welcome comments identifying a strong objection I have not anticipated.
