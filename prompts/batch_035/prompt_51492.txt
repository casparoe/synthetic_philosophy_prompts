I'm on the small working group of our defence policy review (mid-sized European country, we've been neutral since the 50s and plan to stay that way, at least for now), and I've been tasked with writing up the position paper on force structure before the autumn session. The question on the table: our all-volunteer force is stuck around 60% of its recruitment target, retention is bad, and there's a motion to bring back conscription, roughly along the lines of what the Swiss do (universal service, then a militia reserve with periodic refreshers) versus something more like the Finnish model of a large trained reserve under a small active core versus doubling down on a smaller professional force with better pay and drones. The pro-conscription side on the committee keeps invoking armed neutrality -- their line is that neutrality only means anything if you can credibly deter an invasion without allies, and that a volunteer force of a certain size just can't, whereas an entire society that's been through basic training can. The other side says conscription is illiberal, insanely expensive per useful soldier, and that a neutral country's real deterrent is geography and GDP, not rifle-toting accountants.

I want to write the actual recommendation memo, not just a talking-points sheet. It needs a clear bottom line -- which of the three models, or a hybrid -- with the reasoning laid out so my colleagues who disagree could at least respond to it rather than just re-asserting their intuitions. Please take the liberal objection to conscription seriously as a philosophical position (forced labor, years of a young person's life, the state claiming a property right in bodies -- not just as a political inconvenience), but also take seriously the republican/civic line that a shared obligation to defend the polity is what keeps an army from becoming either a mercenary caste with no stake in the country or a proto-political force with too much of one. If it helps, my own hunch is that a lot of this fight is really about what our neutrality is *for* -- some of us think of it as a moral identity, some as a temporary convenience, and the conscription question answers itself once you've settled that -- but I'd want the memo to argue that out rather than assume it.

A couple of constraints to bake in: our demographics are rough (aging fast, shrinking cohorts of 18-year-olds), the treasury has capped defence spending, and there's a vocal pacifist minority with a constitutional right to conscientious objection, so the memo has to say something about alternative service too. Also, if you think the honest answer is "the question is underdetermined by the values and depends on empirical stuff nobody knows" (e.g. whether conscripts or long-serving professionals actually fight better on day one of an invasion), say that plainly and tell us what evidence we'd need to collect over the next five years to make this a real decision rather than a vibes vote. Don't be shy about recommending the unpopular option if you can defend it -- I'd rather circulate something half the committee hates than something nobody reads.
