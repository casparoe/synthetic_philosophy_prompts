Our graduate seminar in political philosophy opens each term with a small ritual: every member circulates a one-page statement of their settled convictions, and the first session is spent tearing into each other's statements, so any incoherence in mine will be found and used in front of everyone. Before I send mine around, I want a private audit of whether it holds together as a package. Here is what I've committed to: (a) the self is to a large degree socially constituted — the values I hold, the projects that feel like mine, even my sense of what makes a life worth living were formed by the communities and traditions I happened to be born into, and there is no standpoint outside all of them from which I could survey the whole set at once; (b) individuals nevertheless retain a genuine capacity for rational self-revision — sometimes one ought to step back, scrutinize an inherited commitment, and drop it, as when someone leaves the religion they were raised in, and this capacity matters politically; (c) the state should remain neutral among competing conceptions of the good, and its coercive laws must be justifiable to all reasonable citizens by public reasons that no citizen could reasonably reject as a basis for shared political life; (d) constitutional rights can properly override what a majority wants, even when that majority is expressing its own considered understanding of the common good; (e) the authority of democratic decisions is not merely instrumental — a law's being the outcome of a fair collective process is part of what obliges citizens to comply with it; and (f) laws inevitably express who a political community takes itself to be, this expressive dimension is a legitimate and important part of politics, and I am skeptical that communal identity can be sealed off from lawmaking in the way strict neutrality demands.

In passing conversations I've been told that (a) makes me a communitarian while (c) and (d) are straightforwardly liberal, and that I can't have both — something about a constituted self being unable to perform the kind of wholesale reflection (b) presupposes, or about neutrality requiring a detached, unencumbered view of the person. I've also heard the mirror-image complaint: that my attachment to (c) and (d) commits me to a thinner theory of the person than (a) permits, and that (f) quietly abandons the neutrality I officially defend in (c). I don't know which of these lines of attack, if any, actually lands, and I would rather not concede anything in the seminar that I needn't. So please examine my commitments pair by pair, but not as a flat catalogue of quarrels: separate the clashes that are genuine from those that only look like trouble, and put the genuine ones in order of how much damage they do to the package as a whole. For each genuine clash, tell me what it would cost to resolve it on either side — what I'd be surrendering, and what else in my set would become harder to defend afterward. Finish by telling me which single adjustment would leave the whole position in its most defensible shape, and be candid about its price; if the honest answer is that two commitments have to go rather than one, say that instead of forcing a tidy resolution.
