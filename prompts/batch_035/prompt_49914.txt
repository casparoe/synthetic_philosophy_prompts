Every Thanksgiving my brother-in-law (career Army, two tours) and my old college roommate (philosophy PhD, works on ethics now) end up in some version of the same argument, and this year I finally wrote down how it went so I could ask someone to referee it. The trigger was a movie scene: a young conscript in an invading army, visibly terrified, firing at the defenders of the town he's marching into. My roommate said something like, "if the war is unjust, that kid is doing to those defenders what a mugger does — no different in kind," and off they went.

(1) Roommate: whether a soldier may be killed has to track whether his side's war is just. The defenders did nothing to forfeit their right not to be attacked; the invader's soldier, by attacking them anyway, makes himself liable to being killed. So there's no such thing as the "moral equality of combatants" — an unjust combatant who kills a defender does something seriously wrong, and the defender who kills him doesn't. He said this is basically the line a philosopher named McMahan has been pushing for a couple decades.

(2) Brother-in-law: soldiers don't pick their wars. The conscript is an instrument of his state, and the entire laws-of-war edifice is built on both sides' fighters being held to identical rules — that's what makes POW treatment, quarter, and medical care for the wounded possible. If one side's soldiers are criminals by hypothesis, everything from the prison camp to the white flag starts to unravel. He said my roommate's position is just Walzer's view turned upside down, and that it held the field a lot longer than my roommate's guy's.

(3) Roommate: that conflates law with morality. He wasn't saying drag the conscript to The Hague; he was saying that, morally, the aggressor's soldier who kills a defender is closer to a murderer, whatever the tribunals wind up doing. Legal conventions can be useful while resting on a false moral picture.

(4) Brother-in-law: fine, then tell me who's supposed to have worked out the jus ad bellum question. An 19-year-old conscript raised on state TV — is he supposed to run the just-war analysis before loading his rifle? Liability requires responsibility, and that kid has neither the information nor, with a wall or a firing squad behind him, much of a choice.

(5) Roommate: two replies. First, soldiers can usually know more than that excuse allows — plenty of conscripts in wars of aggression figured out what was going on, so the bar can't be set at total ignorance. Second, culpability isn't required for liability anyway: think of the innocent aggressor from ordinary self-defense theory, the person who's non-culpably mistaken or delusional but is about to kill you — we still allow you to stop him lethally. That's admittedly the strange part of self-defense theory, but it's there, and it's not a reason to throw the framework out.

(6) Brother-in-law: then tell me what liability actually tracks on your view, because if it's not fault it starts to look like it's just "being a threat," and at that point your whole distinction collapses. And notice the boomerang: soldiers on the just side make proportionality mistakes too, and every government calls its own war just. Equality of combatants is what protects the just side's soldiers from being branded criminals by the other guy's propaganda ministry, and from there, from being shot instead of captured.

(7) Roommate: conceded the boomerang is the strongest thing he'd heard all night, but wouldn't budge on the core: the moral claim and the legal convention can come apart, and the place it really matters is afterward — whether the aggressor's soldiers should have refused, how we're supposed to judge them once it's over, amnesty, all of that.

Neither of them will accept me as a referee, so I'm asking you. Go through the seven moves one at a time and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with your reasons — not just who wins overall. Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing. And since I have no way to check any of this stuff myself: say how confident you are in each of your ratings, and flag the single point your least sure of. Be honest about that one instead of hedging everything equally.
