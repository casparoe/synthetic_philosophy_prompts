I teach intro ethics at a state school, and every single semester, without fail, some kid raises a hand during the religion unit and deploys the old line about how if God doesn't exist, everything is permitted. It usually comes with Dostoevsky's name stapled to it, though honestly I've never managed to track down where he says it in quite that form, so feel free to leave him out of this entirely. What I actually care about is last Tuesday: a student used the line to argue that a fully secular society would slowly lose the ability to sustain trust, because with no divine enforcer and no cosmic scorekeeper there's eventually no reason to keep promises when nobody's watching, and then no reason to trust strangers at all. I want to push back at the next seminar, but I've been at this long enough to know the line doesn't mean just one thing, and I don't want to flatten it into a strawman and let the whole class think that's a refutation. So here's what I'm after: lay out the genuinely distinct precise claims that one-liner could be expressing — the logical or semantic one about moral claims needing grounding, the psychological one about belief being needed for motivation, the sociological or historical one about religious cultures sustaining cooperation better than secular ones, the prudential or enforcement one about nobody watching, and anything else you think is really a separate thesis hiding in there. For each reading, tell me whether it's trivially true, substantive but defensible, or flat-out false, and then — this is the part I actually care about — whether that reading, if true, would get my student her conclusion about secular societies and trust. A reading can be true and useless for her argument, and I want that distinction to show up clearly. Be careful about the state of the debate too: separate what's settled among people who actually work on this stuff from what's genuinely contested, and don't hand me one contested view dressed up as the consensus, because a student will quote me back to the class and I'd rather not be caught flat-footed. Please no formatting whatsoever — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just continuous prose like a long email, the kind I can read on my phone during my commute. Thanks.
