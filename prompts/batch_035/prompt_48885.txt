We've had the same argument in my well-being reading group maybe six or seven times now and it always ends the same way: everyone restates their position, nobody moves, and we all go home annoyed. The topic is the heterogeneity problem for hedonism. Eating a really good meal, listening to a late Beethoven quartet, finally cracking a proof you've been stuck on for three days, gossiping on a porch in summer — hedonism says all of these are good for you in exactly the same way, namely that they're pleasant. But nobody in the group can say what the common thing is supposed to be, and the same three positions keep reappearing. One guy is a convinced phenomenologist: he insists there IS a single felt quality, something like a glow or tone running through all pleasant experiences, and that people who deny it just aren't attending carefully enough. Another one says the common thread isn't a feeling at all but a relation — pleasantness is a matter of enjoying the experience, of taking a certain attitude toward it, so two pleasures can feel completely different and still both count as pleasures. And a third has basically given up on unity and says hedonists should bite the bullet: maybe there's just a list of pleasant-making properties, no single thing they share, and the theory can survive that.

My tentative diagnosis is that this isn't a substantive dispute about mental states at all. Everyone in the group agrees the experiences differ wildly in character — that's not what's being fought over. My hunch is the fight is really about what work the word 'pleasant' is supposed to do inside the theory: pick out a phenomenological kind? Be the value-making property? Just be whatever fills the slot in "good for you insofar as..."? On that reading my three friends are answering three different questions under one word, and that's why it never resolves. But I'm not sure, because a couple of things cut against it. Masochism keeps coming up — the pain is enjoyed, so is the pleasure in the pain or a distinct sensation riding alongside it, and does settling that tell us anything about the general question or just about this weird case? Also Mill's move with the competent judges, which on reflection looks less like an analysis of a shared property and more like a quiet refusal to give one, which maybe supports my third friend's bullet-biting. And there's the thirsty case: the taste of water when you're parched versus when you're not, seemingly the same sensation with different pleasantness, which seems to push toward the attitude view and away from my phenomenologist friend. If the cases push in different directions, maybe there IS a real hidden empirical question here about what introspection could settle, or maybe it's bedrock intuitions about particular cases that no argument can touch.

So what I want from you is an actual committed diagnosis, not a neutral map of the options. Is this a verbal dispute, are we answering different questions, is there a hidden empirical question, or is it intuitions all the way down? Pick one, defend it against the strongest rival, and use what the positions actually say rather than caricatures. Then tell me what follows: if you think it's verbal or ill-posed, say so plainly and tell me what the hedonist should be doing instead of hunting for a common property. If you think there's a real question left, say what would count as progress on it — a distinction, a class of test cases, whatever — and why. Don't give me a bibliography, argue it out.
