I've gotten kind of obsessed with futarchy — Robin Hanson's thing where the voting part of government picks what to measure (GDP per capita, or some welfare index, whatever), and betting markets decide which policies to actually pursue — whichever policy the market prices as leading to better outcomes on that metric down the line. I've been rolling it around in my head for a couple years, no academic background, just reading stuff at night, and I want your honest number on one precise question:

If a real country ran an actual futarchy for 20 years — I mean a mid-sized stable democracy, not a utopia and not a failed state — what's your credence that its policies would turn out better than under its current system, judged by the metric its own legislature picked at the start? I don't want a vibe, I want the decomposition. Here's roughly how I'd cut it up, but fix or add terms as you see fit:

1. Do prediction markets even stay accurate for slow, far-out stuff? Sports and elections resolve in days or weeks. A market on whether a policy pays off in 15 years asks people to lock money up for 15 years, so who trades in it? Maybe only true believers and people with weird discount rates — and then the price isn't collective wisdom, it's just a sample of whoever could afford to park money that long.

2. Is the metric safe from Goodharting? Policies that juice the number while quietly wrecking the stuff people actually wanted. And if the legislature can just redefine the metric whenever results disappoint, hasn't it collapsed back into ordinary politics with extra steps?

3. Manipulation. Everyone says rich actors can't move a market for long because someone will take their money. But on a 15-year horizon, who's still around to collect? The manipulator might have to stay wrong for a decade before anyone profits off them, and the policy gets made in the meantime.

4. Reference class. Even granting markets are good at prediction in general, there's basically zero data on governments being run this way. How much should that discount the whole inference?

Give me a rough probability for each (or for your better decomposition), one line of justification each, then combine them however you think is honest — if some terms are correlated don't just multiply them, tell me which ones move together. Then tell me which term is doing the most work in the final number, what would actually shift it, and whether you think one of my four is a red herring.

One more thing. When you give the final number, tell me straight if the whole thing is just term 1 in disguise — market efficiency at long horizons. My suspicion is everything else is decoration and the entire question lives or dies on that first term, but I've gone back and forth on it and I'd like to know if you think I'm wrong.
