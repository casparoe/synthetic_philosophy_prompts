A few of us in my philosophy of mind reading group — three philosophy grads and one psychology undergrad, which probably doesn't help — have been stuck for over a year on the question of what emotions actually are: judgments, perceptions, or feelings. The judgment camp keeps channeling the Stoics — the idea that fear of the dog just is the judgment that the dog is dangerous, that shame just is the judgment that I've been diminished — and they like that judgments can be assessed as rational or irrational, which seems to fit how we hold people responsible for their envy or resentment. The perception camp says emotions are more like perceptual experiences of value properties: the offensiveness of the insult just shows up to you the way the Müller-Lyer lines show up as unequal even after you've measured them. Their favorite case is recalcitrant emotion — you're still terrified of the dog you fully judge to be harmless, which they say is exactly what perception does when it defies belief. The judgment people reply that this cuts against them: if fear persists against your all-things-considered judgment, maybe fear was never a judgment to begin with — or maybe you just hold conflicting judgments — and they can't agree among themselves which line to take. Meanwhile the feeling camp is getting squeezed out of the room: they say an emotion is the felt bodily change, basically James's old idea, but everyone else points out that undifferentiated arousal seems not to distinguish shame from embarrassment — same racing heart — so bare feelings can't type the emotions. Their comeback is that it's fine-grained patterns of bodily change rather than global arousal that does the typing, and that our introspective access to the body might just be terrible. Nobody has moved an inch in mounths.

My question is what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it substantive, or verbal — a fight about what gets to count as 'cognitive' or about which component of the episode gets to be called 'the emotion'? Or are the three camps answering three different questions under one heading, one about the ontology of emotions, one about what justifies them, one about what they feel like from inside? My own half-baked hypothesis is that the fight is really about priority: the judgment camp wants emotions to inherit their rational assessability from their propositional content, the perception camp wants them to inherit it from a quasi-perceptual faculty that's supposed to be reliable-ish about value, and the feelings camp doesn't much care about rational assessability at all, which is why they keep talking past everyone. I could be totally of though. Give me a diagnosis with actual reasons behind it — verbal dispute, bedrock intuitions, or maybe a hidden empirical question about how fine-grained interoception really is — and tell me honestly whether anything would count as progress here: a conceptual distinction, a psychophysical result, a better-carved question. Or should we just expect this one to stay stalled for another couple of decades, and if so, why has it stalled rather than been settled, given that everyone in that room knows all the same cases?
