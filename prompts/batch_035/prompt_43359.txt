I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in decision theory and the philosophy of economics — 14 students, one 90-minute session — and next week's topic is the backward induction paradox in the centipede game. I want an in-class activity, not a lecture followed by a demo: something the students play before they've read anything about backward induction, so they run into the problem from the inside.

Some constraints and desiderata:

1. The game should be a real centipede-style structure — alternating turns, a strictly growing pot, each player able to "take" and end the game or "pass" and grow it, with a finite and publicly known number of remaining moves. Payoffs should be hypothetical points or something small and non-monetary; I don't want to run money through the university. But the payoff scheme should still make the incentives vivid, so tell me how to make points feel like they matter for one afternoon (a class-wide running tally, a prize, whatever works).

2. Students should play it at least twice in different configurations, and you should design the configurations so that something interesting shifts between them. I'm thinking of one version where play is anonymous and one-shot, and one where the same partners play repeatedly or where players can announce intentions before play — but you're free to propose better variants, as long as at least one configuration is likely to produce behavior that backward induction says is irrational (i.e., passing early on), and one that produces "unraveling" toward the first move.

3. Crucially, the paradox itself should emerge from what the students do, not from what I say. The debrief should get them to articulate the tension on their own: that the standard backward-induction argument predicts taking at the very first node, that this argument seems to require common knowledge of rationality, and yet that if a player ever finds herself at a late node — something the argument itself says shouldn't happen — the premises she'd need to justify taking there have been undercut by the very fact of her arrival. I want the debrief to walk through at least one actual game the students played and ask, at each decision point, what the player could reasonably have believed about her opponent at that point.

4. Tell me honestly what will go wrong the first time I run this: students passing out of politeness or boredom rather than strategic reasoning, confusion about the payoff table, pairs that coordinate cooperatively and thereby feel they've "refuted" the argument when they've really just violated its premises, and whatever else you anticipate. For each predictable failure, tell me whether it's noise to be minimized or pedagogically useful material for the debrief.

5. Finally, be explicit about which feature of the backward induction paradox the activity cannot reproduce — my instinct is that the paradox is really about what a perfectly rational agent could conclude about another perfectly rational agent at a node that both know shouldn't be reached, and no classroom full of humans can instantiate that — and what I'll have to supply verbally in the debrief to bridge the gap.

Please give me the full session plan: materials, the payoff table, round-by-round rules, timing, the debrief script in outline, and your honest notes on limits and failure modes.
