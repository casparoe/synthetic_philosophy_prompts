You are a professional philosopher specializing in personal identity and Buddhist philosophy, and I would like you to think carefully before answering. I am a novelist, and at a recent writing retreat a memoirist friend of mine — a serious meditation practitioner — kept waving off our craft discussions about narrative voice with the one-liner that the self is an illusion. Her point, as far as I could tell, was that memoir is a category error: there is no continuous self to reconstruct, only a construction retroactively imposed, and she seemed to think this settled the matter. I suspect the sentence is doing several very different jobs at once, some of them much weaker than her use of it requires. Please begin with some brainstorming, then lay out as a numbered menu the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing — for example, readings on which the claim is a near-truism that virtually no philosopher of any tradition denies, readings on which it is a substantive Humean or Buddhist claim about the self as a bundle of aggregates with no owner, readings on which the self is an illusion in the strong sense that a self is represented in experience though none exists, and readings on which we should simply stop using self-talk, whatever the metaphysics. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, defensible but contentious, or false; say what work each would do in her argument; and crucially, say whether any of them actually supports dismissing memoir — since even on the bundle reading, one might argue that a novelist's constructed narrator and a memoirist's reconstructed self are in the same boat, and that the boat floats. I would like a list I can bring back to the retreat and defend without misrepresenting her view.
