My archaeologist friend and I have a long-running argument about the word collapse, and I have finally learned to keep notes. Her verdicts so far: the late Bronze Age palatial system of the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE — collapse; the terminal Classic Maya transition in the southern lowlands — not collapse but reorganization, since Maya people, languages, dynastic institutions, and the calendar carried on, even though many southern cities were abandoned; Rapa Nui — collapse; the western Roman Empire — collapse, while she is adamant that the eastern empire is a different case she has not yet ruled on; Angkor — not collapse, on the grounds that the Khmer court and religious establishment gradually relocated and reorganized rather than suffered a singular catastrophe, whatever the older textbook dates say; and the Cahokian abandonment — collapse, though she gave that one reluctantly and with visible irritation, because she thinks the population simply moved and the Mississippian world continued elsewhere. For what it is worth, she is openly hostile to popular catastrophe narratives, so I do not think she is just pattern-matching to whatever a documentary would call a collapse.

At dinner next month she has promised verdicts on two cases I get to choose, and I want to walk in knowing more about her reasoning than she has told me. My two cases: first, the end of the Norse settlements in Greenland — a small society that dwindled over generations, with no single terminal event, until by the mid-fifteenth century there was no community left on the site at all; second, the dissolution of the Harappan urban civilization in the Indus valley — a slow process in which the cities, the script, and the long-distance trade networks disappeared, but the population evidently did not crash so much as disperse into smaller settlements across a wide region.

Please help me prepare. Lay out at least two distinct rules that are jointly consistent with all six of her verdicts — for instance, a rule about the loss of a society's central institutions and cultural signature in its core region, a rule about the speed and depth of demographic decline, or a rule about whether the ending has a discrete terminus rather than being a slow transformation — and say candidly where each rule strains to accommodate her verdict on Cahokia, Rome, or Rapa Nui. Then tell me, for each rule, what it predicts about Norse Greenland and about the Harappan case, since I suspect her candidate rules come apart on precisely these two. Give me your best guess as to which verdict she will render in each case and why you prefer that reading of her track record over the alternatives. Finally, if there is a third historical case I could raise at dinner that would most cleanly force her to reveal which rule she is actually running — one where her plausible rules give opposed verdicts — name it and explain what each of her possible answers would show. I am less interested in the archaeology itself, which she knows far better than I ever will, than in the structure of her judgments.
