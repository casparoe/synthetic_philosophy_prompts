At my university's philosophy society debate on the existence of God, a visiting speaker defended the kalam cosmological argument and repeatedly leaned on the slogan ex nihilo nihil fit — out of nothing, nothing comes — as if it were self-evident: whatever begins to exist has a cause, and the universe began to exist. When someone objected that quantum mechanics appears to show particles coming into being from the vacuum without a cause, the speaker waved this off as merely a different sense of 'nothing'. I left unsure what the slogan was actually claiming. Please lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing — for instance: a stipulation about the word 'nothing'; a trivial truth that rearrangements of existing stuff require prior stuff; an empirical generalization from our experience of objects beginning to exist; or a substantive metaphysical principle that every event, state, or entity has a sufficient cause or explanation — and say for each whether it is trivial, defensible but weak, or contentious, and whether it supports the kalam argument's causal premise rather than merely sounding like it does. In particular, tell me which readings survive the quantum objection and whether the vacuum counts as 'nothing' on any of them, or whether the objection only threatens some readings while leaving others untouched. Finally, does any of these readings do real work in the contingency version of the cosmological argument, or does that argument need an altogether different principle about explanations of contingent facts — and if so, does that tell against treating the one slogan as the common foundation of both arguments?
