I run a biweekly forecasting meetup at a community space near my university — about fourteen regulars, mostly people who came in through the usual blogs on calibration and scoring rules, sessions run ninety minutes. Next month I want a session that does something subtler than our normal calibration drills: I want them to feel, from the inside, the move from credences-as-graded-beliefs to credences-as-moves-in-a-game-you-can-score, and then to feel that the scoring rule itself is not handed down from nowhere. The idea I keep circling is a game where the participants act as forecasters, one participant acts as the scorer/bookie, and the rounds escalate. First, simple binary forecasts scored quadratically. Second, a round with logically related claims — an outcome, its complement, maybe a conjunction — where the payoffs are set up so that any incoherent set of answers is dominated, and the bookie's job is to construct the dominating alternative and show it to the incoherent player. Third, a round where I secretly switch to a linear rule (points equal to the probability you assigned to what actually happened) and everyone's optimum slides toward hedged 50/50 answers without their noticing the rule changed. The punchline I'm trying to engineer: properness is not a technicality — a rule can be an accuracy rule in some ordinary sense and still reward exactly the wrong epistemics, so the accuracy-first program has to say *which* accuracy, and that choice is doing real philosophical work.

Design the full session for me: rules, materials, exact payoff schemes (we use points and a cheap prize, no money), a workable list of claims to forecast that resolve within the session (verifiable trivia, not live events), and how the bookie's role should work in the coherence round so that dominance gets demonstrated rather than asserted. Then the debrief: which moments map onto which parts of the view — credence as action, the dominance argument for probabilism, why properness is the load-bearing requirement — and how to name what they felt without my smuggling the conclusion in. I'd also like a final segment where the group scores the same forecast sheets under two different proper rules (say, quadratic and logarithmic) and discovers the rules can disagree about who forecast better — so they see that even after properness is imposed, the choice of inaccuracy measure is left open; if you see a cleaner way to force that discovery than a rule comparison, propose it. And two things I want you to be blunt about: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and what the activity fundamentally cannot reproduce. My current worry on the second point is that in the game everyone knows they're being scored, whereas the epistemologist's claim is supposed to hold for a lone inquirer with no audience, no prize, and no decision at stake — I don't know how to stage that, and I'd rather the debrief admit it than paper over it.
