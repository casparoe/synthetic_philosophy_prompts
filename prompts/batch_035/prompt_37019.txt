My roommate has an argument that I half-believe and half can't stand. (1) If you can raise the chance that millions of people live under better policies, at a cost to yourself of about an hour, you should do it. (2) In a national election a single vote has roughly a one-in-ten-million chance of being decisive. (3) If your vote is decisive, it decides which platform governs a whole country for years, and even a modest per-person benefit, multiplied across that many people, swamps an hour of your time. (4) So you should vote — fine, I actually buy that part. But then he keeps going: (5) a few dollars of campaign spending reliably buys about one marginal vote, so donating to the best-placed campaign is worth roughly as much as a vote, per dollar; (6) the same arithmetic that got us to (4) makes campaign donations beat mosquito nets by a mile, and also means I should spend real hours every cycle researching the candidates, since a badly-informed vote has the same tiny chance of making millions of people worse off; (7) so I should redirect most of my giving and a chunk of my free time into elections, every cycle, forever. I want to keep (4) and reject (7), but I can't see what breaks between them — (5) and (6) just run the logic I already accepted. Either something went wrong earlier, or the conclusion is right and I only dislike it. Which step or premise do I give up, and what am I paying when I give it up?

Answer in four sections, in this order: "Where it goes wrong" — name the single step or premise you'd reject, ~150 words; "Why it looked fine" — ~100 words; "The bill" — what rejecting that step costs elsewhere, especially whether it still lets me keep the claim that I ought to vote, ~200 words; "Bottom line" — ~50 words.
