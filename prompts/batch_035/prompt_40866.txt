I've ended up, after a lot of reading on Bertrand's paradox, with a position I'll try to state cleanly: the principle of indifference is fine as a rule for idealizing an actually-existing chance setup, and it never has anything to say about an abstractly described problem at all. On my view, "a chord is chosen at random" (or "a cube is chosen at random from those a factory produces") just under-specifies things — there is a fact of the matter about the right probability distribution, and it's fixed by the physical process that did the choosing: which quantity the process treats uniformly, side length or surface area, say, in the cube version. The classic Bertrand-style cases only look paradoxical because the problems are posed stripped of their generating process, and textbook authors then smuggle one in without noticing. So the "paradoxes" are artifacts of bad problem statements, not refutations of indifference, and the principle itself only ever gets applied once you've matched the problem to a real mechanism — a die, a roulette wheel, a spinning pointer, a manufacturing line. Where there is no mechanism to point at, in my view there is no probability assignment to be had, and demanding one is like demanding the average of a hat.

What I want from you: what would actually count against this position? I'm not looking for the standard "any symmetry-based proposal just pushes the problem back a level" line delivered as a slogan — I want the strongest working-out of it, plus anything else: cases where the process seems to fix a distribution yet intuitions still pull apart, empirical setups that could in principle go one way or the other, the possibility that a view like this has been quietly insulated so that every awkward case gets redescribed as "not really a proper problem." And be honest about whether that insulation, if it's there, is fatal or actually fine for a view of this kind — is "match the problem to a physical process first" a substantive constraint or an escape hatch?
