Everyone knows the basic gripe about Whig history — Herbert Butterfield's old complaint that historians kept studying the past for the sake of the present, reading history as a steady march toward us and our nice arrangements. But I've gotten confused about two things that seem deeper than the slogan, and I can't find a clean treatment of either. No reading lists please, and no name-dropping of papers — just get the concepts straight for me.

First: what exactly is the vice being complained about? Is it a moral one (judging dead people by standards they couldn't have known) or a structural one (organizing the narrative so that everything earlier gets its significance from what it later led to)? You can picture a historian who never moralizes at all, never says a harsh word about anyone, and still writes pure Whig history — say, a history of science narrated as the gradual accumulation of the truths we now hold, where every earlier figure is either a contribution or a case of "not yet." That looks like the structural version with zero moralizing. And conversly you can have someone who moralizes heavily about the past but still treats the past on its own terms, so the two vices can come apart. So which one is the actual target, and do arguments against the moral version carry over to the structural one, or do they need separate defense?

Second, and this is the part I really want to get straight: isn't the standard story about Whig history itself kind of Whiggish? The usual telling goes something like "historians used to be blinded by hindsight and presentism, but in the mid-twentieth century they learned better, and the discipline has been improving since." That's a narrative about the past organized around progress toward an enlightened present — us. That seems like alot of the same move. Is there a way to state the case against Whig history that doesn't trip over itself like this, or do we just have to accept some amount of it as the price of asking historical questions at all — since we ask those questions from where we stand, and some selection of what counts as significant, relative to our current concerns, is unavoidable? And if a bit of it is unavoidable, what exactly distinguishes the forgivable part from the part Butterfield was actually complaining about?

If it helps, feel free to answer by inventing a concrete miniature historiography (like, two short paragraphs describing how a hypothetical Whig historian vs. a non-Whig one would narrate the same episode) to pin down where the structural version kicks in. But keep the focus on the two questions.
