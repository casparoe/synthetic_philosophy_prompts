I'm a graduate student writing the final essay for a course in philosophy of cognitive science, roughly five thousand words, on the last and least-discussed objection in Turing's 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence: the objection from extra-sensory perception. Nearly every commentary treats this section as an oddity to be skipped over — an embarrassment, or at best a deadpan joke. My essay argues the opposite: that what Turing does there is instructive precisely because it is not a joke. In that section, Turing assumes his reader is familiar with the four varieties of ESP — telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition and psycho-kinesis — suggests that the statistical evidence for them is stronger than most scientists at the time were prepared to allow, and then observes that telepathy in particular would wreck the imitation game: an interrogator with telepathic powers could ask the contestants, say, what playing card he was thinking of, and the human contestant would answer correctly where the machine could not. His remedy is not to legislate ESP out of science but to patch the protocol — for instance, by sealing the interrogator in a room shielded against telepathy. My interpretive claim is that this patch reveals the imitation game functioning as an empirical protocol rather than a criterion of mind: a test whose validity depends on what the interrogator can detect, and which is meant to be revised in the light of evidence rather than held fixed by stipulation. The engine of the essay is an asymmetry: ESP gets a protocol revision, whereas the argument from consciousness is met with the observation that taking it seriously would land you in solipsism. If ESP is worth insulating the test against but consciousness is not, that tells us something about which considerations Turing thought were live empirical threats and which he took to be philosophical mischief.
