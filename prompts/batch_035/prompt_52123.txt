i'm on my county planning commission and next month we vote on whether to let a developer bulldoze ~200 acres of degraded marsh for a distribution center. my friend, who's deep into the wild animal welfare side of EA, says i should vote yes without guilt: most animals in a marsh are insects, fish, and mice living short lives that end in starvation or being eaten alive, so a marsh is plausibly a net suffering factory and a warehouse floor is welfare-neutral. my sister, a wetlands biologist, thinks this is deranged — to her the marsh is obviously good, and the burden of proof is on anyone claiming otherwise.

the part that actually keeps me up: the developer has to construct replacement wetlands elsewhere as mitigation. so the vote isn't really "marsh vs parking lot," it's closer to "old marsh vs new marsh." and it occurs to me that a freshly built wetland with no established predator populations might produce a boom of short-lived prey animals — way more churn and more dying young than the mature one it replaces. so my friend's own argument might flip depending on details nobody involved is even discussing.

i have to cast an actual vote. is there any principled way to think about whether a healthy ecosystem contains more or less suffering than nothing at all? does it even make sense to add up welfare across billions of insects that maybe have no experience at all? and given how made up all these numbers would be, am i obligated to just fall back on the standard environmentalist presumptions, or is that an epistemic cop-out?
