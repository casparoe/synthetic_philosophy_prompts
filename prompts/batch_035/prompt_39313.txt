For most of my career I was an experimental condensed-matter physicist, and I have recently been reading about interpretability research in deep learning, which strikes me as a field in a position not unlike spectroscopy in its early decades: we have instruments that produce signals, but only the beginnings of a taxonomy of what the signals are signals of. The specific issue I would like explained concerns what researchers call mechanistic anomaly detection. As I understand it, the idea is to monitor a deployed model and flag inputs on which it behaves according to some mechanism different from the ones it was using on the training distribution, the hope being that catastrophic or deceptive behavior will typically involve such off-distribution mechanisms. What I find genuinely puzzling is the individuation problem lurking in the phrase "the same mechanism."

In physics we are used to this sort of question. Whether two systems exhibit the same behavior can depend entirely on the level of description: at the level of microstates no two copper samples are ever the same, yet at the level of universality classes a lattice model and a magnet can instantiate the same critical phenomenon. So when an interpretability researcher says that a model implements, say, a certain modular-arithmetic circuit on the training data and a different circuit on some anomalous input, what fixes whether the two circuits count as distinct mechanisms rather than as one mechanism operating in a degenerate or extrapolated regime? I can think of several candidate criteria and do not know which, if any, the field takes seriously: identity of the computational graph modulo isomorphism, identity of the causal role played in producing the output, identity of the features or directions in activation space being read and written, or merely similarity of behavior under some battery of probes. These criteria presumably come apart in edge cases, and I would like to understand where.

Concretely, please explain the conceptual landscape here: what would a principled criterion of mechanism identity for neural networks have to deliver, which criteria are actually in play in the anomaly-detection literature, and where do they yield divergent verdicts? It would help if you could illustrate with a toy case, such as a model that has learned a trigger-conditioned behavior — say, a classifier that answers normally except when a rare token pattern appears — and walk through whether that trigger behavior should count as an old mechanism extended, a new mechanism, or a recombination of old parts into something whose identity conditions are simply unclear. I am less interested in the empirical performance of any particular detector than in the underlying question of what kind of thing a mechanism is such that it can be the same or different across inputs. Feel free to draw on analogies from physics, chemistry, or biology of the sort an old spectroscopist would appreciate, but please keep the discussion self-contained, as I have no access to the literature beyond what is commonly known.
