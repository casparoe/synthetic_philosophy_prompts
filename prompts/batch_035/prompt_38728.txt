I teach an upper-level seminar on fairness and formal models — fourteen students, mostly philosophy and economics majors, one 80-minute session. In the last session we covered the Nash bargaining solution and its axioms; the next session is supposed to be on the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution from the 1975 Econometrica paper. Rather than lecturing the axioms, I want the students to bargain first and only then discover which axioms they have been implicitly using all along.

Design the session around paired bargaining over small real stakes (bonus points, candy, whatever works). One constraint my last attempt ran into: a plain "split a fixed pie" game won't work, because with a linear frontier and transferable utility the Nash and Kalai–Smorodinsky solutions coincide at an equal split of the surplus over the disagreement point, and my students would correctly find that anticlimactic. So I need concrete payoff schedules — actual numbers I can print on cards, no more than a dozen feasible agreements per pair — with a kinked or curved Pareto frontier and a nonzero disagreement point, such that the Nash solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution land in visibly different places. Give me the actual numbers, state what each solution comes out to in your setup, and make the schedules asymmetric across the pairs so that not everyone converges on the same numbers.

Then design a second phase in which the same pairs bargain again after I quietly remove one alternative from their menu: an option that was never a plausible agreement (say, one that gives one player nearly everything and the other almost nothing), but that nonetheless sets that player's best-case payoff. The point I want them to feel is that the Nash solution says the agreement should not move — the removed alternative was irrelevant — while the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution says it should move, because the removal changes a player's utopia point and with it the ratio of gains that the solution equalizes. I want students arguing over whether their prior agreement still binds before anyone knows there is a formal issue at stake.

For the debrief, map the arguments students will actually make — things like: you would never have gotten more than such-and-such anyway; we are not renegotiating just because the menu changed; my outside option is what it is — onto independence of irrelevant alternatives on the one hand and individual monotonicity on the other, and say which conception of fairness each argument presupposes.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — pairs defaulting to a 50/50 split out of habit, a student seizing the entire surplus because the schedule confuses their partner, disagreement points not being credible — and be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce. In particular, I am aware that payoffs printed on cards are utilities by fiat, so the deep problem of interpersonal utility comparison gets assumed away rather than solved, and that the students who already know game theory may coldly compute the solution instead of bargaining toward it. Say plainly which parts of the Kalai–Smorodinsky story the game can carry and which parts I will have to supply myself in the debrief.
