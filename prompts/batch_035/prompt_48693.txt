I work on the foundations of rational choice, and I want a stress test for a familiar claim: that the money-pump argument establishes the irrationality of intransitive preferences because they expose the agent to exploitation over time. Construct a thought experiment to the following specification, and then demonstrate that it meets each part.

The scenario: an ordinary consumer and three mid-priced durable goods — three used cars, say, differing on at least three attributes (or substitute comparable items). The consumer's honest pairwise rankings, elicited the usual way — which of these two would you rather have, with both described in front of you — form a strict cycle. Design the case so that the following all hold:

1. The elicited preferences form a strict cycle over the three options.
2. The agent is fully informed about the options and about its own rankings, applies its values consistently, and undergoes no change of taste at any point in the case.
3. A trader who knows the agent's psychology completely — including everything in (1) and (2) — and who may offer any sequence of voluntary trades, including pure gifts, cash buyouts of the agent's current holding, and multi-leg swaps presented as a single upfront package, cannot extract any net money from the agent.
4. The escape must not depend on the agent anticipating future trades or the trader's plans (it is maximally naive: it considers only the offer in front of it), on precommitment, on transaction costs or other market frictions, or on any refusal stipulated by fiat. It must flow from one stable, empirically well-documented feature of how people value options — a feature on which the value an agent assigns to an option can depend on something more than the option's objective description.
5. Everything stays realistic: no demons, no perfect predictors, no lotteries, stakes and fees an ordinary person could actually face.

After the scenario — one paragraph, with every choice the agent makes derivable from its stated psychology rather than asserted — demonstrate the design:

(a) Run the textbook money pump against the agent: lay out the sequence of trade offers the classic argument says the trader should make, and show, offer by offer, what the agent does and why.
(b) Show that at least two trader redesigns also fail — include a gifting variant (hand the agent the next item up the cycle for free, then offer a trade) and a simultaneous upfront package — unless you think one of them succeeds, in which case say so and say exactly what it extracts.
(c) Name the premise of the money-pump argument that the case severs. The argument is not simply "be transitive"; spell out the diachronic assumption it makes about how preferences elicited in one context transfer to choices made in another, and show precisely where the case cuts that assumption.
(d) Switch the isolating feature off and confirm that the textbook pump then runs to completion, so that the feature really is doing the work.
(e) Give the money-pump defender's best reply, and evaluate it. In particular: is the agent in your scenario rational, or has it merely traded one irrationality for another — a systematic refusal of exchanges it would endorse from a distance? Does the case show that the money-pump argument proves less than it is advertised to prove, or only that this agent was never within the argument's scope?
(f) Comment on the inversion your case exhibits: the standard escapes from money pumps are strategic — seeing the pump coming, or binding oneself in advance — whereas your agent is saved while being maximally naive. What does that suggest about where the argument's real force lies?

The point of the case is to test whether diachronic exploitability is a fact about an agent's preferences themselves or about the interaction between how preferences are elicited and the context in which choices are made. A scenario that blocks the pump by decree, or that quietly lets the agent reason about the trader's future moves, fails the specification however vivid it is.

Conclude with a final line of the form "Verdict: ..." — a short phrase, no more than a few words, naming the premise of the money-pump argument that your case severs.
