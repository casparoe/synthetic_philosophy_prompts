I recently started work as a policy analyst at a famine early-warning organization, where I've discovered that our senior staff split into two camps that have been arguing past each other for the better part of a year. One camp, following Amartya Sen, holds that famines are essentially political events: they are caused by failures of entitlement — people's ability to command food through production, wages, or trade collapsing even when food exists — and by policy choices and institutional failures, up to and including deliberate neglect, so that famine is better described as a political crime than a natural disaster. The other camp is skeptical of what it sees as an over-moralized, human-centered narrative: famines are at bottom ecological and demographic events driven by shortfalls in aggregate food availability — crop failure, blight, drought, weather — with political factors shaping who suffers within an unavoidable shortage, not whether the shortage occurs. The flashpoints are always the same three cases: the Irish famine of 1845–1852, the Bengal famine of 1943, and the famine during China's Great Leap Forward around 1959–1961, including the persistent dispute over Bengal in particular, where one side insists that the rice harvest was roughly normal and the disaster was entirely a matter of distribution and wartime policy, while the other doubts that the underlying data can support so strong a claim.

My director has asked me to broker a truce by drafting the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between these two camps ought to produce, and I'd like you to write that document. Please begin with a numbered section of claims that both camps could sign, covering at minimum: what the historical record establishes about each of the three cases; what the entitlement framework genuinely explains and what it does not; and any points on which you find their dispute is partly verbal — for instance, whether the phrase "natural disaster" is being used by one side as a causal claim and by the other as an exculpatory one. Follow this with a disagreements table, in which each residual disagreement is stated in wording that the corresponding side would accept as a fair statement of its own view — the Senian's position phrased as the Senian would phrase it, the food-availability side's position phrased as it would phrase its own — rather than as each side characterizes the other.

Then identify the cruxes: the subquestions such that settling them would move at least one camp. I suspect they include whether aggregate food availability in Bengal in 1943 was unusually low; whether the demographic pattern of mortality in Ireland and Bengal tracks entitlement failure better than it tracks raw scarcity; whether Sen's claim that no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press can survive the search for counterexamples; and whether "political crime" is the right category for cases like the Great Leap Forward, where information was deliberately concealed and procurement continued, as opposed to cases of passive policy restraint like Ireland. For each crux, specify a concrete case, dataset, or argument that both camps would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp would predict about it. Please keep the tone measured and professional, as befits a document both parties are expected to sign their names to.
