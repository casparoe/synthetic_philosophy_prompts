I defend a fairly strict version of origin essentialism about persons: you could not have originated from a different sperm and egg than the ones you actually came from, and you have existed ever since that origin was fixed. I am now writing a paper on whether the thesis can survive the reproductive technologies we already have, since nearly all the standard arguments for and against it were designed for a world of unassisted conception and teletransporters. I need a properly engineered stress case, and I would like you to build it to the following specifications.

Construct the scenario entirely from materials that exist or have been demonstrated in non-human mammals: IVF, intracytoplasmic sperm injection, embryo splitting at the two-cell stage (artificial monozygotic twinning, which is just the mechanical version of what happens naturally), blastomere biopsy, and vitrification with long-term cryopreservation of the resulting pieces. No teletransporters, no brain transplants, no artificial wombs, no in vitro gametogenesis, no souls as separate existences, and no technology an embryologist would wince at.

The specifications:

1. The scenario itself must be a single paragraph, medically coherent, and must not stipulate any verdicts — whatever the theories say has to fall out of the theories, not the narration.
2. The case must be engineered so that my origin essentialism, animalism, and the psychological-continuity view deliver three different answers to one and the same question about it. The question should concern when one of the resulting persons began to exist, and whether she existed continuously through the period of cryopreservation — but if some other question makes the three-way divergence cleaner, you may use it, provided you state it explicitly.
3. The case must additionally make it a live question whether either of the two resulting persons could have been the other, given that both of them satisfy the origin condition as I have stated it. I want that question forced, not avoided.
4. The case must be minimal: every clinical detail either contributes to the divergence or is cut.

After the scenario, deliver the following:

(a) The three verdicts, each with the reasoning that generates it, phrased so that a defender of the view would recognize herself.

(b) A statement of exactly which structural feature of the case does the work — I suspect it is something about how a totipotent blastomere both is and is not a continuation of the individual it was split from, but you should tell me what it actually is.

(c) An analysis of what happens when that feature is removed: does the case collapse into an ordinary frozen-embryo case, an ordinary twinning case, or an ordinary biopsy case, and which theory's verdict changes as a result.

(d) The strongest reply available to each of the two views that come out awkwardly — the sort of reply a careful animalist like Olson or a careful psychological-continuity theorist like Parfit would actually give — and an honest assessment of whether anything comparable is available to me, or whether the case instead exposes an ambiguity in the word "origin" itself: the gamete pair, the zygote, the event of syngamy, or something else.

(e) A final verdict: does the case show that origin essentialism is false, that it needs refinement, or that it was never really a thesis about persons at all?

If you find the specifications cannot all be met at once, say which one you had to bend and why, rather than quietly bending it.
