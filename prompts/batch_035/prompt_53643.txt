**Convenience or Discovery: Are the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution Real?**

Thesis: these three periods are real as instruments but not as discoveries — they are best understood as institutional facts, in something like John Searle's sense: entities that exist only because a community treats them as existing, like borders and banknotes, and which are nonetheless not thereby illusions.

Notes to develop:

1. Genealogy of the labels. The concept of a "middle" age is not medieval; it is a polemical invention of Italian Renaissance humanists, who imagined antiquity as a lost golden age, themselves as its revivers, and the millennium in between as merely an interval — a gap defined by what it was not. The label "Renaissance" as the name of a period is younger still: contemporaries such as Vasari spoke of a rebirth of the arts, but the period as such was consolidated in the nineteenth century, above all through Michelet and Burckhardt's 1860 study of the civilization of Renaissance Italy. And the "Scientific Revolution" was named by twentieth-century historians such as Koyré and Butterfield. In each case the container postdates the contents.

2. A curiosity about the word "revolution": Copernicus's De revolutionibus used it in its old, astronomical sense of a cyclical return. Only later did the word acquire its modern sense of a decisive rupture. So the period we call the Scientific Revolution could not even have been described as a "revolution" by anyone living through it — the conceptual machinery for the label did not yet exist.

3. The quantifier-shift diagnosis. Historians legitimately point to real changes: heliocentrism, the mathematization of nature, experimental method, new scientific institutions. The illegitimate step — I want to argue — is the slide from "many revolutionary changes occurred between roughly 1540 and 1690" to "the Scientific Revolution occurred," as though we had identified a single event or entity. The changes are real; the Revolution is a reification. The same holds for the Renaissance as an整体.

4. Boundary instability. A second symptom: the dates wobble by textbook. The Middle Ages open with the fall of the Western Empire in 476, or with Constantine, or with the collapse of Carolingian order; the Renaissance begins with Petrarch, or with the fall of Constantinople, or with print; the Scientific Revolution runs 1543–1687, or stretches back into the medieval universities and forward to Lavoisier. A genuine joint in history should not move this much depending on who is drawing the line.

5. Obvious counterarguments to weigh: every category at scale blurs (species boundaries do too); and there was a real, unusual clustering of mathematization and institutionalized inquiry in Western Europe between roughly 1500 and 1700 — the Royal Society, the Académie des Sciences — which is not nothing. Continuity historians (Thorndike is the standard cite here) argue the "rupture" was a fiction flattering to the moderns.

6. Conclusion: the grid analogy. Periodization is like latitude and longitude: the lines are conventional, drawn for navigational convenience, and no one thinks the equator was discovered rather than stipulated — yet the grid is indispensable, and the terrain it maps is real. The periods are real in that sense: institutional, not natural.
