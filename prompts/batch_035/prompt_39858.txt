I am a novelist, and my current project is narrated in the first person by a woman in her sixties who underwent a complete section of the corpus callosum decades ago, back when that operation was still performed for intractable epilepsy. I want a single scene in which the reader, sharing the narrator's point of view, cannot settle whether she is one person or two — and I want that uncertainty earned by the neurology rather than manufactured by vagueness of prose. Before I write it, I need to know whether such a scene can even be coherently specified, and I would like you to build the test case for me.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications:

1. The mechanics must be drawn only from phenomena actually documented in split-brain research: information presented to one visual hemifield reaching only the opposite hemisphere, the two hands following independent and occasionally conflicting instructions, the left hemisphere's habit of assembling a fluent account of behavior whose real cause it never witnessed. No invented devices, no mind-reading, no exotic physics — just ordinary perception and movement.

2. The scene must be domestic rather than experimental. The patient is in her kitchen with her husband; the disconnection does its work through the incidental layout of the room — someone standing to one side, an object entering a single hemifield, a task that gives each hemisphere a distinct role. But the underlying machinery must be the laboratory-documented kind; the home setting is a transfer, not a fairy tale.

3. Within the scenario, the speaking left hemisphere and the acting right hemisphere must each generate a coherent but incompatible interpretation of the same deed, and neither interpretation may be an obvious confabulation. The tension must not consist in the left hemisphere telling a transparently self-serving story; both readings must be reasonable, such that a well-informed observer could honestly hold either.

4. Then walk through the verdicts. Say what the case looks like to the view that the operation left literally two streams of experience in one body; to the view that there remains a single subject whose access to her own mental life has merely been degraded; and to the view that counting subjects is a question about our concepts rather than about the patient, so that nothing in the scenario could settle it either way.

5. Identify which feature of the case is doing the work — the one that would have to be removed for the scene to collapse into the familiar disunity everyone feels when acting against their own better judgment — and explain what a defender of the single-subject view would say to attack the case.

6. Finally, address the question I actually care about: does first-person narration of such a patient take a side? If the narrator's prose is fluent, grammatical, and self-reflective, it is presumably voiced by the left hemisphere — so in my novel, is the right hemisphere an unspeaking character, a silent co-author, or nobody at all? Explain what is at stake in that choice, and what it would mean for the sentence "I did it."
