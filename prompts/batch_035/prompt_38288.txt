I run a small mediation practice that specializes in co-founder and partnership dissolutions — disputes where the parties will spend a day with me, will not retain experts, and need to leave with a signed term sheet. Increasingly I find myself improvising what amount to intuited fair splits, and I would like something firmer: a decision procedure built on two-person bargaining theory, specifically the Nash bargaining solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky alternative, that I can actually run inside a single day-long session.

My setting and constraints, so the procedure fits them: I handle perhaps four or five of these mediations a quarter; the parties are non-technical, emotional, and strategic; I get roughly three hours of joint session and an hour of private caucus with each side; the disputes involve money plus a few indivisible non-monetary issues (title to the codebase, a non-compete, the client list, who tells the investors); and my usual output is a mediator's proposal — one package each side can accept or reject. I understand that the two solutions agree in symmetric cases and come apart in others, and that the divergence is really a divergence about axioms — one solution gives up independence of irrelevant alternatives, the other gives up monotonicity with respect to a single bargainer's opportunities — but I do not know how to convert that into advice at a table with two angry former friends in it.

Please write me the procedure, covering:

1. The steps in order, from opening joint session to final proposal: how to elicit each party's disagreement point (what realistically happens if we fail), how to handle parties who inflate their walk-away alternatives, how to build a candidate set of packages when most issues are indivisible, and how to extract rough cardinal preference information from people who will never submit to a formal utility elicitation — for instance, having each side allocate a hundred points across issues, or choose between whole packages. For each step, say what a sufficient answer looks like and what an insufficient one looks like.

2. Where the choice between the two solutions matters and where it does not: give me a practical test, runnable on the elicited numbers, that tells me when Nash and Kalai–Smorodinsky will come apart on a given dispute — I gather it has something to do with one party's ideal outcome being far larger than the other's — and what to recommend when they do. Is there a principled basis for choosing between them in the room, or is that precisely the point where the procedure hands the decision back to me as a judgment call? Be honest about which it is.

3. The failure modes. The scale-invariance axioms half-remembered from my economics training seem to promise that units do not matter, but I suspect they protect the theorist, not the mediator: tell me exactly what normalization I must impose on the parties' reported numbers before computing either solution, and precisely what a party can achieve by misreporting the shape of their preferences or the size of their best case under each solution. Also: what to do when one party's disagreement point includes the option of destroying value out of spite — should the procedure count that as part of their alternatives, and if not, on what grounds do I exclude it?

4. Three worked cases, run through the procedure end to end. First: two founders dissolving a company, dividing cash, IP, and a non-compete, where one has a standing job offer and the other does not. Second: two equal shareholders separating, where one will keep operating the business and the other will exit — the continuing owner's upside from any settlement is much larger than the exiting owner's. Third: a case where the procedure gives an answer I will not like. Choose that case yourself, though I suspect it is the one where Kalai–Smorodinsky hands the greedier bargainer more money simply because they claimed a bigger number as their best case, or where a party is rewarded for an inflated walk-away. Run it honestly and tell me what I should say to the client who ends up with the short end of that arithmetic.

5. A closing statement of what the procedure cannot do: where the axioms are doing normative work that no amount of elicitation can justify, and where a mediator who follows this procedure faithfully will still be imposing values rather than applying fairness — so I know which parts of my recommendation are mine.

I would rather have the real Nash and Kalai–Smorodinsky solutions with their real axioms, however awkward they are at a mediation table, than a split-the-difference heuristic wearing their names.
