I run a small philosophy-of-physics podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who thinks Gödel was basically right that time is ideal — that the passage of time isn't a feature of objective reality, just something we project onto it. His case rests on the rotating-universe solution Gödel found to Einstein's equations back in the late 40s (the one he presented as a birthday gift for Einstein): a universe where spacetime is curved in such a way that you get closed timelike curves, so in principle a traveler returns to their own past, and there is no physically privileged way to slice things up into a global "now". Gödel's move was that if such a universe is even possible, then the objective flowing present can't be a necessary feature of reality, and so time in our universe must be ideal too.

Draft me eight to ten questions that go beyond having him restate all that. I want at least one question on each of the following, plus a follow-up for each keyed to the answer he's most likely to give:

- The gap between "time is unreal in the Gödel universe" and "time is unreal in ours". Our universe doesn't seem to rotate or have those closed curves, so why should a merely possible world without objective time tell us anything about the actual one? I'd expect him to lean on the thought that possibility is somehow enough here, so the follow-up should push on the obvious counter-analogy: a possible world without, say, magnetic fields doesn't make magnetism "ideal" in worlds that have them — what makes time different?

- Whether the rotating universe is even doing any work. Special relativity already pushed us toward relativized simultaneity before Gödel came along, so maybe you get most of the way to "no objective global now" without any exotic cosmology. If so, is his position really just an argument from Minkowski spacetime plus a distrust of the moving present? And if the guest concedes that, ask what the closed timelike curves add beyond rhetorical drama — is it just the time-travel vibes making the conclusion feel more shocking?

- The closed timelike curves themselves. A standard reaction is that they show our universe simply isn't like Gödel's, not that time is unreal. Related: the grandfather-paradox stuff — does the guest think a traveler could change the past, or is the world self-consistent about it, and does either answer matter for the bigger argument about time's reality?

- Whether the conclusion is too strong. Maybe what the physics really shows is not that time is ideal, but that simultaneity is conventional, or that "now" is indexical like "here" — the moving spotlight dies but temporal structure survives. I want a question that offers him this retreat route and asks him to say why he won't take it.

- Something on how his view cashes out in practice — if the passage of time is a subjective artifact, what does that change about, e.g., our sense that decisions make the future open while the past is fixed? Is the asymmetry between past and future also ideal, and if he says yes, what's left of anything?

Keep the questions conversational, like something I'd actually ask out loud — I don't want them reading like a comprehension quiz from a textbook. And since he's the type to answer everything with "well, that's a question about our concepts, not about reality", make at least one follow-up hard to deflect with that move.
