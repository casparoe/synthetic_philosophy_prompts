After a career in experimental physical chemistry — four decades of least-squares refinement, yield optimization, and the occasional unconscionable number of failed reactions — I retired and have since devoted an embarrassing amount of time to philosophy. I now convene a monthly discussion group of about ten retired scientists and engineers, and next month's session falls to me to lead. The topic I have chosen is Herbert Simon's work on bounded rationality and satisficing, partly because the man won a Nobel in economics for it and my members respect prizes, and partly because I have slowly come to suspect it is aimed squarely at people like us.

The difficulty is precisely that my audience will arrive confident they already understand the idea, and in the wrong way. These are people who spent their working lives maximizing objective functions and will therefore hear "satisficing" either as a polite word for laziness and corner-cutting, or as nothing more than optimization under resource constraints — the same maximization they always did, with a search cost tacked onto the objective. I suspect the deeper point is that Simon was revising what it means for a decision to be rational at all, not merely describing the frictions that keep us from attaining classical rationality, but I am not confident I can draw that boundary sharply myself, which is one reason I want a plan rather than a lecture script.

So please plan the session rather than explain the material to me. The session is ninety minutes with a whiteboard, and the members, while sharp, distrust anything that smells of psychology and will roll their eyes at the word "heuristic." I would like: the order in which the components should be introduced and what depends on what (for instance, whether my idea of starting from our own practice — model selection and curve fitting, where nobody ever searches the space of all possible models — is a genuine bridge or a trap that reinforces the constrained-optimization misreading); the single carrying example for the whole evening, and what it must have and lack (I have considered apartment hunting, a foraging animal, and Simon's chess players, but I would gladly be talked into something better suited to this crowd); the misconceptions they will form, in the order they will form them, and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; one question I can pose at the end that would distinguish a member who has genuinely grasped the revision of rationality from one who has merely nodded along; and what I should openly decline to cover at this level — I assume the later fast-and-frugal research program and the dispute over whether satisficing is a normative or a merely descriptive claim are candidates, but tell me if I am wrong.
