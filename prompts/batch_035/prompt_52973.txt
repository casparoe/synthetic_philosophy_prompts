I'm a PhD student in philosophy of law, and I'd like help unpacking a puzzle about the idea/expression dichotomy in copyright. Copyright protects the expression of a work but, in principle, never its underlying ideas; courts implement this through doctrines like merger, under which expression loses protection when there are only a few practical ways to express an idea, and scènes à faire, under which stock elements conventional to a genre are treated as unownable, and in infringement cases judges compare works at successively more abstract levels of description, protecting the plot at a fairly abstract level while refusing protection to a one-line premise. The practice thus seems to presuppose a substantive theory of what an idea is and how ideas are individuated — one on which ideas come already carved into levels of abstraction, with a natural cutoff somewhere between the free premise and the owned plot — and yet the same judges insist that ideas as such are simply not a proper subject of property. What exactly does the practice presuppose here, which rival theories of idea individuation would quietly rule it out, and where is it philosophically underdetermined, such that several different theories would license the same doctrinal results? Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
