At our reading group on Wittgenstein's On Certainty, our convener has taken to issuing rulings on the cases we bring her, and we have started keeping a record. Her verdicts so far:

1. A subject looking at her own hands in good light, with no one challenging her: she knows she has hands.
2. The same subject, with a Cartesian in the room demanding that she rule out being a brain in a vat: the convener refuses to say the subject lacks knowledge. Her ruling was that the demand never gets a grip — nothing in the subject's life could count for or against it, so it is not a doubt in the sense that matters, and asking for evidence concedes too much to the challenger.
3. A person holding one of ten million lottery tickets who says he knows his ticket will lose: no knowledge. Here, she said, the doubt does get a grip; the possibility of winning is live in the practice, unlike the vat.
4. A subject who has never doubted that the Earth existed long before his birth and has no evidence for that proposition, as such: his certainty is in order. It is not grounded on evidence and does not need to be; it is the background that makes his other inquiries — dating rocks, reading histories — possible.
5. A man who has come to genuinely wonder, daily, whether the world might have begun five minutes ago, complete with apparent traces: his problem is not a shortage of evidence he could go and get. The doubt has slipped out of any use; offering him evidence misses the point, and what he needs is not an argument.

Next week we bring her three new cases, and I would like your predictions before we meet, along with the reasoning behind them:

Case A. A student stands up and performs the Moorean routine: he holds up his hands, announces that there are at least two external things, and concludes that skepticism about the external world is refuted. Does she accept this as a proof? In particular, what does she say about the premises — that they are known, that they are warranted without evidence, or that the question of warrant simply does not apply to them — and does the conclusion inherit whatever status the premises have?

Case B. A surveyor takes a theodolite out of its box for the first time and, having no way of checking it that does not already involve using it, trusts it and takes a reading. Does he thereby know what the reading says about the terrain? And if he does not know, is his trust nevertheless in order?

Case C. A community in which a sect has made the five-minutes hypothesis locally pressing: the question is raised at dinner, children are taught it, and doubts about traces are treated on a par with ordinary doubts about whether the stove is lit. A member of this community looks at a photograph and claims to know it was taken last year. Has the doubt, now that it has a place in their practice, deprived him of knowledge — or does her ruling on the obsessive in verdict 5 carry over unchanged?

What I want is not just the three predictions. Her track record looks consistent with more than one rule: a hinge rule, on which certain propositions are exempt from doubt and certainty about them is not an evidential achievement; a relevant-alternatives rule, on which knowledge requires ruling out the error-possibilities that matter in the practice, and the vat possibility simply never matters; and a non-evidential entitlement rule, on which trust in such background propositions is rationally permitted without amounting to knowledge. For each new case, tell me what each rule predicts, note where the rules diverge, give your best guess as to her actual ruling together with your reasons, and — this is the part I most want — invent the further case that would most cleanly separate the rules that her record so far leaves tied.
