I work for a small foresight unit that advises a government department. We don't predict the future — we write scenario briefs — and one thing that keeps happening is that a brief leans on a historical analogy: someone will write that a trend could produce "a Bronze Age style collapse," or that a region is entering its "Terminal Classic," and nobody downsteam knows what work the word "collapse" is actually doing. Meanwhile whenever we touch history we get letters from archeologically-minded readers telling us the Maya didn't collapse, they reorganized, and that "collapse" talk is basically Victorian. I've half-convinced myself there's a middle position here: "collapse" is a legitimate word but only under conditions, and most public uses fail those conditions. I have a sketch of one test I call the obituary test — talk of collapse is licensed only if the people who lived through the aftermath would themselves, looking back, report a discontinuity: their world's institutions and way of life ended, not just our maps of it. But that's a sketch, not a procedure, and I suspect it has holes (which survivors count? the elites lost everything, the peasants carried on — who's the informant?).

So: turn this into a procedure a junior analyst can run in an afternoon on any use of "collapse" in a draft brief. The procedure should list its questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, mark the points where it returns a flat "rewrite the sentence" and the points where it has to hand off to a senior editor because the question is one of values not facts, and be honest about which steps are really judgment dressed up as process. Then run it on three cases: the Maya lowlands in the Terminal Classic, the end of Roman Britain, and Easter Island after European contact. At least one of the three must come out against my own instinct — I'm fond of the "it was transformation, not collapse" line for the Maya, and if the procedure says that's wrong for our purposes, I want to hear it.

Some constraints, so you know what it's for: maybe half a dozen briefs a year raise this, so the procedure can afford to be a bit slow, but it has to be runnable by someone with no archaeology background and no time for anything past the standard textbook story. If a historical detail matters, describe it as "the standard story goes" rather than asserting numbers.

Format the answer with these sections in this order: "The idea in one paragraph" (short), "The procedure" (numbered, roughly a page), "Three worked cases" (a few paragraphs each, including the one I won't like), "Where a human has to decide" (short list), and "What this procedure can't do" (one honest paragraph). Plain language, no citations.
