I'm a retired pharmacologist — thirty-odd years running toxicology screens, so forgive me if I keep wanting to put numbers on things — and since retiring I've got properly stuck into philosophy, mostly ethics. Most Thursdays I play chess with Margaret, who sat as a Crown Court judge for twenty years and is the only person I know who'll argue about moral luck over the board. We got onto it after I read one of those old essays from the seventies with the two drivers — the careless driver who kills a child and his twin who gets home — and I was surprised how little her instincts line up with the essayists, or for that matter with actual sentencing practice.

Here is her record, as it has come out across our arguments. On the two drivers she is definately firm: equally guilty, and she'd have given them the same sentence — she called the alternative a lottery, and said the lucky one isn't a safer driver or a better man, just a fortunate one. She says the same about a pair of equally sloppy surgeons when one of the patients dies on the table. And when I asked about a hit-and-run driver who only failed to flee because a patrol car happened to be behind him, she shrugged that the police car changes nothing about his guilt. She also has no time for excuses from upbringing or temperament — that a habitual drunk driver's disposition was the bad luck of his childhood cut no ice with her at all. But then the assassin: a man who fires at his victim and the gun jams — him she'd sentence lighter than if it had gone off, and she wasn't having it when I pointed out that this is the same lottery again. She just said the two situations aren't alike and left it there, which as an old lab man I find maddening. I want a rule, not a vibe.

So here's the case I mean to put to her next Thursday, and I'd like your help thinking it through beforehand, because I honestly can't predict her. Two men each plant an incendiary device at a warehouse, both intending only to burn the company's records, both fully aware that a guard is on site all night, both aware he will very likely die, and both equally indifferent to whether he does. One device ignites and the guard dies; the other fizzles out and the guard lives. Same intention, same knowledge, same indifference — the only difference being which way the luck fell. Does she treat them like the drunk drivers, outcome irrelevant, or like the assassin, outcome aggravating? Tell me what rules could actually fit her record so far — I can see at least the one where outcomes only matter when they were aimed at, versus one where they matter once the risk was near enough certain, and I suspect there are others — then say which rule fits best and why, give me your honest prediction for Thursday, and tell me which further case, depending on how she answers, would best seperate the remaining candidate rules. And please don't just tell me she's being inconsistent; I'd worked that much out for myself.
