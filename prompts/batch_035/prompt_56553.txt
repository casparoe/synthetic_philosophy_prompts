So heres my situation: I organize monthly continuing-ed sessions for a mixed audience — addiction counselors, a couple of probation officers, one or two social workers. Nobody in the room has read philosophy. Next month I drew the short straw for a session on Bernard Williams's distinction between internal and external reasons for action. Our clinical director heard about it somewhere and thinks it connects to motivational interviewing, the whole "meet the client where they are" thing. I've got 40 minutes, a whiteboard, no slides.

I don't want you to just explain the distinction to me — I more or less get it. I want a plan for the explanation. Act like a philosopher who's spent years training non-academics, and before you commit to anything, brainstorm two or three possible orderings and tell me briefly why you rejected the ones you rejected. Then give me:

- The order the pieces have to go in and why. I keep going back and forth on whether to define the internal reason first through the agent's motivational set (Williams's "S", though I dont want the session leaning on jargon), or instead start from the cases the counselors already fight about — "he has every reason to stop and no reason to drink." What has to be in place before the distinction does any work at all?
- One carrying example for the whole session. My instinct is a client whose motivational set plausibly contains something deliberation could reach — he genuinely loves his kid, say — but whose pull toward the substance is drowning that out right now. The example has to be one where it's NOT tempting for the room to just say "well, the external reasons are the real reasons" and be done with it. If you know what I mean.
- The misconceptions this audience will form, in the order they'll form them, and where in the sequence to head each one off. My guesses: internal = selfish reasons, external = moral reasons; internal reasons are fixed facts about the person that counseling can't touch; and "external reasons" just means reasons God or morality hands you, which half the room will love and half will hate.
- A closing check: one question I could ask that would tell me whether they actually got it, especially something that could catch the difference between "internal reasons are whatever the client currently wants" and the subtler version.
- What I should openly refuse to cover at this level. I'm assuming the stuff about reasons for belief is out, and probably the whole debate with McDowell and Korsgaard too — but tell me if I'm wrong about that.

Also, and this matters to me: be up front about where this plan is most likely to fail. If you think the counselors' "client-centered" framework actually cuts against Williams rather than mapping neatly onto him, I'd rather hear that now than after the session. And tell me what would change your mind about the ordering you end up proposing — what would a room like this do, in the moment, that would show the sequence was wrong?
