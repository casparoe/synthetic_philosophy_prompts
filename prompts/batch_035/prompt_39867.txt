For a chapter on commodification, I've been cataloguing my adviser's rulings on contested markets, and her track record is starting to look like a little jurisprudence. So far: she holds that a market in kidneys from living sellers should stay banned; that selling one's vote should be banned outright, not merely regulated; and that charging ruinous prices for generators after a hurricane is exactly the sort of transaction the law should stop. On the other side, she has no problem with the American practice of paying plasma donors; she approves of paying healthy volunteers in phase-one drug trials, including fairly risky ones; and when our seminar debated egg donation, she defended donor compensation without hesitation. I once pressed her on the fact that many paid plasma donors in the United States are themselves poor, and it didn't move her at all. Two new cases have now come up, and I have to predict her line on both. The first is a reformed, regulated kidney-purchase system — modeled on the Iranian one but with long-term follow-up care and a screening process that reliably excludes anyone selling out of financial desperation, so the sellers would be comfortable volunteers accepting a fair, posted price. The second is a small EU member state selling citizenship outright to wealthy investors, with no residence or integration requirement. I can see at least two principles that fit everything she has said so far — one that asks what money does to the meaning of the good itself, and one that asks about the position and options of the parties at the moment of sale — and I suspect they come apart on these two cases. Predict her verdict on each, set out the candidate principles that fit her record, tell me which one you would bet on and why, and propose the single further case that would best discriminate among the principles.
