I'm a senior analyst at a nonpartisan legislative research office. My team drafts and reviews policy briefs for state legislators — about six to ten a month — and one recurring failure mode is what our director calls "evidence drag": a brief piles up descriptive findings (obesity rates, crash statistics, wage data) and then slides, without ever stating the premise doing the work, into a recommendation. When challenged, drafters say the evidence "speaks for itself." Sometimes it nearly does; sometimes the missing premise is the whole argument, and one that a sizable share of our readers reject. Because we are nonpartisan, we cannot tell drafters which values to endorse — but we do need a defensible way to tell them when their argument silently relies on one.

What I want from you is a review procedure, grounded in the actual philosophy here, that my analysts can run on any draft brief. To build it well, you'll need to respect a distinction that popular treatments blur: the is-ought problem is about inference (descriptive premises purporting to entail a normative conclusion), whereas Moore's naturalistic fallacy and open-question argument are about definition (identifying the property of goodness with some natural property). The procedure should draw on both where each actually applies, and should also engage the standard challenges to the gap's universality: Searle's derivation of an obligation from institutional facts, the phenomenon of thick ethical terms that are simultaneously descriptive and evaluative, and the point that many apparently factual premises are already covertly normative.

The procedure itself should be an ordered checklist — the questions to ask of a brief, in sequence, with criteria for what counts as an adequate answer at each step, explicit stopping points where the analyst flags the brief for revision, and clearly marked handoff points where the question is one of values rather than of argument structure and must go to a person rather than a rule. It should distinguish at least these three outcomes: (1) a genuine unbridged inference, where a normative conclusion rests on purely descriptive premises and the bridge premise must be surfaced; (2) a false alarm, where the normativity was present in the premises all along — in institutional facts, in thick terms, in evaluatively loaded concepts like "harm" or "need" — and flagging a gap would be pedantry; and (3) the genuinely hard middle, where the descriptive and normative can be pried apart only at the cost of mangling the argument. It should incorporate a usable version of the open-question test as a diagnostic for outcome (1) — when substituting the natural property for the normative term leaves a substantive question rather than a triviality — while being honest about the test's limits as Moore himself and later critics understood them.

Then run the procedure, step by step and showing its work, on these four passages of the kind we actually see:

A. "Seat belt use reduces fatal-injury risk by roughly 45%. Despite decades of public campaigns, compliance in our state remains 88%, well below the national leader. We recommend primary-enforcement seat belt legislation."

B. "Humans are an intensely social species with an evolved need for belonging; loneliness among adults under 30 has risen sharply over two decades. Civic associations — leagues, choirs, service clubs — are the natural vehicle for this need. The legislature should fund community-civic grants."

C. "Under the state statute, a landlord must return a security deposit within 30 days of lease termination or provide an itemized statement of deductions; the deposit in question was neither returned nor accounted for after 90 days. The tenant is therefore entitled to the statutory penalty."

D. "Paid family leave is associated with measurable improvements in infant health and maternal workforce retention. Several peer states have enacted it. We recommend a twelve-week paid family leave program."

Note that our office endorsed D in principle last session, and I personally support it — so pay special attention to what the procedure does with it. If the procedure flags D's inference in exactly the way it flags A's, that is a feature, not a bug, and I want the write-up to say so plainly; a procedure that goes easy on arguments we like is worthless to us. Include at least one passage where the procedure returns an answer I will find inconvenient, and at least one where it correctly declines to flag.

Finally, tell me where the procedure fails. I'm thinking specifically of: briefs whose normative premises are so widely shared in our readership that surfacing them would look obtuse (does the procedure have a materiality threshold, and can one be principled rather than political?); cases where every available phrasing of the bridge premise itself contains thick terms, so the flag just relocates the problem; and the risk that a formal gap-flagging procedure becomes a rhetorical weapon — a way to bury arguments one dislikes under procedure. Say what the procedure cannot do and where it should simply hand the draft back with a note saying the value question is the whole question.

Assume the analyst running this has a bachelor's degree, no philosophy, and about twenty minutes per brief. Keep the philosophical content load-bearing but invisible in the final output: the checklist itself should use plain language a legislative staffer would not blush to circulate, with the theory confined to the rationale you give me for each step.
