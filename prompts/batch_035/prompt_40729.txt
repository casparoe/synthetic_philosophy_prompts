I'm deep in one of those decisions everyone warns you about: I have two job offers and about a week to pick. One is at a small nonprofit in the college town I already live in, walking distance from my partner's lab, pays okay, work I believe in. The other is a corporate job in a city I've never lived in, way more money, a much faster career track, but I'd be leaving basically everyone I know. I sat down and did the responsible thing, a big pro/con list, and when I showed it to my friend she just kind of waved at it and said I was comparing apples and oranges, that the list was pointless because the things in the two columns aren't the same kind of thing at all, and I might as well flip a coin as pretend some weighing exercise was going to settle it. That bugged me but I couldn't quite say why, since honestly the columns DON'T line up nicely -- I can't put "meaningful work" and "$40k more a year" on the same scale in any obvious way.

Then, to make it worse, I mentioned this to a coworker and he said oh yeah, there's a philosopher who talks about this, Ruth Chang, who has this whole thing about hard choices where the options are what she calls on a par -- neither better nor worse, but not exactly equal either -- and her takeaway (as he told it, maybe badly) is that in those cases you can actually create reasons by committing to one option, which is either really profound or complete nonsense, I can't tell. I watched some of her TED talk but I'm not sure how the pieces fit together with what my friend said. My friend's move seems to be: the comparison is impossible, so the deliberation is a sham, coin flip. Chang's move seems to be roughly: the comparison is weird in a specific way, and that's exactly why deliberation still matters. Those can't both be right about my situation, or maybe they're using "can't compare" in different senses entirely.

So here's what I want: take the apples-and-oranges line apart. What are the actual distinct precise claims someone could be making when they say you can't compare two options like this -- everything from the obviously false reading (we compare incomparable-seeming things all the time, that's what supermarkets and pro/con lists are) to readings about there being no common measuring unit, no single scale, no way to aggregate, vs. just no fact of the matter about which is better. For each reading, say whether it's plausible as applied to my choice, and crucially whether it actually supports what my friend used it for, i.e. ditching the pro/con list and going to a coin. And then fit Chang's on-a-par idea into that menu somewhere -- which reading of incommensurability (if any) is she working with, and does her claim about creating reasons through commitment survive if you read her the way my friend reads the slogan? I want a list of the readings with verdicts I can actually think against, not just a summary of her talk. Please write it in American English.
