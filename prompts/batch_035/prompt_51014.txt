I have taught a seminar on liberalism and its critics for over a decade, and the same pattern repeats every year: we work through Rawls, then Sandel, MacIntyre, Taylor, and Walzer, and by the end the students divide into camps that argue past one another without either camp giving ground. What strikes me is how little of the impasse tracks the arguments as written. When my liberal students are pressed, they concede readily that Rawls never needed the "unencumbered self" the communitarians attack — the original position is a device of representation, not a theory of personhood — and when my communitarian students are pressed, they concede that no serious liberal ever held that individuals are prior to their attachments in any metaphysical sense. Yet the concessions change nothing. Meanwhile, the historical literature has absorbed the clarifications — Rawls recharacterizing his view as political rather than metaphysical, liberals taking on board that selves are socially constituted, communitarians retreating to pluralism about value — and the debate did not resolve so much as deflate, with both sides quietly annexing the other's points and continuing to disagree about something.

My working hypothesis is that this was never one debate but two run under a single label: a debate in moral psychology about how selves and their commitments are constituted, and a debate in political theory about what a state may legitimately presuppose about its citizens — and that the two are largely orthogonal, so that each side's standard replies address a question the other side is not asking. But I have begun to suspect something further, from an episode outside the seminar: on a faculty committee drafting a speech-and-inclusion policy, my self-described communitarian colleagues argued exactly like procedural liberals, insisting that the policy be neutral among conceptions of the good, while my liberal colleagues argued from thick, constitutive commitments about what a university is. That reversal suggests the positions may be indexed to context rather than to a stable disagreement at all.

Diagnose the disagreement for me. Is it verbal, resting on an ambiguity in words like "neutral" and "constitutive"? Is it substantive but at a level deeper than either camp's stated claims — perhaps a dispute about whether political justification must be categorical or can be tradition-anchored? Are the sides, as I hypothesize, answering different questions under one name, or is my two-debates framing itself too tidy? Draw on what the principals actually argue, not on textbook caricatures, and be specific about which passages of the argument do the stalling. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a distinction that would disaggregate the debate, a case or thought experiment that would force the camps apart in a way seminar discussion has not, or an argument that no resolution should be expected and why. Finally, state plainly how confident you are in each part of the diagnosis, and single out the point about which you are least sure — I would rather know where your account is thin than receive a seamless one.
