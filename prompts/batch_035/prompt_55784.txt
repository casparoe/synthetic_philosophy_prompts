I've been a small-animal vet for about twenty years, so I've done more euthanasias than I've had hot dinners — most of them, honestly, a relief for everyone in the room, including the patient. Lately the assisted dying debate is everywhere (there's a bill going through parliament here at the moment), and I keep feeling like the two sides are speaking a language I half recognise but can't quite place. So here's my ask: restate the whole debate — the case for legalising assisted dying and the case against — in the vocabulary I actually use at work, as faithfully as you can.

To be clear about what that vocabulary is: quality-of-life scoring sheets we send home with owners rather than 'dignity'; the welfare needs; the question of whether the animal is suffering versus is going to suffer; the owner as proxy decision-maker when the patient can't speak for itself; economic euthanasia (putting to sleep a perfectly rehomeable dog because the owner can't afford the surgery or is emigrating); the 'when to say when' conversation at the consult table; drug doses as one continuum — a bit more buprenorphine tonight, a bit more, and eventually the pentobarbitol — rather than a bright line between relieving and killing; the clients who push back and say we're playing God; the newer pawspice idea of trying to let them die naturally at home with palliative care instead.

Translate both sides honestly — don't make the anti side look silly by mapping it onto the guilty owner who refuses euthanasia because they can't face the grief, and don't make the pro side look obvious by mapping it onto my normal Tuesday afternoon. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the human debate translate cleanly, which parts only survive with distortion, and which parts have no expression at all in my vocabulary. My hunch is the sticking point is consent, since none of my patients has ever asked me for anything — but tell me if that's right, or whether there's more in the residue than just consent (sanctity of life, maybe? I genuinely don't know what I'd map that onto). And then tell me where the residue points: is my vocabulary missing something the human debate genuinely needs, or is the human debate partly a fight over words that my profession quietly settled decades ago, and if the latter, why does it still feel so much heavier to me than a normal Tuesday?

No reading lists and no citations — I want the reasoning, not a bibliography. At the very end, put a final line of the form \answer{...} containing a short phrase naming the single concept from the human debate that translates worst into my vocabulary.
