I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training beyond what leaks into podcasts, and I've spent the last month in a running argument with my old roommate, who minored in philosophy and is a devoted virtue ethicist. It started when a podcast I listen to covered the famous study where seminary students hurrying to give a talk about the Good Samaritan parable stepped around a man slumped in a doorway, and the strongest predictor of whether they stopped was whether they'd been told they were already late. I took this as proof that "character" as my roommate describes it barely exists; he countered that a virtue is precisely a disposition that holds up under pressure, that the study showed the students lacked virtues rather than that virtues are fictional, and that honestly-as-a-global-trait has been shaky since those 1920s studies of schoolchildren cheating in some contexts and not others anyway. We've reached the stage of the argument where we're each talking slightly past the other, which I'm told is traditional.

For his birthday I want to commission a piece of satire that's fair to both of us. Write a parody of a consumer warranty agreement — the folded leaflet kind, with numbered clauses — for a company that sells certified character traits as commercial products: "Honesty (Home Edition)," "Compassion Pro," "Courage 3.0: now with anger management." About 700 words, in deadpan legalese throughout. The comedy should come from the fine print quietly conceding everything the situationists found: exclusions of liability when the user is in a hurry, in a bad mood, or has recently found a coin on the ground; a "cross-situational consistency" clause in which the trait is only warranted to manifest in statistically aggregated form; a deductible for hostile bystanders; a forum-selection clause requiring disputes to be adjudicated in whichever environment flatters the product. But work his side in too, so it reads as fair rather than as my side gloating: buried clauses where the company's lawyers seem to have been forced to acknowledge that a genuine trait differs from a situational accident, that long-term patterns can be real even when any single behavior isn't predictive, and that a virtue supposedly includes noticing what the situation calls for — perhaps a clause distinguishing product failure from the trait correctly declining to fire. The target is the whole idea of traits as detachable products, not virtue ethics itself.

Two constraints: no named living people as characters, and the piece should stay deadpan — the reader should sense both the situationist concessions and the virtue-ethicist rejoinders without the narrator winking at either. After the piece itself, append a short author's note (a few sentences, not a bibliography) flagging which gags lean on classic findings that are contested or have replication problems — I'm told some of the helping-behavior literature has taken hits — and which jokes depend on a reading of virtue ethics that an actual Aristotelian would dispute. In that note, also say outright which single liberty you took with the philosophy or the psychology you're least confident about.
