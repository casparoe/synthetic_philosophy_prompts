Two coworkers of mine have been debating how to classify, in Daniel Dennett's terms, the AI assistant our company uses. (His hierarchy, roughly: Darwinian creatures have fixed, hard-wired behavior shaped only by natural selection; Skinnerian creatures can be re-shaped within a lifetime by conditioning on the consequences of their actions; Popperian creatures test candidate behaviors internally before acting, so that bad moves can be discarded in preview rather than tried out in the world; Gregorian creatures, named for Richard Gregory, additionally import cultural mind-tools — words, notations, algorithms, instruments — that boost their thinking.)

The dispute went as follows. (1) Marta argued the assistant is at best Skinnerian: its behavior was shaped during training by trial and error against rewards, and at deployment it merely emits whatever that history selected, without trying anything out or learning anything new. (2) Jonas replied that when the assistant drafts a candidate answer, checks it, and revises it before responding, it is doing exactly what Popperian creatures do — previewing candidate moves and rejecting the bad ones internally rather than in the world. He added that because it can call external tools like web search and a calculator, it plausibly counts as Gregorian as well. (3) Marta countered that the draft-check-revise routine was itself installed by training, so the system is Skinnerian all the way down: the previews are not its own hypotheses but patterns its training happened to reward, and a Skinnerian creature that runs quickly is still not testing hypotheses. (4) Jonas said that by that standard no human is Popperian either, since our capacity to imagine and internally test scenarios is likewise a product of evolution and past learning; Dennett's hierarchy classifies what a system does now, not the causal history of the capacity that lets it do that. He also noted that the tool-use point survives her objection untouched, since profiting from cultural mind-tools is Gregorian even if the disposition to use them was trained in. (5) For a last word, Marta claimed the hierarchy was never meant to grade artifacts at all: Dennett's rungs correspond to different processes that shape behavior — selection, conditioning, internal hypothesis-testing, transmission of cultural tools — and shaping by gradient descent is simply not one of the processes the framework recognizes, so applying it to a trained model is a category error.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. Then say who is more right overall, and whether Marta's final challenge really shows the hierarchy cannot classify such systems, or whether it merely shows the classification is contested.
