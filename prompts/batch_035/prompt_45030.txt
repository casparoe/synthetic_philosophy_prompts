For most of my career I was a behavioral ecologist, and I spent a good many field seasons watching solitary wasps, so I was delighted when the philosophy reading group I joined after retirement came around to Douglas Hofstadter's notion of sphexishness. You likely know the background: the digger wasp Sphex drags her paralyzed cricket to the threshold of her burrow, leaves it outside, goes in to inspect, emerges, and only then hauls the cricket inside. If an experimenter nudges the cricket a few centimeters away while she is inside, she drags it back to the threshold and inspects the burrow all over again -- indefinitely, without ever catching on. Hofstadter coined the term for this kind of rigid, pre-programmed routine, and I gather it has become a stock illustration in free will discussions. I had always taken the wasp's behavior in stride as a professional matter, so I was unprepared for how badly the question of what, if anything, it says about human beings fractured our little group.

Two members of the group, Marcus and Elena, have been at it for three sessions now. Marcus, who writes control software for warehouse robots, claims the wasp experiment is the entire free will debate in miniature: every behavior is a triggered routine, ours included, and our felt deliberation is a narrative laid on top after the fact -- the wasp differs from us only in the complexity of her triggers. Elena, a neuroscientist, answered that this misses a difference in kind: the wasp cannot notice her own loop no matter how many repetitions, whereas a human can simply be told about the experiment and will not fall for it; we have exploitable blind spots, certainly, but having a blind spot is not the same as being trapped in a loop, since we can be fooled once by a magician and then never again in that way. Marcus replied that this only pushes the routine one level up -- noticing is itself a triggered process -- and that a sufficiently patient experimenter could trap any of us in some loop: he pointed to our habit-driven errors, to how we re-read a flawed proof of our own without seeing the flaw, and he proposed what he called a universal Sphex test, an experimenter with unlimited time and freedom to reset conditions who could eventually loop any mechanism, ourselves included; he added that bees and wasps can learn simple associations and still get trapped, so learning cannot be the line either. Elena's latest move is to accuse him of equivocating between two different claims: that we are bundles of fixed action patterns, and that we are deterministic mechanisms. If sphexishness means only the second, she says, the wasp was window dressing for an old compatibilism debate; if it means the first, then level matters, and being loopable in principle at some level no experimenter will ever actually reach is not being sphexish in any sense that threatens our agency.

They have each asked me privately whether I think they are winning, and I have honestly been unable to say, though my years with insects do make me suspect that whatever separates Elena from Sphex flavipennis is subtler than either of them has yet put it. Would you go through their exchange move by move -- Marcus's initial reduction, Elena's loop-noticing reply, Marcus's one-level-up and universal Sphex test, and Elena's equivocation charge -- and say who is right about which specific point, where each of them concedes more than they should or claims more than they have earned, and what, if anything, both of them are missing about what the wasp's rigidity actually shows?
