I'm writing an essay for a philosophy of mind course on Hofstadter's dialogue in Gödel, Escher, Bach — the one where Achilles and the Tortoise meet an Anteater who claims to be friends with an intelligent ant colony (Aunt Hillary, if I remember the name right). The idea is that the individual ants are dumb, but the colony-level activity constitutes a mind, sort of like how dumb neurons give rise to a brain. I've drafted some notes (below) and want feedback before turning them into the full essay.

The central argument I'm building is that the colony case forces a choice between two views of mind. Either mind is substrate-independent and multiply realizable, in which case the colony is genuinely intelligent (just implemented in a bizarre medium, with a bizarre clock speed and sensory apparatus), or you bite the bullet and say there's something special about biological brains, at which point you owe an account of what that something is. I claim that every disqualifying feature people point to — the loose coupling between "neurons" (ants wander around, a given ant isn't dedicated to one role like a neuron is), the slow timescale, the lack of a fixed architecture — also has some analogue in brains, just less extreme, so the colony sits on a continuum with us rather than on the far side of a sharp line. To back the continuum claim I want to bring in a couple of comparisons: slime molds solving shortest-path problems, and markets computing prices out of individually smart agents — the market case being the interesting one because it goes the other direction, a mind made of minds.

A few specific things I'd like comments on. First, is the continuum argument actually good, or does it just show that boundaries are vague? I vaguely feel like vagueness doesn't automatically get the colony into the club, and I'm not sure how to respond to that. Second, a counterargument I'm struggling with: in the colony case, the "mind" seems to exist mainly from the Anteater's point of view — he's the one who interprets the ant-level patterns as intelligence. Is this a serious objection (the mind is in the eye of the beholder, and without an interpreter like him there's no one home), or just a version of the standard observer-dependence worry about representation? Third, does the market analogy earn its place, or does it water the essay down? And finally, fact-check me: I describe the dialogue as including the Anteater claiming his eating of colonies actually helps them, and I call the ants "the symbols" of the colony-level mind — am I representing Hofstadter's actual view correctly, or am I smuggling in my own spin? General comments on structure and what's most/least interesting are welcome too.
