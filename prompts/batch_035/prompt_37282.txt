I am a PhD student in philosophy of history, and my advisor and an external examiner have been arguing for years about the slogan that history is written by the victors. My advisor holds a strong version: the asymmetries in source survival, narrative production, and canonization give us systematically distorted access to the past, and any history of the defeated has to be reconstructed against the grain of an archive built by the winners. The examiner thinks the slogan is a lazy aphorism that fails empirically, noting that some of the most durable narratives were produced by the losing side, from the Lost Cause mythology that dominated American memory of the Civil War for decades to the way Norman rule over England is known to us largely through monastic writers of the conquered population. Write the joint statement the two of them should be able to sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration: the claims both accept, including about archival asymmetries, curriculum canonization, and the cases where the defeated won the narrative; the precise points where they still disagree, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; the cruxes such that settling one would actually move at least one of them; and, for each crux, a concrete case, source corpus, or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If you conclude that part of their dispute is merely verbal, for instance about what counts as a victor, or about whether source survival and narrative control are the same thing, say which part and why. Please write the statement as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the style of a memorandum one of them would circulate to the other before a meeting.
