I've been reading Plutarch's Life of Pericles in the old Loeb translation (Perrin, 1916), and the little preface he attaches to it has a stretch of reasoning I can't reconstruct. He's explaining why writing lives is worth doing at all: he thinks admiring virtuous actions somehow produces an impulse to imitate, which he claims doesn't happen with other admirable things. Here's the relevant part, copied from the translation:

"It is meet, therefore, that he pursue what is best, to the end that he may not merely regard it, but also be edified by regarding it. . . . In other cases, admiration of the deed is not immediately accompanied by an impulse to do it. Nay, many times, on the contrary, while we delight in the work, we despise the workman, as, for instance, in the case of perfumes and dyes; we take a delight in them, but dyers and perfumers we regard as illiberal and vulgar folk. Therefore it was a fine saying of Antisthenes, when he heard that Ismenias was an excellent piper: "But he's a worthless man," said he, "otherwise he wouldn't be so good a piper." And so Philip once said to his son, who, as the wine went round, plucked the strings charmingly and skilfully, "Art not ashamed to pluck the strings so well?" It is enough, surely, if a king have leisure to hear others pluck the strings, and he pays great deference to the Muses if he be but a spectator of such contests. . . . No generous youth, from seeing the Zeus at Pisa, or the Hera at Argos, longs to be Pheidias or Polycleitus; nor to be Anacreon or Philetas or Archilochus out of pleasure in their poems. . . . But virtuous action straightway so disposes a man that he no sooner admires the works of virtue than he strives to emulate those who wrought them. The good things of Fortune we love to possess and enjoy; those of Virtue we long to perform. The former we are willing should be ours at the hands of others; the latter we wish that others rather should have at our hands."

Two things I'm stuck on. First, what is the actual argument here? I can see there's some snobbery about manual and banausic arts going on, and some idea that a king should be a spectator rather than a performer, but Plutarch mostly just asserts that admiring a craft makes you despise the craftsman while admiring virtue makes you emulate the virtuous man, and I cant see what separates the two cases. Is the thought that in a craft the excellence is separable from the person — the statue could've been made by anybody — while in virtue the "work" just is the man himself? Or is it purely the status thing, dyers and pipers are illiberal, end of story? And what work is the Antisthenes line even doing — being a great piper is supposed to be evidence of a worthless character? Why?

Second, and this is the thing I actually care about: I've been playing with the idea that ancient biography works structurally like later hagiography — a catalogue of small notables assembled on the theory that a concrete deed trains the reader's character better than a precept does, so the biographer's real unit of selection is "what's imitable," not "what's historically important." Does this preface support that reading, or am I overreading a rhetorical set piece that Plutarch dusts off for each new pair of lives? He does say the point is to be "edified by regarding" the good, but is that anything more than a fancy way of saying reading about good people is good for you?

Walk me through it like I'm smart but not a classicist. No need to cite scholarship at me, just explain what the passage is actually claiming and whether the imitation mechanism it gestures at could ever do the work he wants it to do.
