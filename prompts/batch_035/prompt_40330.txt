So our book club finally picked The Remains of the Day (Ishiguro), and I want to bring a specific ethics debate to the discussion that I keep turning over. The debate is about moral worth: does an action get extra credit from the reasons it's done from, and if so, what kind of reasons earn it? There are roughly three versions I care about:

1. the strict Kantian one — the action has moral worth only if done *because* it's right, which is why Schiller joked that you'd have to help your friends with visible aversion, so duty could show through;
2. the reliability one — what matters is that the agent dependably lands on the right thing, even via a slightly off mechanism (think of someone who's honest purely out of loathing for lying rather than out of principle);
3. the responsiveness one — the credit comes from the agent's motivation actually tracking the morally relevant features, even when the agent herself misdescribes what she's doing (Huck Finn helping Jim while sincerely believing he's sinning is the classic case).

Stevens seems like a perfect stress test for all three. In the scene where his father is dying upstairs during the big 1936 conference and he keeps serving drinks, he's about as from-duty as a human can be — but the duty is professional, arguably servile, and he also seems to be using it as a shield against grief. Or take his refusal to really engage when Miss Kenton tries to talk about her engagement, or the way he keeps insisting that questions about Lord Darlington's judgment aren't his to have, and then near the end admits he may have given his best to the wrong cause. And the very last stretch, the bench at the seaside and his resolve to practice bantering for his new American employer — is that the one morally creditable act of the whole book, and why does it feel so thin and yet so moving?

So the questions for you: which of the three views (if any) gives Stevens credit, and for what exactly? Does the strict Kantian view have to say the father scene has moral worth (duty!) or no moral worth (servility!), or is that a dilemma the view can escape? Does the novel show something all three views miss — Stevens is so self-deceived about his own motives that the question of what he acted *from* might not even have a determinate answer, which none of the theories seem built for? And be honest about where the lens distorts: its plausibly a novel about regret and wasted life rather than moral worth at all, so tell me if you think the moral-worth reading is forcing it.
