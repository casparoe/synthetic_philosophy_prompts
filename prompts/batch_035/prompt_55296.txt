I teach a monthly philosophy evening at my local public library. The audience is curious non-philosophers — mostly retirees and a few working professionals, several of whom have read a popular philosophy book or two, none with formal training. Next month I have forty-five minutes and a whiteboard on deflationary theories of truth, and I've learned from experience that this topic either lands beautifully or collapses into an hour of people talking past each other. Don't explain deflationism to me; plan how I should explain it to them.

What I want from you is the explanatory arc. Consider what has to be in place before the thesis itself can even be understood: I suspect we have to start with the *job* the truth predicate does rather than with any theory of it — specifically its role as a device of generalization, where "everything the meteorologist said this morning is true" lets us endorse claims we can't enumerate and couldn't list even in principle. If that's the right starting point, say so, and say what it must achieve: it should make the audience feel the puzzle that deflationism is an answer to, namely that once you've granted that snow is white iff the proposition that snow is white is true, it's hard to see what further property truth could be. Then tell me where the equivalence schema comes in the sequence, and how to present it so it reads as a generality about how we use the word rather than as a theory with a name attached.

The one example that carries the whole session, I'm thinking, is the unenumerable endorsement case — but you should judge whether that single example can do all the work (the utility of the predicate, the deflationist's diagnosis, and the response to the redundancy objection) or whether I need a second case where 'true' does something else, such as expressing agreement without re-asserting, as in "That's true" said to someone's story about their childhood. If you add a second case, tell me exactly where it enters and what misconception it heads off.

On misconceptions: I know the ones I'll get, but I want you to map them and place them in the sequence. The audience will arrive mixing deflationism up with relativism ("so truth is whatever you believe"); they'll hear the deflationist as saying truth doesn't matter or that we shouldn't care about it, which for many of them touches something close to a religious nerve; at least one person will propose that truth is what most people agree on; and the sharper ones will form a subtler error on their own, namely that deflationism claims 'true' is always deletable — at which point the generalization example is no longer doing its job and I'll need to show precisely where deletion fails. Tell me at which point in the sequence each of these should be intercepted, and how, without turning the evening into a defensive lecture about what the view is not.

Finally: what should I openly decline to explain at this level — I assume the Liar paradox is on that list, but say so explicitly and tell me how to wave it off honestly without implying it's unimportant — and what single question could I pose in the last five minutes that would let me see whether they've actually grasped the deflationary move rather than merely tolerated it?
