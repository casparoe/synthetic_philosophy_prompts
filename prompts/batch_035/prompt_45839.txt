A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about Keynes's philosophy of probability, and we ended the night exactly where we began, which is why I am writing this down. She is a historian of economic thought; I am a Bayesian by training and by trade. I build probabilistic models for a living, and my native vocabulary is priors, likelihoods, likelihood functions, conditioning, calibration, exchangeability, posterior predictive checks, and — when I am being careful — the distinctions between parameter uncertainty and model uncertainty, and between aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty. Her claim was that Keynes's A Treatise on Probability (1921) is not an early, half-formed version of Bayesianism but a genuinely different theory of what probability is, and that when people like me read it in our own vocabulary we quietly delete the most interesting parts. She may well be right. I am not in a position to check, because I cannot read Keynes in his own terms: every sentence of his I reach for turns into a sentence of mine before I have understood it.

So I want you to do the translation for me, honestly, and then report the residue. Restate Keynes's theory of probability in my vocabulary, component by component:

First, probability as a logical relation. Keynes held that probability is not a frequency and not a measure of anyone's actual confidence, but an objective relation between a conclusion and the premises that partially support it — with full entailment as the limiting case. In my vocabulary this presumably wants to be a prior, but that cannot be right as stated: priors in my world are chosen, by convention or regularization or reference rules, and nothing in my machinery makes one prior logically mandatory in the way Keynes seems to have wanted. Is there anything in my vocabulary that could play the role of a uniquely correct rational degree of belief, and if not, what exactly is lost?

Second, non-numerical and non-comparable probabilities. Keynes denied that all probabilities are even comparable: two probabilities can be such that neither is greater, nor equal, nor intermediate in any recognizable ordering. The natural translation on my side is imprecise probability — a credal set of distributions rather than a single one — but tell me whether that is faithful, or whether Keynes's non-comparability outruns any set of distributions, since a set still generates lower and upper expectations that are themselves comparable.

Third, the weight of arguments, to which he devotes a chapter of the Treatise: his distinction between how balanced the evidence is and how much evidence there is. My nearest analogues are posterior concentration and sample size, but Keynes thought added evidence could raise weight while leaving the probability where it was, and my conditioning rule has no separate dimension along which that happens — updating moves the distribution, full stop. What in my vocabulary, if anything, is weight?

Fourth, his later, more radical position. By the 1930s, most bluntly in his 1937 Quarterly Journal of Economics article, he was claiming that for many long-horizon economic magnitudes — the prospective yield of an investment over its life was his recurring example — there is no probability statement to make at all: not a flat prior, not a wide variance, but nothing that behaves like a distribution. The closest things I have are unmodeled structural uncertainty, model misspecification, and the informal practice of refusing to take a model's outputs seriously outside the regime it was fit in. Is that the same thing, or a polite family resemblance?

Then give me the residue report. Which of the four components translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion (and be explicit about what the distortion is), and which cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. Do not smuggle Bayesian assumptions into the translation and call them translation — if something only fits by assuming a unique prior or a total ordering of credences, say so. Finally, the question my friend and I could not settle: when something of Keynes's cannot be said in my vocabulary, is that because my vocabulary is missing something — should I want non-comparable credences, a weight-of-evidence dimension, logical constraints on priors that no machinery of mine enforces — or because he was pointing at a confusion that my vocabulary, by refusing to let it be stated, has actually resolved? Take a side, and tell me what would count as evidence for it.
