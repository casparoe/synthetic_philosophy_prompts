I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, so please go easy on the jargon. A while back I fell down a rabbit hole on the situationist critique of virtue ethics, and it seems like the whole debate just... froze around the mid-2000s. As I understand it: one side points at social psych experiments (Milgram, the seminary students who stepped over a slumped guy in a doorway because they were told they were late, the 1920s studies where kids who cheated on a spelling test were honest on the next one) and says behavior is driven by tiny situational details, not stable character traits, so a theory built around cultivating virtuous character is built on a fiction. The other side says the experiments only debunk a kind of cartoonish "person is honest in all contexts" trait that nobody actually believed in, and that everyday life gives us tons of evidence that some people are just more patient or kinder than others. Then everyone stopped moving.

My hunch, offered as just a hunch: the two camps aren't really answering the same question. One is asking what a scientifically respectable psychology would have to look like, the other is asking what an ethical theory is even *for* -- guidance, or an inventory of the personality -- and "character" means different things in each, so the experiments can't settle it the way the situationists want them to. Is that diagnosis right? Or is there a genuine empirical question hiding in here that, say, better longitudinal data on people over decades could actually resolve? Or maybe the ethical theory doesn't need the psychology at all, in which case the whole fight was confused from the start?

Whatever you conclude, I'd like you to say how confident you are in each step of the diagnosis, and call out the single part you're least sure about -- I'd rather know where the shaky joint is than get a confident-sounding answer that papers over it.
