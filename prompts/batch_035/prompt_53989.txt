The exam question was: "On Plato's Euthyphro: (a) State Socrates' question about piety and the gods, and explain why each of the two options is supposed to be a problem for the view that piety — or, in its modern form, moral obligation — consists in what God wills. (b) Describe one reply a contemporary divine command theorist might give, and one objection that can be raised against that reply."

The student wrote:

"Socrates asks Euthyphro whether the gods love the pious because it is pious, or whether it is pious because the gods love it. Both horns embarrass the divine command theorist. On the first, the gods' love is a response to something already pious, so the gods do not create the standard; their approval merely tracks an independent standard, and at most they inform us of it. The theorist wanted God's will to be the source of morality, and now it is not. On the second, piety is whatever the gods happen to love, so morality is arbitrary: had the gods loved cruelty, cruelty would have been pious, and it is a brute fact that they love what they love. The first horn makes God irrelevant to ethics; the second makes ethics a matter of divine whim.

The divine command theorist's best reply distinguishes God's will from God's nature. God's commands express a necessarily good nature; because God is essentially good, God cannot command cruelty, so the arbitrariness objection loses its grip. And since God's commands make things good, goodness itself is no longer independent of God either. Leibniz made essentially this argument in his correspondence with Clarke, concluding that because God always acts for sufficient reasons, God's commands are necessary rather than arbitrary, which vindicates the second horn.

The objection is that the modification only relocates the difficulty. Ask whether God's nature is good because God has it, or whether God has that nature because it is good. If the former, goodness is again arbitrary; if the latter, the standard of goodness is independent of God after all. Still, the reply succeeds against the original dilemma, which asked about the gods' love, not about God's nature, so the modified theory stands."
