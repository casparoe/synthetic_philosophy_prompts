I teach metaethics to third-years (about 14 students, one 2-hour session, they've already done the realism/anti-realism rounds and read Street's Darwinian Dilemma paper) and I want an in-class game that makes evolutionary debunking arguments land as an experience rather than a slide. The key thing: they shouldn't just be told that their moral intuitions have a genealogy — they should live through a selection process, and only afterwards find out it applies to their actual judgments.

Rough idea, feel free to rework it: a multi-round simulation where they play small bands of foragers, and the payoff rules quietly reward bands that develop intuitions like cheater-punishment, kin loyalty, disgust at freeriders — so that after a few rounds the surviving "tribes" have basically built the moral psychology we actually have. Then the twist: hand out a sheet of present-day moral claims and let them notice their own real judgments line up suspiciously well with whatever the game selected for. The debrief is where the actual philosophy happens: does selection-for-usefulness undermine truth-tracking? Does it matter that we'd probably have different intuitions if the payoffs had been different? And where does the debunker's argument break — our belief that fire is hot also has evolutionary roots but doesn't seem debunked by it, so what exactly is the difference supposed to be?

Please give me: rules and materials (no real money needed), round structure with timings, the payoff scheme, the twist sheet, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific steps of the debunking argument. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the real philosophical argument no simulation this simple can ever reproduce — the bit I'll have to just supply in the debrief.

One more thing: please dont make it feel like a lecture with dice bolted on. The selection pressure should be something they discover happened to them, not something announced up front. Untill the reveal they should think they're just playing a resource game.
