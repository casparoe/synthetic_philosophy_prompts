been wrestling with Kim's causal exclusion argument and the responses to it, and they all kind of blur together for me. The way I understand the argument: if a mental event M causes a physical event P, and M's physical realizer also causes P, then either P is overdetermined (weird when it'd happen with literally every action), or M causes P only by causing its own realizer (which just pushes the problem back a level), or M does no causal work at all. So mental causation is in trouble.

The escape routes I keep coming across, roughly:

(a) the "cause under a description" line — the mental and the physical event aren't two causes, they're one cause picked out in two ways

(b) the determinable/determinate move — the mental relates to the physical like being red relates to being scarlet, and scarlet doesn't compete with red for causal relevance

(c) interventionism — causation is about what would change under interventions, and you can intervene on a mental variable without holding the underlying physical stuff fixed

(d) just bite the bullet and go reductionist — the mental causes only because it just is some physical property

(e) accept epiphenomenalism or something close to it

Rank these five from most to least promising as a defense of mental causation actually being real, and give each a score out of 10. To keep it concrete, evaluate them against this case: a guy decides to wave at a friend across the street, and his arm goes up. For each of the five, say specifically whether the decision counts as a cause of the arm movement on that view, and whether it does any explanatory work that the full neurophysical story doesn't already do on its own. And say whether you actually buy that answer or if it's more of a "sounds nice, doesn't survive scrutiny" situation.
