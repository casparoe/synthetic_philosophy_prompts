I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm at work on a long-form feature about how different societies carve up work and leisure — the French 35-hour week, the Dutch part-time norm, Japanese overtime culture and its medicalized vocabulary of overwork death, the afterlife of the Protestant work ethic in American "workism," and the question of why Keynes's famous prediction of a fifteen-hour week never arrived. Before I draft, I've written down where I actually stand, and my editor has poked at two of the claims in ways I can't quite answer. Rather than have her do it again, I'd like an audit.

Here is the package, roughly as it appears in my notebook:

1. Keynes was essentially right in 1930 about technological capacity: we could now produce his era's standard of living in around fifteen hours a week, and the forecast failed not because he was wrong about productivity but because of choices we collectively made about what counts as enough.
2. Those choices are best explained by positional consumption — households chasing relative standing in a way that converts efficiency gains into arms races rather than leisure.
3. At the same time, a large share of the failure is explained by bullshit jobs: parasitic employment that exists because of managerial feudalism and the moralization of work itself, not because anyone wants the output.
4. The Protestant work ethic is a contingent cultural artifact, and on net a pathology — its distinctive move, treating industriousness as a virtue that signals worth, is something a good society would retire.
5. But work is also, and irreducibly, one of the few robust sources of meaning and structure in most lives, and I would be genuinely worried about a society that solved the problem of work too well and left people to their hobbies.
6. France's 35-hour week was defensible and mostly successful in the only sense that matters: it redistributed work and constrained employers without collapsing productivity, and doing it by statute was the point.
7. The Dutch model — where part-time work is a protected, widespread, individual choice rather than a mandate — is nonetheless the best actually existing arrangement for work time, precisely because it lets households differ.
8. Japan's sickness is not hours per se but the company-as-family fusion of identity and employment; interventions that merely shave hours while leaving that fusion intact, like the four-day schemes and leave policies that white-collar workers decline to use, are theater.
9. Even so, karoshi and its cousin karo-jisatsu justify legally binding hard caps on weekly hours, enforced regardless of employer culture.

Please assess whether this set hangs together. Which commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision?
