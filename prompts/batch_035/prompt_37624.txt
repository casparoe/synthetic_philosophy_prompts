I've never studied history past school — I work in insurance claims — but for the last few years I've been in a small history reading group that meets at a pub, and this year we've stumbled onto something I can't stop chewing on. It started when one member gave a talk on the Lost Cause of the Confederacy, the post-Civil War story that the war was about states' rights rather than slavery and that the defeated South had been noble rather than wrong. A few months later someone else presented on Masada, where the Jewish defenders held out against Rome and, according to Josephus, died by mutual agreement rather than surrender — a story modern Israel turned into a founding legend of defiance, with soldiers' swearing-in ceremonies on the plateau and the vow that Masada shall not fall again. Then came the Battle of Kosovo in 1389, where Serbian Prince Lazar was defeated by the Ottomans, and where the legend grew that he had been offered a choice between an earthly kingdom and a heavenly one, and deliberately chose the latter — a story invoked by Serbian nationalists six hundred years later. And finally Dunkirk, a military catastrophe in 1940 that the British recast almost immediately as a triumph of grit and civilian courage. Four defeats, four nations, four myths.

Here is the argument my group can't settle. My friend Rob claims that there's nothing special about defeats: nations mythologize their victories too, so the difference between the Lost Cause and, say, a celebration of some famous victory is just truthfulness — the Lost Cause lies about slavery, while Dunkirk and Masada are at bottom honest stories with some patriotic inflation. I'm not so sure. It seems to me that a defeat needs something a victory never does: an explanation for why the right side lost. And every one of these myths supplies it the same way, by detaching the moral verdict from the military outcome — Masada's defenders lose their fortress but win the argument; Lazar trades an earthly crown for a heavenly one; Dunkirk turns a rout into a moral victory; and the Lost Cause relocates the South's defeat onto a higher plane of gallantry and grievance, or blames it on betrayal. If that's the shared mechanism, then maybe the problem isn't just the false details — even the respectable myths quietly drop inconvenient parts, like the fact that our only source for the Masada story, Josephus, describes those same defenders as the Sicarii, who had earlier raided the nearby settlement of Ein Gedi and, by his account, killed some seven hundred people there. Rob says the Ein Gedi business just shows all commemorations are selective, and that selective isn't the same as falsifying.

So I'd like you to write an essay on this controversy: is the commemoration of lost causes falsifying by its very structure, or only by its content? Weigh the case that noble-defeat myths like Masada and Dunkirk are morally different in kind from the Lost Cause and its kin, against the case that any story whose job is to make defeat bearable must smuggle in the verdict that the cause deserved better than it got — and that this is corrosive no matter how accurate the details. Along the way, consider whether it matters that some of these myths consolidate a community against its own grief while others arm it against future enemies, and whether that difference tracks the noble/betrayed distinction at all. Please reach and defend a definite conclusion — ideally one that would tell our group whether a nation can ever commemorate a lost defeat honestly, and if so, what such a commemoration would have to leave out or refuse to claim.
