A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about reduction and emergence, and the phrase that kept doing the work on his side was "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts." He comes from systems biology, and I think he was using that slogan in two different ways at once. Sometimes he seemed to mean that a living cell has properties that no completed inventory of its molecules and their interactions could account for — a genuine metaphysical claim about what a full microdescription would leave out. Other times he meant only the practical point that we cannot, in practice, predict the cell's behavior by working up from molecular physics, because the behavior depends on organized structure: feedback loops, spatial arrangement, the fact that the components are wired into loops rather than chains. When I pressed him on which he meant, he said I was trying to explain the organism away, and the argument ran out of light before we got anywhere.

I would like you to give me a careful disambiguation of the slogan. Lay out the distinct precise claims "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts" could be expressing — from readings that are trivially true (perhaps merely that some properties of wholes are not properties of any part, or that certain behaviors of a system are not additive in the way, say, masses are), through readings that are substantive and possibly correct, to readings that are false or unfalsifiable — and, for each, say whether it would actually have supported his side of our argument, namely that there is something in the organism that even an ideally completed molecular description would leave out. Distinguish carefully, along the way, between claims about the limits of our current science and claims about the limits of any possible microdescription, since I suspect the plausibility of his position shifted each time he slid between them.

Please set the readings out as a numbered list I could have taken back into the conversation, and close with your own judgment about which reading he most probably meant, given how the argument went.
