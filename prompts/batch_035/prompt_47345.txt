I am writing my extended essay for the IB on the philosophy of language, and I have chosen to examine Frege's puzzle about identity statements through The Lord of the Rings — specifically through Aragorn, who is introduced to the hobbits at Bree under the name Strider, a shabby ranger they have every reason to distrust, before it emerges that he is the heir of Isildur and the future king. My supervisor warned me not to let the novel simply decorate the philosophy, so I would like your help working out whether Tolkien actually dramatises something in Frege's puzzle.

The puzzle, as I understand it, is that the statement that Strider is Aragorn can tell Frodo something new and even change what he does, whereas the statement that Strider is Strider tells him nothing at all; yet if both names pick out the same man, the two statements seem to be about the very same things. Frege's answer was that a name carries a way of presenting its bearer, and that informative identity statements connect two such presentations. My tentative thesis is that Tolkien's narrative is a machine for generating exactly these cases: the same man is Estel to Elrond's household in Rivendell, Strider to the folk of Bree, and Aragorn son of Arathorn when his lineage is declared, and the hobbits' shifting trust in him seems to track not the man but the name he goes by — which looks like evidence for Fregean senses over a Millian view on which names merely tag their bearers.

There is a second case I want to fold in, and I am not sure whether it strengthens or weakens my thesis. When the shards of Narsil, the broken sword of Isildur, are reforged in Rivendell, Aragorn gives the reforged blade a new name, Andúril. Unlike Strider and Aragorn, the two sword-names do not even seem to refer to the same object under different presentations — the renaming treats the reforged sword as in some sense a new thing, even though one could argue it is the same metal. I would like your view on whether this shows that the informative-identity puzzle and the persistence-of-objects question are coming apart inside Tolkien's story, or whether a Fregean can handle both uniformly.

Please give me a reading that takes both the novel and the Frege, Mill, and Kripke debate seriously. Specifically: which scenes would you point to as the clearest dramatisations of the difference between the two identity statements, and do any of them trouble the Fregean reading — for instance, moments where the characters' attitudes change because they learn something new about the bearer rather than merely about the name? Where does the novel resist being read as a philosophy-of-language illustration, and where might it be showing something about names that the philosophical literature leaves out — perhaps that a name's significance in Middle-earth is partly constituted by its history, by who is entitled to use it, or by what it conceals? I would rather be told my thesis has problems now than after I submit the essay.
