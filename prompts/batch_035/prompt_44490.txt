I'm an ML engineer who's been reading the rationalist blogosphere on and off since roughly 2014, and I've finally hit a philosophy problem I can't resolve with my usual toolkit. At my local EA-flavored meetup we keep relitigating the demandingness objection to consequentialism — the Singer-style drowning child argument that if you're obligated to wade in and save the kid, you're obligated to keep giving to distant strangers until it starts to hurt comparably, so the line between duty and charity dissolves — and the pushback that any moral theory telling you to give away nearly everything and never buy nice things for yourself has clearly gone wrong somewhere. The standard replies I've bumped into: satisficing versions (do good enough rather than max out), two-level or indirect consequentialism (aim directly at your kids and your local causes and let the good consequences come out the other side), and various appeals to agent-relative constraints or the seperateness of persons to limit aggregation.

Here's the ask: I'm fluent in ML — objective functions, constraints, regularization, proxy objectives and specification gaming, reward hacking, KL penalties that keep a policy near a reference, compute budgets, early stopping, satisficing vs. argmax, global vs. per-agent reward in multi-agent RL — and took exactly one ethics course in college. Restate the whole demandingness debate in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. My own first pass: demandingness complaints sound like claims that the moral objective is mis-specified — it's unregularized, there's no penalty term for the agent's own budget, it optimizes global welfare with no constraint on what it costs the optimizer, like a loss that will happily burn unbounded compute chasing marginal gains. On that reading Singer's slide from the pond to global poverty is just noticing the objective doesn't care about distance, and satisficing consequentialism is early stopping, and the two-level view is basically an RLHF-style KL penalty — don't chase the reward signal directly because it's too sparse and hackable, stay near the human prior. I want to know how much of that survives contact with the actual philosophy.

Then give me the residue report: what translates cleanly, what only fits with distortion, and what can't be said in my vocabulary at all — and when something can't be said, whether that's a gap in my vocabulary or a sign the philosophers' claim was less clear than it looks. I'm especially worried about one thing: does my framing smuggle consequentialism in from the start, since "morality is an objective function to be maximized" is already the entire contested thesis rather than neutral scaffolding? Is "too demanding" a claim that the objective is mis-specified, or more like a type error — morality was never an argmax over world-states to begin with? Also curious whether "the separateness of persons" has any clean analogue, like per-agent reward functions it's illegitimate to just sum, and whether philosophers consider that a real constraint or mostly cope for people who don't want to sell the gaming PC. Don't be gentle with my framing — it's a first pass and I suspect it cheats somewhere, I just can't see where.
