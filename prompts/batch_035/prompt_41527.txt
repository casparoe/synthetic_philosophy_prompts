Two members of my ethics reading group, Elena and Marcus, stayed after our session on distributive justice to argue about whether prioritarianism actually escapes the leveling-down objection. I believe both the objection and the equality/priority split trace back to Parfit, though the two of them used the terms freely without citing anyone, and I couldn't referee it at the time. The exchange went in four moves; I'll give it as I remember it.

(1) Elena's opening. Telic egalitarianism holds that inequality is intrinsically bad: that some are worse off than others is itself a defect in an outcome, regardless of how it came about. Hence the leveling-down objection: move from a state in which some people are much worse off than the rest to one in which everyone is at the lower level — nobody gains, the already-worse-off stay exactly where they were, and the better-off are brought down to join them — and the telic egalitarian must say the new state is, in one respect, better. Elena called this absurd and claimed that prioritarianism avoids it by construction: the prioritarian cares only about how well individual lives go, gives extra weight to benefits for the worse off, and assigns no value to equality itself. The leveled-down state is therefore worse, full stop, with no residue of being better in one respect. That, she said, is the entire point of the view.

(2) Marcus's reply. The escape is shallower than it looks, because the weighting function can generate the same verdict with a worse gloss. Suppose the weights are steep: a unit of well-being at the very bottom counts, say, a thousand times a unit near the top. Then consider a choice between leaving things as they are — one person at the bottom, everyone else flourishing — and a policy that improves the bottom person's life by a single unit while knocking everyone else down to just above that level. With sufficiently steep weights, the prioritarian must prefer the policy: the theory declares it better, flatly, not merely better in one respect. That, he said, is leveling down with the hedging stripped off, and if the egalitarian's qualified verdict is damning, this should be more so.

(3) Elena's answer. She granted the case but denied its force. The steepness of the weights is a parameter, not a commitment: no actual prioritarian needs weights that extreme, and the absurd verdict requires weights that would already be implausible for independent reasons, so the case discredits one configuration of the view rather than the view itself. The egalitarian's residue, by contrast, follows from the definition of telic egalitarianism — if inequality is intrinsically bad, then greater equality is in one respect better, whatever else is true — so the egalitarian cannot tune it away without ceasing to be an egalitarian of that kind. Her conclusion: the two objections are not parallel. One strikes a core commitment; the other strikes an edge setting.

(4) Marcus's last move. The contrast in (3), he said, is really between an unconfigured theory and a configured one, and the egalitarian can configure too. She can adopt a relational view on which inequality is bad only when, and because, it leaves the worse off worse off than they could otherwise be. On that view, pure leveling down is not even in one respect better, and the objection dissolves exactly as cleanly as the prioritarian's does under moderate weights. If both views can be adjusted out of their embarrassments, the leveling-down objection decides nothing between them. What it exposes is common to both: each compresses all distributive considerations into a single ranking of outcomes, so that any pro tanto judgment our intuitions insist on — that vast inequality is somewhat regrettable even when no one is badly off, or that the worse off have some claim to priority — must either be promoted into that ranking or discarded as noise. Far from refuting egalitarianism, he concluded, the objection merely bounces it into a different form.

They ran out of time there, and I honestly could not tell who had won what. Please adjudicate the four moves in order: for each, say whether the speaker is right, and about what exactly. I am especially unsure about the two load-bearing claims — Elena's, that a parameter is being confused with a commitment, and Marcus's, that a symmetric escape is available to the egalitarian. Finally, tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing.
