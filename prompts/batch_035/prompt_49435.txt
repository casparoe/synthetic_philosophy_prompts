Two members of my philosophy of mind reading group have spent the past year arguing past each other about whether dreams are experiences. One holds a Malcolmian position, in the spirit of Norman Malcolm's Dreaming (1959): on a Wittgensteinian view of how psychological vocabulary acquires meaning, there can be no criteria for checking a putative report of an experience undergone while asleep, so the ordinary notion of dreaming as a stream of experiences occurring during sleep is confused, and dream reports are best understood as present-tense expressions rather than descriptions of earlier mental goings-on. The other holds the position Daniel Dennett took in 'Are Dreams Experiences?' (1976): dream narratives are composed at the moment of waking recall, in seconds, by unconscious authorial processes, so what we call a dream is not an experience we undergo in real time but a post-hoc construction — although the composing process itself is real and its products reportable.

Write the joint statement these two should be able to sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It must contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims: what both accept about the phenomenology and the data (that dream-like narratives are sincerely and vividly reported on waking; that the reports have characteristic confabulatory features; that 'it seemed to me that I dreamt' cannot serve as a self-verifying datum; that neurophysiological sleep stages do not settle the conceptual question on their own), stated in neutral wording.

2. A table of residual disagreements, each formulated so that the side holding it would accept the phrasing as a fair statement of its own view. Include at least: whether the concept of experience requires the possibility of contemporaneous access or checkability; whether authoring a narrative on waking is itself, in any sense, an experience of the dreamt events; and what, if anything, waking reports are reports of.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. At least two cruxes must be specified. One must concern lucid dreaming, and one must concern the following stipulated experiment. Suppose trained lucid dreamers, monitored by polysomnography and confirmed to be in REM sleep, occasionally respond to simple pre-agreed questions using eye-movement signals, with some of their answers veridically keyed to stimuli (e.g. counting tones) delivered during the sleep episode itself. The experiment: a faint, distinctive stimulus (the smell of burning toast) is delivered at a random point in the night under one of four conditions — during REM only, in the waking room only, in both, or in neither — and subjects give an unprompted dream report on waking before any further questioning. For each crux, state the case, argument or experiment both parties agree would bear on it, and record each party's advance prediction about it, including the pattern of toast-inclusions across the four conditions that each expects and what each would take the pattern's absence to show.

4. A final section in which the parties state, with reasons, whether any part of their dispute is merely verbal — for instance, whether each uses the word 'experience' univocally — and, if so, which part; and whether any of the stipulated evidence would count for both as progress rather than as another word-bound standoff.

Write formally and in British English. Do not adjudicate between the views, and do not supply citations or further reading: the joint statement should stand on its own.
