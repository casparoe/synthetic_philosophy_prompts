i write a column on science and ideas for a general readership — US outlet, so please write your whole answer in american english, spellings and all. i'm planning a piece about quitting: jobs, projects, relationships, towns. and before i write it i want somebody to check my own position for cracks, because right now it feels like i believe incompatible things. here's the honest dump:

1. the textbook bygones principle is right. money and time already spent are gone no matter what i do next, so they can't be a reason for anything going forward. i'd defend that in front of an economist without flinching.

2. that said, i think a lot of what gets diagnosed as sunk cost behavior in real organizations isn't that at all. the exec who keeps funding a doomed project is often managing a reputation for resolve, and since that reputation pays off with future counterparties, it's forward-looking, not fallacious. i think the fallacy gets massivly over-diagnosed.

3. personally i keep finishing things — books i'm not enjoying, a reporting project i should have killed months ago — and i half believe this is fine, even good. the fact that i don't bail cheaply is the mechanism that makes my commitments credible, to editors, to sources, and to future me. if i quit whenever quitting got uncomfortable, future me would know that about myself and plan accordingly. so honoring sunk costs is, for a certain kind of agent, part of how the agent works at all.

4. i also buy the old line from the 90s psych literature, the arkes and ayton "humans less rational than lower animals" paper — that the animal cases (digger wasps, birds, that whole 70s wave that gave us the term "concorde fallacy") were explained away without any real sunk cost reasoning involved, and that the human version is a genuine error that comes from misapplying the waste-not rule. so: real fallacy, human-specific, bug not feature.

5. and the column i'm about to write urges readers to quit more things — to treat their sunk costs as noise. i think that advice is correct and most readers would be definately better off taking it. i'd also want my kid to grow into the kind of person described in (3), the one who doesn't bail.

so tell me: does this hang together? which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it? rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one tell me what giving way would cost me. then tell me which single revision would clean up the most — because if i have to drop something, i genuinely don't know which one i'd drop, and i'd rather find out now than after the column runs.
