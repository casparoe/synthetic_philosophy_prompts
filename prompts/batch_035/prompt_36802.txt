Write a formal mock arbitration judgment, approximately 700 words, issued by the Identity Subcommittee of a fictional international sports-governing tribunal. The case before it: Athletic Club Omicron claims entitlement to the championship won in 1978 by its putative predecessor, but over the intervening decades the club has relocated twice, renamed itself three times, replaced every player, coach, employee, and shareholder, lost its original stadium to a fire, and at one point consisted of little more than a fax machine in a rented office and a single dues-paying member who kept the registration current. The tribunal must nonetheless rule, with the full apparatus of legal reasoning — findings of fact, holdings, concurrences, dissents, and a schedule of appeal deadlines and filing fees — on whether the present club is the same club.

The comedy should come from getting the metaphysics right rather than from mocking it. The majority opinion should rest its reasoning on spatiotemporal continuity and causal connectedness — the registration number and unbroken dues record functioning as the club's "body" — while a concurrence should argue in terms of the closest continuer: the present club is numerically identical to the 1978 club not because of any deep fact but because it is the best candidate on the scene, no better-placed rival existing. A dissent should complain that the majority has confused identity with what matters for survival, arguing that the question of whether the club has persisted is distinct from the question of whether it matters that it has, and that the tribunal has jurisdiction only over the first. If the dissent instead bites the bullet on temporal parts — the club as a four-dimensional entity whose successive states are related like the stages of a road — so much the better, provided it is argued rather than name-dropped.

The satirical target is the bureaucratic domestication of metaphysical questions: the comedy of a legal institution being forced, with filing deadlines and standard-of-review language, to decide something no metaphysician considers settled. The judgment should treat borderline cases of identity as if they were questions of fact to be resolved by a preponderance of the evidence, and it should award the trophy accordingly — perhaps with a footnote conceding that if the club is not identical to its predecessor, the trophy must be held in escrow pending a determination of who, if anyone, owns it.

Two constraints on tone. First, sustained deadpan: no jokes about philosophy of identity from the outside, no winking, no dissents that dissolve into the absurd; the absurdity should emerge from the collision of rigor and subject matter, and a reader should be able to identify what is being ironized without assistance. Second, internal consistency: each opinion must be one a careful metaphysician could sign, exaggerating a genuine position rather than inventing a silly one, so that a specialist finds the satire fair.

The piece should remain readable to someone with no background in personal identity: the metaphysical vocabulary should be introduced through the tribunal's own fact-finding (e.g., the parties having briefed the tribunal on the replacement of parts, the question of whether gradual replacement differs in principle from sudden substitution, and the relevance of a single continuous thread — here, the registration record — across wholesale turnover). Format the judgment with numbered paragraphs, as an arbitral award would be.
