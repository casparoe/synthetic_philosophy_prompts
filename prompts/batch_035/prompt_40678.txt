My mate and I had a four-hour arguement last night about whether the agricultural revolution was a mistake, and it ended the way it always ends: both of us repeating ourselves. I do roughly the Sahlins line — shorter working hours, better diet, less infectious disease, and above all farmers as a trapped population who couldn't go back even once they were worse off. He does the counter: hunter-gatherer life was nasty and short, the skeletons show violence and episodic starvation (he kept going on about enamel defects), and in any case agriculture is what made everything else possible. And I've noticed the same stall in the actual literature — the same points recycled by both camps for decades. So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a genuine empirical dispute about the archaeological record, or are we comparing incommensurable things — he's arguing about whether early farmers were worse off than actual hunter-gatherers, while I'm arguing about a counterfactual trap in which the comparison barely matters because the transition was irreversible? And there's the population problem lurking under it: even if per-person welfare fell, total population exploded, so if you count aggregate welfare rather than average, agriculture wins trivially — is a hidden moral disagreement about averaging quietly doing all the work here? Diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is, using what each side actually claims, and tell me honestly what would count as progress: better skeletal data, a settled convention for aggregating welfare, or admitting the question is ill-posed — and if it's ill-posed, why do clever people keep arguing about it anyway?
