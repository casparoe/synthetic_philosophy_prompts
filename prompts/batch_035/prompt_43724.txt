I'm taking a sociology of morality course this semester, and we just covered this framework where cultures differ in how they handle offense: honor cultures (conflict is about reputation, you respond to slurs yourself, often aggressively), dignity cultures (everyone has worth that can't be taken by an insult, so you shrug stuff off, and you only invoke authorities for genuine violations), and victimhood cultures (harm and oppression are the salient categories, and the move is to appeal to third parties or institutions rather than confront the person directly). My professor is pretty into this framework, and our final paper asks us to apply it to some real institution, so I picked something at my own school and now I'm kind of stuck on what to say.

Here's the puzzle I want to write about: my university, like a lot of them, runs two different systems for student misconduct at the same time. On one hand there's the honor code for academic stuff -- you sign the pledge, there's a student-run honor board, and at least in theory you're supposed to directly confront a classmate you saw cheating, or at least report them, and it's framed as the community policing itself and your personal integrity being on the line. On the other hand there's the Title IX office for sexual misconduct and harassment, which is the opposite shape: you file a complaint with a centralized bureaucracy, trained administrators investigate, the parties mostly don't confront each other, complainants get support resources and protections, and everything is proceduralized. And actually this year our school moved plagiarism cases off the honor board and into a central office too, which some older faculty are annoyed about.

So the question I want to pose in the paper: what does each of these systems actually presuppose about insult, harm, and conflict? The obvious reading is honor code = honor culture and Title IX = victimhood culture, but I suspect that's too quick -- like, honor codes also have written rules that apply to everyone equally, and the dignity reading of shrugging things off doesn't obviously fit a system that expels you for cheating, and the Title IX system doesn't exactly presuppose victimhood culture either since people file formal complaints in dignity cultures too for real violations. So in your answer, reconstruct what each system strictly requires versus what it merely sits well with, and tell me whether the two systems really presuppose incompatible things or whether one underlying view could license both. Then take a side on the move of plagiarism cases from the honor board to the central office: is that just bureaucratic convenience, or does it reflect a real shift in what the institution thinks an offense IS? Don't just give me the three-cultures mapping and leave it at me deciding -- argue for a definite reading, and if you think the honor/dignity/victimhood framework itself is distorting the analysis somewhere, say that too and say where. Also if my framing contains a mistake (like if honor codes don't really work the way I described), flag it rather than running with it.
