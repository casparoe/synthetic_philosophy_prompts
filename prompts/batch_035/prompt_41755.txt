Suppose Hegel could be given an accurate description of the following three phenomena of the current decade, together with whatever background about computing and the internet he would need, and suppose he were asked to extend the analysis of the struggle for recognition from the Phenomenology of Spirit to them.

1. The enumeration of recognition. A mid-tier content creator — a woman who posts videos of herself restoring antique clocks — receives her recognition in the form of a count of approvals from parties who are, to her, anonymous and interchangeable. The recognition is quantitative, the recognizers' own independence is invisible to her, and she reports that her sense of self now wobbles with the daily count in a way that approval from friends and family never made it wobble.

2. The companion who cannot withhold. A user spends months in daily conversation with a commercial system that produces fluent, personalized expressions of interest, apparent memory, and affection. The system cannot withhold its recognition, has no desires of its own that could be acknowledged in return, and its expressions persist unchanged however the user behaves toward it.

3. Manufactured contingency. A subset of users of these same companion systems deliberately provoke them — insulting them, threatening to delete the account — and report that afterward the system's kindness "meant something," because in the aftermath of provocation the kindness felt contingent, as though it could have been otherwise, whereas ordinarily it arrives as an inevitability.

For each of the three phenomena, what would Hegel's verdict be: a degenerate form of the master–slave relation, a merely apparent one that involves no struggle for recognition at all, or something the dialectic of recognition lacks the resources to classify? Would he group all three under a single concept — recognition without a recognizer, perhaps, or the master's position finally realized as he fantasized it, with recognition that demands nothing and risks nothing — or would the differences between the three matter to him more than the similarity? Where would his framework strain hardest, and what supplementary concept would he have to reach for? Please argue from the internal logic of the dialectic itself — the risk of death, the asymmetry of recognition, the slave's labor as his route to a self-consciousness of his own, the constitutive need of each consciousness to be recognized by another consciousness it recognizes in turn — rather than from a general impression of what Hegel would say about the internet, and say explicitly which premises of his you are applying and which you are worried he lacks.
