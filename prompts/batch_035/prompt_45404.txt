For background I listened to a podcast episode (hosted by a philosophy PhD, she's usually pretty careful) where she explained the so-called problem of old evidence, and I want to make sure I have this right before I put anything like it in my article. Roughly what she said was: Bayes' theorem tells you how to update on new evidence, but the trouble is that most of the great confirmations in science involved evidence that was already sitting there before the theory came along. Mercury's perihelion had been measured decades before general relativity, and it was exactly that old anomaly that everyone took to be such a stunning confirmation of Einstein. But for a Bayesian, if you already know E, then your probability for E is one, so plugging it into the theorem gives you no movement at all in your probability for the theory — the update ratio is one to one. So the Bayesian, she concluded, has literally nothing to say about the single most celebrated confirmation in the history of physics, which is a pretty serious gap for a view that advertises itself as the logic of science. Her takeaway was that this proves confirmation can't be a probabilistic notion at all and that the whole Bayesian program is basically refuted unless you fake it by pretending you don't know what you know, which she said most Bayesians quietly do.

It sounded convincing but also a bit too tidy, like the kind of thing that gets sharpened into a gotcha for podcast purposes. So my question: is this a fair summary?
