I'm a retired physicist who's gotten well and truly into philosophy in my old age, mostly epistemology and decision theory. Now I've got a real-life puzzle I can't quite crack and I'd appreciate your thoughts.

When I moved to my new town a few months back I needed to pick a GP. There are exactly two practices here: Riverside and the one on the high street. Practically everyone I've met goes to Riverside and raves about it; the other one is nearly empty. Being an old scientist, I went and dug up the numbers myself — published outcome data, complaint records, that kind of thing — and honestly the high street practice comes out slightly ahead, though it's close. Switching later would be a hassle, so I'd rather get this right the first time.

My neighbour tells me all these people can't be wrong. But it nags at me. One friend admitted she first picked Riverside mainly because her sister is the recpetionist there, and another said he just signed up wherever his neighbour had signed up before him. So I keep wondering: maybe the first few people chose Riverside for random reasons, and everyone after that just followed the crowd, each new person reading the earlier choices as evidence. In which case "everyone goes to Riverside" might not mean very much at all.

So, should I trust my own numbers, or the revealed preferences of the whole town? More generally: when is it rational to treat other people's choices as evidence about the world, and when does that kind of reasoning just feed on itself? Is there some principled way to estimate how much independent information is actually in a crowd, versus how much of it is everyone copying everyone else?
