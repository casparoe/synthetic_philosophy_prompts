My closest friend from university now works as a research assistant in a sleep laboratory, and last weekend we had a long argument about what her lab is actually doing when it "collects dreams." She walked me through the standard protocol: participants sleep wired to an EEG, are woken at targeted points in REM or NREM sleep, give an immediate verbal report, and the transcripts are coded for content and emotional tone. The lab then speaks with complete confidence about dreams as experiences that were unfolding during sleep, dated by physiology — a report collected forty seconds into an awakening from REM is treated as a window onto something that was happening right before the tone. What struck me, though, is how much philosophical weight that confidence carries. She admitted that the memory researchers down the hall treat exactly these reports as reconstructions assembled at the moment of telling — plausibly with nothing before them but a few scattered fragments, or even nothing at all — and that Dennett once seriously proposed that the composition of a dream might occur at the instant of reporting, the dream written on awakening rather than experienced during sleep. And behind that sits Malcolm's even more radical old challenge: that saying "I dreamt that I was flying" is not a report of an experience at all, any more than "I slept badly" is, and that there is therefore nothing for the lab's procedure to be a window onto.

So here is what I want worked out. What theory of dreaming — of what a dream is, and of what a dream report is a report of — does this experimental practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view as carefully as you can, and then tell me whether the practice strictly needs it, or whether it could run identically on some thinner, more defensible assumption (say, that reports are systematically correlated with pre-awakening brain states, whatever their ontological status). Consider in particular the lucid-dreaming paradigm, where trained participants signal their lucidity with pre-arranged eye movements during REM: does that two-way communication settle the question of whether dreams are ongoing experiences, or does it merely push the problem back, since even a signaled dream could in principle be composed and reported at the moment of waking? And is the practice internally consistent — can one enterprise coherently use physiology to date experiences to moments during sleep while its sister discipline treats the very same transcripts as confabulations? Finally, describe concretely what would have to change in the lab's methodology if a hard-line Dennettian picture were adopted wholesale: what would still count as data, what conclusions would have to be withdrawn, and whether anything in the existing findings would survive the shift.

I'd like a serious, well-organized answer that reaches a verdict rather than surveying the options neutrally — my friend and I ended the evening exactly there, each retreating to our own side, and I want to know whose picture the sleep lab is really, perhaps unknowingly, committed to.
