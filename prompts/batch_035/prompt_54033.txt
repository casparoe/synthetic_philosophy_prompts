I'm taking a survey course on classical Chinese philosophy, and we've just moved from Confucius to the debate between Mencius and Xunzi on human nature. In the seminar we read the famous exchange in Mencius 6A2 with the philosopher Gaozi, in James Legge's translation, and I keep coming back to one part of it that I can't quite get straight. The passage, as we read it, goes:

"The philosopher Gao said, 'Man's nature is like water whirling round in a corner. Open a passage for it to the east, and it will flow to the east; open a passage for it to the west, and it will flow to the west. Man's nature is indifferent to good and evil, just as the water is indifferent to the east and west.' Mencius replied, 'Water indeed will flow indifferently to the east or west, but will it flow indifferently up or down? The tendency of man's nature to good is like the tendency of water to flow downwards. There are none but have this tendency to good, just as all water flows downwards. Now by striking water and causing it to leap up, you may make it go over your forehead, and, by damming and leading it you may force it up a hill – but are such movements according to the nature of water? It is the force applied which causes them. When men are made to do what is not good, their nature is dealt with in this way.'"

Here is what's bothering me. In class, someone claimed that Mencius is merely stipulating an analogy rather than arguing: from the fact that water flows downward (and from the conceded premise that water is indifferent between east and west), nothing follows about the moral direction of human nature unless you already assume that "down" corresponds to "good." In other words, the charge is that Mencius has smuggled his conclusion into the analogy by choosing which feature of water to map onto goodness. But another classmate defended Mencius, saying that the analogy is not doing deductive work but pointing to something phenomenologically real: when people do evil, something has to interfere, coerce, or damage them, just as water going uphill requires external force — and this asymmetry, not the analogy, is the actual content of the claim that human nature is good. I honestly can't tell whether the second reading is a charitable rescue or a genuinely better reading of the text.

Could you please act as an expert in classical Chinese philosophy and help me work through this passage carefully? Before answering, I'd appreciate it if you would think through, step by step: (1) what exactly each side of the debate is claiming about "nature" (xing) — Gaozi seems to treat nature as whatever is inborn and spontaneous, while Mencius seems to treat it as a directed tendency, and it matters a lot whether those are the same concept; (2) whether Mencius's reply is a genuine rebuttal of Gaozi or a shifting of the question, given that Gaozi's east/west example was only ever meant to illustrate moral neutrality, not directionlessness in general; and (3) how Xunzi, who apparently held that human nature is bad and that goodness is the artificial product of deliberate effort and ritual training, would attack Mencius's asymmetry claim — for instance, couldn't he argue that the "external force" of culture and moral education is needed for any human goodness whatsoever, so that Mencius's evil-requires-interference point gets the direction of explanation exactly backwards? I'd like your considered judgment on whether the analogy can carry the weight Mencius places on it, and on what the dispute between Mencius and Xunzi is ultimately about — human nature, or two different conceptions of what counts as "natural" in the first place.
