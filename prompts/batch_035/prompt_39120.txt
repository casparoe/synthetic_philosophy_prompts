I'm a science-and-ideas journalist (think longform magazine stuff, not daily news) and our outlet runs an interview series where we sit down with philosophers and scientists for an hour-plus. Next month's guest is a scholar who's just published a book arguing that the impartial spectator in Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments isn't just a nice piece of moral psychology — it's Smith's actual answer to the question of what a fair, fully informed judgment of our conduct would look like. She reads the spectator as doing real normative work, not merely describing how people happen to regulate their behavior by imagining how others see them. I've read the Theory of Moral Sentiments once, years ago, and I mostly remember the bit about how we can never directly observe anyone else's feelings — we only see their causes and effects and imagine our way in — so I'm worried about being out of my depth in the room.

Last time I interviewed someone in moral psychology my questions were too soft and I bascially got an hour of restatements of the book's blurb. So this time I want eight to ten questions that press where Smith scholars actually press. At minimum: one on the idealization worry — the spectator starts out as an imagined real person and gets gradually corrected and idealized, and I want to ask her whether that correction has a principled standard or whether it just smuggles in whichever moral commitments the theorist likes; one on the conformism problem, since Smith himself admits we have a bad habit of admiring the rich and powerful, so how is the spectator anything more than our internalized class prejudice with a halo; and one on the desire to be praiseworthy, which Smith only added in the last edition he prepared before dying — I'd love to ask whether that late addition signals a change of view or was a clarification he'd had in mind all along. I'd also like at least one question on whether Smithian sympathy, understood as a judgment about what it's fitting to feel rather than mere emotional contagion, is meant to compete with or complement Hume's account, since the two are always lumped together. For each question, include the follow-up you'd expect to need given the reply she's most likely to give.

One more thing, and this matters to me editorially: for every question, add a brief note telling me whether its premise is settled among people who work on Smith or a genuinely contested reading — for example, I gather there's actual disagreement about how to interpret that last-edition addition — and flag any spot where the question leans on a contested interpretation of the text, so I don't accidentally present it to her as just what Smith said when it's one reading among several. She's a living scholar, so please don't put specific claims in her mouth beyond the general position I've described; build the questions so she's the one who has to take the stands.
