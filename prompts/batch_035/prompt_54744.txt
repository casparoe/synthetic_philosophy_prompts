I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and I'm putting together an interactive quiz for our site — the working title is "What kind of voter are you actually?" — on the ethics of voting, specifically the whole lesser-evil mess. The idea came out of an argument with my editor about whether voting third party in a swing state is morally lazy or morally clean, and I realized we were just shouting labels at each other. So I want a sorting instrument that classifies readers by what they say about concrete cases, not by which camp they identify with. No question should just ask "do you believe in voting your conscience?" — the cases have to do the work.

Here's what I need: eight to ten questions, each a concrete scenario with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. The positions I want as leaves in the key:

1. Straight consequentialist lesser-eviler — your vote's tiny chance of mattering means you should always support the best candidate with a realistic shot, and the math doesn't care about your feelings.
2. Threshold consequentialist — vote lesser evil, but only when the difference between the evils is big enough to clear some bar; a coin-flip between two bad options doesn't oblige you to pick one.
3. Expressive / low-stakes voter (the Jason Brennan-ish view that voting is more like cheering at a game than like pulling a lever that might move the world, so per-election the stakes are low enough that you're entitled to vote for who you actually like).
4. Clean-hands / conscience voter — voting is more like giving testimony or casting a judgment than like engineering an outcome, so voting for someone you think is bad corrupts you even when it's strategically pointless not to.
5. Duty-first civic deontologist — there's a genuine (maybe even enforceable) duty to participate, and the question of who you vote for is partly about affirming the system, not just outcomes.
6. Abstentionist / participation skeptic — voting is more like playing the lottery crossed with a ritual, and there's no duty to do it at all.

Some cases I definitely want in there, phrased so a reader can answer without knowing where they lead:

- The classic swing-state third-party case (your favorite candidate is polling 4%, the two viable candidates are both bad, one clearly worse).
- The safe-state mirror of the same case — does the same person answer differently when the odds of their vote mattering drop by orders of magnitude, and if the expressive voter and the consequentialist give the same answer there, flag it.
- A write-in or spoiled ballot scenario.
- Vote pairing with a friend in another state (you vote their viable candidate where you live is close, they vote your third party where they live is a blowout).
- A threshold test case: two candidates where the "lesser" evil differs from the greater only trivially, versus a case where the gap is enormous.
- An epistemic humility case: you're honestly unsure which of the two is actually the lesser evil.
- Something about compulsory voting / whether staying home needs a justification.

Also: the scoring key should note any answer combinations that don't cleanly sort into a leaf — I'd rather have an "hmm, you're in uncharted territory" result than force people into boxes — and briefly say whether you think nobody lands there because the combination is incoherent or just unexplored. And if two of the six positions can't be separated by any of the questions (I suspect the expressive voter and the clean-hands voter might answer almost everything the same way and only differ on why), tell me which ones and propose one extra question that would split them.

Audience is smart general readers, so no jargon in the questions themselves — put the philosophy only in the scoring key. Keep the whole thing usable as an actual quiz: I want to paste it more or less straight into our quiz tool, so make the answer options short. And please don't have the questions reference any books or living writers by name — the key can mention Brennan, but the reader-facing part should be pure cases.
