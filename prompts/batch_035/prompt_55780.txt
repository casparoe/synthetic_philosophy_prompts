I'm officiating—well, toasting—at my former students' wedding next month and I've gotten myself into a philosophical bind, which honestly is very on brand for me, but I need your help getting out of it.

Priya and Marcus both did their PhDs under me. Their whole relationship, personal and intellectual, has been a decade-long argument about moral luck. Priya's dissertation was on Nagel and Williams, and she's the type who will tell you, cheerfully, that everything you're proud of is luck all the way down — the circumstances you were born into, the temperament you never chose, and above all the outcomes your choices happen to collide with. Marcus is a committed Kantian about all of it: blameworthiness and praiseworthiness attach to the will, full stop, and the whole moral luck literature is, in his view, an elaborate confusion between judging people and judging what happens to them. They met because of a delayed train to a conference — stranded, one shared cab, the rest is history — and Priya treats this as exhibit A for her side, while it makes Marcus quietly nuts that the founding story makes it sound like the marriage is just something that happened to them rather than something they chose and keep choosing.

So their requests for the toast: hers is that it honor the fluke, the sheer contingency of it; his is that it not imply they're passive beneficiaries of cosmic dice. He said, only half joking, that he'll be grading the toast, and he's right that the two requests aren't actually incompatible — the luck rerouted the train, but the ten years of decisions since are theirs, and maybe that tension is what the toast should be *about*. But every draft I write either turns into a seminar or just... avoids the issue, and both halves of the couple would notice.

Constraints on you, since the room is roughly half philosophers (who will catch any fumbling of the constitutive/resultant/circumstantial distinction, and will absolutely be checking whether my Kant holds up) and half family from two different countries, most of whom have no idea what my discipline is on about. It has to land as a toast, not a lecture — one real laugh somewhere, a little warmth, and nothing that Marcus would consider actually false, because if I say something sloppy about the will he WILL hold it against me and he will be correct to.

Five minutes at most. If you can find a way to make the "for better or for worse" line do some of the work — the vows basically being a pre-emptive position on circumstantial luck — that would be great, but don't force it.
