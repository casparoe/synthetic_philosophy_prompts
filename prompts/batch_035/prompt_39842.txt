I hold, and have defended in print, a fairly strong version of the quantum-immortality thesis: that a consistent Everettian is committed to expecting, from the first-person point of view, indefinite continuation through any death-scenario whose lethality is decided by quantum events — the familiar setup of a gun triggered by a spin measurement or a radioactive decay, but also, by extension, any death that ultimately turns on quantum indeterminacy at the molecular level, which is arguably every death. I do not mean the pop-science version ("you cannot die, ever"). I mean the narrower claim: that in every run of such an experiment there is a branch in which an experiencer continues, that there is no further fact about which branch "I" will be in beyond the existence of continuers, and that therefore the rational first-person expectation, conditional on Everett being the correct interpretation, is that I will never experience quantum-decided death. I am aware of the obvious rejoinder — that the Born-rule measure of the survival branches shrinks toward zero, so that "expecting" survival trades on an equivocation between counting branches and weighting them — and my current reply is that measure governs what to predict before branching, not which futures will contain someone to be. That reply is the part of my position I trust least.

What I want from you is a map of what would actually count against the view, and I want you to commit to a verdict rather than surveying options. Specifically:

1. Which assumptions, if they failed, would sink the narrow immortality claim? I take these to include at least: the reality and non-collapse of the branches; the availability of survival branches at all in the relevant scenarios (does aging and neurodegeneration leave "surviving" continuers that preserve enough function to be me, or does the selection pressure in the lethal scenarios select only for survival of the physics, not of the person?); the no-further-fact claim about post-branch identity; and the sufficiency of first-person expectation over measure. Tell me which of these the view's serious defenders would themselves concede as load-bearing, and which they would treat as critics' confusions.

2. What observable or formal results would be genuine defeaters? For example: should empirical progress on objective-collapse models be treated as counting against Everettian immortality specifically, or only against its Everettian presupposition? Does a successful decision-theoretic derivation of the Born rule — one that gets from branch weights to rational action — undermine my equivocation reply by showing that weight is all the "expectation" there is? Is there a case the view simply cannot describe, in the way a theory of persistence cannot describe fission?

3. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say plainly which one you would endorse as decisive and why.

Finally, an honest assessment of insulation: I have noticed that whenever an objection lands, the view's defenders retreat to a narrower formulation — from "you cannot die" to "you cannot experience quantum-decided death" to "there will be continuers, though of vanishing measure and perhaps degraded function." Tell me whether this progression is a legitimate refinement, a degenerating series of qualifications of exactly the kind Lakatos warned about, or the ordinary fate of a metaphysical interpretation that was never falsifiable in the first place — and, if the last, whether that indicts the view or merely reclassifies it. I will be most persuaded by an answer that names the single consideration I have most likely insulated myself against.
