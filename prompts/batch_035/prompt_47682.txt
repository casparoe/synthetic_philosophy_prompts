I am a novelist, and for twenty-five years I have earned my living doing, or attempting to do, the thing that a certain corner of philosophy insists cannot be done: giving a reader who has never had an experience something that is recognizably like having it. A woman who has never lost a child reads my chapter about the funeral and closes the book shaking. Whether that counts as her coming to know something is exactly the question I want put into my terms, because the philosophers' terms for it no longer help me.

The debate I mean is the knowledge argument. Frank Jackson asked us to imagine a scientist confined from birth to a black-and-white room, where she learns every physical fact about color vision — neurophysiology, optics, the lot — and then steps outside and sees red for the first time. Jackson concluded that she thereby learns something new, so the physical facts were not all the facts. The physicalists have mounted several escape routes since, and I want all of them translated: Lewis's ability hypothesis, on which she gains not new facts but know-how — the abilities to imagine, recognize, and remember; the phenomenal concept strategy, on which she gains a new way of thinking old facts rather than any new fact; and Dennett's response, which leans on a robot version of Mary who, given a sufficiently complete database, would supposedly complete the deduction in the room. Please resist the urge to adjudicate. I am not asking who wins. I am asking what each position looks like when stated in the only vocabulary I trust, which is the vocabulary of craft: showing versus telling; scene versus summary; free indirect discourse; the telling detail; defamiliarization, by which a familiar thing is made strange enough to be seen; and the doctrine drilled into every apprentice that some knowledge cannot be delivered by summary and must be staged.

So the assignment is this. Take each of the four positions and restate it, as faithfully as you can, using only the craft vocabulary. Jackson's conclusion, I suspect, is nearly the doctrine of show-don't-tell already: certain content cannot be transmitted by report and must be enacted. The ability hypothesis looks like a claim I can almost test against my own practice, since my business is arguably training readers' capacities for imagination and recognition rather than handing them propositions. The phenomenal concept strategy sounds like free indirect discourse — a new mode of access to unchanged content — but I suspect the translation distorts something there, and I want to know what. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most: which claims survive the translation cleanly, which only survive because you quietly bent them, and which cannot be said in craft vocabulary at all.

My own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: the translation will fail in a revealing direction. The debate assumes a two-way split between knowledge-that and know-how, and my trade seems to live in a third place the split doesn't cover — something like acquaintance at one remove, in which a reader who has never had the experience acquires something neither a fact nor a skill but which the craft tradition has never had a name for. If the residue shows that, tell me whether it points to something the philosophers are missing or to something fiction flatters itself about. And if the residue points the other way — if my vocabulary cannot express, say, the distinction the phenomenal concept strategy turns on — say so plainly, and tell me whether that is a defect of my vocabulary or of the distinction.
