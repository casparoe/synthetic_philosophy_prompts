I'm a PhD student in machine learning working on interpretability, and I've been reading about Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes in a philosophy of science course. I have a working ML vocabulary — training and test distributions, ablation studies, benchmark overfitting, scaling laws, inductive biases, model selection, holdout sets, generalization gaps — and only a lay philosopher's grasp of the post-Popper landscape. Restate Lakatos's picture of science in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report.

Specifically, I'd like you to work through the mapping piece by piece. Where the hard core of a programme — the propositions its practitioners refuse to give up when anomalies arrive — sits in the picture, and whether an ML analogue would be a set of architectural commitments, an implicit prior, or something else entirely. Whether the protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses corresponds to the ways we explain away benchmark failures (data issues, hyperparameters, insufficient scale) before ever questioning the architecture. What a "progressive" problemshift, one that predicts novel facts, looks like when the currency of the field is leaderboard movement and ablations rather than novel predictions, and whether our habit of testing on ever-shifting benchmarks makes the progressive/degenerating distinction even well-defined — the analogue to evaluating on the test set seems to cut both ways, since a field that retrofits its auxiliary hypotheses to every anomaly is degenerating for Lakatos but also just sounds like ordinary fitting, and I can't tell if that means ML research is degenerating by construction or that the concept doesn't transfer.

Then the two questions I actually care about. First: people in my circles talk about the "scaling hypothesis" as if it were a hard core — a commitment that held through embarrassing years of small-model results until compute caught up — and I want to know whether that usage is faithful to Lakatos or whether it quietly converts his descriptive history into something like investment advice about which bets to keep funding. Relatedly, does the Lakatosian apparatus of rational reconstruction, where we judge a programme by its best versions rather than its average practitioner, have any counterpart in how we credit a research direction versus the papers that happen to instantiate it? Second: the residue. Which parts of Lakatos's picture have no expression in my vocabulary at all — I suspect the notion that the hard core is accepted by convention or methodological fiat rather than evidence is one of them, since in ML a prior is something we can in principle measure the effect of — and for each piece of residue, tell me whether it points to something my vocabulary is missing or to something in Lakatos that was doing less work than it appeared to. I'd rather come away knowing where my translation is lossy than with a tidy metaphor.
