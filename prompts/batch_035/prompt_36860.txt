I'm at work on a novel that follows three generations of a family in the Hungarian provinces, roughly 1740 to 1870, and the question of when and how my characters begin to feel themselves part of a nation — rather than being merely subjects of a crown, parishioners of a church, and speakers of a language — has become the book's spine. My editor, hoping to spare me anachronism, sent me an essay from a literary supplement that summarizes the scholarship as follows: Hobsbawm showed in The Invention of Tradition (1983) that the traditions nations cherish — coronation pageants, anthems, regimental tartans — are modern fabrications passed off as ancient; Benedict Anderson, in Imagined Communities (also 1983), extended this from national traditions to nations themselves, arguing that the nation is a fiction invented in the late eighteenth century, essentially by rulers and printers exploiting the new mass press, since people had to be conned into dying for strangers; and it follows that national identity is fraudulent from top to bottom, that no educated person before about 1789 could have felt genuine national belonging, and that attributing such feelings to earlier characters is like giving them wristwatches. Something about that last comparison makes me suspicious — an imagined community doesn't sound like quite the same thing as a fabricated one, and I suspect these two books, published the same year, are being blended into a single doctrine — but I don't know the scholarship well enough to say where the essay goes wrong, if it does. Please go through the summary claim by claim: tell me which parts Anderson and Hobsbawm would accept as written, which parts conflate the two men's rather different theses or inflate a modest one, and whether my editor's practical conclusion — that depicting pre-1789 characters with national feeling is outright anachronism, and that the feeling itself, once it arrives, is fraud — actually follows from whatever in the summary survives scrutiny. A subsidiary question I'd appreciate your taking seriously: given the corrected picture, how should a novelist represent the moment (if it is a moment) when national belonging becomes available to people like my characters? Please write in American English.
