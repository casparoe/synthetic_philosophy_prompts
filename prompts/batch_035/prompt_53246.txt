After a career in experimental physics, I have taken up philosophy in retirement, and I have run into a puzzle about the Epicurean treatment of death that I cannot resolve on my own. Epicurus held that death is not bad for the one who dies, on the ground that when death is present, the subject who might be harmed is not. My thought is this: throughout my working life I was trained to regard a claim as respectable only if some conceivable observation could count against it. Now consider the proposition that death is bad for the deceased. No possible observation could confirm or disconfirm it — the subject whose welfare is at issue has ceased to exist — so by the criterion of falsifiability I was raised on, the claim is not even false but empty. If that is right, the entire contemporary debate over the badness of death rests on a pseudo-question, and the Epicureans win by default rather than by argument. Yet philosophers I have spoken with treat the deprivation account — the view that death is bad because it robs you of goods you would otherwise have enjoyed — as a perfectly intelligible rival to Epicurus, worth arguing about at length. Are they clinging to an unfalsifiable hypothesis out of sentiment, or am I applying a criterion of scientific demarcation to a question it was never meant to govern? I suspect the latter, but I would like to understand precisely where the transfer of falsifiability from laboratory hypotheses to claims about posthumous harm goes wrong, and what the deprivation account is even asserting if not something that could, in principle, be checked.
