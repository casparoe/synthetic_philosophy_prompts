I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about mind uploading. My editor is fond of repeating that "the brain is just hardware, and the mind is software running on it," and in this case he deployed it to support a specific claim: that since software can be copied and ported to new hardware, a sufficiently detailed scan of a person's brain could in principle be run on another substrate — and, more contentiously, that the resulting system would be that person. Something about the inference felt too easy to me, but I couldn't say precisely where it goes wrong, or whether it goes wrong at all. Could you lay out, as a list, the distinct precise theses that one-liner might be expressing — starting with readings so weak that no one would dispute them, up through readings strong enough to entail the porting conclusion — and for each reading indicate whether it is plausible, and crucially whether it would actually deliver what my editor wanted from it? In particular, I'd like to know where the analogy genuinely holds (e.g., substrate-independence as a formal property of some computations) and where it quietly assumes a separation between program and machine that the brain may not have. Please close by saying which reading people in the AI and neurosciences communities most likely have in mind when they repeat the line casually, and whether that reading is the one that does the work.
