I'm a high school junior doing an independent project on the philosophy of time travel, and I'd like your help with a puzzle about a friend's views. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher who specializes in the metaphysics of time and causation, and work through this carefully and patiently — I'd genuinely rather have a step-by-step analysis than a quick answer, and it may help to do a short brainstorm of the possibilities before you commit to anything.

My friend Maya and I have been arguing about time travel scenarios for weeks, and she has now issued verdicts on five of them. I've started to suspect that her verdicts are jointly consistent with more than one underlying rule about how time travel works, and I want your help figuring out what those rules are and what she would say next. Her verdicts so far:

1. The grandfather case: A traveler goes back to keep her own grandfather from ever meeting her grandmother. Maya says the traveler can arrive, track him down, and even pull a trigger — but the grandfather will always survive: the gun jams, a passerby stumbles into her, she mistakens a stranger for him. Maya insists the survival is guaranteed, not a matter of luck.

2. The sightseeing case: A traveler watches a rocket launch in 1969 from a public beach, speaks to no one, and touches nothing. Maya says this is perfectly possible.

3. The sandwich case: A traveler stops at a diner in 1975, buys a sandwich, pays, and leaves without incident. Maya says this is fine — in her words, the universe doesn't care about a sandwich.

4. The loop case: A traveler receives the schematics for her time machine from an old man, builds the machine, and later goes back to hand the very same schematics to that man when he was young. Maya says this is possible, because nobody changed anything — the schematics were always going around and around in the circle.

5. The prevention case: A traveler attempts to prevent a major historical catastrophe and return to a present in which it never happened. Maya says this is impossible and will be foiled by coincidence, exactly like the grandfather case.

Here are the new cases I'd like you to predict her verdicts on:

Case A — the drowning child: A traveler goes back to 1901 and pulls a drowning child out of a river in a remote village. No book or record anywhere mentions the child or the drowning. The child grows up and has descendants, none of whom has any connection to the traveler or to anything in the traveler's life. Will Maya say this is possible, and if so, how do you think she would describe what happened?

Case B — the famous poet: A traveler goes back to 1882 to save a well-known poet whose death in a shipwreck is described in every biography ever written about him. The traveler has no connection to the poet or his family. Will Maya say this is possible?

What I'd like from you, in order: First, lay out the candidate rules that fit all five of Maya's verdicts — I believe there are at least two, and I suspect a third that only survives under one particular way of reading her views, but please work this out independently rather than letting me steer you. Second, for each candidate rule, state clearly what it predicts about the drowning-child case and the poet case. Third, give your best guess as to Maya's verdicts on the two new cases, and explain why you favor the rule you favor — pay attention to how she tends to talk about these scenarios, since I suspect her phrasing is a clue. Fourth, tell me where her verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer, without papering over it. Finally, describe one further scenario, different from all the ones above, that would most cleanly distinguish between the candidate rules, and tell me what each rule predicts about it.
