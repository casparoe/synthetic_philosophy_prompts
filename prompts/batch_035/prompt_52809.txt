I am a novelist at work on a book-length project modeled on Borges's treatment of time, and I want to get his system straight in my head before I build anything on it. As I understand the doctrine, the pieces assemble into a single coherent picture. In The Garden of Forking Paths, Stephen Albert explains that Ts'ui Pên's chaotic novel was an attempt to depict a universe in which time perpetually forks, so that every outcome that can happen does happen, each in its own branch. And in A New Refutation of Time, Borges proves, on idealist premises, that time does not exist at all. Read together, these give me the metaphysics I want for my narrator: every choice he makes spawns the worlds in which he chose otherwise, his own timeline is simply the branch we happen to be reading, and in the deepest sense no branch is more real or more truly his than any other. It strikes me as essentially the many-worlds picture arrived at a decade and a half before the physicists worked it out, which is precisely the sort of prescience that makes Borges worth borrowing from. My editor, who studied physics as an undergraduate, is delighted by this framing and keeps urging me to lean on it in the jacket copy.

My difficulty is dramaturgical rather than conceptual, or so I hope. I want a climactic scene in which my narrator, having fully absorbed this branching-and-illusory structure of time, nevertheless experiences his own single, unbranching life — the wife he lost, the sentence he actually served, the one letter he did not send — as unbearably real and uniquely his. But if Borges's determinism is right that all outcomes occur, nothing is ever really lost; and if time is unreal, nothing ever really passes. The grief threatens to collapse into self-contradiction. How should I construct the scene so that his mourning reads as consistent with the philosophy rather than as a relapse into the common-sense view of time that Borges had already demolished? I refuse to have him simply forget his metaphysics when it starts to hurt, which strikes me as cheap, but I equally cannot have readers conclude that the philosophy was decorative all along. A friend in my writing group suggested that the answer lies in making the narrator fatalistic rather than bereaved, since the spy in Borges's story moves through his evening as though the ending were already fixed; I confess I do not see how fatalism is supposed to coexist with a picture in which every ending happens somewhere. But perhaps she is onto something, and I am missing it.
