I'm writing a paper that reads Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through hinge epistemology, and I'd like a reading that takes both the novel and the later Wittgenstein seriously rather than using one to decorate the other.

The interpretive thesis I want to test: Stevens's certainties — that a great butler serves a great man and thereby does his own small part for humanity; that dignity consists in emotional restraint and the suppression of personal feeling; that the domestic hierarchy mirrors a legitimate political one — are not beliefs he holds for reasons, but hinges: certainties exempt from doubt that constitute the framework within which his evidence about Darlington's character, Miss Kenton's marriage, and his own life get weighed and discounted. On this reading, his trouble is not that he draws bad inferences but that the propositions that would defeat his worldview never register as evidence at all, because doubt has no purchase where the hinge hasn't first been loosened. The obvious scenes to work through are the repeated failures of banter with Mr. Farraday — where Stevens, needing wit that cannot be composed in advance, has to confront that his competence presupposes hinges the new world no longer shares — and the final scene at the pier, where the ex-entertainer's consolation about the remainder of the day is offered by a stranger in plain terms Stevens has spent the novel unable to use about himself.

Where I want the resistance to the reading to be genuine: Wittgenstein's riverbed belongs to a shared form of life and shifts only slowly and communally, whereas Stevens's hinges are private, class-inflected, and borrowed from his father and his employer — a manufactured certainty rather than an inherited one. Does this break the analogy, or does it expose something On Certainty leaves underdeveloped — namely that hinges can be chosen, imposed, and pathological, and that the framework picture has no obvious resources for distinguishing a healthy hinge from an ideological one? I suspect Stevens's late-night confession that he can't even confirm he gave his father his best is the moment the novel forces that question, and I want to know whether hinge epistemology can answer it or only redescribe his self-deception.

Please give me a structured reading: which scenes carry the interpretation, where it strains, what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone doesn't — and whether the right conclusion is that Stevens is a hinge-epistemological case study or a counterexample.
