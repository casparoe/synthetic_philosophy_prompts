I teach a philosophy of mind elective for undergrads, about 18 students, and I've got one 90-minute session on Dennett's taxonomy of creatures -- the idea that minds can be sorted by where the "testing" happens: Darwinian creatures have their hypotheses tested by selection killing the unfit ones, Skinnerian ones test by acting in the world and getting rewarded or punished, Popperian ones let their hypotheses die in their stead by simulating internally before acting, and Gregorian ones import tools (writing, diagrams, notations) that expand the space of hypotheses they can even entertain.

I want an in-class game where the students are literally sorted into these four kinds and have to solve the same problem under four different information regimes, so that the hierarchy gets felt rather than explained. My vague idea so far: some kind of resource-gathering or survival task over multiple rounds, maybe finding a safe route through a grid or figuring out which of a set of objects is "edible," where the four groups differ only in what feedback they get and when. Darwinian players would commit blind and the failures just get eliminated from the pool (with their "offspring" mutating their strategy slightly); Skinnerian players get to keep playing but only learn from what they actually did; Popperian players get to peek at the consequence of a move before committing to it, but with some limit so it's not trivially better; Gregorian players get some artifact -- a partial map, a memory aid, or the ability to pass written notes between team members -- that restructures what strategies are available to them at all. I'd like the payoff scheme to actually reflect fitness rather than money, and I want the asymmetries in information flow to be the whole point, not an accident.

Please give me the full run sheet: setup and materials, exact rules for each creature type, how the rounds and the selection/elimination mechanic work, how to keep eliminated Darwinian players engaged (maybe they become the "environment"?), and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the four categories and onto the idea that each level buys you a faster or cheaper search, not a different kind of competence. Also tell me honestly what the game can't reproduce -- e.g. real Darwinian creatures don't learn at all within a lifetime, and I suspect students will smuggle in Popperian reasoning (planning, whispering "don't pick that") no matter which group they're in, so tell me how to design the rules to plug that leak or at least catch it in the debrief. And a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it would be great.
