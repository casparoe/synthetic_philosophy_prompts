For about a year now, my brother-in-law, my neighbor, and I have been stuck in a running argument about when several things add up to one further thing. My brother-in-law holds the anything-goes view: whenever there are some things, there is also a single thing they all make up, no matter how scattered — his favorite illustration is that his left boot and a distant galaxy jointly compose one enormous object, and he insists this is the only position that never has to draw arbitrary lines. My neighbor, who read a popular philosophy book years ago, took the opposite extreme from it: the only things genuinely made of parts are living organisms; everything else — chairs, cars, mountains — is just many tiny particles sitting in a chair-like or mountain-like arrangement, with no further object there at all. I have been trying to develop my own middle position: several things compose a single object exactly when they share one fate — when they are so bound together that whatever happens to one of them happens to them all, so that they cannot be pried apart without being wrecked. On my view a hammer is one object, since head and handle share a fate, but a pile of gravel is not, since each stone minds its own business.

I want a thought experiment that stress-tests all three of us at once, and I would like to set it on my cousin's hillside farm, which conveniently contains the following: an aspen grove in which all the trunks grow up from one shared root system; one trunk in that grove killed by lightning a month ago, still standing and still held upright by the roots; a decorative hook by the barn door that the farrier made by welding two horseshoes together; and, for my brother-in-law's benefit, my work glove lying dropped by the gate together with the weathervane on the barn roof. Some constraints on the construction: keep it realistic — a working farm, no science fiction, no supernatural framing; the scenario itself must stay neutral, never declaring in the setup that any candidate is or is not a single object; and the candidates should be matched as closely as you can manage, so that the dead trunk and the living trunks differ as nearly as possible only in the life that flows, or fails to flow, through them, while the welded hook and the grove are alike in being inseparably bound.

After writing the scenario (one paragraph), please do the following. First, give a verdict table: four rows for the candidates — the grove, the dead trunk, the welded hook, and the glove-and-weathervane pair — against four columns for the anything-goes view, the living-things-only view, my shared-fate view, and what I would want to say before reflecting; every cell should contain the verdict plus a one-sentence derivation from that view as stated, not from intuition. Second, for each disagreement the table surfaces, name the single feature doing the work and say what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed — the roots are severed, the weld is ground through, the glove is carried indoors. Third, give the strongest counterattack available to each of us against the cells that embarrass us; I am especially curious whether the living-things-only view can say the dead trunk is still caught up in the grove's life, the way a splinter in my thumb can be part of me without being alive itself, and whether it wants to say that. Finally, answer the question this test is really for: can my shared-fate view be patched to handle its worst cell without collapsing into one of the other two views — and if the only workable patch amounts to piggybacking on the life view, say so plainly. If you think part of our three-way argument is really a verbal dispute about the word "object," identify which part and why, because we have begun to suspect exactly that.
