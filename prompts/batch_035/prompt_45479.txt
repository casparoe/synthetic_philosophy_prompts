I'm a sixth-form student working on an extended project about whether machines can understand language, and I've run into a wall that I suspect is more interesting than the topic itself. The debate, as I understand it, goes like this. On one side are the people who take Harnad's symbol grounding problem seriously: a system whose only access to its symbols is other symbols — like someone trying to learn Chinese from a Chinese-to-Chinese dictionary — is trapped in a circle of uninterpreted tokens, and meanings must be connected to the world through perception and action before any symbol can mean anything. On the other side are people who reply that statistical patterns in text carry information about the world, that much of human conceptual life is acquired through language rather than direct experience (I have never seen a neutrino, yet I know roughly what one is), and that a system which models text well enough has thereby latched onto worldly structure. The two sides have been in roughly this position since about 1990, with various incarnations of the neural network crowd rotating through, and as far as I can tell nobody has moved an inch: the grounding side says the statistical story is parasitic on interpreters who already understand, and the statistical side says the demand for grounding smuggles in a claim about origins that is irrelevant to whether the finished system understands.

I don't want a verdict on which side is right. I want a diagnosis of the deadlock. Is this a substantive disagreement that could in principle be settled — say, by a system that acquired its meanings from text alone and then passed every behavioural test we could devise — or is it verbal, with the two sides meaning different things by 'meaning' or 'understanding'? Or are they answering different questions under one heading, one asking about the metaphysics of where content comes from and the other about whether a given system has it now? My tentative hypothesis, which I'd like you to challenge if it's mistaken, is that the grounding side is driven by an intuition about parasitism — that derived meaning requires a source with underived meaning, the way a copy of a copy requires an original — and that this intuition does all the work while being treated as though it were a discovery.

If that hypothesis is right, what would progress even look like? A sharper definition, a decisive thought experiment, an actual system that forces one side to concede, or an admission that we are protecting a concept rather than investigating one? Please be concrete about what each side would have to give up for the debate to count as resolved, and be honest if your conclusion is that no resolution should be expected and why.
