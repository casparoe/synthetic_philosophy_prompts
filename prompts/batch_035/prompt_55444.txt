After a mathematics PhD and several years in industry, I have secured two years of independent funding to work on a single conceptual problem: the status of heuristic arguments and, in particular, whether the presumption of independence can be made principled. By the presumption of independence I mean the practice of treating a statement as independent of everything else one knows when one can find no concrete connection between them — as when one argues that the millionth digit of some distant physical constant is unrelated to whether a particular button inside a sealed box was pressed, and therefore that a probability estimate formed without conditioning on the button survives unchanged once the button is observed. The background worry is that some judgments of this kind are extremely reliable while others fail catastrophically — two facts about the same computation are never independent in the way two facts about different continents can be — and that the difference between the reliable and unreliable cases is exactly what we cannot yet articulate.

Rank the open questions in this area for someone in my position, and defend the ordering. Among the candidates I can see: whether the presumption can be given a semantics at all, or whether "no connection" only makes sense relative to a particular way of carving the world into questions; where the boundary lies between facts that are independent because their causal histories are genuinely disjoint and facts that are independent only relative to an argument that would have to run longer than the universe to find a connection — and whether the second kind deserves the name at all; whether heuristic estimates are usefully modeled as compressed simulation, so that "no connection" means something like "no short compression ties these facts together," or whether that framing discards the cases where a short argument establishes a dependence that no amount of simulation would reveal; whether independence claims can be checked against anything — is there an analogue of calibration, in which a class of presumptions could be shown to fail at a measurable rate, or does the whole enterprise rest on untestable judgment; and what happens when a heuristic argument about a physical system implicitly contains a model of the very processor running the argument, so that treating the argument as independent of its subject becomes self-undermining in a way I can gesture at but not state precisely. There may be better candidates I have not listed; include them if so.

For each question you rank highly, tell me what depends on it — which of the others become well-posed or tractable only after it is answered — how far it can be pushed by someone with my background in logic and probability but no physics, and what would count, over the next two years, as evidence that I had mis-ranked it. I care more about the dependency structure than the list itself: which question is genuinely upstream of the others, and which ones are tempting mainly because they are well-formed rather than because answering them would unblock anything.
