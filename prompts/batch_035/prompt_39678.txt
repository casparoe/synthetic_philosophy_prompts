I'm a longtime member of a community theater company, and this season we're putting on As You Like It, which I've loved since I first played a small part in it years ago. I never studied philosophy formally, but a while back I picked up a used book about how some sentences don't describe the world but actually do things — so that saying you promise is promising, and a minister's words at a wedding make the marriage rather than report on it. From what I understand, the philosopher behind this idea, Austin, catalogued all the ways these speech acts can go wrong — the wrong person says them, the circumstances aren't right, the speaker isn't sincere — and if I remember correctly, he included one striking exception: an actor saying the wedding words in a play marries nobody, because theatre somehow hollows the words out. That claim has been rattling around in my head during rehearsals, because it seems to me that As You Like It is built entirely on the opposite idea.

Here's what's bugging me. Rosalind, disguised as the boy Ganymede, promises to cure Orlando of his love for Rosalind by having him court Ganymede as if he were Rosalind, and in the middle of this fake cure they hold a mock wedding — Celia acting as the officiant, Rosalind-as-Ganymede standing in for the bride. So the ceremony is a fiction stacked on a disguise stacked on the fact that, in Shakespeare's theater, the whole part was played by a boy: a boy playing a woman playing a boy playing the bride. And yet by the end of the play everyone's marriage, including Rosalind's, is treated as completely real and binding — the god Hymen arrives to tie off four couples at once. So my question: does the mock wedding in the forest actually do something? Did Orlando really consent to something in that scene, and to whom? Is marrying someone who isn't who you take them to be a defect in the ceremony, no defect at all, or something the theory simply has no box for? And is Hymen's appearance at the end the play admitting the fake ceremony wasn't enough on its own — or the opposite, a theatrical flourish on top of a marriage the words had already accomplished?

I'd like you to take the role of a professional philosopher of language who also genuinely loves Shakespeare, and please think carefully before answering — maybe start by laying out the framework (performatives, felicity conditions, the various ways utterances misfire) in your own words, since I may be misremembering Austin and you should correct me where I've garbled him. Then give me a reading of the play through that machinery: which scenes you'd point my cast toward, where the theory genuinely illuminates what's happening, and where the play resists the reading and an honest critic has to say so. I'm especially interested in whether the Forest of Arden, where nearly everyone is playacting some version of themselves, breaks Austin's theatre exception — if the whole world is a stage in there, does that make every utterance hollow, or does the play show that these words need something other than sincerity to work, like the other party's acceptance? I'd rather have a reading that takes both Shakespeare and the philosophy seriously and admits friction where there is friction than one that forces the play into an illustration. And if you think Shakespeare dramatizes something the theory misses — some third thing between a hollow performance and a binding act — I'd love to hear what it is, because I suspect that's the actual heart of the play we're rehearsing.
