So I've been reading Zhuangzi on and off for a couple years, and I want to sort out what my gut verdicts on death-related stuff actually add up to, and whether the thing I think I take from him is really the thing I believe. My reactions to a bunch of cases:

- My friend's grandpa dies at 94, full life, everyone saw it coming for a year, and at the funeral his daughter gives a eulogy that's mostly laughing about his terrible puns. That feels completely right.
- A different friend's dad dies suddenly at 58. This friend is wrecked, cries for months, can barely talk about it. I do not think he should hurry up and get back to normal. If someone quoted Zhuangzi at him right now I'd be kind of furious, but I also don't think the grief itself is a failure of understanding.
- I have a chronic health thing, and thinking about my own eventual death honestly doesn't scare me much. I think that's fine. Feels like the equanimity is actually working.
- But when a healthy 25-year-old I know says he genuinely wouldn't mind dying young because death is just part of the same transformation, I find the attitude smug, almost creepy. Not fine. And it's not that I think he's lying — I think something's wrong with the attitude itself.
- Someone whose dog just died says "he's gone back into the flow of things" with a shrug. I half respect it and half find it cold, like a part of the response is missing.
- A terminal patient in constant pain decides to stop treatment, and I'm fine with it. A physically healthy but depressed person makes what they describe as the same calculation, and I'm not fine with it — but I have a hard time saying what the difference is, since "death is nothing to fear" reasoning would seem to bless both equally.

My tentative principle is something like: acceptance of death is admirable when it comes after or alongside the grief, and objectionable when it's used to skip the grief — equanimity as a destination, not an entrance. But that seems to get the Zhuangzi cases wrong if I read him right, since he seems to recommend arriving at the acceptance pretty fast, like the same day. So is there one principle that saves all my verdicts, or close to it, and if so which verdicts does it have to overturn? And if my skip-the-grief principle is too neat, what happens to the 25-year-old — where does the wrongness in his attitude actually live, if not in insincerity or in the fearlessness itself?

Also, whatever principle you land on, tell me what it predicts for the case I keep going back and forth on: a woman I know lost a child three years ago, and now she says the grieving and the loving were always one thing, so she refuses to treat the grief as a problem to be solved or a phase to exit. I genuinely can't tell if that's the most Zhuangzian position I've ever heard or the exact opposite of it, and I'd like to know what the principle says about her rather than what I want it to say.
