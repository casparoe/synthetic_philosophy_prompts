I'm TA-ing an intro epistemology course and need help grading a midterm answer on the surprise exam paradox, and I want a real point-by-point job, not a vibes check. (Also, if you could keep everything in American English, that'd be great -- the course style guide is picky.)

Here's the question that was on the exam:

"A teacher announces to her class on Friday afternoon: there will be exactly one exam next week (Mon-Fri), and on the morning of the exam you will not know that the exam is that day. (a) Carefully distinguish two readings of the announcement: on one, the students will not *know* on the morning of the exam that it is that day, given everything they know including the announcement; on the other, the day of the exam cannot be *deduced in advance* from the announcement (plus the calendar) alone. (b) Present the backward-induction argument that no exam satisfying the announcement can occur. (c) Explain Quine's diagnosis of where the induction goes wrong. (d) Say whether the announcement can be true when only one day of the week remains, and whether it can be true when two days remain."

And here's the student's answer, which is longish:

"(a) The announcement says the exam will be at an unknown time. Two readings: (1) you won't know the morning of that it's that day; (2) you can't deduce the day beforehand from what the teacher said. These come to the same thing, since knowing means having deduced it, and you can only deduce it from the announcement anyway.

(b) Suppose the exam were Friday. Then Thursday night, knowing there's exactly one exam next week, and it hasn't happened Mon-Thu, the students would know it's Friday. So a Friday exam would be known in advance, and the announcement rules that out. So Friday is out. Now suppose it were Thursday. The students know it's not Friday (that's what we just proved), so if Wednesday noon passes without an exam, Thursday is the only day left, and they'd know Wednesday night that it's Thursday. So Thursday is out too. Repeat: Wednesday, Tuesday, Monday are each eliminated once the later days are gone. So there's no day the exam can be on, so the announcement can't be fulfilled -- the students triumphantly conclude there's no exam.

(c) Quine's diagnosis: the students assume they'll still know the announcement is true all the way through the week, e.g. on Thursday night when they're eliminating Friday. That assumption is what fails. In the one-day case, the student who concludes 'the exam must be today' has to assume the exam will occur at all, and that's exactly what he can't take for granted. So Quine showed the announcement is actually self-contradictory -- that's why it can't be fulfilled and no surprise exam can ever happen. His fix only really works for the one-day case though; with five days there's too much going on for it to apply.

(d) With one day left the announcement is simply false: the student knows the exam is today. But since the announcement is inconsistent, the students can't rely on it at all, so really any exam on any day is a surprise -- the teacher just needs to not let the students trust her. Also note something interesting: shorten the week to two days, say Monday and Tuesday. The induction 'proves' no exam can happen, and yet if the exam is on Monday, the students didn't know Sunday night that it would be Monday, so everything the teacher said came true. So the announcement can be true with two days left, which is why the paradox is a real paradox."

Grade this out of 10 (or give a letter grade, your call, but be explicit about the scale), and then go through it point by point: what's correct, what's wrong, and specifically where the errors are, since a couple of them are subtle enough that they could slide.
