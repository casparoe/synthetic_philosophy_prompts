quick context: I'm a third year student taking a value theory / well-being course this semester and we just finished the unit on spectrum arguments and whether "better than" is transitive. We read some Temkin among other stuff, and I got voluntold to present my own positions in section this week so I wrote up a little list of what I believe, and now I want to know if it's even a consistent package before I defend it in front of everyone. Here's what I wrote down:

(1) "Better than" is transitive. If A is better than B and B is better than C, then A is better than C. This one feels non-negotiable to me, and I said as much — if you tell me there's an A and C where all three comparisons hold except the last one, I feel like you must have changed the meaning of "better" halfway through the argument.

(2) Equal goodness is transitive too. And I convinced myself this one actually follows from (1) plus completeness — for any two lives/options, either one is better or they're exactly as good, no fourth option. The derivation went something like: if A is as good as B and B is as good as C, suppose A is better than C... and then some steps... and you're forced to say A is as good as C. It felt airtight when I did it but tbh I can't reconstruct it now.

(3) My answer to the spectrum arguments is that the tiny steps just don't matter. Picture the one where one life contains a year of serious daily headaches and you move along a spectrum where each step removes a single minute from one headache somewhere. No single step makes the life better than the life one step back — a minute is too small to register. Adjacent lives on the spectrum are equally good, that's how I resist the conclusion.

(4) But I also wrote this down separately and now I'm not sure it can live with (3): if you make someone better off and nobody worse off, the outcome is better, full stop. We had a whole section debate about this. Like, a minute of headache is a real harm that happens to a real person, and I don't see how adding a harm, with nothing anywhere in the picture to offset it, leaves the total value exactly where it was. So the life with one fewer minute of headache is better.

(5) And obviously, completely obviously, the life at the far end of the spectrum (a year of serious headaches) is worse than the life with no headaches at all. I'm not giving that one up no matter what.

(6) One more that's bugging me. When I justified (1) out loud I used the "goodness as a quantity" picture — A is better than B when A has more goodness in it, and "more than" is transitive because quantities are. But in my last essay I argued pretty hard that values aren't all on one scale — I don't think justice and pleasure go into a common currency, and I even said some comparisons are apples and oranges. So did I saw off the branch I was sitting on for (1)?

So that's my mess. Which of these actually conflict, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest repair? Two things I genuinely can't sort out on my own: whether the real fight is between (3) and (4) or somewhere else entirely, and whether (6) is fatal to (1) or just means I owe a different justification for it. Also be honest with me — if the whole package is cooked and something big has to go, tell me which one, don't just spread the blame around evenly. I'd rather know now than get eaten alive in section.
