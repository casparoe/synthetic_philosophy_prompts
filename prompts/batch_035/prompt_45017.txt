A guy in my local EA meetup has been on a wild-animal-suffering kick for months, and he finally produced an argument I can't get out of my head. It goes:

(1) What makes suffering bad is how bad it is for the one feeling it — it doesn't matter whether that one is a person, a pig, or a mite.
(2) Most individual wild animals live lives of net negative welfare: r-selected species pump out enourmous numbers of offspring, and the typical fate is to die of starvation, exposure, or predation within days, so for every animal that gets a decent run there are thousands that get a short miserable one.
(3) A life dominated by suffering is worse than no life at all.
(4) Destroying habitat prevents those lives from starting.
(5) Preventing something bad is a good, even when there's no particular someone who is better off as a result — compare deciding not to have a child you know would live in constant pain; nobody is saved, but you didn't do anything wrong.
(6) Add up the numbers — insects alone are, what, 10^18 at a time — and the good of razing a wild wetland that no human depends on swamps everything on the other side, since biodiversity and beauty aren't suffering-relevant.
(7) So paving over wild habitat is a moral improvement, and we should be doing alot more of it.

I pushed back with the obvious stuff — that by the same logic we should painlessly kill every wild animal alive, and that you could run the identical argument for not having children in poor conditions — and he just cheerfully said yes to both. That's when I knew the thing proves too much. But I can't actually find the broken step. I want to keep (1) and (2), since I do think those lives are mostly awful, and I can't accept (7) or the euthanasia corollary. So where does it break? And if the only way out is dropping one of the premises I like, tell me what dropping it costs me elsewhere.
