A friend of mine who swears by Hofstadter keeps arguing about machine consciousness, and his case runs entirely through the Gödelian picture: just as a formal system, once it is rich enough, can be given a coding scheme that lets statements be made about the system's own theorems, so a brain, once it is rich enough, can form representations of its own representing — and that folding-back, he says, is all that consciousness is. From this he draws two conclusions: that consciousness comes in degrees tracking how much of the system the system can represent, and that a current language model with any self-model is already partway to being a mind. When I objected that such a model cannot inspect its own weights, he replied that Gödel numbering never got to inspect the axioms either — the trick was only ever finding a way to talk about the system from inside a setting that was not built for such talk. He also points to the Gödel sentence's suspended status, neither provable nor refutable, and says that gap is where the "I" lives, the same way a mind outruns any fixed inventory of its own beliefs. I want an audit of the analogy as he is using it, not a lecture on incompleteness. Please respond with exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction, no conclusion — each item a sentence or two, covering: (1) the correspondence, however compressed, stating what plays the role of the formal system, the Gödel sentence, provability, truth, and the level-crossing, and flagging any slot his argument requires that has no tenant; (2) the single disanalogy that does the most damage to his two conclusions, and why; (3) the disanalogies that look damaging but can fairly be set aside for this particular argument, and why they do not matter here; (4) your verdict on whether either conclusion survives once (2) is on the table, and, if one does, whether a different analogy would carry it more honestly; (5) how confident you are in the verdicts above and the one point you are least sure of.
