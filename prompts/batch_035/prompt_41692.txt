I write novels for a living, and for the past month I have been unable to shake an argument that a friend — a moral philosopher — laid out across my kitchen table one evening after reading the draft of my new book.

(1) The badness of an outcome is additive over the bad experiences it contains: if one outcome contains a worse collection of experiences than another, and the two outcomes are otherwise alike, the first is the worse outcome.

(2) As drafted, my ending will move roughly a million readers to genuine sorrow. Each of them, alone with the book, will feel a distinct pang of grief — call it ten minutes of unmixed negative feeling, not offset at that moment by anything.

(3) Additivity is indifferent to whose experience an experience is; the sorrows of distinct readers count against the outcome no less than the sufferings of distinct persons count in any other aggregation, and they neither cancel each other nor fade for being widely distributed.

(4) By the same arithmetic that makes a momentary speck of dust in the eye of an unimaginably vast number of people worse than fifty years of torture for one person — a comparison my friend endorses and that I am half inclined to endorse myself — a million ten-minute sorrows exceed in badness one hour of severe suffering for a single person.

(5) So, holding everything else fixed — suppose I could write either the tragic ending or a flat one of precisely equal artistic merit, and suppose my readers, asked in advance, would each choose the tragic one — publishing the tragic ending is the worse act, and I ought to write the flat one.

I cannot accept (5). Yet I cannot locate the failure. I have tried rejecting (1), and my friend answers that I have then abandoned the specks arithmetic too, which I am reluctant to do. If I keep (1), the trouble must lie in (2) or (3); but (2) seems undeniable — my readers' sorrow is real, and I will not claim their tears are nothing — and (3) appears to be the entire point of aggregation. What unsettles me further is that if the argument stands, it seems to show not merely that I should flatten my ending but that no one should ever choose an evening of tragedy over an evening of comedy, a difficult climb over an easy one, a bitter medicine over a placebo — and my friend, asked directly, refuses to accept any of that either. So the argument appears to prove too much, and I would like to know exactly where. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs: in particular, whether the cost must also be paid in the specks case itself, which has the same structure in miniature — a momentary irritation embedded in a life that is otherwise going perfectly well.
