I'm a PhD student in comparative literature, and this spring I'm running the discussion sections for a survey course on the nineteenth-century novel. My section has fourteen undergraduates, meets for eighty minutes, and the week in question follows their assigned reading of the Legend of the Grand Inquisitor in The Brothers Karamazov. I don't want another session where we agree that the Inquisitor is a chilling figure, trade a few platitudes about freedom, and disperse. I want the students to run into the Inquisitor's actual claim in their own choices: that the mass of people will surrender freedom for bread, certainty, and someone to obey, and — the part that stings — will feel relieved when they do.

Design a staged deliberation for me, a game with rules and a points-based payoff scheme (no real money), with these requirements:

1. The activity must come before the mapping. Students should be placed in a fictional decision-making body under mild time pressure — a colony, a town council, a startup commune, whatever you judge best — and only in the debrief should the correspondence with the Legend be made explicit. Nothing about Dostoevsky or the Inquisitor may surface before then.

2. The decision structure should reproduce, in miniature, the three temptations as the Inquisitor reads them: bread, meaning guaranteed material welfare at the price of each member carrying the burden of choosing their own ends; miracle and mystery, meaning some staged spectacle or manufactured certainty that binds the group's belief without argument; and authority, meaning irrevocably handing future decisions to one participant. These should arrive as three sequential forks, each individually defensible, so that the cumulative surrender happens one reasonable step at a time.

3. At every fork, surrendering the freedom must be strictly dominant in expected points — engineer the payoff tables so this is true — yet the debrief must be able to recover what the points didn't price. Include one additional role: a silent stranger, a participant who may not speak at all and whose only permitted move in each round is a single nonverbal gesture of their choosing. Tell me what you predict the silent stranger will do, and how I should handle it if their gesture electrifies the room or if they sulk.

4. The debrief must map specific moments in the activity onto specific moves in the Inquisitor's argument: the claim that Christ's refusal of the three temptations bought freedom for the few at the cost of burdening the many with a freedom they cannot bear; the accusation that Christ loved humanity too much to enslave it, and the Inquisitor's insistence that he and his kind correct that error out of pity; and the ending, the stranger's wordless act that the Inquisitor carries with him even while holding to his idea. Say which of these mappings you expect to land cleanly and which will need my scaffolding.

5. Predict at least two things that will go wrong the first time I run this, and how I should handle each — be concrete about failure modes like students gaming the payoff tables or correctly smelling the setup from the start.

6. Close with an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce. My worry is this: the Inquisitor knows the whole edifice is a lie, takes on the burden of the lie out of love for the weak, and suffers under it — and no points game in which everyone knows it is a game can generate that. Tell me whether any design choice can partially approach it (for instance, giving one participant a concealed role with genuine stakes attached to keeping the rigging hidden from the others), and what I must simply supply in discussion because no mechanism will produce it.

Give me a timeline for each phase, materials, groupings, and rules written plainly enough that a fellow TA could run the thing from your write-up alone.
