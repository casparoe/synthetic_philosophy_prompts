You are a senior research analyst at an organization that ranks causes for large-scale philanthropic funding, with formal training in decision theory and welfare economics. Please think carefully and in depth: begin with a brief brainstorm in which you enumerate the candidate principles that might capture my verdicts, test each against the cases below, and only then settle on a final recommendation.

I have been recording my gut verdicts about which causes deserve priority, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them. The framework I was raised on scores a cause roughly as the product of its importance (the scale of what's at stake), its neglectedness (how little money and attention it currently receives), and its tractability (how much good a marginal unit of resources can realistically accomplish). My verdicts keep diverging from the arithmetic, and I can't tell whether the framework needs a repair or whether my gut does. Here are the cases:

1. Rare disease versus malaria. A genetic disease affecting a few hundred people worldwide, with no existing funding or research program, scores enormously on neglectedness and decently on tractability. Malaria control receives billions of dollars annually, so its neglectedness is mediocre. My verdict: additional spending on malaria still beats spending on the rare disease. Scale swamps neglectedness here, in a way the multiplicative formula doesn't seem to allow.

2. Asteroid detection. Highly important, plausibly tractable, and — once the survey telescopes are built and the big near-Earth objects are catalogued — essentially no longer neglected, because the job is done. My verdict: fund it until the catalogue is complete and then stop almost entirely. Some causes seem to have a fill level, and drops past the rim are worth nearly nothing, while the last bucket before full is worth a lot.

3. AI safety versus peer review reform. My gut ranks AI safety near the very top even though I'd concede its tractability is a mess: nobody can measure progress, and a marginal million dollars might accomplish nothing. Meanwhile, reforming academic peer review strikes me as important, genuinely neglected, and quite tractable — I can picture specific interventions that would work — and yet I rank it low. These two verdicts pull in opposite directions on how much tractability should matter, and I don't know how to stop them from doing that.

4. Awareness as hazard. Publicizing a dangerous research technique, with the aim of getting it regulated before someone misuses it, scores well on all three factors: important, neglected, and tractable in the sense that a media campaign would certainly change the discourse. My verdict: it can be a mistake anyway, because drawing attention to the technique is itself a mechanism of the harm. The best-known versions of the framework have no slot for interventions whose neglectedness is partly protective.

5. Factory farming versus wild animal suffering. If importance is total suffering at stake, wild animal suffering dwarfs factory farming; it is also far more neglected. It is much less tractable. My verdict: factory farming first, and it isn't close. But this seems to contradict case 1, where I let importance steamroll neglectedness; here I'm letting tractability steamroll both.

6. Smooth versus threshold causes. Two causes with nearly identical scores, but one produces benefits smoothly per additional dollar while the other needs a critical mass — say, a lobbying campaign that achieves nothing at all until it crosses some unpredictable threshold of funding and staff, then delivers everything at once. My verdict: the smooth one is the better bet, and I'd discount the threshold cause even though the framework treats them identically. Though I notice I don't feel this way about asteroid detection, which is also all-or-nothing, so discounting thresholds can't be the whole story either.

7. Trajectory versus headcount. My gut elevates causes that would change the long-run direction of civilization even when the identifiable present-day beneficiaries are few, over causes that reliably help many people in fixed, bounded ways. This isn't in the framework at all, as far as I can tell, unless importance is silently being redefined as something like expected value over the whole future.

My tentative repair is to drop neglectedness as an independent factor and instead ask about marginal impact per additional unit of resources, given diminishing and possibly discontinuous returns — which I think handles cases 1, 2, and maybe 6 — while rethinking tractability as a discount factor rather than a multiplier. But I can see this makes case 3 worse, not better, since my high ranking of AI safety seems to require ignoring exactly the thing a tractability discount would punish.

So here is my request. Please state, precisely, the principle or small set of candidate principles that best captures these verdicts; identify which of my cases motivates each clause of your principle; say what your principle predicts about a few cases I have not listed (I would especially like one where it disagrees with the standard ITN arithmetic, and one where it disagrees with my own gut); and tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, so I know which ones I ought to consider abandoning rather than saving at the price of a hopelessly baroque framework. If you think my proposed repair is on the right track, stress-test it against the cases rather than merely endorsing it, and repair the repair where it fails.
