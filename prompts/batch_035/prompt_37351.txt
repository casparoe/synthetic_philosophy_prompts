I am a software engineer with no formal philosophical education, though I have read enough about modal metaphysics to know the vocabulary. Please make an argument, on my behalf, for Lewis's counterpart theory over transworld identity, using an analogy from my own work as the load-bearing intuition pump.

Here is the analogy. In a typical engineering organization, a record in the production database — say, a customer entity — is periodically cloned into isolated environments: a staging replica for testing, several analytics extracts, a sandbox snapshot. In practice, nothing we do treats the staging copy as the very same object as the production row: we mutate it freely, roll it back, delete it, and no one thinks we have thereby harmed the customer. At the same time, all of our reasoning proceeds as if the copy were that customer: we ask what the system would do for this user, run experiments against the copy, and treat the copy's behavior as informative about the original. Notice, too, that the copy relation fans out: the production row can be cloned into many environments, and two copies need not resemble or correspond to each other in the way each corresponds to the original — the relation is not symmetric, not transitive, and nothing in our practice requires that it be.

As I understand it, counterpart theory holds that when we say someone could have been otherwise — that I could have become a musician, say — the person in that counterfactual situation is not literally me but a distinct individual who resembles me, a counterpart, while the transworld identity view holds that one and the same individual exists across many possible worlds. Please construct the strongest argument you can, from the standpoint of the analogy, that the counterpart theorist's picture is the correct one.

I would like you to develop the analogy carefully rather than merely gesturing at it. State exactly what in the analogy plays the role of a possible world, of an individual, and of the counterpart relation. Identify the points where the analogy is strongest and where a defender of transworld identity would insist it breaks down — for instance, that the production row and its copy are both concrete objects in one shared world, whereas possible worlds are supposed to be isolated. Then argue that those breakdowns do not ultimately save transworld identity, or at least show what they reveal about why the analogy is as persuasive as it is. Where the engineering practice has features with no modal-metaphysical analogue, please say so explicitly rather than papering over them.
