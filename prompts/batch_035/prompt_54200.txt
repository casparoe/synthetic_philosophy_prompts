I keep tripping over the question of which historical periods are real things and which are later inventions, and to make progress I've been recording my gut verdicts on a list of cases, in the hope that some principle lies underneath them. I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures the list; what it predicts for cases I haven't decided; and which of my verdicts, if any, it cannot accommodate — and if some verdict has to go, I want to know which one and why it's that one rather than another.

Here is the list, with the verdicts as they actually come to me:

1. The Middle Ages: real, I think. Latin Christendom between the collapse of the Western Empire and whatever happens at the far end has a genuine character, even though I know the very idea of a "middle" age was a humanist's put-down coined centuries after the fact, and even though the canonical opening date, 476, is basically bookkeeping — nobody at the time believed a Roman empire had just ended.
2. The Renaissance: real in the history of art. Italian painting, sculpture, and architecture really do change gear in the fifteenth century, even though the label is a nineteenth-century coinage. But not real as a period of European history generally, because "the Renaissance" as a period label treats Florence in 1450 and rural England in 1450 as the same period, when the change hadn't arrived in the one place and wouldn't for a century. Same word: real in one discipline, not real in another.
3. The Baroque: the same pattern again — real in music (roughly from the early operas to the death of Bach), real in architecture, but not a period of economic or diplomatic history.
4. The Industrial Revolution: real, and I don't care that it took the better part of a century, that no one living through it experienced a revolution, or that the label was applied long afterwards by people looking back. Later coinage doesn't seem to bother me here at all.
5. The Dark Ages: not real — drop it. This verdict bothers me, because by my other criteria there is a real discontinuity in the West after the empire falls: long-distance trade, literacy, and urban life genuinely crater. I just think the label is a polemic that does more harm than good, and I want whatever principle I end up with to be able to say that.
6. The Axial Age: not real. Calling roughly simultaneous developments in Greece, India, China, and Israel a single "period" strikes me as pattern-matching: the peoples involved had, for the most part, never heard of one another. A simultaneity isn't a period.
7. The Enlightenment: real in intellectual history, whatever we do about its hopeless boundaries.
8. The long nineteenth century, 1789–1914: real; it has an arc.
9. The Victorian era: not really real — a bookkeeping device tied to one woman's reign, even though it covers nearly the same clock time as a stretch of the long nineteenth century, which does feel real to me.
10. The Scientific Revolution: the case I keep going back and forth on. Everything in me says something enormous happened in the study of nature between, say, Copernicus and Newton, and that "revolution" is the right word for it; and everything the revisionist historians say — that the label is a twentieth-century invention, that the change was slow and geographically scattered, that many of the participants were astrologers and alchemists, and that "science" in our sense didn't yet exist as a category — cuts the other way. By my Industrial Revolution verdict (slow, unremarked-at-the-time change, later label) it should be real; by my Dark Ages verdict (later label with an agenda, flattening what it names) it should be suspect.

So: is there one principle that gets all of this, or close to it? My own tentative attempt, which I'd like you to test and repair, runs as follows: a period is real only relative to a domain — political history, art history, music, the study of nature, and so on — and it is real in that domain if and only if that domain's trajectory shows a marked, non-arbitrary discontinuity or a coherent unity across the stretch in question, and the label does not systematically misrepresent what the stretch was like. On this account the Renaissance is real in art, the Baroque real in music, and the Dark Ages not real because "dark" misrepresents. But the account doesn't yet tell me what to do with the Scientific Revolution, where the label arguably misrepresents (no unified "science," no sharp break) while the change in trajectory seems unmistakable; and I can't tell whether the Axial Age falls to a "connected tradition" requirement or whether that requirement is just me rationalizing a verdict I'd already reached.

Please state the principle — or a short list of candidate principles, if my verdicts won't yield a single one — as precisely as you can; tie each clause to the verdicts that motivate it; tell me what it says about the Scientific Revolution, the case I cannot settle; tell me what it predicts for at least two cases I haven't listed, such as whether the "Digital Revolution" is a real period of anything, or whether the "Anthropocene" is a period or merely a label; and be explicit about which of my ten verdicts your favored principle cannot save, and why it's those rather than the others.
