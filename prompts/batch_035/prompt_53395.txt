I'm a control systems engineer by training and trade; my working vocabulary is state variables, controllability and observability, feedback and feedforward paths, disturbance models, the internal model principle, robust control under model uncertainty, cascade loops, integrator windup. I've recently been reading the Stoicism revival — the Enchiridion, its afterlife in contemporary self-help and productivity culture, the apps and newsletter-style content it has spawned — and I keep having the feeling that the ancient dichotomy of control, the claim that some things are up to us (chiefly our judgments and impulses) and some are not (essentially everything external), is either the same insight control engineering formalized centuries later or something importantly different that my vocabulary will mangle if I force the mapping.

Restate the dichotomy of control and the surrounding Stoic apparatus in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Translate impressions and the assent we give or withhold from them, externals as disturbances, preferred and dispreferred indifferents, the discipline of rehearsing future setbacks in advance, and the popular modern reformulation that softens the dichotomy into a trichotomy — some things entirely up to us, some partly, some not at all — into control-theoretic terms. For each element, say what maps cleanly onto something I already use, what translates only with distortion, and what has no expression in engineering language at all.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Two questions in particular. First, control theory grades everything by degree: a state is controllable or not given the actuators available, partial controllability is the ordinary case, and robust control exists precisely because we can neither fully reject disturbances nor perfectly model the plant. The Stoics, by contrast, seem to draw something like a binary line through the world — the trichotomy reformulation looks, from where I sit, like an ad hoc patch motivated by exactly the problem my field solves with graded notions. Does that difference survive the translation, or does it dissolve once impressions, assent, and externals are properly mapped onto states, actuators, and disturbances? Second, no control engineer assumes the controller itself is immune to disturbance: sensors are noisy, actuators drift, the plant changes, and even the controller's own dynamics are affected by what it regulates. But the Stoic position seems to be that one subsystem — my faculty of judgment — is somehow perfectly insulated from the disturbances affecting everything else. Is that insulation claim even statable as a control-theoretic proposition, and if not, does the residue point to something the engineering vocabulary cannot say, or to something the ancient framework was too quick to assume? Finally, tell me whether my sense that the modern revival is using the dichotomy of control to mean roughly focus on your error signal and forget the plant is a mistranslation of the original doctrine or, uncomfortable as it may be, the most faithful translation available.
