I'm a science-and-ideas correspondent, and I've been asked to sit on our outlet's standards desk while it drafts a policy on a recurring fight: what separates reporting from merely passing along what we've been told. The immediate case is this. A colleague has obtained what appears to be an internal regulatory document suggesting that the pivotal trial behind a widely prescribed drug had serious data irregularities. She believes the document is genuine, her source has been reliable before, but nobody can authenticate the document, and the regulator won't comment. She wants to write, in our voice, that the trial was compromised. Our current style guidance — buried in a decades-old handbook — says reporters should publish what they know to be true, and doesn't say anything more.

The desk has split into three camps, and I recognize the shape of the argument from the philosophy I've covered. One camp holds a knowledge rule: assert only what you know; since we don't know the document is genuine, we can assert only that it exists and what it says. A second camp holds a truth rule: what matters is that the claim itself be true; if the trial was in fact compromised, running the story is fine, and the risk is just ordinary newsgathering risk, to be weighed editorially rather than as a matter of principle. A third holds a sincerity rule: publish what the reporter responsibly believes on good evidence, provided the story flags the basis of the belief — attribution, sourcing caveats, and all.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before next week's meeting. It should recommend one of these norms for our newsroom's official policy, and it should do so with the following on the record. First, the desk must also settle two adjacent cases our current guidance handles inconsistently: our science desk routinely reports preprint findings with hedges like "scientists believe," and our breaking-news desk will state something as fact, update it hours later as the situation develops, and then run a correction — treating the earlier assertion as somehow retroactively repaired. Second, whatever rule we adopt has to give practical guidance a reporter can apply on deadline, not just a slogan. Third, the losing camps' strongest consideration should be treated seriously — in particular the worry that a sincerity rule lets us assert things that later turn out false, and the worry that a knowledge rule would make breaking news and even ordinary science coverage impossible, since we almost never know in the demanding sense.

Finally, address a wrinkle that keeps coming up in our internal debate: whether publishing under a hedge — "according to a document we could not independently verify" — is genuinely a different kind of assertion from flat-out stating the claim, or just the same assertion with a liability shield attached. My own hunch is the hedge changes something important, but I can't say what, and the desk deserves better than a hunch. Recommend, with reasons, and be explicit about what your recommendation asks the losing camps to give up.
