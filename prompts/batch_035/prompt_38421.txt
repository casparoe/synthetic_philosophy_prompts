In my philosophy of art seminar we have been working through Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste," and our instructor has been ruling on illustrative cases all semester, several of them her own. So far: she holds that a forgery which fooled experts for decades becomes an inferior work once its provenance is exposed; she insists that Milton's superiority over a hopeless hack versifier is a plain matter of fact, not mere preference, yet allows that two informed readers who disagree about whether a good but minor contemporary novel is beautiful are simply differing, with no fact of the matter; she counts the moral viciousness of a technically dazzling propaganda film as an aesthetic defect in the film itself; and she cheerfully calls unspoiled mountain scenery beautiful even though no mind designed or intended it. Next week's case, on which she has promised a verdict: an image produced by a generative model that, in a blind test, a panel of seasoned critics rated above every human entry. Will she say it is beautiful? What I would like from you is less the answer than the reasoning behind it: what rules fit her verdicts so far, what each of those rules predicts for the machine-made image, which rule she is most plausibly running and why, and which further case — one she has not yet ruled on — would best tell the rival rules apart.
