I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to contribute an essay to a craft journal on what exactly it is for a character to *have* the traits I give her — courage, stinginess, vanity — as opposed to merely enacting them on the page. Before drafting it, I'd like an audit of the position I've drifted into over the years, because I suspect it doesn't fully cohere. Here are the commitments, as best I can state them. First: a character's traits are dispositions. To say my protagonist is patient is to make a claim about how she would tend to act and react across a range of situations, not about any single scene. Second: a character can possess a trait that is never manifested anywhere in the book. I know my current heroine would walk into a burning building for her brother, though no fire ever occurs in the novel, and I think a reader who inferred as much from what the text does show could be correct. Third: manifestation doesn't suffice either. A character who performs generous acts purely out of vanity is not generous but vain; the mask doesn't count. Fourth: there is a fact of the matter about a character's traits over and above anything the text displays — trait ascriptions to fictional people can be right or wrong, and not merely by my say-so at the moment of writing. Fifth, a craft doctrine I hold firmly: the only honest way to establish a trait on the page is to test it — to place the character in the situation that would call the trait forth and write what happens; otherwise you're just asserting. And sixth: if I resolve, as author, never to let my coward face a real test — always to ring the phone, always to contrive his escape or anesthetize him with borrowed courage before the danger can arrive — I have not thereby made him not-a-coward. The cowardice is still there; I've only made sure it never shows.

Some of these, I suspect, are pulling against each other, and some only look like it. The one that gnaws at me: if a trait is a disposition, and a disposition is a matter of how the character would tend to act, what fixes the "would" for a woman who never faces the fire? In a real person the unmanifested disposition has a ground in her history and makeup; in my heroine there is nothing but prose, and I could write her either way tomorrow. Does the fourth commitment survive that? Does the sixth sit with the first, given that on the simplest reading of dispositions my perpetually rescued coward comes out brave — danger arises, a brave act occurs, by my contrivance? And is the fifth in genuine conflict with the second, or does that tension dissolve on inspection?

Please go through the six commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and how badly; for each, what would have to give and what the revision costs; which pairs only look to be in tension and why the appearance dissolves; and which single revision, if any, would clean up the most. Treat me as a colleague who can handle the technical literature on dispositions — finks, masks, antidotes, conditional analyses — but wants the verdicts applied to fictional characters rather than to fragile glass or irascible wires. Please answer in American English.
