I teach an upper-level seminar in philosophy of cognitive science, and I'm writing the take-home final. I want one essay question on Dennett's four-fold taxonomy of minds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures — that cannot be answered by reciting the taxonomy and giving one tidy example of each. The question should force students to confront the internal structure of the scheme, not just its contents. In particular, I want the question built around two pressures. First, Dennett presents the four kinds as successive refinements of a single theme — generate and test, with the locus of testing migrating from whole populations, through individual reinforcement histories, to offline inner simulation, and finally to cultural tools like words and maps. That framing makes it pressing to say whether the difference between a Popperian and a Gregorian creature is a difference in kind or merely a further relocation of the same trick: if a Gregorian creature is just a Popperian whose inner environment is partly furnished by others, does the taxonomy have four categories or three-and-a-half? Second, there is a tension I want them to see between the taxonomy and Dennett's own instrumentalism. On the intentional stance, attributing beliefs and desires is a predictive strategy that does not commit us to any particular inner architecture; yet the taxonomy reads like a claim about inner architecture — about what is actually happening inside the creature that earns it its place in the scheme. Students should have to say whether the four kinds are natural kinds of cognitive architecture, artifacts of what an observer with our evidence could distinguish, or something in between, and what would follow for the taxonomy if the Gregorian layer turned out to be scaffolding that, once installed, transforms the Popperian machinery itself rather than merely feeding it. Please write the essay question (suitable for about 1,200 words in response), a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain — and tell me, in a line each, the two or three ways students typically go wrong on this material, so my graders know what to watch for.
