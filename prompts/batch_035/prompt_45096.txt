I keep running into these deals where two parties in a standoff agree to replace the all-out version of the conflict with a cheaper, randomized version, and I want to figure out what principle actually explains my gut reactions to them. Take my verdicts: two duelists flip a coin instead of actually shooting at each other -- obviously great, both walk away and one still counts as winning. Two companies in a bidding war agree to submit one fixed price and draw straws for the contract -- seems fine, they just split the bidding costs they'd have wasted. Two countries that would go to war, where each is confident it would win, agree to a weighted coin flip instead -- I can't tell whether this is a genuine improvement or whether they're each just wrong about the odds and the deal only looks good to both because of that. Two restaurants on the same street agree to alternate closing days so they stop undercutting each other on price -- here I balk, even though by the same logic it's "safe" for the two of them, the customers are the ones eating the loss. And two enemies who honestly relish the fight agree to arbitration instead -- on paper it leaves them both better off, but if part of what they were paying for was the confrontation itself, I don't see how the deal leaves them as well off. So, assuming you as someone who thinks carefully about game theory and ethics: is there one principle that captures all of these, something like "a deal is kosher when it's an expected-win for every party to it", and which of my reactions does that principle miss?
