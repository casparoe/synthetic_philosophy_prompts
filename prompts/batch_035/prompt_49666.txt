I'm a novelist (three books out, working on a fourth that's half memoir and half invented family history, which is doing things to my head I did not expect). Last month at a literary festival I was on a panel with a philosopher of mind, and at the afterparty she told me, very casually, that the self is just a center of narrative gravity -- Dennett's thing apparently -- and that this means my characters, my readers, and my own narrator-voice are all the same kind of object: abstractions a story needs in order to stay coherent. I've been chewing on that ever since. The thing is, she used it to do two jobs at once: to comfort me (weaving autobiography into fiction doesn't make you a fraud, because there's no fact of the matter about which parts are "really you") and to unsettle me (also your sense of being one continuous person with a life is itself a narrative contrivance, so enjoy). I genuinely can't tell if those are the same claim, or whether either of them follows from the phrase, or whether the phrase follows from Dennett or is a folk-garbled version of him.

So: you are a professional philosopher of mind who has spent years on personal identity, narrative identity, Dennett, the self-model literature, all of it. Think carefully and do some actual brainstorming before you settle on anything. What are the distinct, precise claims that "the self is a center of narrative gravity" could be making? Lay them out as a menu, from the deflationary to the radical, and for each reading tell me three things: is it trivial, substantive-but-defensible, or just false; does it support the comfort version, the unsettling version, or neither; and would the philosopher at the party plausibly endorse it. I especially want the center-of-mass analogy pressed hard -- a center of mass is a mathematically well-defined point posited by a true theory of genuinely physical objects, and my suspicion is that once you lean on that, the self comes out either more real than she wants or way less well-defined than the analogy permits. Also flag any reading that would have embarassing consequences for a novelist specifically, since I keep half-noticing that she was maybe telling me my job is more metaphysically strange than I'd assumed. I want a list I could realistically bring back to her at the next festival.
