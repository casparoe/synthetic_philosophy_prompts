I am a retired condensed matter physicist who has spent the last two years reading philosophy of mathematics with the enthusiasm of a convert, and I have become increasingly impatient with the platonism–nominalism debate as it is usually run. The stock arguments on both sides feel like they were designed for people whose paradigms of mathematics come from the theorem–proof culture of pure mathematics, whereas my own decades of practice involved something rather different: mathematical objects that were manifestly our own artifacts, freely built for convenience, and yet strikingly effective. In physics we rely constantly on what we call effective theories — frameworks, like Landau's treatment of phase transitions, that deliberately coarse-grain away microphysical detail and introduce collective variables that are not fundamental constituents of anything, yet which make quantitatively accurate predictions within a well-defined domain of validity. Nobody in my field takes the order parameter to be a primitive ingredient of the world's inventory; it is a bookkeeping device whose usefulness is itself explained by the underlying physics. I have come to suspect that all of mathematics is like this — a family of extraordinarily successful effective theories whose ontology we should treat the same way we treat the order parameter — and I would like this suspicion turned into a proper argument.

Please argue for nominalism on roughly these grounds, but develop it beyond my sketch. In particular, I would like you to address what I take to be the strongest platonist rejoinder: that mathematics displays features effective theories lack, such as unexpected applicability in domains far removed from any practical motive (complex numbers in quantum mechanics being the tired but real example), objective independence results like the independence of the continuum hypothesis from the standard axioms, and the apparent convergence of mathematicians who work in isolation. A merely convenient artifact, the platonist will say, could be revised for convenience; the continuum hypothesis's undecidability seems like a fact discovered about something, not a limitation of our bookkeeping. If you think the analogy with effective theories helps answer this, show how; if you think it doesn't, say so plainly and lean on whatever does the work instead. I would also like you to take seriously a point from my own training that philosophers often underweight: that physicists' mathematics has historically tolerated divergent series, formal manipulations, and heuristic leaps that were later either vindicated or quietly rationalized — which suggests to me that even our standards of mathematical rigor are domain-relative instruments rather than windows onto a platonic realm.

Please organize your response under the following headings, in this order, with roughly the indicated lengths: "The position stated" (about 100 words), "The argument from effective theories" (about 300 words), "The strongest platonist objection" (about 200 words), "The nominalist reply" (about 300 words), and "What would change my mind" (about 150 words). In that final section, please identify the specific argument or consideration that would most plausibly move you to abandon the nominalist verdict, and indicate where in the foregoing reasoning you think the argument is most vulnerable to failure — I would rather have an honest map of the weak points than a confident flourish at the end.
