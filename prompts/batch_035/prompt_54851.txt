My ecology professor gave us what she called the standard story on species, and I want to know how much of it to trust before I repeat any of it on the exam. Her summary, as I wrote it down: A species is simply a group of organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring — that is the biological species concept, and it is essentially what Darwin himself used. It handles nearly all cases perfectly well, and its only real weakness is asexual organisms like bacteria, where there is no interbreeding at all, so they just have to be sorted by arbitrary cutoffs in DNA similarity. This weakness, plus the way evolution is a continuum with new species shading gradually out of old ones — the textbook example is the gulls that circle the Arctic, each population interbreeding with its neighbors until the ring closes and the ends no longer interbreed at all — is why most biologists today have concluded that species are not objectively real. They are convenient labels, a filing system imposed on an unbroken continuum of life. Genomics has pretty much settled this: sequence enough individuals and you find there are no boundaries out there in nature, just one variable gene pool, so every species boundary is a line we draw for our own convenience.

I suspect some of this is right, since I have heard versions of most of it elsewhere, but the confidence bothers me, and I do not know enough about systematics to say where the story bends. Please go through it more or less sentence by sentence: which parts would a working systematist accept as stated, which would they accept only with an important qualification, and which are just wrong? In particular, I would like to know whether the interbreeding definition is really the mainstream one or one camp's proposal among several, whether the professor's history of the view is accurate, whether the situation with asexual organisms supports the conclusion she draws from it, and whether the gull example still does the work she wants it to do. Finally — and this is the part I most care about — does her overall conclusion, that species are not objectively real, actually follow from the accurate parts of what she said, or does it depend on the parts that would not survive the audit? If the accurate parts support something weaker, tell me what.
