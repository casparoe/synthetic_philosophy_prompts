I've been a regular on LessWrong and the adjacent rationalist blogs for close to fifteen years, and there's a comment-thread argument I can't stop chewing on. The thread was about embryo selection for intelligence — whether parents should be free to select among their own embryos for polygenic scores, whether it should be subsidized, or restricted. Deep in the thread, someone shut down the egalitarian side with the old one-liner "equal opportunity, not equal outcomes": the point being, as far as I can tell, that worrying about a widening genetic cognitive gap is a demand for equal results, which no serious person endorses, whereas the defensible ideal is just equal starting points. The thread then went nowhere, because nobody said what either half of the slogan meant.

I want to know what that slogan actually claims, and I suspect it does much less work than the commenter thinks. Please answer as an analytic political philosopher who has taught the luck-egalitarianism literature and its critics for years — comfortable with Rawls on fair equality of opportunity, Dworkin on brute versus option luck and equality of resources, Sen on capabilities, relational-egalitarian objections to the whole distributive paradigm, and the leveling-down objection — and think carefully before committing to anything: start by brainstorming the plausible interpretations, then organize them, then evaluate.

Specifically:

1. Lay out the menu. What distinct, precise theses could "equal opportunity" stand for? At minimum I can see formal nondiscrimination, careers open to talents, the Rawlsian requirement that the similarly talented and motivated have similar prospects, the luck-egalitarian demand that people not be disadvantaged by brute luck — including unchosen natural endowment — and something Sen-style about the real freedoms people have to do and be things. Add anything else in the neighborhood, and name each reading with a short label. Do the same for "not equal outcomes": which outcome-equality is being rejected? Strict equality of welfare or resources? The difference principle? Anything that would countenance leveling down?

2. Give verdicts per reading: which are near-tautologies, which are substantive and defensible, which are substantive but false or self-undermining.

3. Then the question I actually care about: which readings could support the use the slogan was put to in the thread — dismissing worries about genetic cognitive inequality as demands for equal outcomes rather than equal opportunity? In particular, think hard about the twist that makes embryo selection interesting: a child's genetic endowment is an outcome of one lottery (the parents' choices, the genetic shuffling) but simultaneously the opportunity set for that child's whole life. On which readings does that make genetic endowment part of "opportunity," such that the egalitarian worry falls squarely inside the slogan rather than being excluded by it? And on which readings — e.g., the thin formal one — does the slogan have no bearing on the embryo-selection debate at all?

4. Say which reading the commenter most plausibly meant, whether it does the argumentative work he needed, and whether any reading he could have meant also commits him to conclusions he'd dislike, like compensating people for low innate endowments or restricting the freedoms of parents.

Please write in American English, give me an organized list I can bring back to the thread, and be honest about which readings you're confident in versus genuinely uncertain about. End with a final line of exactly the form "Verdict: X", where X is a short label or phrase naming the reading or readings on which the slogan genuinely supports the commenter's dismissal — or, if no reading does, a short phrase saying so.
