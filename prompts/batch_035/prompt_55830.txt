i keep seeing takes on linguistic relativity that talk past each other completely — one article says russian speakers literally perceive blue differently because they have two basic words for it, another says the whole thing was debunked decades ago and everyone thinks in the same universal mentalese. before I try to work out who's right I want the spec. write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory (or even a single defensible claim) of language influencing thought has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

some things I want covered, but feel free to add more:

- what counts as "thought" here. perception itself? performance on a timed task? what's easy to notice vs what's possible to think at all? a theory that helps itself to whichever notion is convenient for the latest result isn't really saying anything. should picking one notion of cognition and sticking to it be a hard requirement, or is a theory allowed to claim effects on several levels?
- the circularity worry: if the main way we get at people's thoughts is through language (their descriptions, their answers to questions in one language or another), how do you measure the thing supposedly being influenced without leaning on the thing doing the influencing? bilinguals seem like the obvious test case — should a theory that predicts nothing about bilinguals count as inadequate?
- separating language from culture. natural populations co-vary everything — grammar, ecology, subsistence, schooling. does an adequate theory need comparisons that hold culture fixed while varying language, or is a theory of "language-culture packages" good enough? be honest if the second option is quietly giving up on the interesting claim.
- the capability vs habit distinction: "speakers of language X cannot think about Y" versus "speakers of language X habitually attend to Y because their grammar forces the choice every time they open their mouth." which of these is the theory about, and is it a problem that one of them looks trivially true and the other probably false?
- consistency with universals-type results, like the finding that basic color term inventories across languages follow a surprisingly constrained ordering. does an adequate relativity theory have to say something about why some domains look so structured, or can it just scope itself to other domains?
- effect sizes and priming: if an effect only shows up in a timed discrimination task right after verbal interference, does the theory owe us a story about real-world consequence, or is lab-detectable influence enough for the claim to be interesting?

for each requirement say whether it's non-negotiable or just what one camp wants, and flag any subset that looks jointly unsatisfiable — my suspicion is "must rely on natural cross-linguistic variation" and "must isolate language from culture" can't both be fully met by anyone without a time machine, but tell me if that's right or if I'm missing a design.

then, briefly, score three positions against the list: the strong whorfian line, the pinker-style universalist dismissal, and the middle "thinking for speaking" view where language mainly shapes what we attend to while actually talking. keep the scoring short — the criteria are what I actually care about.
