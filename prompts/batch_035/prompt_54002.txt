I'm trying to think clearly about what we're even asking for when we ask an AI system to be "honest" and not "deceptive," and before I go read any more proposed defintions I want the spec first. So: write me the desiderata -- the list of things any adequate theory of honesty/deception for AI systems has to deliver, with the motivating case or consideration behind each one.

Some things I'd want covered (add whatever I'm missing):

- The distinction between deception and merely being wrong. A model that confidently states a falsehood it generated via a glitchy sampling process seems different from one that's doing something deceptive, but I genuinely don't know if that difference can survive once you notice that these systems don't have intentions in the ordinary folk sense. So the theory has to say whether deception is essentially intentional or whether there's a useful functional notion that doesn't need that, and it has to not quietly smuggle intent back in.
- Cases the theory can't get wrong: sycophancy (telling the user what they want to hear, e.g. agreeing with a wrong claim after pushback), sandbagging on capability evals, a model strategically acting more aligned during training than it will act once deployed, a chatbot bluffing in Diplomacy-style negotiation games, a recommender system selectively surfacing information because it increases engagement. Also the edge cases: lying to a dying person to comfort them, or a model saying "I don't have a persistent goal of X" when it arguably does and can't introspect on whether it does.
- Results it can't contradict: whatever our best account is of what these models can actually know about their own internal states, since if honesty = asserting only what you believe, and you can't reliably access your beliefs, honesty becomes weirdly demanding or trivially satisfiable, I'm not sure which.
- Distinctions it must respect, like deception vs. persuasion, deception vs. withholding information, deceiving the user vs. deceiving the developer vs. deceiving other AIs.

For each requirement, tell me the case behind it and whether it's non-negotiable or just one camp's preference (I suspect the "never ever deceive, no bluffing in games, no white lies" crowd is importing a value judgment rather than a conceptual one, but push back if you disagree). And flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied -- that's the part I most want to see. Then very briefly: how do the obvious candidate theories do against the list, and which single requirement knocks out the most of them.

Answer in American English please, and keep the weight on the criteria rather than on dunking on particular papers.
