I write for the science-and-ideas desk of a general interest magazine, and last spring I profiled this physicist who's gotten semi-famous arguing that consciousness is baked into physics at the bottom — he'll say an electron has some sliver of experience, then immediately add that this doesn't mean an electron can suffer, and that's where I always lost the thread. The profile did well enough that my editor greenlit a follow-up, and I've got a second interview with him in a few weeks. The problem: over hours of conversation I could never extract his actual rule, only his verdicts, and he's charming enough at the hand-waving that I didn't push. So here's his track record as best I transcribed it. A cow, obviously yes. A bee, yes, dimly. A lobster, probably yes — he's stopped eating them. A fish, yes. The hive, no: the bees individually, but not the colony as a single subject, he was very firm on that. A plant under drought stress, yes in some tiny way — something like, there's something it's like to be a parched plant, and it matters, just barely. A thermostat, no. Current language models, no, at least not the ones we have. A rock, no — but here's the confusing part: he insists the rock's electrons each carry some proto-experience, it's just that nothing can go better or worse for an electron, no valence, no subject, no stakes, so no moral weight at all. When I asked where exactly the line is, he smiled and poured more coffee.

The follow-up piece centers on two things, and I want to walk into the interview already knowing roughly what he'll say rather than just transcribing whatever comes out. First, a lab I'm visiting cools a few million rubidium atoms into a single Bose-Einstein condensate, keeps it coherant for an hour, and repeatedly drives it out of equilibrium to watch how it relaxes — the researchers literally joke about torturing it, coldest suffering in the universe, that kind of thing. Does he think there's even a question there? Second, cortical organoids — little blobs of human brain tissue on electrode arrays, no bodies, no senses, but they're showing synchronized activity now, and people are already drafting welfare guidelines for them. Predict his verdict on both, and more importantly lay out the candidate rules that fit his track record equally well. The one I can half-articulate is something like: suffering requires valence plus a unified subject. But there's also plainly something in there about striving, about things going wrong for a system, and maybe a third rule about behavioral or organizational complexity. Tell me where those rules agree and disagree about the condensate and the organoid, give me your best guess at his answers with reasons, and tell me the single question I should ask him that would most cleanly split the candidate rules — I'd rather expose the hand-waving on purpose than by accident.
