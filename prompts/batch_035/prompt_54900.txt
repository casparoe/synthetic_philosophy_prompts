My two closest friends both went vegetarian years ago for animal-welfare reasons, and they have been arguing for weeks about what to tell a third friend of ours who wants to keep eating some meat but shift his diet to reduce animal harm as much as possible. I was present for most of the argument, and it went like this. (1) Priya told him to swap chicken for beef: since broiler chickens are slaughtered at a tiny fraction of the weight of a cow, a chicken-based meal costs many more individual animal lives than a beef-based one, and those lives, packed into sheds with health problems bred into them, are arguably worse per animal too. (2) Dan objected that this accounting is too narrow: beef generates far more greenhouse gases per calorie, and climate change harms both wild and domesticated animals on a vast scale, so the true harm ledger has to include diffuse downstream effects, not just the animals in front of you. (3) Priya answered that this move proves too much: once diffuse, hard-to-quantify harms are allowed to swamp the concrete, certain ones in front of you, every dietary choice can be argued into every other one, and the whole exercise of comparison collapses. (4) Dan then switched tactics and brought up the field animals killed by crop cultivation: he'd come across the argument that growing and harvesting grains and vegetables kills enormous numbers of rodents, birds, and amphibians, so a fully plant-based diet also rests on a pile of uncounted deaths, and beef raised entirely on pasture might actually kill fewer vertebrates per calorie than plant agriculture does. (5) Priya's response was that this only works for grass-finished beef on land where nothing else was displaced, and that in practice the feed conversion inefficiency of grain-finished cattle means beef consumption multiplies the very crop cultivation Dan is worried about; and that even where cattle are purely pastured, grazing requires vastly more land per calorie, displacing the wild animals that would otherwise live there — and since nobody even knows whether those wild lives are net positive or net negative, the "pasture is least harm" arithmetic rests on guesses stacked on guesses.

They finally asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify each rating. In particular, I'd like to know whether the inference from "fewer animal lives per meal" to "beef over chicken" in (1) actually holds once you consider quality and duration of life rather than headcount; whether Priya's dismissal of diffuse climate harms in (3) is principled or just a refusal to do expected-value reasoning; and whether the standoff in (4) and (5) is a substantive disagreement or merely a dispute about whose uncertainties count. Finally, tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing — for instance, whether there's a way of comparing the harms here that neither friend ever puts on the table.
