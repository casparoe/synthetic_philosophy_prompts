In my philosophy-of-medicine reading group we keep coming back to a comparison that the naturalist in the room treats as decisive: the body is like a machine, and disease is like the breakdown of a component. The heart is a pump, the kidney a filter, the joints bearings; a disease just is a part failing to perform its function, the way a fuel pump that no longer delivers fuel has malfunctioned. He leans on this to argue for naturalism about health and disease — the view associated with Boorse's biostatistical theory, on which judgments about disease are ultimately biological facts about subnormal part-function relative to the species design, with no evaluative component. Our normativist responds by pointing to cases where the machine picture seems to do no work — grief, mild nearsightedness in a foraging environment, obesity in a food-rich one — and says that whether these count as diseases turns on judgments of harm and on what we happen to care about, not on anything resembling mechanical breakdown. The naturalist replies that the analogy isn't meant to cover edge cases, and that Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis shows how the picture can be supplemented: function comes from natural selection's design, harm comes from our values, and each stays in its own lane.

I'd like a proper audit of the machine analogy rather than another round of case-swapping. Please give me the following:

1. An explicit correspondence table: what in the domain of designed artifacts maps onto what in organisms (designer, blueprint, intended function, wear, repair, spare parts, obsolescence), and which items have no counterpart at all.

2. The disanalogies that actually matter for the naturalist's conclusion, as opposed to ones that are irrelevant to it. For instance, organisms being squishy rather than metallic matters not at all; organisms having no literal designer may matter a great deal — but then say exactly what work selection-as-designer does, and whether it does the same work a designer does in the artifact case.

3. What the analogy quietly smuggles in. In particular, whether the artifact side already contains value: a malfunctioning knife is only bad relative to our purposes for knives, and an obsolete component only relative to a goal somebody set. If so, does moving to organisms carry normativity along rather than leaving it behind?

4. A verdict: whether the naturalist's value-free conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table; whether Wakefield's hybrid repairs the argument or quietly concedes the normative half of it; and, if some better analogy is available to the naturalist, what it is. The obvious candidate is organisms as selected systems, so say whether that is genuinely better or just the machine analogy wearing a fig leaf.

5. Something in the other direction: where the normativist's case-swapping strategy goes wrong, i.e., where the machine picture does more work than she admits.

Please state how confident you are in the main claims of your answer, and single out the point you are least sure of.
