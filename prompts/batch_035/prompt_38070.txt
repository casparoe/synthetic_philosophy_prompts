Here's a thing I keep going back and forth on. In AI safety discussions there's this contrast between goal-directed and approval-directed agents: a goal-directed one gets an objective up front and optimizes it into the far future, while an approval-directed one just looks at its options for the next action and picks whatever a human overseer would endorse right then, no long-run goal baked in. The argument for the second kind is that we're bad at specifying goals — a small misspecification gets amplified by a powerful optimizer — whereas checking each action against actual human judgment keeps a human mind in the loop, catching weird failure cases no rule ever anticipated.

And it struck me that a lot of our institutions are already built that way. IRBs don't apply some fixed formula for what counts as acceptable risk to research subjects — a committee reads each protocol and votes yes or no. Juries, parole boards, zoning boards, ethics committees at hospitals — case-by-case human sign-off rather than a fully codified rulebook. What does that design choice presuppose, exactly? Something like "the best rule we can write down is worse than an ordinary person's on-the-spot judgment, because the world keeps throwing up cases the rule didn't foresee"? And does it also presuppose that the approvers can't be gamed, or is that a separate assumption the practice never really confronts?

The wrinkle is that the same institutions flip into the opposite mode elsewhere: mandatory minimum sentences, hard quantitative thresholds in environmental regulation, standardized test-based admissions — places where we deliberatly strip out case-by-case discretion precisely because we don't trust the human judgment, or we want consistency, or we fear the approver is biased or corruptible. So is there some coherent implicit theory in this mixed practice about when approval beats rules and when rules beat approval, or is the mix just historical accident plus political distrust dressed up as design? Reconstruct what the approval-based parts strictly require, versus what they're merely compatible with. And then: if you took the approval-directed argument seriously — if you really believed goal specification is the dangerous, brittle part — what would follow for how we build institutions? Would you want judicial review of agency rules run more like an IRB, reviewing outcomes case by case instead of testing rules against a fixed standard? Or does the analogy break somewhere, since a human committee that approves each case is slow, inconsistent, and subject to capture in ways an approval-directed AI presumably wouldn't be?
