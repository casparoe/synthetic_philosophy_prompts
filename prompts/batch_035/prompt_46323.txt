A colleague in the psychology department has sent me page proofs of a trade book he has written on sleep, and I agreed to check the chapter that touches on philosophy of mind. The chapter tells the story roughly as follows: in the 1950s, the philosopher Norman Malcolm argued that dreams are not experiences at all; since nothing can verify what, if anything, passes through a sleeper's mind, statements about dreams are unverifiable and therefore meaningless. When REM sleep was discovered and experimenters began waking sleepers in the laboratory, eliciting vivid dream reports from the middle of REM episodes, this verificationism was empirically refuted. Daniel Dennett, though, is said to have revived half of Malcolm's argument: because a dream report's content cannot be fixed independently of the moment of reporting, Dennett concluded that dreams must be composed retroactively, in a fraction of a second, at the instant of waking. On this basis my colleague invites his readers to regard dreams as a kind of confabulation. I have doubts at several points: whether Malcolm's case rested on a blanket verificationism or on something specific to the concept of a dream and its temporal location; whether the laboratory findings established when dreams occur, rather than merely when they are reported; and whether Dennett advanced retroactive composition as his considered view or as one alternative that the standard picture is not entitled to exclude. Please assess the chapter claim by claim: which parts a philosopher of mind would accept as they stand, which are mere compressions, which are errors that both Malcolm and Dennett would disown, and whether the confabulation suggestion follows from whatever survives the corrections. I owe him a page of specific comments, not a general lecture on the philosophy of mind.
