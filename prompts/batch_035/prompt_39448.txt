Is there an established name for a pattern of reasoning I keep catching myself doing, and if so, a literature on whether it is any good? The pattern: when I face a one-shot decision together with people I cannot communicate with or causally influence, I treat my own choice as evidence about what they will do, because people very much like me will tend to reason their way to the same choice. And in some cases I find myself going further, choosing the act that would be best if everyone like me chose it, or even randomizing at exactly the probability that would be ideal if all of us randomized that way.

The case that made me notice it: a puzzle from a 1980s recreational mathematics column. A trillionaire summons twenty strangers and offers a billion dollars to whichever of them sends him a single telegram by noon the next day, provided he receives exactly one telegram in total; if he receives none, or more than one, nobody gets anything, and no communication between the twenty is allowed. My instinct was that if all twenty of us reason the same way, each of us should send a telegram with probability one in twenty — the shared gamble giving each an even shot at a billion dollars, rather than all of us certainly nothing. What strikes me is that this presents itself as a deduction one makes entirely alone, and it outputs not just an act but a precise probability. The same column later ran a real contest of this shape — readers submitted postcards standing for any number of entries, with a fixed prize divided by all entries and awarded by a random draw — and the same reasoning told me to stay out unless a simulated die with one side per expected entrant came up right.

The everyday version: in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with a colleague I know thinks like me, I find myself concluding that if I am the sort of person who defects here, he probably is too, and cooperating purely out of self-interest — no fairness, no affection, no repetition. I would like to answer my friend who says voting is pointless because one ballot never decides anything with this same pattern, but I want to know what I am saying first.

Two nearest concepts, neither of which seems right. One friend says this is just the categorical imperative in a business suit, but that seems off: Kant asks what would happen if everyone did the act, whereas this reasoning never universalizes — it asks what my doing it tells me about what similar people are doing, and it yields probabilities, which no universalization can. Another friend says it is the one-boxer's fallacy again, choosing as though your choice could reach back and affect an already-settled fact. That feels closer, but in the Newcomb case the correlation between my choice and the other fact was manufactured by something that literally observed me; here it is only that similar causes produce similar effects in similar people. I cannot tell whether that difference matters.

Where I get genuinely stuck is scope. The reasoning seems to require that the others be like me, but how alike? If I learn that one of the twenty is a fanatic who will certainly telegram, does my one-in-twenty deduction collapse, and into what? And I would never cooperate against a known defector, so some threshold of similarity seems to do all the work without ever being stated. So I am not looking for a bare label: I want the established concept if there is one, the nearest standard accounts and exactly how this differs from each, whether the literature handles the how-similar question, and if nothing established fits, a definition of the pattern with its boundary cases marked out — including a name you would defend.
