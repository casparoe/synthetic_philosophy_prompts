Two friends of mine have a long-running dispute. One holds that the world has become dramatically less violent over the past two centuries; the other holds that this is an artifact of what gets counted, and that under a fuller count the modern era fares no better, and possibly worse. I have concluded that we cannot argue productively until we have a specification: a list of the desiderata that a definition of violence, together with a way of measuring it, would have to meet before it could adjudicate between them — exacting enough that two careful researchers applying it to the same historical record would agree on the direction of change. Please write those desiderata as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. At least three of the five should be drawn from the following topics, in whatever order you judge best: whether the count is absolute or per capita, and what the measure should report when the two disagree; whether agentless harms — deaths from hunger, exposure, or unsafe water under policies someone could have changed — fall inside the definition or outside it; whether the measure needs a common currency across severities, such that many minor harms can outweigh a single death, and what that currency would have to be; whether the verdict must be robust to the choice of baseline year, so that it cannot come out true relative to 1800 and false relative to 1970 without the measure itself indicating which question was being asked; and whether the measure must be computable from fragmentary evidence — coroners' records, parish registers, skeletal trauma — rather than only from modern crime statistics. If you suspect that one item on your list cannot be jointly satisfied with another, say so within that item rather than adding a sixth. Please answer in American English.
