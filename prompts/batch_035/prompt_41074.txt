Next week I am to give the one lecture on time's direction in our team-taught philosophy of science course — fifty minutes, second-year philosophy majors, no physics beyond school, and no equations on the board beyond an occasional arrow. The topic is why physics appears to be silent about which way time runs: the fundamental laws, as far as we know, are time-symmetric, yet nearly every phenomenon we care about — milk dispersing in tea, eggs breaking and never unbreaking, our having records of the past and not of the future — has a direction. I understand the material well enough; what I want from you is not an explanation of the physics but a plan for the explanation. First, the order of ideas: does the time-symmetry of the laws have to be established before the catalogue of asymmetric phenomena, or the other way around, and what exactly breaks if I get it backwards? Second, the carrying example: I have used milk in tea in past years, but it keeps dragging students toward "entropy is disorder," so I need a single concrete case that makes the statistical-mechanical explanation feel inevitable rather than hand-waved — tell me what such an example must have and what it must lack. Third, the misconception map: the predictable errors ("entropy is messiness," "entropy is a substance things run out of," "the second law alone explains why the past is different, since entropy was lower yesterday too and that is all we need," and the one clever student who will ask why the entropy gradient itself doesn't distinguish past from future and whether the direction of time is just a perspective) — for each, where in the sequence it should be pre-empted rather than allowed to fester until questions. Fourth, what I should openly decline to cover at this level and say so in so many words: fluctuation theorems, Boltzmann-brain worries, gravitational entropy, the metaphysics of tensed time? And last, a single closing question I could put to the room whose answer would tell me whether the idea has landed rather than merely been admired. One constraint you should work around: a physics colleague covered entropy the previous week under the heading "things tend toward disorder," so part of my job is undoing that without undoing him — say where in the sequence that correction belongs.
