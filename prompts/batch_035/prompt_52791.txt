I teach political philosophy, and every autumn my grad seminar on democratic erosion goes the same way: week two, someone declares Hungary the paradigm case of backsliding, someone else says that's just a partisan label for "elections the wrong side won", and then we spend the whole term relitigating individual countries instead of working out what the concept is even for. So this year I want to do it in reverse. Before we touch a single country, I want the spec.

Write me the list of desiderata that any adequate account of democratic backsliding has to meet. For each one, give the case or consideration that motivates it, so it's not just a bullet floating free. Off the top of my head the list has to handle at least these things, but add or argue with them as you see fit:

- It has to seperate backsliding from ordinary policy alternation — a new government slashing welfare or appointing sympathetic regulators isn't automatically eroding democracy, or the concept just collapses into "governance I dislike".
- It has to capture change through ostensibly legal means — court packing, capture of media regulators, prosecutorial pressure on owners — without the net being so wide that every government's ordinary conduct counts. This is where most definitions either miss Orbán-style gradualism or criminalise politics as such.
- It has to be gradualist-friendly: with a slow trajectory there may be no single day you can point to, so "when did it happen" can't be part of the criterion. Call this the boiling frog requirement if you like.
- Here's the one I actually care about, and it's the reason I'm asking for a spec rather than a definition: the criteria have to pass what I'll call an adversarial test — they have to be such that, in principle, they could classify a case you or I are sympathetic to as backsliding, and classify a case we dislike as NOT backsliding. If the criteria only ever fire in one political direction, they're not an analysis of a concept, they're a cudgel with footnotes. I don't know how to make that requirement precise, which is partly why I'm farming this out.

Then, briefly, I want two more things. First: flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — I have a feeling decidability and the legal-means requirement are in tension, since the more you lean on "trajectory" and "pattern of behaviour" to catch gradual capture, the more the judgement looks like an inference about intent, which observers who disagree about the government will disagree about too. If some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly; that would be the most useful result of the whole exercise. Second, a very short scorecard — two or three sentences each, no more — of how the standard framings do against your list: Nancy Bermeo's old typology of coups and executive aggrandisement, the informal-norms approach people associate with Levitsky and Ziblatt, and a bald "index of institutional change" approach that just counts rule changes. The point of the scorecard is to test your criteria, not to adjudicate those authors, so keep it light.

One more thing: don't structure this around current US politics. It can use the US as one case among several, but if the whole list turns out to be reverse-engineered from one country, I'll notice, and so will my students.

Please write the answer in American English. Length doesn't matter to me; getting the requirements right, with their motivating cases, does. Its the criteria that do the work — the scorecard is just a sanity check at the end.
