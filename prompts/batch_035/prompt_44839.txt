I'm on the grantmaking committee of a mid-sized health research foundation (endowment around $500M, we disburse maybe $25M a year), and we have a proposal in front of us that's splitting the committee down the middle. I need a decision memo I can circulate before thursday's meeting. Please act as a decision theorist who's spent years advising philanthropic boards, and think it through carefully before drafting — maybe do a quick brainstorm first about where the committee members actually disagree, because the reasoning will matter more to them than the verdict.

The proposal: a PI wants money for a staged research program. Stage 1 costs $200k and succeeds with probability roughly 1/2. If it succeeds she moves to stage 2, which costs about double and again succeeds with ~1/2 chance, and so on — each stage costs roughly twice the last. But the payoff if the program is ever completed grows even faster than the costs do, so on her own numbers the expected value of funding stage 1 is enormous (on one reading it's not even bounded — the structure is basically the St. Petersburg setup wearing a grant application as a costume).

The committee has roughly three factions. One wants stage 1 funded immediately: the entry cost is pocket change for us, so how can we turn down positive expected value? Our finance member says this "proves too much" — if expected value is the whole story, we're committed to funding every later stage no matter how big the checks get, and to any longshot with a sufficiently huge promised payoff; he wants us to reject and adopt a standing rule that no single program ever gets more than ~2% of the endowment. A third member wants to fund stage 1 but with a pre-defined stopping rule (cap total spend at $10M for this program, or pull the plug after two failed stages), though she can't quite say why that cap rather than another.

Draft the memo. It needs: a clear recommendation (fund, reject, or fund with explicit conditions), a concrete stopping rule if you recommend funding, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side — in particular whether the finance member's 2% cap is principled or just arbitrary, and whether saying "the expected value is infinite so the arithmetic alone can't settle it" is a cop-out or the beginning of wisdom. Keep it under 700ish words and write it so the committee members could actually respond to the reasons, not just the bottom line.
