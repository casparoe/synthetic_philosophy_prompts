The midterm question:

"Consider the fitting-attitudes analysis of value, according to which something is valuable just in case it is a fitting object of some pro-attitude, such as desire, admiration, or favour. (a) Present the wrong-kind-of-reasons objection to this analysis, illustrating it with a case in which an agent is bribed or threatened into holding a pro-attitude toward something worthless or bad. (b) Explain the distinction between object-given and state-given reasons for attitudes, and evaluate the proposal that the analysis can be rescued by restricting it to object-given reasons. (c) Briefly address whether an analogous problem arises for belief, given the distinction between evidential and pragmatic reasons for believing."

The student's answer:

"1. The fitting-attitudes analysis is a version of the buck-passing view: it says that for x to be good is for x to have properties that provide reasons for desiring or favouring x. The wrong-kind-of-reasons problem is brought out by cases of coercion. Suppose a demon tells you that unless you desire to drink a vile, painful potion, you will be tortured for a year. Clearly you ought to desire to drink it, since the alternative is far worse, so there is a decisive reason for that desire. But drinking the potion is not in the least good or valuable. By the analysis, the potion would have to be good, which is absurd. The fitting-attitudes analysis is therefore refuted.

2. Philosophers have distinguished object-given reasons, which obtain in virtue of the properties of the attitude's object, from state-given reasons, which obtain in virtue of facts about the attitude itself, such as its consequences. The demon's threat is state-given: it concerns what happens to you if you are in the state of desiring. A reason is of the wrong kind if and only if it is state-given. This gives us the solution to the problem. We simply build the restriction into the analysis, so that x is good if and only if x has properties that provide object-given reasons for desiring x. The demon's reason is state-given and therefore excluded, the counterexample is blocked, and the analysis is saved.

3. The same issue might seem to arise for belief. Evidential reasons concern the truth of what is believed; pragmatic reasons, such as a promised reward for believing that a shaky business venture will succeed, concern the benefits of believing. But the parallel is only apparent. As Bernard Williams argued, we cannot believe at will, so pragmatic considerations cannot be reasons for belief at all; at best they are reasons to bring it about that we believe — say, by swallowing a belief-inducing pill — and those are reasons for actions like pill-taking, not for the belief itself. Since only evidence is a reason for belief, there are no wrong-kind reasons for belief, and the problem does not arise in epistemology."
