I'm a science and ideas correspondent preparing a long-form feature on how we ought to think about the arrival of transformative AI, and I keep colliding with the same three historical analogies in the source material. The nuclear analogy: AI development is a Manhattan-style race, first deployment confers decisive advantage, and the only realistic outcomes are treaties, inspection regimes and mutual deterrence, or catastrophe. The printing-press analogy: AI is a general-purpose information technology whose consequences — the Reformation, mass literacy, pamphlet wars — were diffuse, slow-moving and impossible to steer from any single vantage point, so the right response is social adaptation rather than control of the technology itself. And the Industrial-Revolution analogy: AI is the next general-purpose technology like steam and electricity, whose disruptions were absorbed over generations through labour-market adjustment and institutional reform, with the harms falling on identifiable groups rather than on everyone at once.

My editor wants the piece to pick one analogy and structure the argument around it, and my instinct is that this is exactly the wrong way to use them — that each analogy imports a whole apparatus of assumptions about speed, controllability, concentration of power and who bears the risks, and that the choice of analogy is doing the argumentative work while appearing merely illustrative. But I may be wrong; it may be that one of the three is genuinely better and I should say so plainly.

So I'd like a careful audit. For each analogy, set out what maps onto what — what plays the role of fissile material, of the state secrecy regime, of the arsenal; of the printing press, the scribes, the censors, the reading public; of the factory, the Luddites, the decades-long lag between invention and measured productivity gains. Then tell me where each mapping breaks, and crucially which of the disanalogies matter for the policy conclusions people draw from the analogy and which are merely decorative. Nuclear weapons, for instance, were developed by states, are inherently verifiable from their signatures, and had an obvious detonation demonstration; it's not obvious any of that carries over to model weights trained by firms and diffusing through ordinary commercial channels — does the treaty-and-deterrence machinery then still follow, or was it the whole point of the analogy? Similarly, the printing press and steam were physically self-limiting in ways that software isn't, and their disruptions played out at the pace of human institutions. Which of these differences are load-bearing?

Finally, answer the editorial question directly: should the piece be structured around one analogy, around all three as rival frames whose disagreement is itself the story, or around some fourth comparison — and if you think a fourth is better, say what it is and why. Please be candid about where the honest answer is that the analogies cannot carry the weight placed on them, and if you think my framing — that the analogy is covertly doing the argumentative work — is confused or overstated, I would rather hear that plainly than have the audit shaped to fit my thesis. Please write in British English.
