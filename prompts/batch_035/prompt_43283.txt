I'm a graduate student writing a term paper for a philosophy of mind seminar on personal identity, and I've hit a wall trying to construct a case for a specific argumentative purpose. In nearly everything I've read, Dennett's picture of the self as a center of narrative gravity — the self as a posited abstractum, introduced the way we introduce a center of gravity, in order to organize the interpretation of an organism's stream of behavior and speech — ends up on the same side as the Lockean memory-continuity view of personal identity. Both oppose the Cartesian ego theorist, and in the stock thought experiments (teletransportation, gradual replacement) they issue the same verdicts, so the interesting boundary between them never gets mapped. What I want is a case that splits them, in what seems to me the hardest direction to construct: a scenario in which the memory criterion is fully satisfied — every later phase remembers the experiences of every relevant earlier phase in the direct, first-personal way, with the ordinary causal pedigree, so that a straightforward memory theorist must say there has been exactly one continuously existing person throughout — while the narrative view, applied on its own terms, says that the number of selves is two, or indeterminate between one and two. No branching and no fission: the case must involve a single, spatially continuous organism from start to finish. Constraints: no teletransporters, no uploading, no fantastic technology; build it out of an imagined neurological or psychiatric condition, described in a single paragraph, and make it ordinary enough that you can say what a clinician observing from the outside would report.

Two further constraints to keep the case honest. First, the two-selves verdict must be generated by Dennett's theory from features of the case, not stipulated into it: if the only reason to say there are two selves is that you've labeled the phases "two alters," the case cheats, since the memory theorist will simply deny that the labels carry any identity-theoretic weight. Second, the case must not lean on the mere absence of a unified autobiography — a Dennettian will rightly say a life story can be told about almost anything, so the two-centers reading has to be the most natural interpretation of the evidence, not just an available one. One way to aim, though don't treat it as required: have the phases differ in something interpreters track independently of memory, such that the most charitable parsing of the person's history treats the two phases as answering for their lives separately even though each remembers the other's doings as its own.

After the scenario, do the walkthrough. State what the memory view says and why, making explicit which clause of the criterion is doing the work; then state what the narrative view says and why, making explicit which feature of the case makes the interpretively simplest story posit two centers of narrative gravity rather than one. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work overall, and what happens if you remove it — in particular, whether the case collapses back into something like ordinary mood swings or a rich inner life, where both views comfortably agree there is one self. Then give the strongest counterattack available to whichever theory comes out least comfortable: for the memory theorist this will presumably be some further appropriateness or ownership condition on which memories can ground identity; for the Dennettian it will presumably be some appeal to interpretive charity favoring a single self whenever the evidence underdetermines the count — but note that the analogy with centers of gravity may cut against him here, and say whether it does. Finally, since I need to know how much weight my paper can put on this: tell me how confident you are that the case genuinely meets each specification rather than merely appearing to, and single out the one specification you are least sure it satisfies, together with what makes you unsure about it.
