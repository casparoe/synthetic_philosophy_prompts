I am a novelist preparing a trilogy that treats Arthur as a historical figure — a real late-fifth- or early-sixth-century Romano-British war-leader around whom the later legend accreted — and in the author's note I intend to defend that choice as historically warranted rather than merely convenient. Before I commit anything to print, I would like an audit of the commitments behind it, because I have come to suspect they do not entirely cohere. I hold the following. First, that a historical Arthur existed: some commander of that era and region around whom the stories later formed, even though nothing recoverable survives about him as an individual. Second, a general principle: legends of this kind rarely coalesce around nothing — where a comparatively early and earnestly believed tradition attaches itself to a particular era and region, there is usually some historical nucleus at the bottom of it. Third, that the Exodus as narrated in the Pentateuch is essentially a founding myth: the archaeology of the Sinai and the central highlands of Canaan, and the silence of the Egyptian record, rule out anything of the biblical scale, and I take the Israelites to have emerged from indigenous Canaanite stock rather than from a departure from Egypt. Fourth, about Troy: I think it plausible that some real conflict, or something like one, occurred at Hisarlik in the Late Bronze Age — the site is real, its destruction layers fall in roughly the right period, and the Hittite texts' references to Wilusa, and to the Ahhiyawa across the Aegean, keep open the possibility of genuine friction — yet I also hold that the Iliad, put together centuries later, preserves essentially nothing recoverable about any such war, and that its world is largely a creation of the Iron Age. Fifth, on method: I accept the historian's rule that the burden of proof falls on whoever asserts a historical nucleus, and that belief should be proportioned to what the sources can actually support.

Here is what troubles me. My principle about legends rarely coalescing around nothing is, I realize, at least as true of the Exodus tradition — early, detailed, earnestly believed, central to a people's identity — as it is of the Arthurian one, whose first datable mention of Arthur arrives roughly three centuries after the supposed events, in a ninth-century compilation whose reliability is widely doubted, while the nearest thing we have to a contemporary account, Gildas, describes the wars of that age and even names Ambrosius Aurelianus, yet never once mentions Arthur. Yet I believe in the Arthur nucleus and not in the Exodus nucleus, and I cannot currently state a rule that yields that result rather than the reverse. It looks to me as though I am carving my positions along the grain of my projects rather than along any principle: defending the Arthur I need for my novel, while treating the Exodus — which I would render as pure mythopoesis — as the counterexample that proves how easily founding legends fabricate themselves. I am also unsure whether my Troy commitments are internally at odds: if the poem preserves nothing, in what sense is the legend evidence of anything? And I do not know whether my fifth commitment, on burdens of proof, leaves my first any standing at all.

So: go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. For each genuine tension, say how severe it is, what exactly would have to give, and what the revision would cost me — including for the author's note I plan to write, since I would rather say something defensible there than something a reviewer will dismantle. Then tell me which single change would bring the largest share of the set into line. I would rather learn this from you now than from the reviews later.
