I'm a second-year student taking a course on Kant's moral philosophy, and I've become fixated on a problem our lecturer raised only in passing: the universalizability test is supposed to be applied to the maxim of an action, but nothing in the theory seems to settle how finely or coarsely that maxim is to be described — and the verdict can apparently flip depending on the description chosen. I'd like to see this made concrete rather than left as a hand-waving sceptical gesture.

Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. There must be a single action, performed by one ordinary agent in an everyday setting — a shared flat, a workplace, a favour between neighbours; nothing exotic, and nothing lifted from Kant's own examples (so no lying promise to obtain a loan, no false deposit with a banker, no refusal of aid on Tuesdays, no murderer at the door). For this one action there must be two maxim descriptions, each of which the agent could sincerely offer — not a contrived disjunction, and not a description smuggled in to rig the outcome, but two honest ways of saying what one is about. Under the first description the maxim must fail the universalizability test, and the failure must be of the contradiction-in-conception kind: once universalized, the maxim becomes self-undermining because the practice or institution it presupposes collapses. Under the second description the maxim must pass not only that test but the stricter contradiction-in-will test, the one Kant uses to ground imperfect duties of beneficence: a rational agent could will its universalization without thereby willing the frustration of any end she necessarily has.

After presenting the scenario, please do three things. First, walk through both tests for both maxims, spelling out what the universalized world looks like in each case and why one collapses while the other survives. Second, tell me honestly which feature of the case is doing the work — my suspicion is that it is the freedom to redescribe, and I'd like to know whether your example genuinely depends on that or whether one of the two descriptions is in fact illegitimate, say because it omits something the agent is actually relying on. Third, set out how a defender of Kant would block the redescription — for instance, by insisting that a maxim must include whatever purpose or expected means actually motivates the act, or by demanding a certain level of generality in maxim descriptions — and then tell me whether that blocking move can be made principled rather than ad hoc: how would we know it is not simply a rule to pick whichever description makes the test come out as the Kantian already wanted? If you think my specifications are jointly unsatisfiable without cheating, say so plainly and explain exactly where the tension lies.
