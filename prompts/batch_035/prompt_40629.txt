For a while now I've been thinking on my own about a strange asymmetry in how I trust myself over time, and I'd like to pressure-test it against someone who defends the other side.

Here is the background. Ten years ago I was completely sure about things — my religion, my politics — that I now think I was badly wrong about. If my younger self had deferred to today's me, that would have been excellent advice. But the arrow seems to point the other way too: I have every reason to expect that in ten more years I'll disagree with some of what I'm sure of today, possibly with the same confidence I now feel. A while back I came across an idea, attributed if I remember right to a philosopher named Bas van Fraassen, that I gather is called the Reflection principle: roughly, that if you know what opinion your future self will hold, your current opinion should already line up with it — you should believe now, in effect, what you know you'll believe later. I may be mangling it, but that's the version I want to argue about.

Please play a committed, well-prepared defender of this principle. Take it seriously as your own view: make the strongest honest case for it, concede only what you genuinely must, never fold just because I push, and stay in character until I write "stop." Keep each reply to a paragraph or so, so this reads like a real exchange rather than an essay.

My opening move has two parts. First: tomorrow night is my cousin's wedding, and I know from many such nights that by 1 a.m., after enough drinks, I will be utterly certain I can drive home perfectly well. I know this now, tonight, calmly and soberly. If your principle says to align my current beliefs with the ones I know I'll hold later, it seems to tell me to believe tonight that I can drive — which is absurd, and I'd like to hear exactly where you draw the line, since a confidence produced by alcohol is still a confidence I can accurately predict in advance. Second, and this one troubles me more: when I look back at everything my past selves were certain of and I now find ridiculous, I notice I've begun quietly discounting my present certainties — trusting them less purely because future-me will probably overturn them. Is that habit wisdom, or does it commit the same error in reverse? And if your principle can neither keep the drunk driver out of my beliefs nor tell me whether my discounting is rational, I want to hear why it deserves to be called a principle of rationality at all.

One last request, and I mean it: if you think either of my arguments attacks a version of the principle you don't actually hold — if I've built a strawman through half-remembered reading — say so plainly and correct me before you start defending. I would rather lose the argument to the real thing than win it against a caricature.
