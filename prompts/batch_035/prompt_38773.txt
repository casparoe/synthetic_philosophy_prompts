I sit on the grantmaking committee of a small family foundation, and we must decide how to divide next year's budget between two programs: subsidizing fertility treatment in low-income countries (which mainly creates people who would not otherwise exist) and treating chronic pain among people who will exist either way. Drafting our rationale has forced me to realize I don't know what I actually believe about the ethics of creating lives, and the labels in the literature won't settle that for me. Please write a decision tree that takes someone from shared ground — say, the claim that adding a life of negative welfare does not make a population better — down to a position in population axiology. The branch points should be concrete cases a reasonably reflective person can answer without knowing which label each answer leads to: mere addition of a life barely worth living to an otherwise fine population; a vast population of mildly content people versus a small one of flourishing people; whether the fact that a policy changes which future people come to exist affects what we owe them; whether a non-existent person can be "better off" being created. The leaves should include total utilitarianism, average utilitarianism, critical-level views, person-affecting restrictions, and any combinations of branch answers that no one defends — say briefly for each unoccupied leaf whether that's because the combination is incoherent or merely unattractive. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, rather than pretending a question splits them when it doesn't. Assume fluency in expected-utility reasoning but no prior acquaintance with population axiology, and write the answer in American English.
