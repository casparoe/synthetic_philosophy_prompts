I'm a teaching assistant for a large intro political-philosophy course, and after our unit on democratic theory versus its epistocratic critics I've noticed my students can recite the labels ("epistocracy," "proceduralism," "sortition") but have no idea which position they actually hold when the labels are stripped away. For the review session, I want a sorting quiz in the style of the personality quizzes my students already love: eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete case with two or three answer options, and a scoring key at the end that maps answer patterns onto positions in the democracy/epistocracy debate. The whole point is that students must answer from their case intuitions, so no question may use any of the standard terms, ask them to self-identify, or reveal where an answer leads.

The cases should do real work. Please include at least: a voter-comprehension test for a technically complex bond referendum that turns out to pass 70% of one income bracket and 40% of another, and the question of whether certification should go forward anyway; a stratified citizens' assembly that outperforms the legislature on a policy problem while being demographically unlike it; a hypothetical panel of the ten best-informed citizens whose recommendations you know in advance will be systematically skewed against a minority; an electorate that will simply vote the wrong way on a matter of fact; and a trolley-ish case about an emergency decision (a pandemic lockdown, say) where speed and accuracy seem to pull against inclusive process. The questions must separate at least these leaves: the democratic egalitarian who values equal political power intrinsically, the epistemic democrat who thinks broad and diverse participation is itself the best truth-tracking procedure, the instrumental democrat who supports universal suffrage via something like a jury-theorem argument, the competence-weighting epistocrat (plural voting, enfranchisement cutoffs), the defender of a limited epistocratic veto that can block but not originate legislation, and the lottocrat who thinks elections rather than voters are the failure. The tree must be built from the logical space of answers, not from a list of famous names: work out which combinations of case-intuitions the questions allow, and then locate the positions in that space. Where a combination is unoccupied, say so in the key and diagnose why -- whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. And honestly flag any pair of leaves the quiz cannot cleanly separate (I suspect the egalitarian proceduralist and the jury-theorem optimist will blur together on several cases, but I'd like your assessment of whether that's fixable).

One more practical constraint: I'll be projecting this in a lecture hall, so the scoring key should open with a single sentence stating what dimension the quiz actually measures, and each leaf should carry one sentence about its most awkward commitment, so students can see the cost of the position they've been sorted into. Please begin your response with the quiz's first question directly, with no preface, no commentary on the assignment, and no restatement of these instructions.
