For several years I have kept notes on cases, most of them from the news and a few from my own workplace, in which a semi-autonomous system caused a harm and some human or other was blamed or excused. My notes record my own gut verdicts, and I would like to know whether a single principle lies behind them. Here are the cases, stripped down to what I think matters.

1. A vendor ships a vehicle control system with a documented defect: the release notes concede that under a rare combination of lighting and occlusion the classifier will miss a pedestrian, and management ships anyway. A pedestrian dies in exactly that way. My verdict: the company is fully culpable, but no individual engineer is; I feel no impulse to name a person inside the firm.

2. The same system, but the defect is now genuinely undiscoverable: it met a reasonable specification, and the situation was one nobody had imagined. A pedestrian dies. My verdict: no one is to blame, and this does not disturb me — it seems like a lightning strike — until I imagine the victim's family asking whom to hold accountable, at which point it disturbs me a great deal.

3. A highly automated ship: one officer on watch, required by procedure to monitor a display that has shown nothing anomalous for six hours. When the system finally fails, a correct response is available to the officer but must be made within about forty seconds. The officer is slow. My verdict: the officer bears some blame, but less than a helmsman steering by hand would have borne; the vendor bears some too.

4. A lawyer uses an automated research tool that produces plausible but fabricated citations, and files the brief without checking. My verdict: the entire blame falls on the lawyer, none on the tool's vendor.

5. A hospital runs an algorithmic triage tool that, on a badly miscalibrated day, down-flags a patient who later dies; the attending physician relied on the flag, though nothing prevented a manual examination. My verdict: blame is split between the physician and the vendor, and I cannot make the split feel principled.

6. An operator supervises a system with an override control whose every use is logged and counted against the operator's performance rating. The system begins doing something questionable; the operator, rationally protecting the rating, sits on their hands, and a harm follows. My verdict: I blame the designer of the incentive and not the operator — the reverse of case 4, where I blamed the user rather than the designer.

Cases 4 and 5 look symmetrical — in each, a professional trusted an automated output that a modest check would have caught — yet my verdicts differ, and I cannot say why. The same goes for the company-guilty-but-every-individual-innocent pattern in case 1, and for the reversal between 4 and 6.

My tentative principle is this: a person is responsible for a harm to the degree that, at the relevant time, they had an available action that would have prevented it, weighted by the personal cost of that action. Please test this principle against the six cases, repair or replace it, and tell me what it says about the case I keep going back and forth on: a system with a learning component, certified safe at deployment, that drifts over months of operation into behavior no one at the vendor could have anticipated, and kills someone. At no moment in that history did any human have an available preventive action, so my tentative principle says no one is responsible; and yet I cannot accept that a death of that kind should belong to no one, the way a death by lightning does. If your principle saves that verdict, show me what else it costs me; if it cannot, tell me which of my other verdicts to surrender. Please state the principle in its precise form, give its verdicts on a few cases I have not listed, and include an honest accounting of which of my reactions it cannot accommodate. Please answer in American English.
