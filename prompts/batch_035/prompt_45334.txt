I teach logic and philosophy of language, and I'm writing a paper section on the problem of logical omniscience. The standard fixes — awareness-style models, impossible worlds — have always struck me as handling only the boring failures: cases where the agent just didn't chew through enough steps. I want a case that pressures them from a different direction. Build it to these specs:

- One agent, everyday setting (a department office works fine; no savants, no software, no sci-fi).
- She sincerely asserts P, sincerely asserts if-P-then-Q (or asserts an identity she could use to get Q from P), and sincerely denies Q. The suppressed inference must be exactly one step — one modus ponens, or one substitution of an identity she herself has asserted, in her own words, that same week.
- Every relevant term has to be in her own vocabulary and her own mouth. No missing concepts, so it can't be an awareness-of-vocabulary story. And it can't be a processing-cost story either — she'd do the same inference in ten seconds on a logic work sheet.
- Each of the three attitudes must be backed by ordinary evidence (a printed calendar, a confirmation email), and nothing about Q can be unwelcome to her. No motivated reasoning, no akrasia, no lying.
- When you confront her with all three of her own sentences side by side, she shouldn't fold or get embarrassed. She should be genuinely puzzled, and should insist each attitude is better supported than the other two.

Then walk me through it. What is an awareness-style solution commited to saying she believes? What can an impossible-worlds solution represent? Can either get her right without a patch that smells ad hoc? Tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, and what kind of case you're left with if you delete that feature. And no cheating: the case has to make the frameworks deliver the wrong verdict on their own — you can't just stipulate that she's rational. Last thing: I have a suspicion that the interesting failures of logical omniscience aren't about deduction limits at all, and I want to know whether your case bears that out — and if so, what they're actually about.
