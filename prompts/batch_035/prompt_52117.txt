I spend a lot of time in animal-welfare and AI-welfare circles, and one comparison keeps doing heavy lifting: the claim that consciousness research today stands to the debate about moral status roughly as physiology stood to vitalism. The story goes like this: people once thought that being alive required an irreducible vital force, and that anything alive was somehow set apart from the merely mechanical; then biology dissolved the mystery by showing that life is nothing over and above certain complex, fully mechanistic processes — metabolism, reproduction, self-maintenance — which come in degrees, admit of marginal cases (viruses, self-replicating molecules), and nowhere mark a metaphysical frontier. Consciousness, the argument continues, will go the same way: what now looks like a hard line between the sentient and the insentient will fragment into a cluster of capacities that slide gradually from insects through fish to large models, and the question of where moral status begins will look as confused as asking how many molecules make something alive rather than a heap of chemistry. The conclusion being drawn is not just scientific but ethical: since sentience admits of degrees, moral status must too, and campaigns to draw a bright protective line — above insects, below AI — are vitalism all over again.

I'd like an audit of this analogy rather than a verdict on its conclusion. Specifically: (1) a correspondence table — what plays the role of the vital force, of the mechanistic processes that replaced it, of the marginal cases like viruses, and of the explanatory successes of biochemistry; (2) the disanalogies, with attention to which ones actually matter for the ethical use the analogy is being put to — for instance, that life was always characterized functionally and publicly, by what living things do, while sentience is at least arguably given to each of us in a way that resists third-person criteria; that the vitalists were positing an extra ingredient over and above the phenomena, whereas critics of the analogy will say consciousness is the phenomenon to be explained and cannot play the role of the discarded ingredient; and that an a posteriori success of one reduction is weak evidence that another will succeed; (3) whether the ethical conclusion — that moral status comes in degrees and any line-drawing is confused — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the degree-talk of the biological case is doing the work; note that life's admission of marginal cases never made anyone conclude that viruses have partial moral status. Finally, if the analogy is not salvageable, say what a better comparison for the ethical question would be — an analogy that connects gradations in a natural property to gradations in moral status without importing either the tidy dissolution or the vitalist foil.
