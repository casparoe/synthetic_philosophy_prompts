I'm a law student doing an internship with the legal office of the truth commission here in Marovia (fictional country for you, very real civil war for us, ended '97, commission set up two years later). Part of my job is cataloguing the amnesty panel's decisions, and my supervisor wants a memo predicting how the panel will rule on a file coming up next month. The problem is I can see at least two ways to read their track record, and I genuinely cannot tell which rule they're actually running, so I'd love a second brain on this.

Here's the record so far, all from this year's session. A colonel in the old regime's security service, who directed house raids and was present while detainees were beaten under standing orders, confessed in full detail at a public hearing, described the whole command structure, and wept through most of it: amnesty granted. A defector who blew up a regime ammunition depot at night, no casualties, full confession, visibly remorseful: granted. A guerrilla commander whose bomb hit a military convoy but also killed a street vendor nearby; authorized by his movement, fully disclosed, deeply remorseful: denied, the written decision calling the method indiscriminate and disproportionate to the political objective. A sergeant who shot a detainee during an authorized sweep, but out of rage after his brother was killed — full confession, remorseful: denied, motive held to be private revenge, not political. An applicant who confessed to everything about his own conduct but refused to name the officers who were still at large: denied for incomplete disclosure. And the one I keep coming back to: an interrogator whose confession was factually complete — every date, name and method checked out — but who joked about it at the hearing and called his victims complainers. Denied, and the written reason is a single sentence about his account lacking candor, which the lawyers in the office read two different ways.

The new file is a lieutenant who led night arrests of opposition figures under written standing orders. His conduct on the raids was within orders, no excess violence, nobody hurt beyond the arrests themselves; his motive, by his own account, was the belief that the movement threatened the state; and his disclosure is genuinely complete — names of superiors, locations of the record caches, all of it, and the investigators confirm everything checked out. But at the hearing he was perfectly polite and perfectly unrepentant. He said he did his duty correctly, that he would do the same again in the same circumstances, and that suppressing the movement had been justified. So: what rules fit the track record? I can see a four-factor test — political motive, proportionality, within authorization, factually complete disclosure — under which he gets amnesty, and I can see a reading where the candor denial means the panel treats amnesty as requiring some kind of acknowledgment of wrongdoing, under which he doesn't. Are there other rules that fit the precedents just as well? Which way should I put my money in the memo, and why? And finally, what later case this year would best tell the competing readings apart — I'm already wondering about an applicant who is unrepentant but quiet and non-inflammatory at the hearing, or one who is remorseful in private but whose public testimony reopens wounds. Its for a prediction memo, not a recommendation, so if you think the precedents genuinely underdetermine it I want that stated honestly rather than papered over.
