My partner and I have been in the effective altruist-adjacent world since college — we took the giving pledge, read the cost-effectiveness reports every year, the whole thing. Now the topic has suddenly stopped being abstract for me, because I'm sitting on two job offers and they pull in opposite directions. Offer one: a quant firm. Pays absurdly well, and if I took it and donated half my salary to the top-rated global health charities, the "lives saved per year" math is genuinely hard to argue with — the donations would plausibly fund on the order of a hundred additional malaria nets or treatments a year, possibly more. Offer two: a policy job at the state housing agency where I'd be working on tenant protections, rent stabilization rules, stuff like that. It pays maybe a third as much, and here's the thing — I can't tell you how many people it helps. Nobody can. There's no spreadsheet. My EA friends are pretty unified: take the quant job, the housing work is the kind of thing where you tell yourself a story about "systemic change" and ten years later nothing measurable happened. My friends from outside the EA bubble think that whole attitude is a bit deranged — they say the reason there's a housing crisis at all is political, that the measurable stuff is just what's easy to count, and that "I'll fix the symptoms on my own dime while the machine keeps producing them" is a weird place to land morally.

I keep going back and forth and I've noticed my disagreement with my EA friends isn't about the arithmetic — on the arithmetic they win hands down, and I accept that. My unease is about something like: why does the housing job feel like it could be the more important one even though no metric would ever say so? Is there actually a principled version of that feeling, or is it just romanticism — me wanting to feel like a person who does direct work rather than a checkbook? One of my friends put it in a way that stuck with me: she said the question isn't "which does more good" but "which good are we even counting," and that we systematically count the kinds of good that look like charity and not the kinds that look like the absence of a crisis. I don't fully know what to do with that.

So I guess what I'm asking is: help me think this through. Is there a real distinction here between doing measurable good and contributing to something structural, or is that distinction just a rationalization people use to avoid the demanding math? And if the distinction is real, how should someone in my position actually weigh it — is it enough to tip the scales away from the quant job, or does the sheer size of the donation gap swamp everything else? I'd appriciate if you didn't just tell me to follow my heart, and also didn't just re-run the numbers at me — I've done both with friends already and it went nowhere.
