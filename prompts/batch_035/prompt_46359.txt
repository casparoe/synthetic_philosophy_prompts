So here's my situation: I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a mid-sized paper, and every December I end up half-watching It's a Wonderful Life at my in-laws' place, usually with a drink in hand and zero critical distance. This year I want to actually write a piece about it, and I've got a thesis I can't quite get to sit still, so I want you to pressure-test it.

The pitch: everyone files Capra's film under sentiment, but it might be the most famous thought experiment in counterfactual history ever committed to screen. Clarence the angel literally runs the simulation — world minus one George Bailey — and out comes Pottersville. And the mechanism is all small-cause-big-effect stuff: a boy doesn't break through the ice, so years later a transport full of soldiers isn't saved (because Harry, who'd have become a navy pilot, drowned at nine); a kid doesn't delay a grieving druggist, so Mr. Gower goes to prison as a killer; the run on the Building and Loan goes unanswered, so a whole town slides into dives and cemeteries where the housing development should be. Each one is a 'for want of a nail' moment, and the film presents the cascade as if the world is wildly sensitive to initial conditions — basically a butterfly-effect view of a single life, decades before anyone was putting chaos theory on magazine covers.

But here's what bugs me, and I'd like the reading to grapple with it honestly. Historians who think hard about contingency mostly say big outcomes are overdetermined — you remove one assassin and another one turns up, the war happens anyway. And when I look at the film through that lens, it starts to look less like chaos and more like a Great Man theory in disguise: the claim isn't that small causes have big effects, it's that one specific virtuous man is load-bearing for an entire town, which is almost the opposite claim. If George is the one nail holding up the whole county, that's not sensitivity to initial conditions, that's a design with a single point of failure. So which is the film actually committed to? Is there a reading on which both things are true at once?

A few wrinkles I want addressed rather than smoothed over. One: Clarence shows George exactly one branch of the counterfactual, and never explains how it was selected — the film treats the counterfactual as if it has a determinate answer, when the whole fight about contingency in history is arguably a fight about whether those questions have determinate answers at all. Does the film need its determinism, and does the reading collapse without it? Two: the film's cascades seem weirdly moralised — the alternate world tracks George's kindnesses specifically, not his mere causal footprint. A real counterfactual about removing an adult from a town should also subtract his consumption, his arguments, his bad days; the film gives us no Pottersville that's Pottersville because of some neutral causal hole. Is that a cheat, or does it point at something the contingency literature misses? Three: where does the film resist the reading entirely — are there George-shaped effects that conspicuously DON'T cascade, or moments where the film seems to know its own counterfactual is rigged?

Please take both the film and the actual history-and-contingency debate seriously (for want of a nail, overdetermination, path dependence, Tolstoy's alternative to great-man history if you find it useful) — don't reduce the film to an illustration, and don't bend the ideas to fit it. I want to know where the reading works, where it breaks, and whether the film dramatises something about contingency that the arguments by themselves never quite deliver. And please write in British English — I know the film's about as American as it gets, but my editor will come for me if I file 'neighborhood' without the u. Casual is fine, I'm not looking for something I'd have to translate out of jargon before it goes in the paper. One small favour: if you spot any typos in this, pretend you didn't.
