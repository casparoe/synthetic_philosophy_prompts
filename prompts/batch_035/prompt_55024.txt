I've been hanging around rationalist spaces for about a decade now (LessWrong, the old SSC comment sections, that whole ecosystem), and one thing I keep seeing is the Zhuangzi butterfly thing getting filed under "ancient people also invented the simulation argument." Someone brings up Descartes' dream argument, someone else goes "actually the Chinese got there first, like 2300 years earlier," and whilst everyone nods, lately I've come to think this is a misfiling — and I'd like to see the case made properly rather than my own half-formed version of it.

Here's the setup, in case you need a refresher: Zhuangzi dreams he's a butterfly, flutters about happily with no idea he's Zhuangzi; then he wakes up, is Zhuangzi again, and afterwards can't work out whether he's a man who dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly currently dreaming it's a man. The passage closes with a remark about the transformation of things, which the pop treatments almost always skip — and they definately shouldn't. My suspicion is that once you take that closing remark seriously, along with the material scattered through the Inner Chapters about disputes over right and wrong never settling, the passage stops looking like an epistemic-warrant puzzle — how do I know I'm not dreaming right now — and starts looking like something else: the boundaries between self and other, dream and waking, this and that not being fixed joints of the world in the first place. On that reading the Cartesian comparision isn't just loose, it inverts the point, because Descartes needs the dreaming scenario precisely in order to fix a standard for what counts as knowing, whereas (I think) Zhuangzi is undermining the idea that there's any stable standpoint from which to grade states like waking and dreaming at all.

So take the role of a comparative philosopher who works on both classical Chinese thought and early modern philosophy, and do this in two steps: first a short brainstorm where you lay out what the strongest version of the Cartesian reading says and what's genuinely working for it (don't strawman it — the dream-scepticism reading isn't stupid, and it's not obviously wrong that the passage raises a question about warrant); then the actual argument that the passage is better read as I've suggested — as being about the transformation of things and the absence of a privileged standpoint, not about our warrant for believing we're awake. Make the argument even if you end up thinking the pop reading is salvageable; what I want is the strongest case for the reclassification, plus an honest flag at the end on any point where you think the Cartesian reading has a real comeback. Don't recite translations at me from memory, and skip the history-of-the-text background I could get anywhere — I want the philosophical work, done carefully.
