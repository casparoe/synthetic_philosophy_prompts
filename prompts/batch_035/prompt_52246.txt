I just had one of those arguments with my friend Dev that goes on for like three hours and ends with both of us half-convinced the other one is wrong. It was about whether two AI systems that can read each other's source code could manage to cooperate in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma. The setup as he explained it: normally in a one-shot PD you defect, period, because there's no future to condition on and no enforcer. But if each agent gets to inspect the other's code before running, they can make their move depend on what the other one does, and apparently that opens the door to cooperation between agents who never interact more than once. So far so good, I can sort of picture that.

The part that lost me is where proofs come in. Dev said the naive strategy — I cooperate if I can prove you cooperate, you do the same — hits a wall of self-reference: proving you cooperate seems to require proving that you can prove that I cooperate, which requires proving that I can prove that you can prove... and it just never bottoms out. Then he said the actual fix uses Löb's theorem, and this is where he lost me completely. Something like, if I can prove that "if you prove I cooperate then you cooperate", the theorem hands me a proof that you actually cooperate?? Why does a theorem about formal arithmetic apply to two agents reasoning about each other's source code at all, and what is the intuitive reason it collapses that infinite regress instead of just pushing it one level down?

And then there was one more twist he mentioned right as I was leaving, which he called the Löbian obstacle: that two agents with equally strong proof systems can't get cooperation off the ground this way, and one of them has to be provably weaker (or use a weird self-trust scheme) for the thing to go through. That honestly sounded backwards to me — shouldn't MORE proof power be strictly better for establishing trust? So yeah, I'm asking you: explain the Löb move at a gut level, formalism only where you really need it, and tell me whether this asymmetry thing is actually right and why proof strength has to differ. Dev claims it's the key open problem in this whole research program and I'd like to know if he was just hyping it up or not.
