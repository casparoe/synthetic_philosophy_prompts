I've been asked to circulate a short statement of my decision-theoretic views ahead of a reading group on rationality and self-command, and before I send it around I would like an audit of whether what I've written is a coherent package or whether parts of it have to go. I've written it not as a manifesto but as verdicts on cases plus principles I take myself to hold, since that is what the group asked for.

First, the cases, so that we are working from the same page. In Kavka's toxin puzzle, an eccentric billionaire offers me a deal: if at midnight tonight I intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon, he will pay me a million dollars tomorrow morning. The toxin will make me painfully ill for a day but threatens no lasting harm, and the money is mine to keep whether or not I ever drink. In Newcomb's problem, a predictor who is essentially never wrong has already either placed a million dollars in an opaque box or left it empty, and has placed a thousand dollars in a transparent box beside it; I may take both boxes or the opaque box alone. In Parfit's hitchhiker, I am stranded in the desert and will die without rescue; a passing driver will take me to town only if he predicts that I will pay him on arrival, and he leaves predicted non-payers to die; once I am safely in town, paying or not paying has no further effect on anything I care about, and no one will ever know.

My positions:

1. In the toxin case, I take the deal and follow through: at midnight I sincerely intend to drink, the next morning I collect the million, and the next afternoon I drink. I regard the whole package as rational, and I would defend even the drinking itself, considered on its own at the moment I do it, as rational for me then, since it is the execution of the very disposition that earned the million. I also expect that when I wake up that morning, the drinking will look to me like a pointless bout of illness — but I am confident I will do it anyway.
2. In Newcomb's problem, I one-box.
3. In the hitchhiker case, once I have been dropped safely in town, I do not pay. The rescue has already happened; paying now benefits no one I care about; I would feel like a heel, but I would keep the money, and I think that is the rational thing to do.
4. I believe that a rational plan cannot terminate in an irrational act: if the final step of a plan is irrational, then executing the plan is irrational, and having formed the plan in the first place was irrational too.
5. I believe that intentions are not under direct voluntary control — I cannot make myself intend something merely because having that intention would be useful, any more than I can make myself believe something because it would be convenient — and that I cannot sincerely intend to do something I am certain I will not do.
6. I believe that whether an act is rational is fixed by the expected value of its consequences from the moment of acting onward: benefits already banked, or already guaranteed independently of how I act now, cannot by themselves make a present act rational.

When I pair these off myself, whichever friction I notice seems to dissolve when I push on it, which makes me suspect I am not pushing in the right places. More than one person in the group has told me the set cannot possibly hang together, but none of them could say exactly where it breaks, so I am genuinely unsure whether the problem lies with one of my case verdicts or with one of the principles.

Please audit the set. Tell me which pairs or larger subsets are in genuine tension, ranked by severity; which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and what precisely does the dissolving; and, for each genuine tension, what would have to give and what it would cost to give it up. Then tell me whether there is a single revision that would clean up the most, or whether my verdicts in (1) through (3) could survive only as a grab bag that no one unified theory of rationality would deliver. I have no attachment to any particular item being the one to go, so please do not spare me.
