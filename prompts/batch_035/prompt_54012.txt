I'm editing a small anthology of philosophically literate fiction for an undergraduate seminar on modality, and I'd like a short story built around modal realism in the tradition of David Lewis: the view that possible worlds are not abstract descriptions or pictures but concrete things, as real and fleshed-out as our own world, differing from ours only in what happens in them. Crucially, on this view nothing makes our world special except indexicality — "actual" should work like "here" or "now," so that an inhabitant of another world says "actual" of their world and means it exactly as we mean it of ours. The anthology already has stories about time travel and personal identity, so I want this one to keep the metaphysics strict rather than borrowing sci-fi machinery.

The premise I'd like you to develop: a middle-aged academic — or, if you prefer, a bereaved parent who audited a community-college metaphysics course and got more out of it than the students did — comes to accept modal realism, not through any new evidence (Lewis held that no possible observation could decide the matter, only overall theoretical virtue) but through the slow seduction of its economy and elegance. The story should dwell on what this belief does to her afterward. There is comfort: somewhere a counterpart's daughter survived, a counterpart never took the job that hollowed her out. There is vertigo: no causal or spatiotemporal relation picks out which world is "really" hers, and no answer to the question of why she is this one rather than another. And there is a hard edge, because Lewis's counterpart theory denies trans-world identity — the woman whose daughter lived is not her, only someone maximally similar to her. I'd like the narrative to put pressure on that boundary: is the counterpart's survival hers to take comfort in? Is the counterpart's grief hers to grieve? When she deliberates over some small real choice near the story's end, is she hoping, in some muddled but humanly intelligible sense, to end up being the counterpart of the lucky one?

A few constraints. Please keep the worlds causally isolated — no portals, no dreams that are really glimpses elsewhere, no crosstalk; the drama should come entirely from what the characters believe about modality, not from devices that violate it. The metaphysics should do dramatic work rather than sit in the background as flavor, but I'd also rather not have a character lecture about Lewis for two pages; show the doctrine straining against ordinary grief and hope. Aim for roughly 1,500–2,000 words, third or first person as you see fit, and try to end on the indexicality of actuality — the thought that everyone, everywhere, in every world, is the actual one — without stating that as a thesis. If the ending can be quiet and specific rather than cosmic, so much the better.
