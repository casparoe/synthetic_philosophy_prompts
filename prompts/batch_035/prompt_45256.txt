In a 1982 essay, Susan Wolf argued that a person who was as morally good as possible — one whose every practical concern was subordinated to benevolence, whose character was free of any trace of vanity, idle curiosity, or attachable idiosyncrasy — would be what she called a moral saint, and that such a person would be unappealing: a life without room for the humour, passions, and cultivated eccentricities that make people interesting and worth loving, and an ideal it would be strange to want our children to aspire to. Imagine Nietzsche were shown this essay and asked to comment. How, in your view, would he respond? In your answer, address three things: (1) whether he would endorse Wolf's picture of what maximal moral goodness amounts to, given his own suspicion that the ascetic ideal is itself a form of sickness rather than a genuine ideal; (2) whether he would treat her dichotomy between moral perfection and personal flourishing as a symptom of the very morality he criticizes, or as a point in his favour; and (3) where his alternative ideal of self-cultivation would remain vulnerable to her underlying worry — namely, that once morality is demoted from the governing standard of a life, it becomes unclear what stops the pursuit of excellence from collapsing into self-indulgence. Please confine yourself to Nietzsche's actual views and texts rather than caricatures of him.
