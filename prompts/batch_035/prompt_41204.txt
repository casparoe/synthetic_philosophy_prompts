I've been going back and forth with my roommate for weeks about what "freedom" even means (she took a political philosophy course, I didn't), and instead of more abstract arguing I decided to just go by my gut on concrete cases and see if there's a pattern. So, my verdicts. A guy locked in a room he has no desire to leave, doesn't even know it's locked: I want to say he was still unfree, even though it made no difference to him. A woman whose husband could legally veto any trip abroad but never has and never will: unfree, even though she travels constantly. A worker in a company town whose boss could fire her at will for union organizing but never has, and she doesn't even know the policy exists: unfree, though somehow less vividly than the wife case. Meanwhile someone deterred from jaywalking by a fine: totally free, just law-abiding. That pairing already bugs me — both are just living under a standing threat they've never actually bumped into.

Then the internal ones. An addict who loves his addiction and would vote to keep it: I keep flip flopping but lean unfree. Someone with a genuine phobia of flying who has never flown and honestly doesn't mind: unfree, I think, though nobody's doing anything to her. Someone who never learned to swim: just unable, not unfree. Someone too poor to afford a plane ticket: unlucky, but I don't want to say unfree — or at least not unfree in the same sense as the locked-room guy. My tentative principle was: you're unfree when some agent has deliberately arranged things so that you can't do something, whether or not they ever act on it. That handles the locked room and the husband, sort of. But it apparently makes the poor person unfree too (landlords do "deliberately" set rents he can't pay), which I don't want, and it can't touch the phobia or the addict at all, since no one arranged those. And it strains to explain why the jaywalker feels free and the union worker doesn't.

So: is there one principle that captures all or nearly all of my verdicts, or is there a small set of competing senses and I'm sliding between them without noticing? (I gather there's a literature on "negative" vs "positive" liberty and maybe a third thing about domination, but please don't give me a survey of the literature or a reading list — I want to know what's going on with MY verdicts.) Where saving them all would require something baroque, tell me which verdict to drop and why. And then tell me what your principle says about two cases I haven't listed: a teenager whose parents track her location and would ground her if she went somewhere they disapproved of, but who has never once wanted to go anywhere they'd disapprove of; and a villager who's been fed false stories his whole life about what's beyond the mountains — everyone who did the feeding is long dead and nobody is doing anything to him now.
