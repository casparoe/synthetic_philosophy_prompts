Next month I'm giving a job talk at a department that is, roughly: half formal epistemologists who cut their teeth on epistemic logic, a quarter philosophers of logic who distrust epistemic operators on principle, and one very senior Wittgensteinian who has taught On Certainty for thirty years and thinks most of analytic epistemology rests on a confusion about the grammar of "know." My thesis: the student's backward induction in the surprise exam case is not a fallacy to be diagnosed away but is, in a limited sense, sound — what it establishes is not that the teacher's announcement is false but that the announcement is a blindspot for the student: it can be true while the student is in no position to know it, because the announcement, conjoined with the student's knowing the announcement, generates a contradiction. On this reading the exam can still genuinely surprise the class, since the backward induction only gets going by assuming, at each step, that the student knows the exam has not yet occurred. I also want to make the more aggressive claim that Quine's familiar response in his 1953 Mind paper — that the student is never entitled to assume she will know the exam hasn't already happened — and the blindspot line are two faces of a single diagnosis rather than rival resolutions.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections in roughly the order this particular audience will produce them, phrased as they would phrase them: the epistemic-logic objections (what exactly is the operator I am attributing to "knows," whether the argument needs positive introspection or omniscience about the announcement's publicity, what happens once the announcement is treated as common knowledge and iterated); the philosophers of logic pressing on whether "blindspot" does any real work beyond relabeling Moore's paradox, and whether my resolution quietly changes the subject from whether the exam can surprise to whether the student can derive anything; and the Wittgensteinian's likely charge that I have formalized a grammatical oddity and mistaken the artifacts of my formalism for a discovery. For each objection give me a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft — I would rather know my weak points than be surprised by them, so to speak. Make sure to include the one-day-case follow-up — what happens to my diagnosis when the announcement is simply "there will be an exam tomorrow and you will not know in advance," where the announcement seems flatly self-undermining rather than blind-spot-like — because someone will raise it and I do not yet have an answer that satisfies me. And identify the question I am hoping nobody asks: whether the blindspot treatment, if it works here, also dissolves the Kaplan–Montague knower paradox, or whether the knower case is precisely where it breaks — since if the latter, my unifying claim about Quine is in serious trouble.
