I'm a novelist, deep enough into a draft that I cant restructure it now, and the book centers on a fractious family whose arguments keep resurfacing at dinner. One brother, an economist by trade, explains climate change to his grown kids as an overdraft: humanity has a joint account, his generation has been spending it down and taking out loans in the grandchildren's name, and every ton of carbon is a withdrawal the kids will eventually have to repay, with interest. The daughter, who by the second half of the book is getting arrested at climate protests, says the analogy is rotten -- you can't owe money to people who don't exist yet, and the "repayment" isn't tidy sums being handed back anyway, it's a whole climate. I want the argument on the page to be genuinely good on both sides, not one of those fictional debates where the author has picked a winner and quietly sandbagged the loser.

So please audit the overdraft/loan analogy for me. Lay out what maps onto what -- the loan itself, the interest, the creditor, the default, the consent to borrow -- and then where the mapping breaks. Which features of the debt picture does the economist's use quietly smuggle in: that debts are zero-sum transfers rather than destruction of value, that a creditor exists and can demand payment, that debts get settled in the same currency they were incurred in, maybe that moral obligation works like double-entry bookkeeping? Separate the disanalogies that actually do damage to his conclusion from the ones that don't (the daughter, being angry, reaches for all of them, but I suspect some are duds). And tell me whether the conclusion he leans on -- that there's a determinate, quantifiable debt the current generation owes, so much and no more -- survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether he's left with something weaker but still real.

One more thing: if you think there's a better analogy lurking that gets what the overdraft gets wrong -- a trust held for beneficiaries, a commons being fenced off, an inheritance being gambled away, whatever fits -- say what it gets right and what it gets wrong in turn, because I'm tempted to give it to the daughter as her counter-move at the Thanksgiving scene, and I don't want her winning on a clever-sounding dud.
