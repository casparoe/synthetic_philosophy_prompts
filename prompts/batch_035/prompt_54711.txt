I'm taking a graduate seminar on AI alignment, and this week's question was whether safety arguments should be built on worst-case or average-case guarantees. Twice now the proverb "a chain is only as strong as its weakest link" has been invoked to settle it: once by a classmate, to argue that a model which misbehaves on one input in a thousand is thereby unsafe, since the minimum over conditions defines the system rather than the mean; and once by our instructor, to argue that a safety property which holds only on the deployment distribution is no safety property at all, because an adversarial or merely out-of-distribution input plays the role of the weak link. I left the seminar unsure what precise claim the proverb is actually making, and I suspect the two uses needed different claims. Could you set out, as a list I can bring to the next session, the distinct theses "a chain is only as strong as its weakest link" could be expressing once transplanted from reliability engineering into AI safety? I can see at least these candidates, and I'd like each evaluated on its own terms: the structural thesis, that system behavior is the minimum over components, which is only true for series composition, so everything turns on whether the system in question is actually chained the way a chain is — say, an agent pipeline where any subcomponent's failure propagates to the output — or instead has parallel redundancy, where voting or averaging genuinely does rescue the minimum; the distributional thesis, that the relevant inputs are adversarially or inadvertently selected rather than drawn from the training distribution, so the average being averaged over is the wrong average; the normative thesis, that safety standards ought to be set by the minimum as a matter of policy, whatever the mathematics says; the decision-theoretic thesis, that with catastrophic and possibly unbounded losses, aggregating by expectation over a distribution is the wrong operation, or at least that the mean is dominated by the tail; and the epistemic thesis, that for novel systems we can at best bound the worst case, so the average is not even on offer. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or straightforwardly false. Then the part I most want: identify which reading each of the two seminar uses actually required, and whether that reading holds up — in particular, whether my classmate's argument needed the structural thesis to be true of the architecture in question, and whether my instructor's argument needed something stronger than the distributional thesis alone. If either use depended on a false reading, or on an unstated architectural premise, flag that explicitly rather than softening it.
