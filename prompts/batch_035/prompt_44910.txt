Compose a parody of a consumer warranty document — the glossy pamphlet that ships with a toaster, crossbred with a software terms-of-service agreement — issued by a fictional company that sells performative utterances. Invent a plausible corporate name for it; its catalogue includes wedding vows in three finishes, formal apologies, christenings, contract acceptances, and a budget line of congratulations. The piece should run about 700 words, in a flat corporate-legal voice, organized as numbered warranty clauses followed by a short FAQ.

The humor must come from getting J. L. Austin's How to Do Things with Words right, so that a philosopher of language laughs rather than winces, while a reader who has never heard of felicity conditions still finds it a warranty absurdly mismatched to its product. In particular, the fine print should quietly sort Austin's infelicities into the categories a warranty department would need:

- Misfires, where the act never happened — the vows pronounced by an unauthorized officiant, the procedure botched halfway through — treated as defects in workmanship and fully covered.
- Abuses, where the act happened but was hollow: the apology delivered without remorse, the promise made without intent. Here the company must take Austin's line that an insincere performative is still a performed act, so these are excluded from coverage — sincerity is not a covered component, and a dissatisfied customer is not, on that account, an unmarried one.
- Perlocutionary failures, where the act succeeded but the sequel didn't arrive: the apology in all in order, the forgiveness not secured. These fall under a separate, markedly weaker warranty, since effects on the addressee's feelings were never in the locution and the company will not be liable for hearts.

Further clauses should play the theory just as straight: a warning that utterances used on stage, in fiction, or at open-mic nights are etiolated and carry no force, all such sales final; a provision making uptake the customer's responsibility, since without securing of uptake even a flawless utterance misfires; and at least one clause about the warranty document itself, whose operative sentence is a performative of the very kind it covers, so that if the signatory who issued it was never authorized, the warranty never existed — and the reader should notice that the company cannot repair this with a rider, because a failed procedure cannot be re-performed retroactively by the same office that failed to perform it. Close with an FAQ in which a customer asks whether a wedding can be returned, and the answer must turn entirely on whether the marriage was void or merely unhappy.

The satire should stay deadpan throughout: no winking, no aside about lawyers, no announcement that this is a joke. It should be fair to Austin, exaggerating the real tension between conventional illocutionary force and consumer goods — force that exists only if a convention, an authorized speaker, and an audience's uptake cooperate — rather than inventing features of the theory. Ideally, by the last clause, a reader sees without being told why force is not the sort of thing a warranty can cover: not because the company is scurrilous, but because a speech act is not manufactured, and only manufactured things can be defective in the way this document keeps promising they are.
