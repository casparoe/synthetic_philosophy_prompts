I lead the evaluation team at a company that ships language-model features — a drafting assistant for technical writers and a triage assistant for customer support. Two or three times a month a product manager escalates an incident to us with the same question in different words: did the model misunderstand the situation, or did it just pattern-match badly? Last quarter we answered this badly: we treated a failure to generalize from US refund policy to a newly launched market as a comprehension problem and rewrote prompts for a week, when the real issue was that the new market had almost no comparable data in front of the model and a simple retrieval patch solved it in an afternoon. I want a reusable procedure my evaluators — sharp engineers with no philosophy background — can run on a single incident in a single afternoon, with no access to model internals, that converts "does it understand?" into observable, testable steps.

Ground it in what the debate over whether these systems understand actually turns on — the distinction between riding the form of sentences and tracking what they're about, generalization and robustness as the observable signatures of understanding, the worry that fluency can mask the absence of any world behind the words — but operationalized rather than argued. The procedure should specify the questions to ask in order, what evidence counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates with a definite verdict, and where it should stop and say "what remains is not an empirical question" and hand the incident back to a person. One test I'd like included: a paraphrase-and-substitution check, where we swap the surface vocabulary of the failing task for nonsense tokens or reshuffled names and see whether performance tracks the underlying situation or just the strings — but design the full battery yourself.

Then run it on three incidents. First, the US-to-new-market refund failure described above, so we can see what the procedure would have concluded and how fast. Second, a case where the triage assistant passed every behavioral check we could devise, including stress tests and adversarial paraphrases, yet a senior stakeholder insists it plainly does not understand a word it processes and wants the feature shelved — I want to know what the procedure says, and what it defers to the stakeholder as a non-empirical call. Third, a case where the drafting assistant produced output so fluent and apparently insightful that the team was delighted with it, but where your procedure should nonetheless flag it as likely shallow — the procedure must be capable of contradicting the answer we would prefer. For each incident, say what the procedure settles, what it leaves genuinely open, and where a human judgment remains that no checklist can absorb.
