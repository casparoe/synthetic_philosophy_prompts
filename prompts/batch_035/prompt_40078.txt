I'm in a philosophy of law class and we just started intellectual property, so this has been bugging me all week. In the US (and most places) you can copyright the exact way you wrote your novel but not the plot itself, and you can patent a newly engineered mouse or an industrial process but not a law of nature, a mathematical formula, or something you merely found already existing in the world. So the whole system seems to draw a line between creations/expressions that can be owned and ideas/discoveries that cant be. My question: what theory of the ownership of ideas does that line presuppose? Is it basically Lockean -- you own what your labor genuinely mixed with, while ideas are held in common like unenclosed land? Does it presuppose a real metaphysical difference between creating and discovering, and does that difference hold up for hard cases like software, engineered organisms, or drugs isolated from plants? Or is the line just a pragmatic fudge that several rival theories would all be compatible with? I'd also like to know what would actualy have to change in the system if we decided abstract ideas themselves were ownable -- like, what breaks, and does anything get better? Please answer in American English.
