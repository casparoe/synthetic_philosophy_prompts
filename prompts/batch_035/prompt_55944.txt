The question read: Bertrand's chord paradox is often said to refute the principle of indifference. Set up the paradox, presenting at least two of the standard methods of selecting a random chord in a circle together with the probability each yields for the chord being longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Then assess what, if anything, the paradox shows about the principle of indifference: does it refute it, restrict its domain, or leave it untouched? Justify your assessment.

The student's answer: Bertrand's paradox concerns a chord chosen at random in a circle, and asks for the probability that it is longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Three natural methods give three different answers. (1) Choose two points independently and uniformly on the circumference and join them: by symmetry we may fix one endpoint, and the chord is longer than the side exactly when the second endpoint falls in the middle third of the remaining arc, giving 1/3. (2) Choose a radius at random and a point at random along it, and draw the chord through that point perpendicular to the radius: the chord is longer than the side just in case the point lies within half a radius of the centre, giving 1/2. (3) Choose the chord's midpoint uniformly over the disk: the chord is longer just in case the midpoint falls inside the concentric circle of half the radius, and since areas go as the square of the radius, this gives 1/4. So the three methods yield 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4, which is supposed to be a contradiction. But it is not. Each method describes a different experiment: (1) generates two random boundary points, (2) a random point on a radius, (3) a random interior point. The principle of indifference tells us to spread probability evenly over the outcomes of the experiment actually performed, and each calculation does precisely that. The principle is therefore vindicated: Bertrand's paradox is not a counterexample to it but a lesson that the phrase random chord is incomplete until a generating procedure has been specified. The paradox accordingly shows that the principle needs care in application but is consistent whenever the experiment is fully described, and that its difficulties arise specifically for continuous outcome spaces, where the same set of outcomes admits infinitely many parametrizations. For finite outcome spaces the principle is unproblematic, which is why Keynes, in his Treatise on Probability, defended the unrestricted principle: equal probabilities go to equally possible alternatives, which he regarded as the foundation of the whole theory.
