The dispute over what heritability estimates actually mean has now run for decades between people who agree on all the mathematics, and I would like a diagnosis of why it never resolves rather than a verdict on who is right.

Here is the shape of the impasse as I understand it. Everyone involved accepts the arithmetic: the ACE decomposition, Falconer's formula, the fact that heritability is a property of a population-environment pair rather than of a trait, and the fact that a trait can be highly heritable in one environment and barely heritable in another. The critics of heritability research nonetheless insist that the numbers are nearly interpretively useless outside plant and animal breeding: they are indexed to the actual distribution of environments, so they license no claims about how malleable a trait would be under interventions that change that distribution, and they say nothing about individual development, still less about differences between populations. The defenders reply that this is a strawman, that nobody serious ever claimed heritability measures immutability; they point out that estimates from twin studies, adoption studies, and molecular methods now roughly converge, that convergence across methods with different assumptions is exactly how science measures a quantity, and that knowing which proportion of variance tracks genetic differences matters for finding the relevant variants and for predicting how a population will respond to a uniform intervention. Each side grants the other's premises and then produces the same conclusions it started with. Notably, neither side is much moved by the two facts that get waved around most: that measured heritability of some cognitive traits appears to rise with age (which the defenders treat as vindication and the critics as evidence that environments amplify genetic differences, e.g. through a social-multiplier mechanism), and that certain traits were once highly heritable and became less so as environments equalized.

My working hypothesis is that the two camps are using cause to mean two different things: the defenders mean a decomposition of actual variance in a fixed background, while the critics implicitly evaluate heritability claims against a much larger space of counterfactual environments and thus hear them as claims about intervention sensitivity. In other words, they may be answering different questions under one label, with one group describing a property of a population as it stands and the other demanding something like an interventionist heritability that no standard estimate even attempts to estimate. But I am not sure whether that diagnosis dissolves the disagreement or merely restates it, since the choice of which counterfactual background is relevant seems itself to be the substantive issue.

Is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or two questions under one name? Diagnose it using what each side actually claims, and tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a sharper conceptual distinction between variance-decomposition and intervention-sensitivity, a formal result showing when they come apart or coincide, better molecular data on gene-environment interaction, or a demonstration that the question is ill-posed and the dispute should simply be split into two research programs. Wherever your diagnosis depends on an interpretive step rather than a settled fact, flag it, and tell me what evidence or argument would lead you to a different diagnosis of the impasse altogether.
