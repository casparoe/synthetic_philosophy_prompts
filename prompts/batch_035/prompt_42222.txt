My decision theory reading group has been stuck on imprecise probabilities for the better part of a year and I want a diagnosis of the stall, not another round of it. The imprecise side leads with Ellsburg-type cases: one urn is known to be 50/50 red/blue, a second urn's red fraction could be anywhere in [0,1], and people prefer betting on the known urn in either direction -- which no single sharp credence seems to allow, since whatever number you assign red, betting blue at the same odds should look equally good once that number is fixed. The sharp side has two standard comebacks. First, that Ellsberg just measures aversion to unresolved uncertainty, a preference and not a belief, so it can't be evidence about what credences are. Second, dilation: an imprecise agent can start at a crisp 1/2 for some event, then learn the outcome of a coin flip that is independent of the event, and end up spread back over a whole interval just from conditioning -- which they call a reductio, since learning something irrelevant shouldn't make you less able to act, and an imprecise agent will sometimes pay to avoid free information. The imprecise side replies that dilation is the theory correctly registering that the flip revealed which of two contingent setups you're in, and that the sharp side's axioms just smuggle in a demand for completeness nobody ever argued for. Nobody has moved in months. My working theory is that the two sides are answering different questions -- one describing what actual agents are like, the other legislating for idealized ones -- and that once you see this the fight dissolves, except I'm not sure the imprecise side will accept being relegated to psychology. So what kind of disagreement is this actually: verbal, normative, a dispute about what counts as data (do Ellsberg preferences count?), or something else? And what would count as progress here -- a decision rule everyone can live with, a no-dilation result, a clean distinction between credence and willingness to bet, or nothing?
