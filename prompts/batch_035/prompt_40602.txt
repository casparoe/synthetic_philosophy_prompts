I write about science and ideas for a national outlet, but I spent my first fifteen years in journalism on the criminal courts beat — suppression hearings, competency evaluations, sentencing memos, the occasional capital appeal. On my own time I have worked through the free will literature, and I have reached the point where I can follow the arguments but cannot see which of them would ever show up, in disguise, on the courthouse steps. So here is what I want, and I want it done systematically rather than illustratively.

Restate the major positions in the free will debate in the vocabulary I actually used for fifteen years, as faithfully as the material allows. That vocabulary includes: the division between the physical act and the mental element, the culpable mental states ranked from purpose through knowledge and recklessness down to negligence; the doctrines that diminish or defeat responsibility — infancy, insanity, duress, provocation, involuntary intoxication, diminished capacity; the distinction, which took me years to internalize, between an excuse, which concedes the act was wrong but denies that this person is properly answerable for it, and a mitigation, which concedes answerability but argues for a lighter penalty; and the sentencing rationales — retribution, deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation — that statutes list without ranking and that judges weigh without announcing a method.

Against that background, translate each of the following: (a) the hard determinist's claim that nobody ultimately originates their actions and therefore nobody deserves punishment in the deep, desert-based sense; (b) the libertarian's claim that some agents are genuine first causes and that responsibility requires this; (c) the compatibilist's claim that responsibility requires only the right kind of connection between the agent's reasons, character, and conduct; and (d) Dennett's reframing, in which the real question is which varieties of free will are worth wanting. For each, tell me which legal doctrines it maps onto cleanly, which it maps onto only by distortion, and which parts of it have no expression in the courtroom at all.

Then I want the residue report, which matters more to me than the translations. Three questions in particular. First: the philosophers argue endlessly about whether the agent could have done otherwise, and I cannot find that test anywhere in the actual law — the doctrines turn on what the defendant knew, intended, and could control, not on whether, holding everything fixed, an alternative act was available in some metaphysically robust sense. Is that because the law has quietly settled a question the philosophers consider open, because the law is answering a different question under the same word, or because the legal tests smuggle in the metaphysics at a level nobody examines? Second: when brain science enters a case, it does so almost exclusively as mitigation — as an argument about degree — and courts treat the stronger claim that a brain scan negates responsibility as a category error. What does that pattern do to the philosophical claim that determinism undermines desert rather than merely adjusting its degree? Third: does Dennett's criterion, once translated, simply become the observation that the law already presupposes certain rational capacities, so that the worth-wanting test reads off the law rather than adjudicating between the positions — and if so, is that a strength of his approach or a circularity?

A constraint on your answer: keep the translation honest. Do not silently equate the law's implicit picture of the responsible person with philosophical compatibilism and present the match as a discovery; if the law's picture is doing philosophical work that the compatibilists only claim to be doing, say so, and if it is doing less, say that instead. And end with your own verdict: is the residue a sign that courtroom vocabulary is missing something the philosophers can see, or a sign that the philosophical debate is less substantive than it looks to those of us on the outside?
