Some background so we're on the same page. Douglas Hofstadter, in a couple of Scientific American columns in 1983 (later collected in his book Metamagical Themas), set up two related scenarios to test what he called superrationality. First the thought experiment, usually called the Platonia dilemna: an eccentric trillionaire gathers 20 people and tells them that if exactly one of them sends him a telegram by noon the next day (cooperation between players forbidden, telegrams sent reverse charges), the sender gets a billion dollars; if zero telegrams or more than one arrive, nobody gets anything. Hofstadter's line was that the superrational move is to send a telegram with probability 1/20 — everyone randomizes identically, so exactly one telegram arrives with decent probability. Then he actually ran a real contest, the Luring Lottery: Scientific American offered a million dollars, but divided by the total number of entries received, paid to the sender of one randomly drawn entry — and you could submit as many entries as you liked just by writing a large number on a single postcard. More entries meant better odds but a smaller prize. The strategy the magazine printed was roughly: estimate how many readers will respond, roll a die with that many sides, and only send an entry if you roll a 1. In practice the readership nuked it — people submitted numbers like a googolplex, some crammed the postcard with expressions evaluating to something enormous, the prize shrank to a microscopic fraction of a cent, and the magazine couldnt even determine a winner.
