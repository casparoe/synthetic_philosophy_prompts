I'm on the volunteer board of a small-town community foundation — twelve of us, mostly retirees, small-business owners, and a couple of teachers, none of us academics. I'm writing to you because a decision has split the board down the middle, and I've been turning over the underlying question in my own head for months without getting anywhere.

Here's the situation. The largest church in our town announced it will close within eighteen months; the congregation has aged out and can't sustain the building. Over the decades that church quietly became the town's main social institution: it ran the food pantry, the after-school tutoring, the holiday dinners for people who'd otherwise be alone, and — maybe more important — it was the one place where the hardware store owner, the new family from the city, and the widow on disability actually saw each other regularly. A group of nonreligious residents, organized by a retired schoolteacher, is now asking our foundation to fund a successor: a weekly "community gathering" with music, a short talk on some ethical theme, shared meals, and coordinated volunteer drives — basically the church's programming with the religion removed.

The board is split into two camps that talk past each other. Camp one says this is "church with the God taken out," and that it will wither within two years, because people don't give up a Sunday morning for a moral lecture unless they feel something sacred is at stake; the church's good works, they say, were a byproduct of shared worship, not the main event. Camp two points out that plenty of countries manage high trust and civic cooperation without churchgoing, so there's nothing magical about it.

I have no academic training, but I've been thinking about this on my own, and two rough ideas keep coming back to me, and I'd like your memo to engage with them rather than sidestep them. First, from what I've observed, churchgoing people seem notably generous mostly toward other churchgoing people — the deacon drives the fellow member to surgery — while their treatment of strangers and of people who can never pay them back doesn't obviously stand out. If that's right, then the real test of whether an institution "makes people better" isn't how warm it is to its own members but how it treats outsiders and non-reciprocators, and maybe we should judge the proposed gathering by that standard. Second, I've started distinguishing what I privately call "porch trust" from "dark-road trust": the kind of trust that exists while neighbors are watching you from their porches, versus the kind that holds when nobody would ever know. My fear is that a tight-knit weekly group is good at manufacturing the first kind and doesn't need to bother with the second — but the church, for all its flaws, at least told people Someone was always watching, which cuts both ways, and I honestly don't know whether an institution without that story can produce the second kind at all.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in moral psychology and the study of religion as a social force. Before writing anything, think carefully and brainstorm the possible positions; then draft the decision memo I can circulate before our next board meeting. It must contain: (1) a concrete, actionable recommendation — fund the proposal outright, reject it, or fund it with conditions — including a time frame and, if there are conditions, specific checkpoints a volunteer board could realistically evaluate (attendance alone won't settle anything, given what I said above about in-group warmth); (2) the reasoning, written plainly enough that a farmer and a retired schoolteacher on the board can follow it; and (3) an honest, respectful treatment of the strongest point on the losing side — especially the "church with the God taken out" worry, which deserves a real answer rather than a dismissal. No reading lists or citations of studies, please; base everything on reasoning and ordinary observable facts about people. If some part of this genuinely can't be settled from the armchair, say which part, and recommend how we should decide it in practice.
