Got into a huge argument with my flatmate over dinner about whether shrimp and crabs actually suffer, and it ended with her proposing something I can't stop thinking about: welfare requirements shouldn't be binary, they should scale with how confident we are that an animal is sentient. So full protections where confidence is high, scaled-down protections where its contested, nothing where confidence is really low -- probability-weighted standards instead of a yes/no line. I work for a small seafood certification scheme and we're rewriting our standards next year, and i keep thinking we could actually use her idea somehow. Can you turn it into a decision procedure my standards committee can run whenever someone proposes a welfare requirement for a farmed aquatic species? The committee members aren't scientists -- mostly industry people and one vet -- so it has to spell out the questions in order, what kind of evidence answers each one (behavioral, physiological, whatever the people who study sentience actually rely on), where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to punt to human judgement because the evidence just runs out. It needs to handle the in-between cases like decapods and cephalopods without the committee basically voting on vibes. Then run it on three proposals we actually have on the table: requiring electrical stunning before slaughter for farmed salmon, banning eyestalk ablation in prawn hatcheries, and a stocking-density limit for farmed crabs. Please be honest if the procedure gives an answer the committee will hate, and say what it flat out can't settle.
