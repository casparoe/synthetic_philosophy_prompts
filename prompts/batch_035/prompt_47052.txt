I run a seminar series at a lifelong-learning institute. The audience is retired professionals — engineers, doctors, a former accountant, one retired high-school math teacher — bright and curious, most of them with calculus from decades ago and nothing since. In two weeks I have a single seventy-five-minute session on chaos and on what chaos does and does not say about determinism. Don't explain the subject to me; plan the explanation I should give.

I need four things from the plan, and I want you to commit to definite choices throughout rather than laying out options for me to pick from.

First, the order of the pieces, with the dependency made explicit. I keep going back and forth between starting on the epistemic side — the fact that any real measurement has finite precision — and starting with a vivid demonstration of two nearly identical trajectories flying apart. Decide which opening is right and defend it, and tell me what the dependency structure of the rest looks like: what cannot be understood until what else is in place, and where the notion of a prediction horizon has to sit relative to the notion of a deterministic law.

Second, the single example that should carry the whole session, and what it must have and lack. I'm torn between the double pendulum — physically demonstrable in the room, but hard to connect to anything quantitative — and the logistic map, where the audience could actually see orbits on a spreadsheet, but which risks the retirees glazing over at iterated maps. Choose one, and explain what work it is doing at each stage of the sequence rather than just which is more crowd-pleasing.

Third, the misconception map. My guesses at what they'll arrive with or form: that chaotic just means random; that the butterfly effect is the trivial truth that small causes can have big effects, true of everything and so of nothing; that chaos refutes Laplace's demon and thereby kills determinism as such; and the opposite pull from the engineers, that if the equations are fully deterministic then prediction is purely a matter of gathering better data. Tell me whether these guesses are right, what I'm missing, and — crucially — the exact point in the sequence at which each misconception should be headed off rather than left to fester until the Q&A. There is also the genuinely subtle bit I want to handle without losing the room: that sensitive dependence at a single point (a pencil balanced on its tip) is not chaos, and that chaotic systems can be statistically predictable even while being unpredictable in detail. Tell me where in the sequence that distinction earns its keep and how to carry it.

Fourth, a closing question I could ask that would distinguish someone who has actually grasped that chaos is a limitation on prediction rather than on determinism from someone who has merely absorbed the vocabulary — and, separately, what I should openly refuse to cover at this level rather than mangle. I suspect Lyapunov exponents are out, and that quantum indeterminism deserves at most a quarantine of one sentence saying we're setting it aside, but rule on both and on anything else that belongs behind that line.

Above all, take positions: one sequence, one carrying example, one disposition for each misconception. If you think one of my premises about the audience or the material is wrong, say so directly rather than routing around it.
