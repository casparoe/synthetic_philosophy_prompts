I'm a software engineer on an ML infrastructure team — my background is in distributed systems and general software engineering, with no formal philosophy training, though I've picked up enough ML to follow most of what our researchers say. My team is currently deciding whether to invest serious engineering effort in interpretability tooling for the models we deploy (we'd use it as part of our safety audit pipeline before releases), and a colleague sent me a long blog post by an interpretability researcher arguing about whether neural networks actually have representations at all. The post frames the debate like this: 'Interpretability researchers split into realists and instrumentalists. The realists say that when a method identifies a "gold detector" or a "deceptive-answer feature," it is discovering a genuine representation that was already there in the weights, not just fitting a convenient story to the behavior. On this view, networks really do represent: their intermediate activations form concepts much like the ones we use in everyday language, which is why the same features found in one model keep reappearing in others trained on similar data. The instrumentalists reply that these are just useful descriptions of input-output behavior, and recent results on interpretability illusions settle it: researchers found that the same methods that find a face detector in one layer will find an equally convincing detector in a random projection of the activations, so the features we "discover" are artifacts of the methods. The superposition results are the final nail — models store more features than they have neurons, so the tidy one-feature-one-direction picture that realism requires is false. And if realism were right, the network's own representations would match ours, but the whole point of interpretability is that they usually don't.'

I found the post pretty persuasive, particularly the random-projection point and the superposition point, and I came away thinking the realist position is basically dead and that interpretability outputs are narrative-fitting rather than discovery — which would mean our tooling investment is a bad idea. But the post was also openly contemptuous of the researchers it labeled realists, and I know enough from experience that blog posts flatten technical disagreements into team sports. I also genuinely don't know whether "the network has representations" is even supposed to be the same claim as "the representations look like our concepts," so I can't tell whether the last part of the post is a real argument or a sleight of hand.

Please go through the summary bit by bit and tell me which parts a researcher working in this area would accept, which are defensible simplifications, and which are conflations or overstatements — especially, where the post presents some result as refuting realism, I'd like to know whether the realist camp would accept that framing or whether they have a straightforward response. Then, given the parts of the summary that survive scrutiny, tell me whether the conclusion I drew — that interpretability findings shouldn't be trusted in an audit pipeline and we shouldn't make the investment — actually follows from them or overshoots.
