Design a thought experiment to the following specifications. I want a case, set in ordinary professional life (a hospital ward, a school, a social-work office — no trolleys, no demons, no perfect information), in which an act utilitarian must clearly endorse an action that a Buddhist ethicist, working from the tradition's intention-centered analysis of action, must condemn. The Buddhist condemnation may not lean on rebirth, on karma as cosmic bookkeeping, or on any empirical prediction about consequences that the utilitarian could simply fold into the calculation as a further input — so make the welfare facts clear enough that the utilitarian's endorsement is not hostage to empirical guesses, and have your utilitarian explicitly address the obvious rule-based worry about eroded trust. Crucially, isolate the intention as the variable: the same physical act, performed with calm and well-informed compassion rather than with what the brahmavihāra commentaries call grief-tinted pity (the near enemy of compassion — treat this as my stipulation if you doubt the attribution), should come out permissible for the Buddhist. The verdict must be generated by the mental root of the act, not stipulated, since I intend to use the case to argue that Buddhist ethics contains a genuinely intention-based constraint that does not reduce to welfare maximization. The Buddhist resources you should draw on are the precept against false speech and the analysis of an act's karmic character in terms of the intention from which it issues. Present your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the scenario; (2) the utilitarian verdict and why; (3) the Buddhist verdict and why, derived from the intention analysis rather than asserted; (4) which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to each verdict once that feature is removed; (5) the strongest utilitarian counterattack on the case and what the Buddhist would have to say in reply.
