I've been invited to contribute a short statement of my considered views to a workshop on infinity in decision theory — the format is a numbered list of firm commitments with a paragraph of commentary each. Before I submit it, I want an audit of whether the package hangs together. Here is the draft:

1. I accept the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms for gambles over finite outcome sets, and I take expected-utility maximization to be constitutive of rational choice, not merely a useful heuristic that usually comes out right.

2. I hold that welfare aggregates without bound. For any number of happy lives there is a better outcome containing more of them; a world with 10^50 blissful lives is strictly better than one with 10^49, and nothing reverses this as the numbers grow. There is no finite ceiling on how good a world can be.

3. I think the St. Petersburg game is a genuine problem for expected-utility theory rather than an artifact of sloppy formulation. It cannot be rational to pay any finite amount, however enormous, for a single play; in fact I would refuse to pay more than a modest sum, and I do not consider myself irrational for that.

4. I reject the move of dismissing minuscule probabilities. If a charitable intervention has a one-in-a-googol chance of securing a googol additional happy lives and some modest near-certain benefit otherwise, I think the expected-value case for it is decisive, and I make my actual giving decisions on that basis.

5. I believe the ordinary casino version of St. Petersburg — coin flips doubling a monetary payoff — is genuinely defused by the diminishing marginal utility of money. With roughly logarithmic utility for money the game is worth only a few dollars, and I regard that as a real dissolution of what the casino version appears to show, not a dodge.

6. I hold that a rational verdict should not turn on how a decision problem is described or packaged: two presentations involving the same acts, the same states of the world, and the same outcomes should yield the same recommendation, even if one presentation makes certain possibilities salient and the other buries them.

My question is which of these commitments pull against which, how badly, and what the cheapest repair would be. Please rank any tensions you find by severity rather than treating them all as equally damaging, distinguish genuine conflicts from merely apparent ones, and tell me which single revision would restore the most coherence at the least cost — including what that revision would cost me elsewhere on the list. I have suspicions about one or two items myself, but I would rather not bias you by saying which.
