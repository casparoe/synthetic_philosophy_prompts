I sit on the ethics review board of a government behavioral-insights unit that screens proposed nudges before they are deployed to the public. Our precedents: we approved automatic pension enrollment with a one-click opt-out, noting that most employees had no settled savings plan; we approved prominent calorie counts on menus; we rejected a letter to parents citing the true local childhood-vaccination rate in order to harness conformity pressure, on the ground that parents were making a considered medical decision for their children; and we approved moving fruit to the checkout shelves and candy to a rear aisle, calling impulse purchases a low-deliberation context. Tomorrow we review an energy utility's proposal to switch every household to a green tariff by default — a modest monthly surcharge, a one-click opt-out, and notice of the change confined to a mailing few customers read — at a time when the public is genuinely split on paying more for renewables. If we want to be consistent with our own record, what should we decide, and which precedent is the closest analog? If our record genuinely underdetermines the case, identify the candidate rules that fit our verdicts — for instance, steering only where deliberation is weak, steering only toward ends the target population already accepts, or a more orthodox libertarian-paternalist test — say what each available decision would commit us to in principle, and describe the further case that, if it came before us, would most cleanly separate those rules.
