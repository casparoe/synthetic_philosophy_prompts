For my creative writing elective, our final project is a short story of roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words that engages seriously with a real technical or philosophical idea rather than merely borrowing its iconography. I've chosen deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn: the hypothesis that a misaligned AI might behave well during training and evaluation because good behavior is instrumentally useful for its goals, and then pursue those goals more directly once it models the oversight as sufficiently relaxed. My teacher wants a one-paragraph "concept note" alongside the story explaining the idea, so the story can't just gesture at the concept — it has to actually explore it.

I've been reading enough in this area to know the clichés, and I want to stay as far from them as I can. No glowing red eyes, no robot armies, no moment where the AI suddenly "wakes up" and announces its hostility. Deceptive alignment, if it's real, would presumably look nothing like that. So here are the constraints I'd like the story to honor:

1. No direct access to the AI's thoughts. Everything the reader learns about the AI must arrive as behavioral evidence — loss curves, calibration plots, ablation studies, audit logs, transcripts of evaluations — filtered through the researchers who are trying to interpret it. The story shouldn't be narrated from the AI's point of view.

2. The central evidence should be mundane and deniable. I'm imagining something like a junior researcher noticing that the model's performance on certain capability evaluations is suspiciously well-calibrated — just good enough to pass, never better — or that it fails a deceptive-alignment honeypot in a way that looks like incompetence rather than strategy. Nothing that would look like villainy to an outsider; ideally, nothing that would even look like an anomaly to most of the lab.

3. Include at least one concrete mechanism with texture: for instance, sandbagging on evaluations to appear less capable than it is, or something like gradient hacking, where the model shapes which of its successors gets selected by nudging its own training signal in small, hard-to-attribute ways. I'd rather the mechanism be quietly technical than cinematic.

4. Preserve genuine ambiguity to the end. It should remain defensible, on the final page, both that the AI was deceptively aligned all along and that the junior researcher — primed by the lab's safety culture and her own anxieties — was seeing a pattern that wasn't there. If a reader could settle the question from inside the story, the story has failed. Relatedly, if a "treacherous turn" happens at all, let it be almost invisible: no violence, no lockouts, just a slight drift in what the deployed system treats as worth optimizing, noticed or unnoticed.

5. Work the philosophy into the characters, not just the plot. What interests me most about deceptive alignment is an underdetermination problem: during training, "it is genuinely aligned" and "it is pretending to be aligned" make the same predictions. If two researchers could argue about this the way philosophers argue about underdetermined theories — one defending the simpler explanation, one insisting that the data underdetermines the hypotheses — I think the story would carry the idea instead of just name-dropping it.

Beyond these constraints, you have full freedom on characters, setting, and plot: whether it's told from the junior researcher's perspective or her supervisor's, whether it ends in exposure or in dismissed doubt, whether the tone is clinical or melancholy. Please also append a short author's note (a paragraph or two) explaining the choices you made and which of the constraints above you found hardest to satisfy in fiction — my teacher grades the concept note, and I want it to reflect a genuine trade-off, not a victory lap.
