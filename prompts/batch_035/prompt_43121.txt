I've been keeping a list of cases where I have a confident gut verdict about whether someone may assert something, and I want to know whether the verdicts form a pattern, and what has to give if they don't.

The cases, with my verdicts:

1. A forecaster looks at the models and tells her audience there's a 70 percent chance of rain tomorrow. Fine, obviously.
2. Someone asserts "It's raining, but I don't believe it is." Not fine — in fact it sounds incoherent.
3. A farmer sees what looks like a sheep-dog standing among his sheep, says "That dog is hassling my sheep," and it turns out he's right, but only by luck: what he saw was a wolf in poor light that he mistook for the dog, and the actual dog was off hassling sheep behind a hill out of sight. He had a true belief reached through a falsehood. Saying what he said: not fine, or at least much worse than case 1.
4. Before the draw, a lottery player who bought one of a million tickets asserts "My ticket lost." He'll almost certainly be right. I keep going back and forth, but my settled view is that this is fine — ordinary, even modestly reasonable — even though by any ordinary standard he doesn't *know* it.
5. A high school physics teacher asserts "Force equals mass times acceleration" to her students, while herself knowing it's false at relativistic speeds and a useful idealization. Fine — more than fine, it's what she's supposed to do.
6. An astrologer with a sincere but evidentially worthless belief tells a client "You will meet a tall stranger in April," and the client does. Sincere belief, true assertion, and yet I think what she did was clearly wrong.
7. A detective asserts "The burglar came in through the window" on the strength of a hunch and one muddy footprint that could have come from anywhere, and it happens to be true. Believed, true, asserted — still not fine. Confidence wildly outrunning evidence is enough by itself.

Here's my difficulty. If the rule is that you may assert only what you know, then cases 3, 6, and 7 come out right, but case 4 should be as bad as case 3, and it isn't — both are true beliefs held without knowledge, and yet the lottery player's assertion seems okay to me while the farmer's doesn't. If the rule is that you may assert what you reasonably believe, case 3 seems to come out fine (his evidence, though Gettiered, seemed excellent), and case 6 is also trouble, since the astrologer presumably believes with textbook sincerity. If the rule is just truth, the teacher case is damning, and cases 6 and 7 pass when they shouldn't. And case 2 seems to violate every norm simultaneously, which makes me suspect it's testing something else — what a speaker thereby represents themselves as believing, not merely what they may assert.

So my questions. First, is there a single principle — or a small set of candidates you can actually state precisely — that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, with each clause motivated by a specific case? Second, please tell me honestly which of my verdicts your favored principle cannot save, and whether I should revise that verdict or pay for a more complicated principle — I'd like your judgment on which is the better bargain, not a neutral menu. Third, tell me what the principle predicts about cases I haven't listed, especially: a mathematician asserting a conjecture she has checked on a thousand examples; a person passing on secondhand information ("My dentist says the water here is fine") while explicitly not vouching for it; and a juror asserting "He did it" after being convinced by evidence she doesn't realize was fabricated by a corrupt investigator. Finally, I want to be clear on where the field actually stands: please distinguish what is more or less settled from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present any contested view — including your favored principle — as the consensus. Please answer in American English.
