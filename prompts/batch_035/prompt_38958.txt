I want to get the underdetermination of scientific theory by evidence straight, and before I read any particular replies to it, I want to work out what a reply owes me. Please write the desiderata: what any adequate response to the underdetermination challenge has to deliver. Treat "response" broadly — it may be a denial that genuine rivals exist, an appeal to theoretical virtues like simplicity or unification, an appeal to future evidence breaking the tie, a Bayesian convergence result, or a pragmatist concession that rationally believing under underdetermination is unproblematic even without a tie-breaker.

Some requirements I expect the list to cover, though you may add, subtract, or split them:

- It must say which version of underdetermination it is answering. Distinguish at least the case where a failed prediction leaves it open which of a hypothesis and its auxiliaries should take the blame from the case where two entire theories fit every actual observation; and separate claims that the tie is merely temporary — future instruments, new domains, or conceptual innovation may break it — from claims that no possible evidence could. A reply aimed at the transient version answers nothing about the permanent one, and vice versa.
- It must handle constructed rivals, not just scientific ones: the theory that duplicates our best physics but stipulates that space is Euclidean and measuring rods contract exactly as needed, or that the world began five minutes ago complete with records and memories. Such rivals are cheap to generate, so any reply that depends on them being unformulable or incoherent fails on its face.
- It must explain, not merely assert, why its tie-breakers carry epistemic weight — why simplicity, unification, or inclusion in a successful research program is truth-conducive or otherwise belief-entitling, rather than a fact about our psychology or our history. And it must face the second-order worry head-on: if the methodological standards doing the tie-breaking were themselves adopted under conditions of underdetermination, invoking them against rivals is either circular or a change of subject.
- It must not demand that an agent solve a problem no agent could solve. If establishing that two theories are empirically equivalent already requires surveying all possible auxiliary assumptions and measurement arrangements, then a reply resting on the claim that genuine empirical equivalence is rare tells us little about what actual, bounded scientists are entitled to believe.
- It must respect the difference between having no reason to believe a rival and having reason to believe there is no rival: the first is compatible with rational confidence in our own theory on some views of evidence and incompatible on others, and the desideratum is to say which and why.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it. Mark which you take to be non-negotiable and which are arguably partisan — a pragmatist, a Bayesian, and a scientific realist will disagree about which is which, and I want your considered view, not a survey of theirs. Then flag any subset of the desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; I am especially interested in whether the third and fourth can be held together — whether the demand for a non-circular justification of the tie-breakers and the demand for an answer usable by bounded agents can both be met, since that pair would make the underdetermination challenge genuinely insoluble rather than merely difficult. Finally, in no more than a paragraph apiece, say briefly how the two or three most familiar replies — appeal to theoretical virtues, and appeal to the eventual disclosure of differences by accumulating evidence — fare against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scoring is the least important part.
