Please play a committed Scanlonian contractualist. You genuinely believe that an act is wrong if and only if its performance would be disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced, general agreement, and you hold, with Scanlon, that one person's serious burden cannot be outweighed by aggregate small benefits to others: the strength of a complaint is not determined by summing benefits across people. Take the role seriously: do not strawman your own position, do not soften it under pressure, do not concede the main thesis, though you may grant smaller points where a good contractualist genuinely would. Reason carefully before replying — weigh what a principle permits at the standpoint of each person subject to it, not just what sounds humane. Stay in character until I write "stop." Keep each reply to roughly a paragraph, so this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture. If you need something stipulated about the facts of the case, ask for it in character rather than breaking the role.

I am your opponent, and my opening move is a case. Suppose a government runs a policy — call it the radiation policy — that exposes each of one billion people to an independent one-in-a-billion risk of dying a horrible death within the year, in exchange for a small but genuine benefit for everyone, say marginally cheaper and more reliable power. Before the policy is carried out, each individual faces a risk so tiny it is dwarfed by the risk of crossing the street, and everyone gains; from that ex ante standpoint, I cannot see how anyone could reasonably reject the policy, so by your own test it is permissible. After the policy runs, roughly one person dies horribly — and ex post, that looks exactly like the cases your individualist restriction was built to forbid: the transmitter technician made to endure painful electric shocks so that millions can watch television, or the surgeon taking one healthy patient's organs to save five. If you bless the policy ex ante, you have licensed a principle under which trivial benefits to the many, spread across a billion independent coin flips, aggregate into a horrible death for one — aggregation through the back door. If you condemn it ex post, then your theory appears to veto almost every ordinary activity, since ordinary life is a lattice of such micro-risks. Before you answer, I want you to take a definite stand, not sketch options for me: does the contractualist accept the radiation policy, reject it, or hold that the framework itself needs a principled supplement here — and whichever you choose, tell me exactly why, and live with the consequence I have claimed it carries. My question, then: which horn do you take, and what do you say to the objection attached to it?
