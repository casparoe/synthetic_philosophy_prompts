So I'm giving a talk at a biosecurity workshop in a few weeks, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. Figured I'd ask you now rather than panic the night before.

The audience: roughly a third journal editors who got burned during the whole gain-of-function / H5N1 transmissibility publication fights back in the early 2010s and have since overcorrected toward openness on principle, a third straight-up open-science people who think any pre-publication gatekeeping is just elites hoarding knowledge, and a couple of philosphers of science who are mainly there to be skeptical of everyone. No decision theorists, nobody who already thinks in terms of information hazards — if I open with the jargon I'll lose them at the title slide.

My thesis, in two parts. First, the unilateralist's curse framing, but with a twist I haven't seen anyone push: the standard story says that when n people can each independently act on some information, and the group's median judgment is that acting is bad, the group's actual behavior tracks its least cautious member — fine. But the part I want to stress is that the size of the problem grows with the *variance* in caution across the group, not the average. Adding one careful person to a group doesn't help at all if it also adds spread; what matters is the gap between the most reckless member and the median. So as fields get bigger and more international and more interdisciplinary, the curse gets worse for a reason that has nothing to do with anyone getting more reckless. Second, the actually controversial bit: the standard remedy — "more dialogue, discuss it within the community before anyone acts" — can make things worse in the worst case, because discussion is itself a channel. It's precisely how the least cautious member finds out what the cautious ones were sitting on, and often how they find out how to get it. So the fix has to be structural, and my candidate is staged access through something like an information escrow — a neutral holder who releases only when a fixed quorum of designated reviewers agrees — because I claim virtue-based norms ("be responsible, ask around first") predictably fail: everyone rates themselves near the median of their field, the reckless included.

What I need from you: about eight objections I'll actually get from *this* crowd — not the ones an EA/LessWrong audience would raise, these people will come at me from the openness side — ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way an open-science advocate or a burned editor would actually put it at a microphone, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Also: the question I'm hoping nobody asks. I suspect for me it's "who exactly gets to sit in your reviewer quorum," because any answer I give sounds like exactly the gatekeeping they hate, but flag it if you think there's a worse one lurking in the woodwork. And tell me which objection I should just concede up front instead of fighting, because winning it would cost me the room — I'd rather give one away cheaply than die on a hill about, like, peer review being a public good. That one always goes badly.
