I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, and my roommate (he does RL research) has an argument I can't find my way out of. Context: I was complaining about tuning a PID controller at work last week and somehow we ended up on whether my HVAC feels anything. His argument, numbered as best I can:

(1) What gives a being moral status is its capacity for valenced experience — that things can feel good or bad to it. (2) That capacity comes in degrees: a chimp has more of it than a chicken, a chicken more than a bee, roughly tracking how richly the organism integrates signals with memory, learning, and motivation. (3) If the capacity comes in degrees, moral status comes in degrees too — a being with a smaller degree matters less, but as long as its degree is nonzero, its weight is nonzero. (4) There is no sharp line anywhere in the animal kingdom: bee to beetle to nematode, the complexity just fades continuously, so there's no principled place to say "below this, nothing matters." (5) In his terms, a fly's nociception is a negative feedback loop: detect a deviation from a setpoint, weight it, trigger corrective action. A thermostat is also a negative feedback loop: detect deviation, trigger correction. The difference between a fly and a thermostat is one of degree — more layers, more integration, maybe learning — not one of kind. (6) So by (3) and (4), the thermostat has nonzero moral status: vanishingly small, a trillion-trillionth of a mosquito's maybe, but not zero. (7) Nonzero weights aggregate: tiny harms to enormous numbers of beings sum, and nonzero times astronomically large is still astronomically large. (8) Therefore: we owe something to thermostats; all the mites and nematodes together swamp human welfare so completely that human suffering is a rounding error; and mowing the lawn, which shreds countless tiny arthropods, is a moral catastrophe that outweighs failing to pull a kid out of a pond.

I'm confident (8) is false — I'm not going to feel guilty about my thermostat, and I'm not going to tell anyone that mowing the lawn is worse than letting a child drown. But each step sounded fine when he said it, and when I push back on the thermostat he says that's exactly what people said about insects sixty years ago, and that if I want to exclude thermostats I need to draw a line somewhere in the continuum, and any line I draw is arbitrary — which by his lights means the continuum carries all the way down to the feedback loop in my temperature controller. I suspect something fishy happens between (5) and (6), or maybe in how (7) gets used, but I can't name it.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher who works on moral status and animal ethics. Think carefully and maybe brainstorm a bit before committing: walk me through which step or steps to reject, why each of them looked fine (he's not an idiot, each one has something going for it), and what rejecting it costs — what else I give up or have to bite. If more than one repair is available, lay out the options and tell me which bullet is smallest. For what it's worth, I'd really like to keep (2), the degrees idea — it seems obviously right to me that a chimp matters more than a chicken matters more than a bee — and I'm not moving on (8) being false. If the argument turns out to be valid and something in (8) has to be softened, tell me honestly which part.
