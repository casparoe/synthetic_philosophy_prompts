I've held a fairly strong view about moral trade for over a decade now, picked up from the LessWrong/EA Forum ecosystem and never seriously shaken, and I'd like a rigorous map of what could actually break it.

The view, as I hold it, has three tiers. Ground tier: the near-truism that agents with divergent values can usually do better trading than fighting -- if your votes and mine cancel on both factory-farming reform and on something you care far more about, we should each abstain in the other's domain and each come out ahead. Second tier: moral uncertainty reproduces this logic inside a single head. If there's a decent chance my true values are your values, then even with no trading partner in sight, the rational move is a compromise portfolio that hedges across my candidate value systems, weighted by credence. Third tier, and the part I actually care about: the same reasoning is supposed to reach agents no contract can ever bind. Under decision theories that care about evidential or logical correlations rather than only causal ones, I have counterparts in other possible worlds (or branches, or simulation runs -- the substrate doesn't matter to the argument) whose values I find repugnant, including values I expect to dominate futures after mine loses. A rational agent whose value system is destined to be a small minority, on this view, secures more of what it cares about by building successors that honor cross-world deals -- by giving the winners some of what they want, in exchange for the winners doing likewise -- than by grabbing whatever it can.

So the concrete predictions I take myself to be committed to: that a hopeless minority value system should nonetheless spend real resources on cooperation with the value systems that will inherit other futures; that agents under moral uncertainty should visibly hedge rather than act on their favorite moral hypothesis; that compromise is available even where no enforcement mechanism exists.

Tell me what would count against this. I want more than "causal decision theorists will deny the third tier" -- I know that, and I want to know how much of the view survives losing that tier. The objections I'm aware of but haven't seen ranked: that trade presupposes interpersonal comparability of welfare or value that may simply not exist, so "giving them some of what they want" is ill-defined for sufficiently alien value functions; that the bargaining problem lacks a privileged solution, so logically correlated agents with different values have no determinate deal to converge on and will disagree about the fair point on the frontier as bitterly as uncorrelated ones fight; that the correlations the whole scheme runs on are unconditionable by bounded agents like us, making the third tier true but unactionable -- a matter for theology rather than decision-making; and that the second tier quietly collapses, because a compromise across my candidate value systems is just what expected-value maximization under moral uncertainty already tells me to do, in which case "moral trade" adds nothing and the distinctive content of the view lives entirely in the tier I can least test.

Rank the defeaters from fatal through serious through merely awkward. Say which of them the view's more careful defenders -- the people writing about superrationality and multiverse-wide cooperation in the decision-theory-adjacent literature -- would themselves concede as real pressure, versus which they would wave off, and whether the waving-off is principled or motivated. Assess honestly whether the view has been insulated by qualifications until it can barely lose: it now lives mostly in possible worlds I can't observe, with deals whose terms no one can specify, counterparties whose existence I can't confirm. Is that a fatal defect for a view of this kind, or just what adequate generality looks like at this level of abstraction -- and what, if anything, does the view predict differently from causal-decision-theoretic unilateralism or from straightforward maximization under one's best-guess ethics? If the answer is "nothing observable," I want to hear it said plainly rather than papered over.
