Space agencies treat the contamination of other worlds as a serious problem: under the Outer Space Treaty's contamination provisions, missions to Mars and Europa are assigned planetary-protection categories, spacecraft are assembled in clean rooms, components are cleaned or heat-treated to reduce their microbial load, and mission designs are sometimes constrained — lander sites chosen, drilling depth limited, hardware abandoned rather than moved — specifically to keep Earth microbes away from places where native life might exist or be findable. The stated rationale is partly scientific: a contaminated Mars is a worse object of study. But the requirements have grown more demanding in proportion to how promising a site looks for life, which suggests something beyond instrument hygiene is doing the work. Meanwhile, the same institutions and much of the interested public treat the eventual human settlement of Mars as a long-term aspiration, even though human settlement makes sterility impossible — we are walking ecosystems — and beyond settlement sits terraforming, the deliberate conversion of a planet into a habitat, which would extinguish or radically displace whatever native biosphere might be there.

What theory of value does the practice of planetary protection presuppose? Candidates worth distinguishing: that microbial life has some kind of moral status; that pristine or uncontaminated conditions have a value of their own, aesthetic or integrity-based; a precautionary principle about irreversibly destroying something whose significance we cannot yet assess; and the deflationary reading that it is purely methodological, about clean instruments and good science. Which of these, if any, can explain the observed shape of the practice — its severity, its site-sensitivity, and its tolerance for the risk that launch failures themselves deposit contaminated debris on Mars? And is the practice consistent, over time, with endorsing settlement and terraforming, or does it presuppose a view of value that those later projects violate? Please distinguish what planetary protection strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined such that several rival theories would license it as it stands, and say what the practice would have to look like if each of the main candidate views were the operative one. Throughout, please separate what is settled — the empirical facts about what the practice is, and any points of genuine agreement among people who have thought about it — from what is genuinely contested, such as the moral status of microbes or the value of pristine environments, and do not present any particular resolution of those contested questions as if it were the consensus.
