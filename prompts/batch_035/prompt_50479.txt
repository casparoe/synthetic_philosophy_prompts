I'm an ML researcher whose daily vocabulary is policies, demonstrations, behavioral cloning, distribution shift, reward inference, spurious features, and sample efficiency; I also work on post-training for language models, so few-shot learning from examples and imitation from demonstrations are things I think about professionally. I've been reading about the ancient idea that one learns to be good by reading biographies, and I want it translated into my terms. On one side there is Plutarch's project in the Parallel Lives: Greek and Roman figures are deliberately paired, each pair followed by a formal comparison essay; Plutarch privileges small anecdotes — a remark at dinner, a gesture, how a man treated a servant — over battles and treaties, on the theory that the anecdotes carry the characterological signal; and the whole apparatus is aimed at the reader's own moral formation, the reader being moved to emulate what is admirable and recoil from what is not. On the other side there is Christian hagiography: the saint's life as an idealized, typological pattern in which the saint relives the acts of Christ or of earlier saints, sins only before conversion, works miracles, and exhibits no development or doubt after the turning point — read not as historical information but as a template for imitation. Translate the moral-learning theory implicit in these two practices into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can and without smuggling in my framework's assumptions and calling them translation: the paired lives and comparison essays as some kind of controlled contrast or ablation; the privileging of anecdote over event as a claim about where the signal lives and what is confounder; emulation as something that had better not turn out to be behavioral cloning, since Plutarch's reader is supposed to end up with a character, not a copy of Cato's policy; and the hagiographic life as a training corpus with specific, diagnosable properties — no variance across examples, no negative labels, no demonstrated recovery from failure, and a nontrivial fraction of off-distribution events. Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want: what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all; and for each piece of residue, take a view on whether it points to something my vocabulary is missing — perhaps a form of moral formation that isn't inference from examples under any description — or to something the biographical tradition never made precise in the first place. I have a candidate hypothesis I want stress-tested: that hagiography fails as moral training data for exactly the reason a perfect demonstrator fails in imitation learning — the learner's state distribution is never covered, because a sinner mid-fall has no demonstrated policy for that state — while Plutarch's flawed figures and his habit of pairing a great man with a comparable but lesser one exist precisely to cover that part of the space. Tell me whether that is a faithful rendering of a real difference between the two practices or a distortion I have introduced, and if it is faithful, say what it implies about which tradition actually had the better theory of learning from lives.
