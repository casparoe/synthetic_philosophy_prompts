As a software engineer with no background in philosophy, I've ended up in a running disagreement with a colleague on my team. We both work on a content-moderation pipeline, and we were discussing how the system should treat an ethnic slur that appeared in user comments alongside genuinely abusive material. My colleague waved the whole issue away with the line "it's just a word," and argued that since the slur is just another string of characters, the pipeline shouldn't treat it any differently from any other token — no special handling, no extra weight in the classifier. The debate stalled there, and I couldn't put my finger on what bothered me about it.

Digging around afterward, I gathered that people who study language for a living actually disagree about where a slur's sting lives: some hold that its offensiveness is baked into its literal meaning, others that the literal meaning is roughly that of a neutral counterpart and the damage is done by what the utterance communicates in context — what it implies or presupposes about the target group, given who is speaking and to whom. That made me suspect that "it's just a word" isn't one claim but a bundle of very different ones, some of which might be true, some contentious, and some straightforwardly false.

Could you sort this out for me? Lay out, as a list I could actually take back to my colleague, the distinct precise theses that "it's just a word" could be expressing in this kind of debate — from the nearly trivial reading on which it's obviously correct (a slur is a single lexical item, like "spoon" is), through stronger claims about its semantics, to the radical reading in which it's meant to show the word can't do any harm at all. For each reading, say whether it's plausible, and, crucially, whether it would actually support the conclusion my colleague was drawing from it — that a moderation system should treat slurs like any other word. I'd especially like to know whether the readings on which the slogan is true are the same readings that would do the work his argument needed, or whether he was, in effect, resting a strong claim on the trivial one.
