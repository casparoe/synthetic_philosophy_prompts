So I'm a novelist and I've gotten myself in way over my head, philosophically speaking. The novel I'm working on follows a woman who accidentally becomes immortal in 1897 and is still around today, and as I've been drafting the middle chapters I've realized that her whole arc hinges on a question I don't actually know the answer to: is death bad *for the person who dies*? She watches everyone she loves die, and I've been writing that as a tragedy, but then I stumbled onto the old Epicurean line of thought that death can't harm you because when death is, you are not — there's no subject left to be harmed, no date on which the bad thing happens to anyone. And now I'm second-guessing every scene I've written about funerals.

I've also read a bit about the standard modern reply, the "deprivation" view — that death is bad not because of what it does to you but because of all the good life it robs you of — plus the Lucretius-style symmetry argument that if posthumous nonexistence is no harm, then prenatal nonexistence shouldn't be either, and yet nobody mourns the time before they were born. My narrator would actually be well positioned to appreciate that one, since she's got a whole lot of past behind her and none ahead. But I keep finding counterexamples cutting both ways: what about someone who dies after a full, complete life (did the deprivation really harm them?), or a death that's preferable to continued suffering, or the fact that we grieve people for decades, which suggests we don't really believe death only matters to the living.

What I need from you is an essay that takes a real stance, because my character needs a position to argue with, and honestly so do I. Weigh the Epicurean denial against the deprivationist response and the symmetry argument, maybe touch on whether immortality would actually dissolve the problem or just relocate it (does someone with infinite time still get harmed by death, or does death become impossible to even define for her?), and then commit to a verdict. Don't give me a "both sides have merit" ending — my editor already accused me of doing that in the last book. Also please don't just list a bunch of books and papers for me to go read, I want actual arguments I can work with, written out in full, since I'll be having my character riff on them during a long train scene.
