I spent thirty-five years as a fish physiologist, most of them measuring cortisol and ventilatory responses in stressed salmonids, and since retiring I have been working through the philosophy of biology and animal ethics literature — Braithwaite, the sentience debates, the precautionary principle — with the enthusiasm of a convert and, I hope, the habits of someone who spent a career insisting on evidence. That background is why our certification scheme asked me onto its scientific advisory panel, where we decide which welfare standards a farm must meet to carry the label. I now find myself unable to tell what principle our own precedents actually instantiate, and I would like your help before our most consequential case yet.

Our precedents, in the order we set them:

1. Atlantic salmon and rainbow trout farms: full standards — electrical or percussive stunning before slaughter, stocking-density caps, continuous water-quality monitoring, minimal feed withdrawal before killing.
2. Oyster and mussel operations: no welfare standards whatsoever; the panel judged the evidence of sentience too weak for anything to protect.
3. A prospective octopus farm (never certified, but we issued guidance): we said any certified operation would face standards roughly comparable to the salmon ones, citing learning performance and behavioral responses to noxious stimuli.
4. A crab processor holding animals live before cooking: we required that no crab be dismembered or boiled while alive — the nerve centers must be destroyed first — but we declined to impose density, handling, or analgesia requirements, reasoning that the evidence for finer-grained welfare needs in decapods was thin and the costs to the processor were not.
5. We have never ruled on shrimp.

The new case is an application from a very large whiteleg shrimp operation. The practices at issue: slaughter by immersion in ice slurry or simple asphyxiation in air, which can leave animals sensible for minutes to hours; eyestalk ablation of breeding females, performed without anesthesia, to accelerate maturation; and very high stocking densities. For the first time there is a practical alternative on the slaughter side, since electrical stunning equipment designed for shrimp has recently become commercially available and is being trialed by some producers, at nontrivial cost. By individual animal count this is the largest aquatic farming sector in the world, orders of magnitude beyond salmon. Meanwhile, the evidence base on nociception and motivational trade-offs in decapods is stronger than it was when we heard the crab case, though it remains contested, and our jurisdiction has recently recognized decapod crustaceans as sentient in statute.

Please do three things. First, articulate at least three candidate rules or principles that would each render our precedents jointly consistent — for instance a graded neural-complexity threshold, an evidence-of-pain threshold, and a precautionary rule constrained by feasibility and scale — and check each honestly against all four precedents, including the asymmetry between the octopus and crab rulings. Second, tell me what the panel should decide on the shrimp application if it wants to remain consistent with itself: address the slaughter method, the eyestalk ablation, and the densities as separate components, since the rules may not converge on all three, and identify which precedent is the closest analog for each. Be explicit about what principle we would be committing ourselves to with each ruling, since whatever we decide here will surely be cited back to us. Third, name the case we have not yet heard — an animal, a practice, or a sector — that would best discriminate among the candidate rules, and say what each rule predicts for it, so the panel knows in advance what it is really deciding.
