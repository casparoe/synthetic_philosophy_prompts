My friend and I spent the whole evening arguing about Clever Hans, and I want the argument adjudicated properly rather than left where we abandoned it. The facts, as I take them to be: in Berlin in the early 1900s, Wilhelm von Osten, a gymnasium mathematics teacher, exhibited a horse that appeared to do arithmetic and answer questions by tapping its hoof; a commission of thirteen, put together by Carl Stumpf, concluded in 1904 that no trickery was involved, and it was Oskar Pfungst, working afterward, who showed that the horse answered correctly only when the questioner knew the answer in advance and the horse could see the questioner — when either condition failed, the performance collapsed almost entirely. My friend reads the episode as simple: there was no intelligence there, only the illusion of it, and Pfungst's result is exactly what entitles us to withhold the word. I think that is too quick, and Pfungst's own data point the other way: the horse was doing something cognitively serious, detecting postural cues so subtle that Pfungst needed laboratory tests on human subjects to confirm they existed at all, so the episode looks less like the discovery of an absence than like the discovery of a competence in the wrong place — distributed across the horse, the questioner, and their interaction rather than located in the horse alone. My friend's rejoinder is that this stretches the concept until it does no work: on my reading, every animal that reads cues counts as intelligent, and a concept that covers everything explains nothing. Please analyze the episode through the lens of theories of intelligence, saying explicitly which ones you are using and being disciplined about the mapping: what counts as the system whose intelligence is at issue — the horse, the horse–questioner pair, or the loop between them — what the task was under each reading, what would have counted as success, and where each theory is silent rather than forced to speak. Then tell me what the episode shows about the concept of intelligence itself: does it confirm the received concept, expose an anomaly in it, or present a case the concept never anticipated? And say honestly which of us, if either, the analysis favors. I am aware the episode has become a stock parable in machine learning, where models exploiting unintended cues in benchmarks are routinely called Clever Hans cases; I would rather you took the historical case seriously on its own terms than reached for the parable. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of my question.
