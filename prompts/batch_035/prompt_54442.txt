I am a machine learning engineer who spends a good deal of time thinking about symmetry in learned systems: weight spaces in which permuting hidden units yields functionally identical networks, equivariant architectures, losses defined only on the quotient of parameter space by the symmetry group. I have read just enough philosophy of physics to be intrigued by the hole argument, but not enough to trust my own grasp of it. Please restate the argument, in its modern Earman–Norton formulation against manifold substantivalism, in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can, and then report what does not survive the translation.

For orientation, here is my tentative mapping, which I would like you to correct where it distorts. Manifold substantivalism looks to me like the refusal to quotient: the claim that two field configurations related by a diffeomorphism are genuinely distinct models of the world, in the way that two weight vectors related by a unit permutation are genuinely distinct points of parameter space even though they compute the same function. Leibniz equivalence looks like the opposite commitment: that the true hypothesis space is the quotient space, and that the diffeomorphism is a gauge redundancy in exactly my sense — surplus structure with no observable or dynamical signature. And the hole diffeomorphism itself — the one that is the identity up to a time slice and nontrivial afterward — looks like a gauge transformation that only acts on the future, which is precisely the situation that makes me say there is no fact of the matter about which representative of the orbit reality occupies.

Where I suspect the translation strains is on the argument's official engine, determinism. As I understand it, the substantivalist is in trouble because the same initial data on a slice, evolved under the same field equations, give rise to multiple distinct futures — the identity outside the hole, the dragged-along fields inside it. In my world, if the same state run through the same dynamics produces different outputs, I reach for a bug or an unseeded random source, not an ontology. So tell me what exactly the determinism premise is doing in the argument: is it an independent commitment the substantivalist is caught violating, or is it just the device that makes the gauge character of the diffeomorphism visible, so that someone who already accepts quotienting never feels the force of it?

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the debate map cleanly onto gauge symmetry as I know it? Which parts had to be distorted to fit? And which have no counterpart at all? Two residues I specifically want addressed. First, the epistemic one: the substantivalist is said to be committed to unverifiable facts — which point of the manifold plays which role — and this sounds like my no-fact-of-the-matter-about-the-orbit-representative until I notice that in my case the quotienting is something I imposed for engineering reasons, whereas the philosopher seems to think something in the world forces it. Is there anything to that difference, or is it just that physicists and I have better reasons than the substantivalist for the same stipulation? Second, the metaphysical one: whether spacetime points have identity over and above their field-theoretic roles, which I want to compare to asking whether hidden units have identity over and above their role in the computed function — except that my units are artifacts I stipulated into existence and spacetime points are not. Finally, tell me your view on the residue as a whole: does it point to something my vocabulary cannot say, or to something in the philosophers' debate that is less substantive than it looks?
