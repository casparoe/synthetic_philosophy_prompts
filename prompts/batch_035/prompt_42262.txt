I teach a third-year module on utilitarianism and its critics, and I'm setting the three-hour exam. I want one essay question on the separateness of persons objection, but not the standard "explain Rawls's point about treating persons as separate" prompt, because anyone who attended the lectures can pad that out for 90 minutes without understanding anything.

Here's the twist I want built in. It seems to me the objection is ambiguous between two readings: on one, it's a complaint about distribution — you can't treat one person's loss as cancelled out by another's gain, so strict summing is out, but a weighted sum like prioritarianism might squeak through; on the other, it's a complaint about aggregation as such — there is simply no such entity as the total of well-being across distinct centres of consciousness. And a colleague of mine presses a nasty follow-up on the second reading: why doesn't it apply within a single life too? Me-now and me-in-2040 are also seperate points of view, distinct in every way that matters except for psychological connectedness — and that connectedness comes in degrees and fades with enough years. So either the objection overreaches into absurdity (it becomes wrong to trade off my Tuesday against my decade), or it quietly collapses back into the distributional reading and admits weighted aggregation after all. I want a question that forces candidates straight into that squeeze instead of letting them coast on either Rawls or Parrit name-dropping.

So, deliverables. Write the question itself, self-contained enough that a candidate who never took my Parfit seminar can still attempt it (no relying on chapter numbers or anything like that). Then a model answer of about 700 words that actually takes a side rather than surveying — I genuinely don't care which side, only that it commits to something and pays the costs of it. Then a marking scheme out of 20 with five must-hit points and the marks each is worth, plus a one-line note per point on how students typically flub it. And tell me one thing you'd deliberately leave off the marking scheme so that memorising the famous names earns nothing on its own.
