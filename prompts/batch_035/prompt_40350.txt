I'm a backend engineer, zero philosophy training, but I keep hitting decisions at work and in life where my gut has opinions about the *kind* of decision it is, and I want to know if there's an actual principle in there somewhere. I vaguely remember reading that some philosophers think certain values just can't be traded off against each other on one scale, and that seems to be what my gut is tracking, but I never followed up on it.

Here are my verdicts on a bunch of decisions. What I want from you: is there a principle that captures all of them, and where they clash, tell me which verdict has to go. Also tell me what the principle says about cases I haven't listed.

1. My team has to choose between rewriting a crusty service properly versus hacking on it to ship a feature now. Six months of pain later versus angry customers today. I think there IS a real answer here, even though it's hard -- it's just an information problem, you can reason about the expected pain on both sides.

2. Choosing between two job offers: one pays a lot more, the other has a team I actually like and a product I believe in. I flip-flop endlessly, and I've come around to thinking there's no fact of the matter about which is better for me. And that's not a sad thing, it's just how it is -- the money and the team stuff don't line up on any scale.

3. When a choice is like (2), I feel totally fine deciding it with some arbitrary tiebreak -- I once literally picked an offer based on which office had better coffee. But I'd feel gross doing a tiebreak on (1), the rewrite decision, even at the moment where I've decided the considerations are perfectly balanced.

4. If I take the money job and end up miserable, I regret the outcome. If I take the team job and stay poor, I regret that too. But I think I'm allowed to be at peace with the *choice* either way -- no answer for it, nothing to answer *for*. Whereas if the hack in (1) blows up in prod, I feel like I do have to answer for the choice itself, not just eat the bad outcome. Not sure why these feel so different to me.

5. My manager likes converting everything to money ("the rewrite costs 3 engineer-months, the hack costs X in incidents over a year") and I think that's basically fine as a decision aid for (1) but feels fake and like it's losing something important when applied to (2).

6. One I keep going back and forth on: naming a product. On one hand it's pure vibes, tiebreak-worthy, coffee-test territory. On the other hand the name is with you forever, and once it's chosen you can't even imagine having picked the other one. Does that make it a "real" choice or not? My verdict keeps flipping and it bugs me.

7. Edge case: whether to let go of an underperforming teammate who's also a friend, versus keeping them and having the team absorb the cost. I genuinely can't decide whether this is type-(1) (real answer, hard to compute) or type-(2) (no fact of the matter, tiebreak allowed). My intuition here keeps flip-flopping depending on how I frame it, which makes me distrust my classifications above.

So: is there a single principle -- something like, decisions where everything fits on one scale versus decisions where it doesn't -- that gets all of these? If not, which is the weaker link? And what does it predict about (7)? Ideally the principle is precise enough that I could apply it to a new decision tomorrow, not just vibes about vibes.

Also, please be explicit about how confident you are in each part of the answer, and single out the one point you'd least want to bet money on -- I'd rather know where the ground is soft than get a clean story.
