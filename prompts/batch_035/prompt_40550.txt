I'm a graduate student in bioethics writing a term paper on a practice that has quietly become routine in reproductive medicine, and I've gotten stuck on what the practice assumes about who a person is. The practice: when a family has a child with leukemia (or another condition treatable only by a transplant of bone marrow or umbilical-cord blood), clinics can combine IVF with preimplantation genetic testing — create several embryos, test each for tissue compatibility with the sick child, and transfer only a compatible one, so that the resulting child, a so-called savior sibling, can later donate. Ethics bodies in several countries have signed off on this, and the argument they lean on is striking: the chosen child cannot have been wronged by being selected for compatibility, because absent the selection it would never have existed at all; a life that includes a donation request is presumably still a life worth having, and nobody else was harmed. The claim that the child 'would never have existed otherwise' seems to assume that a child grown from a different embryo in the same family — or even from the same embryo, transferred for some unrelated reason — would not have been this child. That looks close to the view philosophers discuss under the heading of origin essentialism: the idea that a person could not have come from a different sperm and egg. But I'm not sure how much of that the licensing of the practice really needs, and that's the first thing I'd like you to work out: reconstruct the implicit theory of personal identity, and of harm, that the justification rests on; say whether it strictly presupposes origin essentialism or whether weaker alternatives (a genetic-relation view, a narrative account of identity, something else) could support the same conclusion; and tell me what happens to the justification if origin essentialism is false — does the 'would not have existed' defense collapse, and if so, could the practice be re-justified along entirely different lines, or would it need to be reassessed from the ground up?

There's also an apparent inconsistency I keep stumbling on, and I'd like you to address it directly. The very same committees that accept tissue-matching selection tend to reject or heavily restrict embryo selection for other, non-medical traits — sex, say, or expected height — even though the non-identity defense applies there too: a child selected for those traits equally 'would not have existed otherwise,' and presumably also benefits from existing. So the two halves of the practice seem to invoke the same identity assumptions but reach opposite verdicts. Is the line being drawn consistent with the theory that licenses savior siblings, or does the practice as a whole rest on incompatible presuppositions — for instance, one view of what a future person is when the selection is medical, and a different view when it is not? If the halves really do pull in different directions, say which one would have to give, and sketch what a coherent policy would look like — including whether it would be stricter about savior siblings, more permissive about trait selection, or reorganized around some distinction neither half currently articulates.
