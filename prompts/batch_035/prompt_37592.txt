My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about Newton's bucket, and by the end we had stopped arguing about Newton and started arguing about each other's intuitions -- which motions are real facts about the world and which are merely facts about how things stand relative to each other. Since then I have been trying to work out whether my own verdicts on a series of cases form any kind of coherent position, and I have come to suspect they do not. I would like help finding the principle behind them, if one exists.

Here are my verdicts, as firmly as I can state them:

1. Newton's bucket, the standard setup: a bucket of water hangs from a rope, the bucket is spun, and after a while the water spins with it and climbs the sides. The water's surface changes shape; nothing about the water-to-bucket distance explains it. I think the rotation is a genuine fact about the world, not a fact about the water's motion relative to the bucket or to anything else nearby.

2. Newton's two globes: two globes connected by a cord, alone in an otherwise empty universe, revolving around their common midpoint. The cord is taut, and it is taut because they revolve. I find this completely compelling: there is a fact about whether they rotate, and the tension in the cord tells you what it is.

3. A single particle alone in an otherwise empty universe, described as rotating. I think this is not even meaningful -- there is no fact of the matter. Nothing could distinguish its "rotating" from its standing still.

4. Two spaceships coasting past each other at constant velocity in an otherwise empty universe. I think there is no fact about which one is moving and which is at rest; the situation is exactly symmetric. Uniform motion is only motion relative to something.

5. The universe as a whole, every body in it rotating together, so that nothing changes any of its relative distances or directions -- a cosmic version of the bucket where everything spins with everything else. Here I want to say there is no fact of the matter, because nothing inside could ever tell. But I can feel this one dragging against verdict 2.

6. The everyday anchors: a Foucault pendulum's plane slowly turning convinces me the Earth really rotates, not merely rotates relative to the pendulum. And when I stand on a moving merry-go-round and am flung outward while my friend on the ground feels nothing, I take it that one of us is really rotating and the situation is not symmetric the way the two passing spaceships are.

7. A variant I thought of during the argument: two bodies alone in an otherwise empty universe, held at a fixed distance by a slack cord, with no tension in it at all. Here my intuition flips, and I find myself wanting to say there is still a fact about whether the pair rotates, even with nothing to detect it.

My tentative principle, offered half-seriously: uniform motion is merely relative, while rotation and acceleration generally are absolute facts, not facts about relations to other bodies. This gets 1, 2, 4, and 6. But it appears to give the wrong answer on 3 and 5, and I do not know what to say about 7. My friend pushed a rival view throughout -- that rotation just is motion relative to the total mass of the universe, distant stars included -- which seemed to handle 3 and 5 neatly, until I pointed out that it makes verdict 2 impossible: if rotation were only rotation relative to other masses, then two globes alone in an empty universe could not revolve at all, and the cord could never go taut. Yet it does. He had no answer to that, and I gloated more than I should have.

What I would like from you: a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough to apply mechanically to new cases. For each clause, tell me which verdict above motivates it. Then run it on cases I have not listed, including at least these: the two bodies of verdict 7 but with the cord removed entirely; a universe in which everything rotates together except one single star, which stands still; and a planet exactly like Earth, with the same day-night cycle, but in a universe containing only that planet, where I want to know whether it still makes sense to say a day takes twenty-four hours. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts no workable principle can save: I would rather overturn one of my own verdicts than pretend they all fit together, but I want to know exactly what I am giving up and why that one rather than another. If you think the right move is to distinguish several different things I have been calling "rotation" under one word, say so and draw the distinctions.
