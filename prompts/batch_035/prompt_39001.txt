I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of science, and we have spent three weeks on the structural realism literature, ending in an impasse that I now suspect is not an artifact of my teaching. The debate between epistemic and ontic versions of the view has been running since the late 1980s, and although both camps publish steadily, almost nobody seems to switch sides. The epistemic structural realist, following Worrall's 1989 paper on the best of both worlds, holds that mature theories get the structure of the unobservable realm right while remaining silent or agnostic about the natures of the objects that instantiate that structure -- the standard illustration being the way the equations of Fresnel's wave theory of light survived into Maxwell's theory even as the ether they were about was abandoned. The ontic structural realist responds that this half-realism is unstable: once you press on what it could mean to know the structure of a domain while knowing nothing about the things the structure relates, you are pushed either back toward standard entity realism or forward to the claim that structure is all there is -- that objects are, at bottom, mere nodes whose identity is fixed by the relations they bear. My seminar noticed, and I confess I agree, that when the ontic side spells this out with examples from quantum mechanics or spacetime physics, the epistemic side typically says it accepts the physics and rejects only the metaphysical overlay; and when the epistemic side retreats to Ramsey-sentence formulations and first-order structure, the ontic side says that this is either trivialism or else a covert object-talk smuggled back in.

Here is my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two camps are not disagreeing about one question but answering two different questions under a single name. The epistemic structural realist is answering a question in the epistemology of science -- what is the strongest claim about the unobservable world that the historical record of theory change actually warrants -- while the ontic structural realist is answering a question in metaphysics -- what does contemporary physics, taken at face value, require objects to be. If that is right, the debate is not substantive disagreement but a near-collision, with each side mistaking the other's indifference toward its question for a denial of its answer. Yet I can also see a rival diagnosis: that there is a genuine substantive dispute, namely about whether object-neutral structure-talk can even be formulated without presupposing objects, with Newman's old objection against Russell -- that any claim to know only structure collapses into triviality once the cardinality of the domain is fixed -- serving as the empirical test case, since it threatens the epistemic version and pushes people toward the ontic one for reasons that have nothing to do with quantum physics.

I want a diagnosis rather than a survey. Tell me which of my two readings of the impasse is better supported, or whether some third diagnosis -- a verbal dispute about the word "object," a disagreement about standards of what counts as a metaphysical commitment, a difference over which historical episodes count as the data (Fresnel-to-Maxwell on one side, permutation-invariance arguments from quantum mechanics on the other) -- better fits what the participants actually say. And then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a formulation of structure that is provably object-neutral, a worked case where the two camps' verdicts visibly diverge, a dissolution that shows one question to be ill-posed, or nothing at all -- with an honest account of why nothing should be expected if that is your conclusion. I should add that in my seminar both sides happily claim Poincaré as a precursor, which either supports the verbal-dispute reading or shows that he changed his mind, or shows that historical precursors are doing no argumentative work here at all; feel free to use that as a datum if it is one.
