I'm a novelist drafting a character — a woman in her fifties, a former union organizer newly elected to her mid-sized city's council — who thinks of herself as a Millian liberal, and I want her to be coherent enough that a careful reader can't dismiss her as a bundle of convenient positions, but realistic enough that she holds her views with the confidence of someone who has never once had them audited. Here is what I've fixed as her creed so far:

1. She takes the harm principle as a complete account of legitimate coercion: restricting an adult's liberty is justified only to prevent harm to others. An adult's own good — physical, moral, or economic — is never a sufficient reason, and neither is the distress her conduct causes to onlookers.
2. She is consistent enough on this point that she votes against helmet and seatbelt laws and defends an adult's right to sell their labor on ruinous terms, and it costs her politically.
3. She nonetheless fought hard to renew the city's ban on payday lenders, which she describes as predatory rather than paternalistic.
4. She wants to ban conversion therapy even for adults who seek it out voluntarily.
5. On speech, she believes no one has a right to be shielded from insult — she would permit an inflammatory march down the main street — but she voted for an ordinance banning targeted picketing outside private residences.
6. And she believes that a coordinated public campaign to destroy a named private individual — publishing her past, calling on everyone to shun her, with no falsehoods and no threats anywhere in it — should be legally actionable, though when pressed she cannot say under what heading.

My questions are these: do the six hang together, and where they do not, is the incoherence one my character would have noticed, or one she could plausibly have papered over? In particular, I would like to know which pairs are genuinely in tension and which merely look like it, and what the cheapest revision would be that leaves her recognizably the person I've written. Two requests about how you answer. First, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this: as I understand it, there are live disagreements about what Mill meant by harm, about whether his own concessions to public decency and provocation break the principle he announced, and about whether he ever intended it as a complete theory of legitimate coercion rather than one weighty consideration among several — so mark clearly which parts of your audit depend on a contested reading rather than the mainstream one, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus. Second, please rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything equally; I need to know which single revision would clean up the most, because I can only show my character revising one thing on the page.
