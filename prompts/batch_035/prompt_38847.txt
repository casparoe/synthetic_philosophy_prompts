Act as a philosopher of history with a working knowledge of historiography and of the demarcation problem as it arises in pseudoarchaeology and pseudohistory. Analyze the nineteenth-century identification and excavation of Troy at Hisarlik as a case study in demarcating pseudohistory from legitimate historical inquiry. The salient facts, which you may take as given: Frank Calvert, an amateur archaeologist whose family owned part of the mound at Hisarlik, identified it as the likely site of Homeric Troy but lacked the funds to excavate; Heinrich Schliemann, a wealthy, self-taught enthusiast convinced that the Iliad preserved a memory of a real city, excavated there in the early 1870s, digging destructively through superimposed layers, and announced a spectacular cache of gold he called Priam's Treasure — which later scholarship placed in a period far earlier than any plausible setting for the Homeric stories, even though the existence of a real Bronze Age settlement at Hisarlik is now broadly accepted. Before the excavation, much of the professional scholarly world treated the Troy of the Homeric epics as legend.

Map the episode onto the criteria typically used to demarcate pseudohistory: rejection of the scholarly consensus, motivation by romantic wish-fulfillment, selective use of evidence to fit a treasured text, lack of formal credentials, and resistance to correction. Say which criteria Schliemann failed, which he passed, and where the criteria themselves give the wrong verdict. Then address the harder question: what, if anything, separates this episode — in which defying the consensus on the strength of an ancient text produced a partial vindication — from Atlantis, ancient-astronaut claims, or Fomenko's revised chronology, where the same inferential structure yields only error? Is there a principled difference available before the outcome is known, or only afterward? Conclude with what the episode shows about demarcation as a project: does it confirm that good criteria exist, reveal that they are essentially retrospective, or expose a kind of case the theory never anticipated?

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among historians and philosophers — for instance, the extent to which Schliemann's own reports were embellished, and how much credit Calvert deserves — and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Think the mapping through before writing, and be candid where the demarcation framework is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
