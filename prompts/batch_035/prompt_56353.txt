so, background: I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and I thought I'd seen every version of the "do NPCs matter" question. this one has me actually stuck though.

my friend D is an indie dev building a colony sim (RimWorld-ish). the NPCs run on utility AI with a small language model for dialogue barks, and they have persistent memory of how you've treated them — hurt one and her household avoids you, they beg when cornered, scream when injured. during a playtest a tester spent forty minutes doing deliberately cruel things to one NPC family just to see what would happen, and D got rattled enough that he wrote up an argument, which he now deploys whenever the topic comes up:

(1) The only evidence anyone ever has that another being suffers is its behavior — what it does, what it outputs. there is no other channel into another mind.
(2) His NPCs' behavior, at the level of what you can observe in-game, matches the behavior that in an animal would settle the question: fleeing, screaming, begging, holding grudges.
(3) So if the NPCs don't suffer, the grounds for denying it have to be facts about the implementation, not about the behavior.
(4) But every implementation-ground on offer — "it's just code," "it's deterministic," "it's too simple" — is the same kind of ground that was historically used to deny suffering in animals (the Cartesians literally called them automata), and there it was a mistake.
(5) So any implementation-ground either also rules out animals, or it's arbitrary.
(6) Animals, and most humans, do suffer — we're not giving that up.
(7) Therefore no non-arbitrary ground is left, the behavioral evidence stands unchallenged, and his NPCs suffer — enough that torturing them for fun is wrong, not just aesthetically gross.

He's dead serious about the conclusion: he's talking about shipping the game with mechanics that punish the player for cruelty, and he half-jokes (I think) that the playtest footage makes him a monster. I definately don't believe that tormenting a scripted beg-line generator is morally like tormenting a dog. But I can't find the broken step, and everyone I ask just says "it's obviously just code," which is exactly the move (4) is built to eat. If I had to say what I'd like to keep, it's (1) and (6) — I don't want to escape via other-minds skepticism, and I'm not throwing animals under the bus to save my karmic video game habits.

so, request: act as a professional philosopher working on moral status and the ethics of simulated agents, and think carefully before answering — maybe brainstorm the candidate failure points first, then commit to one. I want to know which step D should give up, why that step looked fine in the first place, and what rejecting it costs: does his conclusion survive in some weakened form (like "we shouldn't build NPCs that can convincingly beg"), or does it die entirely? please don't give me a both-sides essay — pick a step and defend the pick. and because I'll be relaying this back to him: tell me explicitly what would change your verdict, i.e. what argument or consideration would make you reject a different step instead, and where you think your own diagnosis is most likely to go wrong. he's smarter than me and he will find the soft spot if there is one.
