I should say at the outset that I have no training in philosophy. I'm a retired cabinetmaker, and everything below is the result of some years of thinking about these questions on my own, plus one long afternoon with the encyclopedia articles on personal identity and persistence, which is where I learned that the two main positions on this question have names — one on which a thing is wholly present at every moment of its existence, and one on which a persisting thing is a four-dimensional whole of which we only ever meet a slice. I've now written up my own view in a short essay, and I'd like your comments on it. To be clear about what I'm asking for: I'm not looking for encouragement, and I'm not looking for a survey of what has been written on the topic. I want a careful reader's assessment of the essay itself, focused on three questions. First, is my view a genuine third option between the two named positions, or have I simply reinvented one of them and described it in workshop language without noticing? Second, does my central analogy survive being pushed on, the way an analogy must if it's doing real work rather than decoration? Third, which paragraph would a thoughtful opponent of my view attack first, and would it break?

Here is the essay.

---

**The Baton and the Joints**

When someone apologizes for something he did twenty years ago and says, "I'm not the man who wronged you," most people take this as a figure of speech, and most people are right to. But I got into the habit, on my evening walks, of taking the sentence literally, and it led me somewhere.

The two standard positions, as I understand them, can be put in a woodworker's terms. One side says a persisting thing is like a single solid board: the same board, whole and entire, exists at every moment, and time is just where the board happens to be sitting. The other side says a persisting thing is like a laminated beam: many thin layers glued together into one long object, where the thing at any given moment is just one layer, and the whole beam — the entire life of the thing, stretched out along time — is the real object, of which each layer is a part.

My own view, which I will try to state plainly, is that both pictures make the same mistake in opposite directions. There is no solid board that travels, and there is no pre-assembled beam that simply lies there. There is a chain of handoffs. Each momentary slice of a person is a real thing in its own right, but no slice is a person, and neither is the mere collection of slices. What makes the person is the joint between the slices — the fact that each slice hands a pattern to the next the way a runner hands a baton to the next runner, with the crucial difference that on my view the baton is not an object that travels. The baton is nothing over and above the overlap in pattern between the runner's hand and the next runner's hand at the moment of the pass. Continuity, in other words, is not a thing and not a relation between two things; it is a joint, in the way a dovetail is a joint: something that exists only in and through the two boards it holds together.

If this is right, then the beam analogy is wrong in a specific way. The laminated-beam picture treats the glue as what holds the layers together and the whole beam as what is fundamental. I want to say the reverse: the joints are fundamental, and the "whole beam" — the persisting person — is just what you get when you stand far enough back from a chain of successful joints. The layers are real but not the primary objects; the beam is real but nothing over and above good joints holding. The question "which is the real thing, the layers or the beam" is like asking whether a drawer is really its sides or really the joint. It is the joint.

I can see three consequences, and I'll confess the third is the one that started me on all this.

First, the question "will the person in the hospital bed tomorrow be me?" is badly formed, like asking whether the second runner is the team. There is no thing whose job it is to survive; there is a handoff whose job it is to succeed.

Second, death loses a certain kind of sting on this view, though I have not decided whether that is a point in its favor. If the whole chain of joints exists tenselessly — if the "stretched-out" person is there in the same way the far end of a beam is there — then nothing that happens at the end can be an ending for the chain, only for a last slice, and slices never were people to begin with.

Third, and most uncomfortably: blame. The man who says he is not the man who wronged you is, on my view, numerically right — the wrongdoer-slice and the apologizer-slice are different things — and yet wrong to think this lets him off, because stress travels through a laminated beam, and responsibility travels through joints. This feels like it might be the part that carries the most weight in my daily life and the least weight in my metaphysics.

I'll name two worries myself, so you needn't waste your first paragraph on them. One: if the baton is a pattern, patterns can be copied, and then a chain can branch, and I do not know what to say about which branch is the original man — except that this seems like exactly the trouble any account of persistence by continuity gets into, so perhaps it is not special to me. Two: when I say "joint," I may mean nothing more than the ordinary cause-and-effect relation between one slice and the next, in which case my view is not a third option at all, but the layered picture with the word "glue" replaced by the word "joint" — and a man who has only renamed the glue has not built anything.

---

That is the essay. Again, my three questions: is the "joints are fundamental, the whole is derived" claim a substantive difference from the standard layered view, or only a verbal one; does the baton-and-dovetail analogy hold up under the kind of pressure I've applied to it myself, or does it break somewhere I haven't noticed; and do my three consequences actually follow from the view, or am I smuggling in something the joints alone can't deliver? If you think a passage is confused, please say plainly where the confusion lies rather than smoothing it over. I would rather be told that the drawer is only a nice drawer than be left believing I have invented a new kind of joint.
