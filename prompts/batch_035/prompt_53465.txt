The question was:

"Do neural networks have representations in their own right, or is the vocabulary of 'features' and 'circuits' a projection by interpretability researchers? Distinguish the realist, instrumentalist, and deflationary positions on this question, and explain what would count as evidence for each."

The student's answer, copied verbatim:

"Networks like LLMs clearly process information, so the question is whether the internal states that do this processing deserve to be called representations. The realist says yes: there are facts about the network, independent of what we find interesting, that make certain internal states genuinely about things — a direction in activation space can be about the Golden Gate Bridge in the same sense a mental state can be about something. The instrumentalist says the representation talk is just a useful bookkeeping device: it helps us predict and steer the network, but there's no further fact of the matter. The deflationary position agrees but adds that this is true of human mental states too.

The strongest argument for realism is the empirical success of interpretability itself. When researchers use sparse autoencoders to extract a feature for the Golden Gate Bridge, and then activating that feature makes the model talk about nothing but the bridge, that's a causal story with predictive grip. You couldn't steer the model that way if the feature weren't really there. So the feature isn't just a projection, it's been discovered.

The strongest argument against realism is the underdetermination problem. As Putnam showed with his model-theoretic argument, for any input-output behaviour there will always be some gerrymandered assignment of computational states that 'explains' it, so whatever 'features' we claim to find are just one of many possible mappings, and our choice among them reflects our interests rather than facts about the network. This means realism about network representations is unmotivated.

I think the truth is in between. The model-theoretic problem shows we can't just read features off the network, but it doesn't show there are no privileged decompositions. Some decompositions are more predictive and more causally robust than others, and that's evidence of realism about those features specifically. Also note the instrumentalist position here is basically just behaviourism revived, since both deny internal states any real explanatory role, and behaviourism failed, so instrumentalism is in trouble. Overall, networks do have representations where the interpretability work keeps succeeding, and don't where it doesn't."
