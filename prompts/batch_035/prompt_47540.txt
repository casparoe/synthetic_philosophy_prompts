I teach an upper-level seminar on confirmation and probability, and every year the unit on observation selection effects ends with the doomsday argument. The standard way in — in the popular presentations, in various guises since Leslie's own book on the subject, and in most textbook treatments I know — is an urn analogy. A large urn contains balls numbered consecutively from 1 up to some unknown total N. You draw a ball at random and it reads 7. You should now be fairly confident the urn does not contain a million balls: had it done, a single-digit number would have been a striking coincidence. Now substitute: the urn is the human species, the number on the ball is your birth rank, and the draw is the fact that you find yourself roughly the hundred-billionth human to exist. Conclusion: you should lower your credence that humanity survives to colonize the galaxy or to reach the trillions, and raise your credence in a comparatively near-term end.

My students accept this in about ten minutes, which is precisely what worries me: an argument with such startling consequences should not be that easy. I would like a proper audit of the analogy, at the level I would expect from a strong graduate student.

First, the correspondence table. What plays the role of the urn, the balls, the numbering, the draw, the randomization, and the drawer? Be explicit about what the analogy claims is random, because I suspect that is where all the work is being done.

Second, the disanalogies — sorted by their bearing on the conclusion, not merely listed. Some I can anticipate. In the urn case the draw could have come out otherwise, and there is a definite physical process, the shaking, that explains why the number drawn is representative of the urn's contents; it is far from obvious what plays either role for your birth rank, or whether the supposition that you could have been someone else is coherent rather than a description of a different person existing. The urn also has a fixed and obvious membership, whereas the reference class is up to us — all Homo sapiens, all intelligent beings, all observers — and the posterior shifts with the choice. And there is a purely formal wrinkle: on some ways of modeling the sampling, the mere fact that you exist at all is evidence for hypotheses on which more observers exist, which pushes against doomsday. I want to know whether that consideration shows up as a disanalogy in the urn picture, or as a correction the urn picture cannot even represent.

Third, the verdict. Does the doomsday conclusion survive once the disanalogies that matter are on the table — or was the urn quietly supplying the controversial premise, that your birth rank is like a random sample, all along? And if the urn fails, say whether some better analogy could get the sampling story right, or whether the lesson is that no analogy of this form can, because the argument's force depends on exactly the premise such analogies invite us to assume without noticing.
