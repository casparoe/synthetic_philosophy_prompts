I run a discussion group that meets every other week on lying and deception, and next session is on whether lying and merely misleading someone are morally different (the Kant-at-the-door stuff — saying something literally true that you know will be taken the wrong way, vs. flat-out lying). I want a sorting quiz to hand out at the start, so people find out where they actually stand instead of just nodding along.

Here's the spec:

**The quiz itself.** 8–10 questions, each one a concrete case with 2–3 answer options. Cases I want included, roughly: someone asking whether you know where a person is, when you do and the asker means them harm (the classic — but give me options that distinguish flat-out lying, technically-true misdirection, and refusal); "paltering," i.e., actively volunteering a true statement you know will create a false belief (e.g. a used car ad saying "new transmission!" when it's new to that car); answering a question with a literally true but context-shifted statement ("I have never cheated on you" meaning "not since Tuesday"); and at least one case where the person being misled is kind of asking for it — fishing for information they have no right to, or willfully not putting two and two together. Beyond those, add whatever cases you think split the views well (a white lie case and an "they'd figure it out eventually" case would be good). The options should be plain descriptions of what you'd say or do, never "as a consequentialist I would..." — no labels in the questions at all. If two options are both technically honest, make them differ in some way that matters.

**The scoring key.** Map answer patterns onto positions, and I want the positions located in the logical space, not just a list of names. The leaves should include at least: strict no-lying absolutism (lying is out, misleading is fine — the Kant end); the Gricean-ish view that what you literally say vs. what you merely implicate makes a moral difference (misleading is the lesser wrong or no wrong); the deception-equivalence view (only the intended belief matters, so lying and misleading are exactly equally wrong); the "misleading is worse" view (because it drags the hearer's own reasoning into producing the false belief, or corrupts interpretive trust in a way plain lies don't — you choose the best version and note the other); and a threshold/entitlement view (the difference is real but small, and what really matters is whether the hearer is entitled to the truth — the person hunting your friend forfeits it either way). If there are other coherent positions in the space, add them; and tell me which answer combinations nobody lands on, and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

**What you can't separate.** Flag any pair of positions the quiz can't genuinely distinguish and say why (e.g. if the absolutist and the threshold view give the same answers on all ten cases, say so and suggest what extra case would split them).

**Format for the answer:**
- Intro — 2–3 sentences on what the quiz is for, plain language
- The quiz — the 8–10 cases, numbered, options lettered
- Scoring key — how to tally, then each leaf with its name (or "unnamed" if it has no standard label) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment
- Unoccupied corners — brief, maybe 4–6 sentences total
- What the quiz can't sort — brief

No name-dropping of contemporary authors unless it's actually load-bearing, I don't want people answering based on whose team they're on. And keep the whole thing usable printed on two sides of a page — so questions tight, key punchy.
