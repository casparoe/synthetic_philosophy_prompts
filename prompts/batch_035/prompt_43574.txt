Quick background so this makes sense: I'm a second-year resident, and after my mom died last spring in hospice -- midazolam ramped up over her final three days, all of it charted under double effect -- I somehow got voluntold onto the hospital's ethics committee. Next month I'm presenting the position that the doctrine of double effect does real load-bearing work at the end of life: that there's a genuine moral difference between giving a sedating dose you merely foresee will hasten death and giving a dose in order to hasten death, and that our policies should keep tracking that difference rather than collapse into pure risk-benefit arithmetic. The audience is maybe fifteen people: two intensivists who are blunt utilitarians at heart, a palliative NP who thinks intension talk just gives physicians cover, the chaplain (the only person in the room who actually likes the doctrine), someone from risk management, and a couple of philosophers-of-medicine from the affiliated university who know the trolley literature cold. Prep me for the Q&A: the objections in the order they'll actually arrive, phrased the way each of those people would put them, which ones genuinely hurt vs which I can bat away, what I should concede up front, and the one reply that has to land for the position to survive the meeting. I'm especially worried about the empirical point -- that the doses we actually use may not shorten life at all, which if true seems to leave the doctrine idle -- so don't soft-pedal that one. And flag the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks.
