I'm the one-person analytics shop at a regional health insurance plan (about 400k members, 14 clinics in network). Every quarter I put out a "clinic scorecard" comparing our in-network clinics on a bunch of outcome metrics -- 30-day readmission rates, follow-up after abnormal screening results, medication adherence -- and these numbers actually matter: our steering committee uses them in contract renewal talks, and a clinic got cut from the network two years ago partly off my scorecard.

Here's my problem. The clinics serve very different patient mixes. One clinic downtown skews young and healthy, one out by the retirement communities is basically all Medicare. Last quarter I had a metric where clinic A beat clinic B in the pooled data, and then a friend of mine who works in biostats looked at it and pointed out that within every age band, B actually beat A -- the pooled number was just an artifact of A happening to see the easy cases. Nobody caught it, including me. I'd like to say this was a one-off but honestly I now have no systematic way to tell when pooling is lying to me and when it's fine.

So what I want is a decision procedure I can actually run each quarter, per metric, before anything goes in the scorecard. Something like: what do I check first, what counts as a red flag, when do I stratify and re-run, and -- the hard part -- when the stratified answer differs from the pooled answer, which one goes in the report, and why. I understand from reading around that you can't settle that last question from the contingency table alone, that it depends on whether the thing you stratify on is upstream or downstream of the clinic's own choices, and I'd want the procedure to be honest about that point rather than pretending it has a mechanical answer.

Please build the procedure with these constraints in mind: I have about half a day per metric, I can run regressions but I'm not a statistician, and the audience for the scorecard is a committee that wants ONE number per clinic, not a lecture.

Then run it on three hypotheticals so I can see how it behaves:

1. The one from last quarter: pooled comparison favors clinic A, but within each of four age bands clinic B does better, and I know from claims data that younger patients (who do better on this metric regardless of care) disproportionately go to A for reasons unrelated to quality -- there's a new clinic smell / online booking thing that younger people use.

2. The reverse situation: pooled numbers favor clinic C, but a staffer at clinic C stratifies by whether the patient completed the care-management program C itself runs, and within completers vs non-completers C looks worse than clinic D. Clinic C is furious and says the stratified comparison punishes them for the very program that helps, so the pooled number is the fair one. My instinct is that C is gaming it, and I suspect the procedure will tell me my instinct is wrong -- I'd like it to be honest about that.

3. A small-clinic case: a rural clinic with 90 patients, where one subgroup has like 11 people in it, and the direction flips when that subgroup is included but the counts are tiny. What should the procedure do there -- and where does it just throw up its hands and say this is a judgment call or a "collect more data" call?

Also tell me where the procedure will misfire or get gamed, especially if the clinics figure out it exists and start choosing their own favorable stratifications to lobby me with. Keep in mind I never took causal inference, just two applied stats courses, so translate whatever theory you need but don't dumb it down to uselessness. Answer in American english please. And if there are places where the statistics genuinely underdetermines the answer and I'd be dressing up a value judgment as procedure, flag those explicitly rather than hiding them in a step -- I'd rather know which parts of my scorecard are opinions.
