A friend and I spent most of a dinner party arguing about what explains the behavior of Holocaust perpetrators, and she ended it by summarizing the Browning-Goldhagen dispute like this: "Browning studied a police battalion of middle-aged reservists from Hamburg and found that at their first killing operation the commander offered anyone who didn't feel up to it the chance to step out, and about half took it, which shows they weren't coerced and were acting on conviction. Since Browning's book was written as a direct response to Goldhagen, his explanation is basically the Milgram obedience experiment: a legitimate authority tells you to shoot and you do, which is why his conclusions about conformity transfer straight over from the lab. Goldhagen's counter is that German antisemitism was universal, so ordinary Germans were indistinguishable from Nazi Party members, and the men who stepped out at Józefów are just his evidence that the whole culture, not the situation, was to blame — hence his title 'Ordinary Germans,' a deliberate jab at Browning's 'Ordinary Men.' So the men who declined to shoot prove choice was available, and if they chose to kill, Goldhagen wins." I felt at the time that several of these claims ran together things that should be kept apart, but I couldn't say precisely where. Would you go through her summary and tell me which parts a historian of this debate would accept, which are subtle distortions (including anything about the chronology of the two books), and whether her closing inference follows even from the parts that are accurate?
