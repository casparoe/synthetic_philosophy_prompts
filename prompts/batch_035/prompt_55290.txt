I run capital projects for a county public works department, and I'm starting to think my job is basically running a slow-motion dollar auction. You know the game Shubik invented -- auction off a dollar bill, highest bidder gets the dollar but the *second* highest bidder also has to pay their bid and gets nothing, so once two people are in, each new bid is to avoid eating a total loss rather than to win. We do a version of this constantly: a treatment plant retrofit that's at 180% of budget and "six months from done" (for the last two years), a legal fight with a contractor where we've spent more in fees than the disputed amount and neither side will fold, and a flood-mapping software project where the vendor keeps telling us we're 90% there. My boss calls it "seeing things through." The county commissioners call it waste. I honestly can't tell which cases are which anymore.

What I want is a decision procedure my project review committee can actually run at each quarterly gate -- three people, about a half hour, none of us are behavioral economists. It should be grounded in the actual escalation-of-commitment stuff: why sunk costs should be irrelevant to the continue/kill call, but also the part people skip, which is that some escalation isn't sunk-cost fallacy at all. A friend of mine who did econ keeps telling me that in the dollar auction itself, bidding past a dollar can be rational if you're trying to minimize loss rather than win, and that quitting because "I've paid enough" is its own bias in reverse when the auction is competitive -- like our contractor fight, where walking away hands the other side everything and they know it. I don't fully understand where that line is (irrational escalation vs strategic entrapment vs just being right to finish what you started because most of the remaining cost is genuinely behind you) but I suspect the procedure has to sort those three apart, not just say "don't fall for sunk costs." Also: whatever you write has to be blame-resistant, because the person who champions a project sits on the committee that votes to kill it, so anything that turns into "whose fault is it" gets gamed immediately -- I'd rather the questions force forward-looking numbers than retrospectives.

Run the procedure on three cases: the treatment plant retrofit (I actually suspect the answer is keep going, which will make the commissioners furious and I want the reasoning laid out so it survives that meeting), the contractor litigation, and the flood-mapping software. Tell me honestly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where the committee just has to eat a values question instead of computing one -- e.g. whether reputational damage to the department counts as a real cost. And flag any place where you think the procedure will misfire, like cases it structurally can't distinguish. No citations needed, I just need something we can print on two pages and actually use.
