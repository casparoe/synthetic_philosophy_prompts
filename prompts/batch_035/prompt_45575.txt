I'm a backend engineer, definitely not a philosopher, but I got roped into moderating the Q&A after a talk at our local AI safety meetup. The speaker's core pitch (from the paper abstract and her earlier blog posts) is roughly: take a weak-but-trusted model, amplify it by letting it break hard questions into easier subquestions answered by copies of itself, then distill the resulting behavior back into a single trained model, and repeat. The claim is that alignment survives this loop even when the distilled model is much stronger than whatever you started with, because the aligned behavior is what gets reinforced at each step rather than the model's own native reasoning.

I want the questions to actually stress-test this, not let her do a victory lap. Specifically I want to hit at least: (1) whether decomposition is alignment-preserving at all — seems like a question can be split into pieces that are each individually safe to answer while the recombination is not, and if a subagent doesn't understand what the pieces are for, how does the trust in the whole thing work? (2) the competence side — distillation is lossy compression, so maybe each round loses a little alignment (or a little capability that's being quietly papered over by the model's own pretrained habits), and nobody can tell which; (3) the bootstrapping worry — the whole scheme assumes you can trust the base model's outputs enough to train on them, but that model was trained by gradient descent on human feedback, which is exactly the thing the scheme was supposed to replace. For each question, include the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, since she's sharp and has heard the standard objections. Also at least one question about what evidence, even in principle, could tell us the loop has quietly diverged from the intent.

Lead with the full list of questions, no preamble or setup about my situation — I already know my situation, I just need the questions. And keep them conversational, the kind I can actually read out loud off my phone without sounding like I'm reciting a paper.
