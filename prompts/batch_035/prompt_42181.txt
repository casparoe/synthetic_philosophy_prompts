I am revising a paper on the ethics of belief, and before I send it back to the journal I want a hostile but competent reading of it. The paper argues that Clifford, read through his own argument rather than his slogan, is committed to conceding most of what James wanted in "The Will to Believe." The move I'm betting the paper on is a distinction I introduce between two kinds of belief, which I call reporting beliefs and participating beliefs. I am pasting the full draft below. Please comment on it as specified at the end.

The draft, "Clifford's Exception Clause":

I. The reading of Clifford. Clifford's shipowner case is standardly read as a verdict about belief-formation as such: the man's sin is believing on insufficient evidence, and the famous maxim — that this is always and everywhere wrong, for anyone — just generalizes it. But look at what makes the case a case of wrongdoing for Clifford himself. He is explicit that the question of right and wrong "has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him." And the shipowner "had acquired his belief not by honestly earning it in patient investigation, but by stifling his doubts." Throughout the surrounding argument, the harm Clifford dwells on is not intramural to the believer's mind: the man who credulously believes weakens his own self-trust and becomes an unreliable node in the circulation of testimony; "the sincerity of his conviction can in no wise help him, because he had no right to believe on such evidence as was before him." The wrong is a wrong against the epistemic commons. My claim in section I is that the commons argument, not the bare slogan, is Clifford's operative ground.

II. Rules protecting a good carry exception clauses. If the maxim is a rule protecting a public good, it has the logical shape of any rule protecting a public good, and such rules standardly admit exceptions where compliance in the case defeats the point of the rule. Promise-keeping rules have disaster clauses; rules of evidence can have clauses for cases in which strict suspension would destroy the very evidence-generating capacity the rule exists to protect. Now James's point, stripped of its rhetoric: some decisions cannot be taken on intellectual grounds because the evidence does not yet exist and cannot be brought into existence unless one of the options is first embraced. James marks out the class with his structural test — "let us call an option a genuine option when it is of the forced, living and momentous kind," where a forced option is one like "Either accept this truth or go without it," for which "there is no standing place outside of the alternative." My addition is to observe that in such cases the belief is an input into the future evidence. Call these participating beliefs: beliefs that partly constitute the truth conditions for their own future confirmation. The paradigm is the researcher three years into a program who must decide whether to believe the program will work before the results that could confirm it can be produced at all; James's religious case is in this class insofar as the faith is partly constitutive of the life in which any confirming experience would occur.

III. Reporting versus participating. Beliefs divide, roughly, into those that merely report (they circulate as testimony; this is what the shipowner and Clifford's island agitators formed — what circulates in the commons is the claim, and so the manner of its acquisition is everyone's business) and those that participate (they do not need to be asserted to others at all, and the commons rationale never reaches them). A consistent Clifford — the Clifford of the commons argument — must therefore permit willful belief in the participating class. What remains of the James–Clifford dispute is an empirical quarrel about the size of that class: Clifford can dig in and say it is nearly empty; James says it is not. But that is not a clash of principles, and the principled version of Clifford's position collapses.

IV. The objection I fear. Clifford's deeper ground is not social but reflexive: every act of credulity, even a solitary one, debilitates our own power of discernment; the sin is against our belief-forming nature whether or not anyone else ever hears of it. My reply: this habit-training ground is still consequentialist and inherits the same exception structure. Whether credulity in non-testimony-forming contexts degrades one's reporting beliefs is an empirical claim about compartmentalization, and in the productive cases the opposite habit — trained, reflexive suspension — can be equally corrosive. My honest worry, stated in the paper: I have no argument that the two compartments are separable; I flag it and move on. There is also the slippery slope: everyone thinks his own option is genuine. My answer is that the structural test is checkable from outside — the crank's option usually fails the "forced" condition outright, since there is normally a standing place outside his alternative — but I don't show this in detail.
