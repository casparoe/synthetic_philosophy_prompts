I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophical training, and I've been asked to facilitate the endgame of a long-running argument between two colleagues. One — call her the aggregator — holds that every harm counts, however small, and that a sufficient number of negligible harms is worse than a single severe one: enough dust specks in enough eyes outweighs one person's torture. The other — call him the thresholdist — holds that some harms are so minor that no number of them adds up to a severe one; there is a gap that multiplication cannot cross. After a year of talking past each other, they have agreed to stop debating and instead let me draft a joint statement they can both sign. Please write that document, in four sections.

Section 1, agreed claims. The claims both could genuinely sign about the case itself — for instance that a dust speck is a real, if minute, harm rather than a neutral event; that the torture is far worse for the one person than a speck is for any one of the many; that intuitions pull differently depending on whether the choice is framed as a one-off decision or as a standing policy. Include only claims that carry information — nothing both would sign merely because it commits them to nothing.

Section 2, disagreements, each stated in wording the relevant side would accept for its own view. I want the residual disagreement made exact here. Is it about whether value is additive across persons, with each person's harm contributing an independent amount to a total? About whether a comparison of outcomes must be grounded in some particular individual being better or worse off? About whether the aggregate comparison is well-defined at all when no individual ever faces the trade in question? And if part of their dispute is verbal — both saying "worse" while tracking different comparison relations — identify which part and why, since that part should not appear in the disagreements table at all.

Section 3, cruxes. I suspect at least these three, but add any others you judge load-bearing: (a) separability — whether how bad an episode is for one person is independent of what is simultaneously happening to others; (b) continuity — whether for every harm there exists some finite number of milder harms whose total is worse; (c) grounding — whether a moral comparison between two outcomes must trace back to a difference for some individual. For each crux, describe a concrete case or thought experiment both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance.

Section 4, two wrinkles their year of arguing never reached, which I want handled explicitly.

First, a trace test. Suppose the speck-level harms are so mild they leave no memory a minute later and no downstream effect on mood or behavior — nothing is recorded anywhere, in the person or the world. Does either side's position depend on the harm being registered in some further fact, and does the traced version differ from the untraced one in a way that moves either side? Each should state in the document which version their view is actually about, and the placement should go in the agreed section if they coincide and in the table if they don't.

Second, a scaling test. Replace the single torture with tortures each half as long; then halve again, and again, until the intensity approaches speck level. The thresholdist who refuses to trade any number of specks for one torture must say where on this halving chain he stops refusing, or bite the bullet that he won't trade even one hundred-year torture for a hundred tortures of one year each — wait, that one goes the other direction; the point is that the refusal has to be located somewhere, and the location is a commitment. The aggregator, symmetrically, must say whether his willingness to trade survives all the way down to near-speck intensities or whether he too flinches at the bottom of the scale. Where each places his cutoff, and whether he can give a principled reason for that location rather than the neighboring ones, is the recorded prediction.

Treat the predictions as pre-registration: write them down as definite commitments, not hedges, so that if the collaboration reconvenes each side can be scored against what it forecast. And keep the whole document honest about scope: where the two are genuinely apart, say so plainly; where their disagreement dissolves under careful wording, say that too, and show which section the dissolved part has been moved out of. Assume no new empirical data is coming — the tests are conceptual, so predictions concern what each side would judge on reflection, not what any instrument would measure.
