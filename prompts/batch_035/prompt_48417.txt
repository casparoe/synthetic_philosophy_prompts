Last month our city council empaneled a working group to recommend how public safety services should be restructured, and I was asked to serve on it. The trigger was an incident two blocks from my house: a neighbor's son, in the middle of a psychiatric crisis, was met by armed officers and ended up hospitalized with injuries the department's own review called avoidable. I now have six weeks to submit a written statement of my views, and I have discovered that the views I have been asserting casually for years may not form a consistent package. Before I commit anything to paper, I want an audit.

Here is the package:

1. I hold, following Weber, that the state is distinguished from other associations by its claim to the exclusive right to authorize the use of physical force within a territory, and I believe this monopolization is among the greatest of civilizational achievements: wherever a genuine monopoly holds, most private violence disappears.
2. I hold that the police, as presently constituted in this country, cannot be made acceptable by training, body cameras, or personnel changes, and that the bundled model — one armed officer handling traffic, welfare checks, crises, and violent crime alike — is itself a large part of the problem.
3. Nonetheless, I am a gradualist. I oppose abolition, on the ground that the successor institutions its advocates propose would either collapse into private protection rackets or quietly re-create the police under another name.
4. I hold that even a perfectly just state would be wrong to abolish the citizen's right of self-defense, and that in emergencies — disasters, prolonged breakdowns of public order — private individuals and ad hoc groups may permissibly use force, including lethal force, to defend themselves and strangers. I am not moved by the familiar claim that such force is legitimate only as the state's delegated surrogate.
5. I hold that a person subjected to clearly unlawful violence by an agent of the state retains the right to defend herself, with force if necessary, and that our law is wrong not to recognize this.

Go through the package and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look to be. I do not know where the problem lies, and I do not want you to assume I have a favorite commitment I am protecting. For each genuine tension, tell me what would have to give, what each available revision would cost, and order the tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally bad. Where a pair only appears to conflict, explain what dissolves the appearance.

Two requests about the manner of the answer. First, keep separate what is settled among people who know this area — matters of text, doctrine, and definition, for instance — from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus; if scholars, courts, or historians divide on something I am leaning on, say so plainly rather than papering over it. Second, please write in American English.
