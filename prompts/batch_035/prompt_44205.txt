At a faculty dinner I made the mistake of bringing up Zeno's Achilles, and our head of department closed the subject with solvitur ambulando — 'it is solved by walking' — invoking the old story, traceable to Simplicius, that Diogenes the Cynic simply stood up and walked away while Zeno argued that motion is impossible. That ended the conversation, but I have since been trying to work out what the quip actually claims. Please lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing — from the near-tautology that motion in fact occurs, through the epistemological claim that this very obvious fact entitles us to dismiss any argument to the contrary without locating its flaw, to stronger claims about empirical self-evidence trumping a priori reasoning, or about Zeno having misdescribed what would even count as a solution — and attach a verdict to each: trivial, substantive and defensible, or question-begging against Zeno, who never denied that things appear to move. Finally, tell me which reading my head of department needs for the quip to have amounted to a refutation rather than a refusal to engage, and whether any of the readings genuinely dissolves the paradox instead of merely declining to discuss it. A clear list I could bring to his office hours would be ideal.
