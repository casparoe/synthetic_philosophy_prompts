I'm TA-ing a course on social epistemology (mixed phil and econ undergrads, ~18 students, one 80-minute section). We just covered the standard rational-herding results — the idea that each person's choice is public but their private signal isn't, so early signals get swamped and perfectly rational agents can lock into a wrong consensus that no individual's later evidence can overturn. Textbook stuff, and exactly the kind of thing students nod along to without feeling. Design me an in-class game that makes them actually live through it.

Constraints / what I want:

- Rules and materials I can actually run: cards or colored chips as private signals, sequential public choices, some payoff scheme that doesn't involve real money (candy is fine, or points toward the participation grade). Keep the math simple enough that they can hold the signal structure in their heads while playing.
- The core experience should be the lock-in: a student with good private evidence following the herd anyway, and knowing that's the right call given what they can observe. If nobody ever feels that squeeze, the game failed.
- Here's the part I care about most, and I don't want it to be a lecture add-on: run at least two rounds where the *same* set of private signals gets played in different orders (shuffle the seating, deal the same cards). The class should confront the fact that identical aggregate information produces opposite group verdicts depending on the sequence. That path-dependence result is the thing my students keep treating as a technicality when it's on the page.
- Add a twist round where each player can, once, pay some of their expected payoff to peek at one extra private signal before choosing — I want to see who buys information and whether the purchases are wasted because the cascade is already past the point where one signal moves anyone. Debrief should get at the informational externality idea: your choice leaks information to everyone behind you, and you don't internalize that.
- The debrief needs to map specific moments in what happened onto the formal pieces: the private signal, the publicly observed action, the posterior each player should have, and the threshold at which actions stop being informative. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — students leaking signals, someone deciding to be a hero contrarian just to spite the game, the math being too fast — and how I should handle each.
- Be honest about what the game can't reproduce: real cascades in markets and science run on reputational payoffs, career risk, and the option to be silently wrong rather than publicly wrong, none of which my payoff scheme has. I'll need to supply that gap myself, so tell me how you'd do it in two minutes at the end.

One more thing: I'd rather the students not have read the classic urn experiments before class, so if the design resembles one closely, tell me how to randomize the framing enough that they can't pattern-match it to the textbook figure.
