Something I keep chewing on and can't get straight: pretty much every big platform, newsroom, and broadcaster has quietly converged on the same weird bundle of rules about slurs, and I want to know what theory of meaning that bundle is committed to.

The pattern, concretely. First, newsroom style guides generally won't print slurs even inside direct quotes -- a politician says the word on camera and the write-up reports him saying "the n-word," while the same outlet happily prints other profanity verbatim. Second, moderation policies ban a slur when an out-group member types it but carve out in-group use, and they stumble around song lyrics and quoted speech as awkward special cases. Third -- and this is the one that really bugs me -- court transcripts and TV subtitles routinely mask the written slur (f***-style censoring or just "the n-word") while the audio plays unbleeped right over it. We all hear it; the page just won't "say" it.

So what does this practice presuppose about what slurs mean? On its face it presupposes that the same string can carry different content or a different status depending on who's producing it, which sounds like the speaker-relative/identity-dependent semantics some philosophers of language push for slurs, and which seems flatly incompatible with the older view that a slur has one fixed derogatory content regardless of who utters it. But maybe I'm overreading, and the practice only presupposes something about who is *entitled* to use the word -- a norm about standing, not semantics -- so the fixed-content view survives fine and the whole pattern is just pragmatics plus politeness layered on top.

Here's what I want from you: reconstruct the implicit theory the practice is running on, and be disciplined about what it strictly requires versus what it's merely compatible with -- e.g., could a purely pragmatic account (offense as a matter of speaker intent and audience expectations rather than word meaning) license the exact same bundle of rules? Which semantic views are actually ruled out by the practice, if any? And then tell me whether the parts are even consistent with each other, because the subtitle case feels like a genuine stress test: masking the written token while broadcasting the audio seems to presuppose that the offense lives in something a speaker *does*, not in the string itself... except a subtitle is a token too, produced by a captioner. Who is "saying it" there -- the original speaker, the captioner, the network, nobody? If the implicit theory gives no coherent answer to that, I want to know, and I want to know what minimal addition would patch it. No citations or reading lists please, just the analysis.
