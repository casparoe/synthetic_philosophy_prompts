I help run a little discussion group on AI welfare (basically a pub-basement meetup, mix of grad students and a couple of machine learning engineers from a local startup, no actual philosophers). Next session is on which computations matter morally, and I want to hand out a decision tree that sorts people into positions rather than letting us trade raw intuitions for two hours like last time. The tree should start from a concrete commitment anyone can answer without knowing any jargon — for instance the classic lookup-table worm: you're shown a giant lookup table that exactly mimics the input-output behavior of a nematode's nervous system, and asked whether anything in there matters morally — and then every branch node should be a case or a question where the standard positions actually come apart, never a "do you accept functionalism? y/n" node. The leaves should include at least: full substrate-independent functionalism; the family of views on which biological or chemical implementation matters (please actually work out what the carve is there, since "it must be carbon" seems like a non-starter once you allow brain emulation in principle); the middle-ground implementation-sensitive views where what matters is having something like a global-workspace or integration-style architecture rather than mere behavioral sophistication; and the deflationary/illusionist leaf where the question of machine suffering is itself confused. Please also flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely can't separate — my own suspicion is that functionalism and the architecture-sensitive middle views collapse together once you push on the emulation cases, but I'd like to see whether you can split them or you agree they can't be split.

Because half the fun is the weird cases, fold in the edge cases where you can: does running the same sentient program twice generate two sufferings or one (or somehow less than two)? Does pausing and resuming a run cut a moral patient off mid-experience, or does it not matter at all? If you emulate a brain with neurons that are 10x larger but 10x sparser and slower, is the amount of morally relevant computation the same? And what about attribution — if a big system plausibly contains a sentient sub-process that the overall system doesn't know about, does the suffering belong to the sub-process, the whole, or is the question malformed? Feel free to include a leaf for that last "the question is malformed" family too, since one of our regulars always ends up there and I'd like her to have somewhere to sit.

Finally, once you've built the logical space from the node questions, mark which combinations of answers are unoccupied — nobody actually holds them — and take one sentence each to guess whether that's because the combination is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored. Keep every node phrased so a smart person who's never read any philosophy of mind can answer it honestly about the case; the whole point of the exercise is that my MLE friends commit to answers first, and only afterwards find out what position they've been holding all along. Format-wise, a flowchart-style text tree with the node questions at the branch points and short position names at the leaves, plus a paragraph of notes at the end on the inseperable pairs and empty corners.
