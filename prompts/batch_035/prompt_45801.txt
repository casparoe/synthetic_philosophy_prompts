I teach environmental ethics at a state university, and this spring I am moderating a public forum on suburban wildlife with a senior colleague — a moral philosopher who has written on our obligations to animals for three decades and who prides herself on never being caught in an inconsistency. I would like to anticipate her position before we appear together, so that I can locate the pressure points in advance. Her track record, as I have assembled it:

She holds that buying eggs from caged hens is seriously wrong; that a licensed cull of an overabundant moose herd, in a region where humans exterminated the wolves a century ago, is permissible and perhaps something we owe; that the hiker who stops to free a fawn caught in a wire fence does what duty requires, not a mere kindness; that documentary filmmakers who decline to intervene as a hamstrung wildebeest calf is taken by wild dogs have done nothing wrong; that volunteers who wash oiled seabirds after a tanker spill are repaying a debt rather than performing a charity; and, most recently, that a park service plan to feed elk through a hard winter — sparing thousands of slow starvation deaths — should be rejected, since it would only purchase a larger die-off in some later winter. She has also told a graduate seminar, over my objection, that we cannot wrong anyone by declining to create them: no one is stranded on the threshold of existence, waiting to be harmed by a decision not to bring them about.

The question I must put to her concerns our county's deer herd. Roughly half of the fawns born into this herd die before their first birthday, mostly of starvation in late winter or on the highway; the does that survive lead long lives that most observers of the herd would call worth living. The county proposes a decade-long program of immunocontraceptive bait that would halve the herd. It prevents the death of no existing deer; it prevents the births of the fawns who would starve, and of some who would not have. It would also cut deer-vehicle collisions substantially — a benefit the county's publicity stresses and that I suspect she will want to set aside.

What will she say? I can see at least three rules her record might be running — something about repairing what humans have caused, something about duties arising from encounter and modest cost, something about the arithmetic of total suffering — but I cannot tell which, and I suspect they diverge exactly here. Predict her verdict on the contraception program, lay out the candidate rules and what each predicts, tell me your best guess and why, and tell me what further case I should have ready to put to her from the audience if I want to force the rules apart in public.
