I've been reading the rationalist corner of the internet since the Overcoming Bias days, which is long enough to have watched the comment threads relitigate Bertrand's paradox about once every eighteen months, and long enough to know that the principle of indifference is an old friend with a drinking problem. I've finally written down my actual verdicts on a batch of cases, and I'd like to know whether there's a single principle behind them or whether I've been patching a broken tool with tape. Please reply in continuous prose only, no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, the way one writes a letter.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. A fair die about to be rolled: I say each face gets a sixth, and I don't feel any need to know how it was tossed or by whom; I'd say it even if the die had already been rolled and sealed in a cup. The millionth digit of some mathematical constant no one has yet computed: I say one half that it's even, and this doesn't bother me even though there is a fact of the matter. A randomly chosen natural number being even: I say there is no answer at all, and I'm right, since no uniform distribution on the naturals exists; but I can't say why I'm entitled to one half in the digit case and nothing here.

Bertrand's chord: I accept that the three classical answers — a third, if you pick two endpoints uniformly on the circumference; a half, if you pick the midpoint uniformly along a random radius; a quarter, if you pick the midpoint uniformly over the disk — are each correct for their own sampling procedure, and I believe the bare question has no answer. If forced, I'd blurt "half," because I picture dropping a point somewhere in the disk, but I don't defend the blurt.

The cube factory produces cubes with side length at most two centimeters. Given a cube off the line, what's the probability its side is at most one centimeter? Uniform on the side gives one half; uniform on the volume gives one eighth, since the volume runs up to eight cubic centimeters and one cubic centimeter corresponds to a side of one; uniform on the surface area gives one quarter. My gut says one half, without hesitation.

A vessel contains wine and water, with the ratio of water to wine somewhere between a tenth and ten, that is, between ten times as much wine as water and ten times as much water as wine. What's the probability the ratio is at most two, so that the water is at most twice the wine? Uniform on the ratio over that interval gives about nineteen percent; uniform on its logarithm gives about sixty-five percent, log twenty over log a hundred. My gut sides firmly with the logarithm. It tells me that a ratio of two is as far from one as a ratio of a half, and that orders of magnitude are the natural spacing.

And here is what keeps me up at night: the cube and the vessel are, structurally, the same problem. In each, a positive quantity is confined to a bounded interval and I'm asked the probability it lies in the lower part. In one case I go uniform on the quantity; in the other, uniform on its logarithm. I can tell a story about it — the ratio is dimensionless and the question is phrased in pure ratios, so there's a symmetry under reciprocation; the side length is dimensionful and the factory's tolerances are additive in length — but I notice that if the factory's brochure had quoted volumes, I'd have said one eighth, and if the vessel problem had been phrased in absolute amounts, I'd have reached for something else. My working principle appears to be: be indifferent over whatever variable the problem's wording hands you. That can't be right, and if it is right, the principle of indifference is a machine for laundering the phrasing of a problem into a probability.

My current patch is something like this: indifference is legitimate over a finite set of possibilities we have no reason to distinguish; in the continuous case it's legitimate only where a symmetry group rich enough to determine a unique invariant measure does the choosing, the way permutations do for the die and the way reciprocation tries to for the vessel; and where no such group exists, or several compete, as with Bertrand's three, the right response is silence. I know Jaynes claimed to dissolve Bertrand with transformation groups, and I half believe him, but half is exactly my problem: three groups, three answers. Worse, under this patch I can't place my digit verdict at all — the millionth digit has no symmetry group choosing it, it's just a fact, and I still say one half and sleep well.

So, please: state the principle, or a short list of candidates, as precisely as you can; tie each clause to the case that motivates it; and tell me what it predicts on cases I haven't mentioned — in particular, the prior for the mass of a particle discovered next year, where my gut says logarithmic and I'd like to know whether the principle earns that or whether it's a bias I should shed; the bias of a coin from an unfamiliar mint, where I'm tempted by uniform on the bias but have seen people insist on uniform over the log-odds, which is improper and behaves as though the coin is almost surely almost always heads or almost always tails; and Bertrand's question when I'm told a chord was drawn by a process I refuse to describe. And please be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot save: I'd rather lose the blurt than the pattern.
