I teach an intro phil of mind course with a unit on free will, and I want to open it with the sphex wasp rather than the standard compatibilism stuff, because students immediately get the wasp. You probably know the story: the digger wasp drags her paralyzed prey to the burrow entrance, leaves it on the threshold, goes inside to check the burrow, comes back out, and drags the prey in. If an ethologist moves the prey a few inches away while she's inside, she comes out, retrieves it, and... checks the burrow again. You can make her do this dozens of times. She looks like a competent little agent right up until the one intervention that reveals there's nobody home in the sense we assumed. The students find this funny and then get quietly uneasy when I ask whether humans have an equivalent.

So here's what I want: a classroom activity for my 16 students, one 75-minute session, where they get to *feel* sphexish from the inside rather than just hear about the wasp. My vague idea is that some of them should be made to execute a fixed behavioral script — some procedure with rigid trigger conditions, like the wasp's check-the-burrow routine — while other students play ethologists whose whole job is to probe the script and find the loop, the point where the "agent" keeps repeating a pointless action because nothing in the procedure tracks the goal anymore. Maybe the script-runner gets an index card of if-then rules they must follow absolutely, and the ethologists submit perturbations and record what breaks. I'd like it set up so the script-runners genuinely experience the pull of "but I'm just doing what the rules say" while everyone watching can see the behavior decomposing into mechanism. But you're the designer, feel free to do something structurally different if you have a better mechanism for generating the same feeling.

For the debrief, I want them to hit three things: (1) the difference between a procedure that's goal-sensitive and one that merely produced goal-consistent behavior in the normal environment, since that gap is what the whole wasp story turns on; (2) where they'd place humans on that spectrum — I want them to find their own candidate sphex-loops, like re-checking a sent text or the way you sometimes walk into a room and do the thing you do there even though you came in for something else; and (3) the uncomfortable question of whether "being able to notice the loop" is actually a difference in kind or just a more elaborate procedure that a cleverer ethologist could also crash. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (students intentionally breaking rules to be funny, the script being too easy to crash, ethologists getting bored, whatever), and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce — e.g. that a student following a card knows they could deviate, which the wasp presumably doesn't, and I don't want to paper over that.
