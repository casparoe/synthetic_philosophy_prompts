Two friends of mine have been arguing for like two years and finally agreed to try an adversarial collaboration. One is a behavior-geneticist type who thinks heritability estimates are basically solid science that gets trashed because people are scared of where the numbers lead. The other is a critic-of-behavior-genetics type who thinks the whole apparatus rests on a pile of assumptions that never get stated out loud, and that the numbers get laundered into claims they can't actually support ("genes cause" this or that). Write the joint statement they'd produce after a year of good-faith hashing it out.

I want:

- A numbered section of claims both would sign. There's actually a fair amount to work with here — heritability as a variance decomposition, broad vs. narrow sense, that it's a property of a population in an environment rather than of the trait itself, that a trait can be highly heritable in one population and not another, that heritability by itself says nothing about means or about between-group differences, that "genetic" doesn't mean fixed or immutable. Include whatever they can genuinely agree on, including the points where the critic gets the geneticist to concede something the pop-science version glosses over.

- A disagreements table with the residual disputes, each worded so that its own side would accept the phrasing — the geneticist won't sign "twin studies are worthless" and the critic won't sign "GWAS validates the twin design", so you'll have to find formulations that are exact without being slanted toward either. Cover things like whether the equal environments assumption is a real problem or a distraction, whether missing heritability means anything, and whether polygenic scores change the epistemics or just scale up the same problems.

- The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. My guess is one is whether heritability of cognitive/behavioral traits is systematically lower in deprived environments (the gene-environment moderation question) and another is what gene-environment *correlation* does to the interpretation, but work out what the real cruxes are.

- For each crux, an argument or piece of evidence both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

And if part of what they were fighting about turns out to be verbal — e.g. they mean different things by "informative" or by "matters for policy" — flag which part and why. Also, feel free to have one or both sides actually concede ground where the evidence warrants; the document doesnt have to end in a draw to count as a success.
