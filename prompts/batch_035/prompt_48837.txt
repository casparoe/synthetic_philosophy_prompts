I'm reading a popular book on the science of consciousness, and its chapter on what it calls "the war over qualia" ends with a summary of Daniel Dennett's "Quining Qualia" that I'd like to check before I take it as settled. It reads:

"The most radical attack on the traditional picture comes from Daniel Dennett's paper 'Quining Qualia,' whose title coins a verb in honor of the logician W. V. Quine: to 'quine' something is to deny resolutely that it exists, as Quine denied the existence of unobservable entities. Dennett defines qualia by two features: they are private, in that only you can know what your experience is like, and they are ineffable, in that they cannot be captured in words. His most famous argument imagines two coffee tasters at Maxwell House, Chase and Sanborn, each of whom finds after years on the job that he no longer likes the coffee. Chase reports that the coffee tastes exactly the way it always did and that he simply no longer enjoys it, while Sanborn reports that the coffee now tastes different to him. Dennett argues that since introspection cannot tell which of the two is right, introspection is unreliable across the board, so our supposed infallible access to our own experiences is an illusion. He concludes that experiences themselves are illusions — the brain constructs a user-friendly fiction — and that in reality there is nothing it is like to be us. The felt qualities of experience are not just scientifically inexplicable but incoherent, like a round square, which is why the hard problem of consciousness is really a misunderstanding rather than a problem, and why most researchers in the field now agree that qualia can simply be dropped from the science of mind."

I don't have the background to check this against the original paper, and parts of it are convincing precisely because they sound dismissive in the way I'd expect of Dennett. But a couple of things leave me uneasy, especially the flat statement that in reality there is nothing it is like to be us, which seems like a strange thing for someone to spend a career writing books about consciousness if he really believed it.

How much of this summary is right? Please go through it more or less sentence by sentence: what would a philosopher of mind regard as accurate, what is a simplification a careful writer might make, and what is subtly wrong? In particular, I'd like to know (1) whether the gloss on the verb "quine" is faithful to what Dennett intends by it; (2) whether the definition of qualia in the summary is the definition Dennett actually works with, and whether the difference matters for what follows; (3) what the Chase and Sanborn case is really supposed to show — is it that introspection is unreliable in general, or something more specific about where the change lies between perception and judgment?; and (4) whether the summary's conclusion follows from the parts of the argument that are correct, or whether it slides from "the traditional conception of qualia is incoherent" to something much stronger that Dennett would deny, and whether the closing claim about what most researchers believe is true. I'd rather know what's defensible in the chapter than have it demolished wholesale, but I'd also rather not repeat one of its mistakes to a friend who knows the literature.
