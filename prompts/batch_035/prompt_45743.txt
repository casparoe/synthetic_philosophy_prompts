Please write a parody of a consumer-product safety recall notice, around 600 words, issued by a fictional federal agency (invent its name) for the explanatory term "emergence" as it is commonly used in scientific and philosophical writing. The target of the satire is the habit of treating "it's emergent" as itself an explanation—deployed whenever a system's behavior resists analysis at a lower level—rather than as a placeholder marking where an explanation is still owed. The humor should come from getting the underlying issues right: the difference between weak emergence (surprising but derivable in principle, like the liquidity or phase transitions familiar from physics and chemistry) and strong emergence (behavior claimed to be irreducible in principle, which many think makes it indistinguishable from vitalism with the theology filed off); the way "nothing but atoms" claims quietly coexist with "more than the sum of the parts" rhetoric; the role of supervenience as a fallback position when stronger claims are pressed. Include the standard furniture of a recall notice—the affected models or uses, the reported malfunctions, instructions to consumers, a remedy, and the phrase "no injuries have been reported"—and have at least one passage in which the agency distinguishes acceptable from defective uses of the term, since the joke should be that the word is not being recalled, only its abuse. A reader who knows the reduction-versus-emergence literature should find the satire fair, and a reader who doesn't should still find it funny. Please write the piece in American English.
