I lead a five-person country-risk unit at a development bank. Every quarter we have to produce short risk assessments for the sovereign borrowers in our portfolio, and the credit committee downstream of us wants a single number or at least an ordered ranking for each country — expected probability of default within five years, best to worst, so it can be fed into pricing. My problem is that I've been reading about Keynes's views on probability and uncertainty, and I suspect our current process commits exactly the errors he diagnosed: we treat every question as if it had a numerical answer, we collapse the difference between a well-supported modest probability and a barely-supported confident one, and we have no vocabulary for the cases where the honest output is not a low estimate but something like his admission that on some questions the only honest position is that we simply do not know.

Please turn what is usable in Keynes into a procedure my analysts can actually run on a country in about ninety minutes per quarter. Specifically, I want the procedure to preserve at least these distinctions from his theory, though you should tell me which others deserve to survive the translation and which have to be dropped: (1) the difference between the probability of an argument's conclusion and the weight of the argument — between how likely something is given our evidence and how much evidence we have; (2) his claim that probabilities are often non-numerical and sometimes not even comparable, so that for some pairs of countries the right output may be "cannot be ranked," not a forced ordering; and (3) his suspicion, later developed by Post-Keynesians under the heading of non-ergodicity, that some processes have no stable underlying frequencies to read off, because the relevant future is generated once rather than sampled repeatedly.

The procedure should fix an output format that the credit committee will accept — for instance a small set of verdicts such as numerical, banded, comparable-but-unranked, or suspended — with explicit rules for when an analyst is permitted to give each, and a required statement of weight alongside whatever probability verdict comes out. It should say what the analysts do when the committee pushes back demanding a ranking we have declared non-comparable, and it should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three cases: (a) a mid-income borrower with twenty years of decent fiscal data whose main risk is a plausible but unprecedented constitutional crisis; (b) a borrower where the dominant risk is a livestock pandemic jumping to humans, an event that has never happened in that country but has a well-studied global base rate; and (c) a newly elected government whose fiscal trajectory depends on a reform program that the government itself has not yet specified. At least one of these should come out with an answer the committee will not like, and I want you to show the procedure producing that answer anyway. Finally, tell me where the procedure will misfire — the situations in which Keynes's framework, operationalized this crudely, gives the wrong guidance, and what an analyst should do when they recognize one.
