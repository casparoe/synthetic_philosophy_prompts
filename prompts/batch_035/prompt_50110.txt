I'm on the organizing committee of a community restorative-justice program, and our facilitator settles nearly every disagreement about accountability by invoking the African proverb that a person is a person through other persons, usually rendered as 'I am because we are'. Most recently, when a volunteer argued that an offender's remorse should count in how we respond to a harm, she replied that the proverb shows individual wrongdoing is a category mistake: harms are produced by damaged relationships, so there is no isolated offender whose inner state could matter. That inference strikes several of us as a leap, but none of us can say precisely where it goes wrong. Please disentangle the distinct theses the proverb could be expressing — from the near-trivial (psychological development is socially mediated) through moderate constitutive claims (one's practical identity, obligations, and moral status are partly fixed by actual relationships) to radical ones (there is no individual locus of agency or responsibility at all) — and for each say whether it is plausible, whether it supports anything like the communitarian politics it is often recruited for, and whether it specifically supports her move from relational personhood to the irrelevance of remorse. Lay it out as a menu I can circulate before our next meeting, and be explicit about which reading you would defend if pressed and what argument or consideration would lead you to abandon that verdict — I want to know where the case for or against her use of the proverb is most likely to fail, not just which reading is safest.
