I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on ancient philosophy, and we've spent the last three weeks on Aristotle's account of exchange in Nicomachean Ethics Book V and his distinction between wealth-getting for the household and wealth-getting for its own sake in Politics Book I. Our professor runs the class by ruling on cases: she describes a transaction, asks what Aristotle should say about it, and then commits herself to a verdict, and part of our grade is predicting her rulings on new cases rather than reciting the texts. Her verdicts so far: a fisherman with a surplus trading it to a farmer for grain, each for his own table's use, counts as natural exchange; a retailer who buys goods wholesale in his own city and resells them at a markup is practicing the unnatural kind of wealth-getting, even though his customers genuinely need the goods; a lender charging interest on a consumption loan is condemned outright — she stresses Aristotle's point that money exists to serve exchange, so making money itself the source of more money perverts its function; but a potter who sells his pots at whatever price covers his household's needs for the year, even a price well above his material costs, is fine.

Our term paper asks us to predict her verdict on a new case and defend the prediction. The case I want to write on is a grain dealer who buys up surplus grain in a bumper year, stores it at his own expense and risk, and sells it in a shortage year at the much higher price the market then bears. I can see two rules that each fit all four of her verdicts. On one reading, an exchange is acceptable roughly when it serves need and stops there — the dealer's profit is not bounded by need and he's trading to grow his money, so he's condemned. On another, an exchange is acceptable when the return is proportionate to what the parties actually contribute — and the dealer plausibly contributes something real, the storage and the bearing of risk across time, so his higher price could be proportionate and he'd be acquitted. Where I keep getting stuck is deciding which rule she is really running, whether some third rule fits her track record just as well, and what each candidate predicts not only about my dealer but about the neighboring cases I could use as evidence.

So I'd like your help working this out: predict her verdict on the grain dealer, lay out the candidate rules her verdicts are consistent with and what each one predicts about the new case, give your best guess along with your actual reasons for preferring it, and name the further case — a transaction, not a passage — that would best tell the candidate rules apart. I'm less attached to my framing than I sound: if you think I've misdescribed her verdicts, or that Aristotle's examples never contemplate production or storage across time and so his framework simply runs out on my case, or that the whole question secretly turns on a modern assumption about markets that neither of my rules captures, please say so plainly rather than forcing the dealer into one of them — I'd much rather learn my question is confused now than read about it in my professor's margins.
