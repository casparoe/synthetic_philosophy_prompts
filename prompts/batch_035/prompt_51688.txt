My friend Dara thinks she refuted my whole view of truth over coffee yesterday and I've been in a bad mood about it ever since. The topic was the Liar. Her argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Take the Liar sentence L, which says of itself that it's not true.
(2) L can't be true, since if it were, things would be as it says, and it says it's not true.
(3) L can't be false either, since then things would be as it says and it would be true.
(4) The natural escape is to say L simply has no truth value — it's not false, it's gapped, like asking whether the number seven is tired.
(5) But if L has no truth value, then L is not true, since "not true" applies to everything that fails to be true, gapped or not.
(6) And L says of itself exactly that it's not true.
(7) A sentence that says that so-and-so, where so-and-so is in fact the case, is true.
(8) So L is true after all, and the gap from (4) closed the moment we tried to stand on it.

Her conclusion: there's no stable resting place, truth is an inconsistant concept, some sentences are both true and not true, and everyone who denies it is just protecting classical logic like a comfort blanket.

Here's what bugs me. I can sort of feel that "not true" is doing different work in (5) and (6) — in (5) it's the wide negation ("fails to be true"), in (6) it's whatever L itself means — and maybe (7) is where the two get quietly identified. But when I tried saying that she just asked me which premise I'm rejecting and what it costs, and I realised I couldn't answer. If I keep the gap in (4) but reject (5), do I end up saying gapped sentences are somehow true? If I keep (5) and reject (7), what happens to ordinary sentences like "snow is white" that seem obviously covered by it? And she also made a side point I grudgingly verified: self-reference can't be the culprit, because "this sentence has five words" is self-referential, true, and perfectly well behaved. So whatever kills L has to kill it while sparing that one.

So: which step do you reject, why did that step look fine to me for so long, and what does rejecting it cost elsewhere — does it break anything outside the paradoxes, or only stuff in the neighbourhood? If I'm forced to choose I'd rather save (4) than (7), but tell me honestly if that's the expensive pick. And please flag where your own diagnosis is most likely to fail — what would someone have to point to in order to show you've named the wrong step, or that the slide is somewhere else entirely. I want to walk back into that coffee shop with more than a vibe.
