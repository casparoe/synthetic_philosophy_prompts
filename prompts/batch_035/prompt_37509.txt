For the closing session of my graduate seminar on social epistemology, I would like a philosophical dialogue of roughly 900 to 1,200 words between two colleagues who have just left a faculty meeting at which the following occurred: a junior woman offered a decisive methodological objection to a proposed study; her objection was met with silence; ten minutes later a senior man restated it in nearly the same words, and the room took it up at once, crediting him. The two characters should disagree about what kind of failing this was, and about what, if anything, should be done about it.

One character speaks broadly in the register of Miranda Fricker's work on epistemic injustice: the room's silence was a case of testimonial injustice — a prejudice-driven deflation of the speaker's credibility that wrongs her in her capacity as a knower, over and above any loss to the group's collective accuracy. This character should also deploy, where it arises naturally, two further distinctions. First, Fricker's observation that the credibility excess enjoyed by the senior man on the rebound — the room's inflated assessment of him when he spoke the same words — is not, in itself, an injustice to its beneficiary, though it is structurally connected to the deficit that produced it. Second, the pair of silencing practices identified by Kristie Dotson: testimonial quieting, in which the hearer simply fails to take up the testimony at all, and testimonial smothering, in which the speaker anticipates that her testimony will not be safely taken up and withholds it altogether. The remedy this character favors is in the register of virtue: audiences becoming the kind of people who correct for identity prejudice in their spontaneous credibility judgments.

The other character is a formal epistemologist who wants to resettle the territory in statistical terms. On this telling, what happened in the meeting is a calibration failure: the room's posterior on the junior colleague's testimony was systematically underweighted relative to her track record, and the senior man's was correspondingly overweighted; the remedy is not virtue but machinery — anonymized circulation of objections before meetings, documented track records, calibration training. This character should press the charge that "become a better hearer" under-generates policy, and should observe that quieting and smothering, too, admit of statistical description: an uptake probability below what accuracy demands, and an anticipated uptake probability low enough that silence is the speaker's utility-maximizing strategy.

The crux of the dialogue — and I want it to actually reach this point, not merely gesture at it — should be the hermeneutical case, which I want staged inside the conversation rather than mentioned in passing. Suppose that, partway through, the two characters recall a third incident: for years, members of some group have suffered a harm they cannot articulate; no shared concept exists for the pattern, so they have only fragments and analogies, and cannot lodge a grievance, because the concepts needed to make the complaint intelligible have never been coined. Have the Frickerian character present this as hermeneutical injustice, a wrong done by a gap in the collective interpretive resources. Have the formal epistemologist attempt the calibration treatment — and here I want this character to be genuinely good at their job: they can grant that this is a case where the relevant hypothesis is absent from the partition altogether, so that no amount of updating within the existing hypothesis space can ever reach it; however abundant the evidence, it cannot speak for a hypothesis that no one has yet formulated. The formal epistemologist may then either concede that this case exceeds the calibration framework, or attempt to extend it — for instance, by treating the remedy as a deliberate revision of the partition itself, an act of conceptual engineering that the formal apparatus can describe but not, perhaps, motivate. Which way the character goes is up to you, but the choice should be visible and consequential for the exchange.

Formal requirements: both characters must be equally competent, and neither may be a strawman; each must respond to the other's actual points rather than talk past them. The dialogue should not end in a knockout for either side. Let it close with an honest statement of what remains unresolved — for instance, the Frickerian wondering whether a concession about the hermeneutical case, once made, quietly concedes the calibration story everywhere else, or the formal epistemologist wondering whether a partition-revision remedy is still an epistemology at all rather than a politics by other means.
