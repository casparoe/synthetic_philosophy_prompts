I'm in a seminar on irrationality this semester and I built an argument for my term paper that my TA says proves way too much, and honestly now I can't unsee the problem but I also can't find the actual flaw. Hoping someone can walk me through it.

The topic is self-deception. Here's the argument as it stands:

(1) Deception is an intentional act: someone who knows (or at least believes) the truth arranges the evidence so that someone else comes to believe something false.
(2) In self-deception there's only one person avalable to play both roles, so deceiver and deceived are the same person at the same time.
(3) Nobody can believe p and believe not-p at the same time — if you somehow hold both, you don't really hold either, you're just confused or believing nothing.
(4) So the self-deceiver would have to believe the lump is probably malignant (otherwise she wouldn't know what to hide from herself) and believe it's probably nothing (otherwise she isn't deceived), at the same time.
(5) By (3) that's impossible, so genuine self-deception is impossible, and everything we call self-deception is really just wishful evidence-gathering that conveniently stops before belief forms, or plain akrasia.

Now the part thats bugging me. My TA pointed out that the same reasoning would prove you can't keep a secret from your future self, and you obviously can — I can hide my girlfriend's birthday present in October knowing I'll go snooping in December. And it gets weirder: I can apparently predict my own future motivated reasoning, like "if I check my grades after two beers I'll somehow convince myself the C+ doesn't matter," and those self-predictions are often right. That seems really hard to explain if the self-deceiver literally cannot represent the truth she's hiding from herself. Plus (5) proves too much empirically — there's a whole psychology literature on motivated reasoning where beliefs systematically bend toward what people want to be true, and if my argument worked, those researchers would be studying a ghost.

So which premise do I give up? I'd rather keep (1) if I can, because the deception analogy does a lot of work in my paper, but I'm not sure I get to. What does rejecting each one actually cost? Like, if I deny (2), am I stuck saying the "deceiver" is some subpersonal mechanism and not me at all, and does that save the phenomenon or quietly change the subject? If I deny (3), do I end up saying self-deceivers believe contradictions? And is there some reading of the argument where the deceiver and deceived are the same person but not at the same time, or not in the same state, that dodges the whole problem without giving up any premise outright? I keep feeling like the slide happens somewhere between (2) and (4) but I can't put my finger on it.
