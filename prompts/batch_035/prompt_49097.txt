So, bit of background on me: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training beyond some youtube and one aborted attempt at Reasons and Persons, and I've somehow agreed to give a talk at my local AI/rationalist-ish meetup (about 40 people, mostly engineers, a couple neuro-adjacent folks) arguing that the Libet experiments don't prove what everyone at that meetup thinks they prove. You know the take — "your brain decides 300ms before 'you' do, free will is just a UI illusion" — it gets thrown around there constantly like it's settled physics.

My thesis: Libet's setup shows at most that arbitrary movements have neural precursors, and that flicking your wrist whenever the urge strikes is a terrible proxy for the kind of deciding anyone actually cares about. Sketch of my argument, roughly: (1) the readiness potential in that task is a slow drift that only really shows up when you average over tons of trials, and averaging can manufacture an "early signal" even when nothing got decided early — sort of like how a rolling average on your metrics dashboard can make a spike look like it started minutes before it did; (2) subjects reporting when they first felt the urge by watching a dot move around a clock face is like asking users when a page started feeling slow — introspective timestamps are laggy and partly reconstructed after the fact; (3) even granting all of it, "you didn't consciously initiate this arbitrary flick" isn't the same as "no action you take is yours."

The crowd: a few people who've actually read the papers, a hard-determinist guy who will 100 percent tell me I'm just a laggy front-end on top of the real model, and ML people who'll push some line about the readiness potential being an accumulator, which I only half understand. Somebody will bring up Libet's own "veto" thing, the free-won't move where you can consciously squelch the flick at the last moment, and honestly I don't know what to say about that one.
