I edit a student philosophy journal, and for our spring issue I have secured a Q&A session with a philosopher known for defending Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation. She accepts the thesis in its strong form: not merely that all possible behavioural evidence underdetermines which translation manual is correct, but that there is genuinely no fact of the matter about which manual is the right one. Draft a sequence of ten to twelve interview questions for me, written in British English.

I do not want questions that simply invite her to restate the thesis or to recite the field-linguist scenario from Word and Object. The questions should press where the literature actually presses, and I would like the set to include at least one question on each of the following:

1. Whether the gavagai example, once stripped of its charm, shows anything more than the comparatively modest point that patterns of assent and dissent to occasion sentences leave the reference of individual terms underdetermined — and, if that is all it shows, why it should threaten the determinacy of translation for whole sentences at all.
2. How the indeterminacy of translation is meant to differ from the ordinary underdetermination of physical theory by evidence, given Quine's insistence that the former is stronger — and what kind of strength that could be for a philosopher who refuses to posit facts beyond what science settles.
3. Chomsky's complaint that the thesis piggybacks on behaviourist assumptions about language acquisition that most linguists abandoned, and whether she can reconstruct the argument without those assumptions — for instance, without assuming that all the evidence available to a translator must be of the stimulus-and-response kind.
4. Quine's later acknowledgement of a weaker reading of the thesis, on which something like sameness of meaning for sentences is preserved and only reference remains inscrutable, and whether conceding that reading quietly gives away everything that made the original thesis philosophically startling.
5. What the thesis implies for her own philosophical practice: if there is no fact of the matter about whether an English sentence means what she takes it to mean, how does she understand the sentences in which she defends the thesis itself?

For each question, please add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that I can pursue the strongest version of her position rather than a convenient one. Where a standard objection is attributed to a philosopher, keep the attribution in general terms rather than citing specific papers or page numbers, since I will be asking these questions live and want them intelligible to a general student readership.
