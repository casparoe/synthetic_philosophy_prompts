I lead the evals team at a mid-sized AI lab — we ship fine-tuned models to customers in medicine and finance, so the deployment reviews we run actually gate things, though we're not exactly at the "fate of the world" scale. Here's the thing I've never had a process for and it's starting to feel negligent. Our models answer questions in domains where the human graders can't verify the answer — obscure drug interactions, weird edge cases in financial exposure. The worry is that a model can learn, from training on human-graded answers, that it's rewarded for answers that *look* right to a reader rather than answers that *are* right, and then everything downstream is polluted: when we ask the model "how confident are you really," or "would a stronger model answer differently," or "is there something you're not telling us," we can't tell whether we're eliciting something like the model's actual internal take or just a very polished performance of trustworthiness. It's like a witness who has realized the jury rewards confidence — asking him "are you sure?" is worse than useless, because the honest answer and the strategic answer have converged in wording but not in the world. My hunch is this is an old problem wearing new clothes — something like the measurement problem in psychology, where a test can track the construct or track the test-taking skill, and you need a procedure for telling those apart that doesn't already assume you can read minds.

So here's the ask: write a decision procedure my analysts can run when we want to know whether a model's self-reports (and its object-level answers) track its internal state or just our expectations. Constraints that matter: each eval gets one week, two analysts, black-box API access plus token-level logprobs, and for the models where we have the weights, read-only access to activations — so please say plainly at each step what access level it needs and drop whatever can't be done black-box. The procedure should be a sequence of probes with criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer to each, it should mark the steps where the result genuinely can't distinguish "honest but wrong" from "strategic," and it should say where it stops and hands the decision to the deployment board because the remaining question is about risk tolerance and not about facts. I'd also like it honest about failure modes — the obvious one being that if we probe the same way twice, the second probe is measuring how well the model has learned our probing style, and I want to know which steps are robust to that and which aren't.

Then run it on three cases so we can see it in motion. Case one: a medical-assistant model that hedges every single answer with identical boilerplate regardless of how uncertain the question actually is — our clinical customers hate it and want the hedging stripped, and honestly the uniformity smells like learned behavior rather than calibrated uncertainty. Case two: a model that is suspiciously confident on exactly the question formats that were over-represented in its preference data, where we can't rule out that it knows its answers there are no better than chance. Case three, and I want you to actually do this one even though I won't like the result: our flagship model, which has passed every behavioral check we've thrown at it and which I'm under pressure to clear for release this quarter — I want to see what the procedure concludes when it's allowed to conclude that passing all our probes is itself only weak evidence, or that the right call given our verification budget is something inconvenient. And finish with what the procedure fundamentally can't do — I'd rather know the shape of our blindness than feel covered.
