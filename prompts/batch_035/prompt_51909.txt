ok so, background: I'm a software engineer, zero formal history or philosophy training, but I've gotten genuinely obsessed with the "was the British Raj on net good or bad for India" debate. On team A you've got Niall Ferguson (Empire: How Britain Made the Modern World, 2003), whose whole thing is basically treating empire like a balance sheet: yes there were horrific debits, but also real credits — railways, the english language, rule of law stuff, integration into global trade. On team B you've got Shashi Tharoor (Inglorious Empire, 2017), whose thing is: look at the actual ledger and it's all debits — India's share of world GDP collapses over the colonial period, deindustrialization, famines, the drain of wealth to London. And I've noticed these two camps argue past each other CONSTANTLY. The Tharoor side says "you can't say railways were a gift when they were built to extract resources and indians paid for them out of taxes," the Ferguson side says "the motive doesn't erase the asset," and it just goes in circles.

so here's my ask. Act as a professional historian of modern South Asia who has also facilitated adversarial collaborations before (that thing where two opposed researchers write a joint paper stating exactly what they agree on, exactly where they disagree in wording both accept, and what evidence would actually move either of them). Imagine a committed Ferguson-style "balance sheet imperialist" and a committed Tharoor-style anti-colonialist did this properly, with you as the neutral drafter.

Think carefully and actually brainstorm before you write: start by listing candidate agreed claims and stress-testing each one ("would the Tharoor side really sign this? would the Ferguson side?"), then produce the final document.

The final document should be structured as:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — things BOTH could put their name to. Not mush. E.g. I'd expect them to agree on plain facts like the Bengal famine of 1943 being worsened by policy decisions, and on meta-points like "the exact size of the 'drain of wealth' is contested even among economists who agree it existed." Figure out where the genuine overlap is, including on things people usually assume are contested.

2. A disagreements table — each row is one disagreement, phrased twice: once in wording the Ferguson-style side would accept as fair to their view, once in wording the Tharoor-style side would accept as fair to theirs. The phrasing is the whole game here, don't let either side smuggle in a framing the other would reject ("gift" vs "looted infrastructure" etc).

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where settling one would actually move at least one side. I suspect one big one is the counterfactual baseline: what would India plausibly look like in 1900 or 1947 absent British rule (compare Japan's Meiji path? fragmented princedoms? some other colonial power instead?). Another might be whether "credits" should be discounted by whether they'd have arrived anyway (railways were spreading everywhere — does that matter?). Another might be whether intent and who paid matters vs. just outcomes. But don't just take my list, reason about what the real cruxes are.

4. For each crux: a concrete case, comparison, or argument that BOTH sides would agree bears on it (not a gotcha either side would dismiss), plus each side's prediction written down in advance, like they're pre-registering.

One more thing: at the end, include a short section on whether any part of their dispute is purely verbal — like, are they sometimes using "net benefit" with different implicit accounting rules (does "net" include effects on Britain? on third countries? does it amortize costs over centuries?) rather than actually disagreeing about facts. If you think some sub-dispute is verbal, say which and why.

please don't give me a both-sides mush where every disagreement gets softened into agreement, and also don't have either side cave — the whole point is a document where the residual disagreement is made EXACT, sharp enough that each camp would sign their own row and grudgingly admit the other side's row is fair. Length: go as long as you need, this is the kind of thing where I want the details.
