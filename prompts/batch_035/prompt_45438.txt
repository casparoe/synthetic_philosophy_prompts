I'm taking a grad seminar on vagueness this semester and something about how epistemicism and supervaluationism usually get compared keeps bugging me. The standard story is: epistemicism says there's a sharp cutoff between heap and non-heap that we somehow can't know, supervaluationism says there's no single cutoff but a range of admissible sharpenings of the predicate, and then everyone immediately pivots to the sorites paradox and never looks back. But here's a framing I've been chewing on and want you to take seriously even though I half made it up myself: both views seem to agree that the vagueness lives in language (or in us) rather than in the world — the world itself doesn't care whether 57 grains of sand is a heap. Where they differ, on my framing, is what the indeterminacy is *like*: for the epistemicist it's like an unopened envelope (there's a determinate fact in there, we just can't get at it), while for the supervaluationist it's more like an unmade decision (our linguistic practice simply never settled the question, so there's no fact waiting to be found). So what I'd like you to do is compare the two theories specifically through this envelope-vs-decision lens: which of them, if either, actually delivers on vagueness being a feature of our language rather than of the world, and does the supervaluationist's line that all the sharpenings are perfectly precise and vagueness only shows up at the superlevel (supertruth, superfalsity, truth-value gaps) really work, or does it just relocate the problem one story up? After all, you can ask the exact same question about the set of admissible sharpenings that we asked about the cutoff: is *that* set sharp, and if not, haven't we just started a regress?

Related thing I want your honest opinion on: a friend of mine in the seminar claims the whole debate is kind of fake, because "admissible sharpening" is doing the same work for the supervaluationist that "candidate meaning" or "meaning compatible with our usage" would do for the epistemicist, so the two views are just two bookkeeping systems over the same underlying stuff. She says the only real difference is the rhetoric — epistemicists talk about a hidden fact where supervaluationists talk about a fact that was never there. Do you buy that deflationary take, or is there a substantive disagreement underneath? If you think it's substantive, I'd like to see where exactly it shows up — for example in what each theory should say about a sentence like "there is a fact of the matter whether 57 grains is a heap" (as opposed to the heap sentence itself), or about whether borderline speakers who confidently assert either side are making a mistake vs merely saying something untrue vs something that's in some way neither. And along the way give me your take on whether higher-order vagueness really is worse for one of the two theories than the other, or whether that's a myth that each side tells about the other. Please answer in American English, and don't just give me the textbook summaries — I've read those, they're exactly what I'm trying to get past. Go for the strongest version of each side's answer to my framing, even if the strong version requires fixing up the theory a bit.
