My grandfather has had a pacemaker for eight years, and his dementia has advanced to the point where he can no longer swallow safely. Last week his cardiology team told us that, since he is clearly dying, they can turn the device off non-invasively — it is a reprogramming, not a surgery — and his heart will then fail only if it is too weak to keep a rhythm on its own. The decision has fallen to me as his healthcare agent, and I spent most of Saturday night arguing about it with Daniel, my oldest friend, who retired last year after decades as an ICU nurse. We left on bad terms, and I have until Friday to give the hospital an answer.

Daniel says turning the pacemaker off is something we would be doing to my grandfather rather than something we would be refraining from doing for him: someone has to take a deliberate step, the step changes the condition of his body, and the new condition is what his death runs through. I keep replying that his heart disease, not us, is what kills him, and that the pacemaker never replaced a function the way a ventilator replaces breathing — it only fires when his own rhythm fails, so switching it off just hands the job back to his heart and lets things proceed as they would have without the device.

Late in the argument Daniel offered a test he liked: an outcome counts as something you did whenever it depends on your continued contribution. His examples were that holding someone's head underwater is doing something to them, while failing to throw a rope to a drowning swimmer is not, because the drowning proceeds on its own whether or not you exist. By that test, he says, the pacemaker sits with the held head, not the missing rope: the moment we stop contributing, the death we have been staving off arrives.

I want more than a gut feeling before Friday. Is Daniel right that turning the pacemaker off is best understood as an act against my grandfather rather than a withdrawal of help? Is his continued-contribution test a sound way to draw the line he is trying to draw — and even if the test is sound, does it really classify the pacemaker the way he says? Please also tell me plainly where you think my own reasoning is weakest, and what consideration, if it held up, would move you to the opposite verdict — I would rather know the fault lines in the reasoning than be reassured. Keep your answer under 450 words.
