I'm an undergraduate taking a philosophy of physics seminar that has spent most of the semester on the Everett interpretation. Everyone in the room accepts, or at least is willing to grant for the sake of argument, the same formalism: unitary evolution, no collapse, branching worlds, and the Born weights as part of the formal apparatus. Yet we have now stalled for three sessions on quantum suicide and quantum immortality, and the stall has a strange structure I'd like help diagnosing for my term paper.

The two camps, as concretely as I can state them. One side holds that if Everett is right and experience is purely a physical process, then a person hooked up to a device that kills them iff a quantum measurement comes out one way should expect, from the first person, to see the survival outcome every time: there is always some branch containing a successor who sees it. Extrapolated — every aging process, every bout of illness, every lethal risk being, at bottom, a branching event — each of us should expect to find ourselves among the ever-smaller set of branches where something, however improbable, keeps the body going. Call this the survival-expectation view. The other side holds that this is a straightforward confusion: the surviving branches carry vanishing Born measure, and treating the mere existence of a branch as sufficient for rational expectation is exactly like arguing that because some heavy smoker reaches 112, smoking is compatible with expecting to reach 112. Rational agents weight future experiences by amplitude, not by existence; the rest is anthropic sleight of hand. Call this the measure view. Both sides know all the standard moves, and nobody has budged.

My working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that the disagreement is not about physics at all — the formalism is fixed — but about the bridge principle connecting Born weights to subjective anticipation. The survival-expectation side seems to hold that rational credence about which future experiences are mine is constrained only by the existence of a suitable successor-experiencer, while the measure side holds that amplitude must enter into self-locating credence itself. And here is the wrinkle I find most interesting: the measure side's usual support is the decision-theoretic program — deriving, in the spirit of Deutsch and Wallace, that a rational Everettian agent must act as if weighting branches by Born measure. But that derivation arguably goes through only for agents who receive their payoffs in the branches in question, and death is precisely the case where the agent collects no payoff anywhere. So the survival-expectation side may not merely be confused; they may have found the load-bearing case that the decision-theoretic answer to the probability objection doesn't cover. Is that right, or can the decision-theoretic machinery be extended to cover mortal stakes — say, by framing survival itself as the payoff, or by involving beneficiaries of one's gamble?

More broadly, I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey. Is this a substantive disagreement, or partly verbal — for instance, if the two sides mean different things by expecting an outcome (betting readiness versus felt anticipation of waking up)? Is there a hidden empirical question doing real work, such as whether a brain undergoing lethal injury always has continuous-in-experience branches of nonzero amplitude, or whether experience can degrade discontinuously enough that there is no successor at all in some death events? Is one side answering the question of what to expect before a measurement while the other answers what to anticipate from within the post-measurement set, so that they are, strictly, not contradicting each other? And given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a sharper formal account of self-locating credence in branching, a clarified constraint from physics or neuroscience on the continuity of experience, a decision-theoretic result covering gambles with death — or an argument that, as with some anthropic puzzles, the impasse reflects an ill-posed question and the right move is to stop asking it. If you think part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why; and if you think part is genuinely substantive, tell me exactly which sub-question it is that settling would move at least one camp.
