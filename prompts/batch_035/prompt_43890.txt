I've been reading the decision-theory corners of the internet for over a decade, and I thought I'd seen every variation on St. Petersburg that matters — finite bankrolls, unbounded utilities, Pascal's mugging, the Kelly crowd telling everyone to stop worrying. But a variant I started building to amuse myself on the train has landed me somewhere I can't find a name for, and I'd like to know whether the configuration has a label and a literature of its own, or whether I've genuinely wandered off the map.

Here is the game. Flip a fair coin until it first lands heads. If the first heads comes on flip n, the payoff is (−1)^n · 2^n / n dollars, where negative amounts are ones you pay to the house. Heads immediately and you lose $2; tails then heads and you win $2; tails, tails, heads and you lose about $2.67; and so on, with the stakes growing without bound in both directions.

Now compute the expected value. The probability of first heads on flip n is 2^-n, and the 2-powers cancel against the payoff, so each flip contributes (−1)^n / n. The expectation is the series −1 + 1/2 − 1/3 + 1/4 − ... — the alternating harmonic series, which converges to −ln 2, roughly negative 69 cents, if you add it in the natural order. But it converges only conditionally: the sum of absolute values diverges. And I remembered enough calculus to know what that buys you. Rearrange the terms and the sum can be made to equal anything you like, or to diverge. Meanwhile the natural order — by flip count — has no obvious claim to special status. An expectation is supposed to be a property of the distribution over payoffs, and a distribution doesn't come with an ordering attached. If two descriptions of the same game, with the same probabilities and the same payoffs, yield different fair prices depending on how the outcomes happen to be listed, then something in the machinery has broken — and I want to insist it's not the same thing that breaks in St. Petersburg. There, the expectation at least points in a direction: every truncation says huge and increasingly so. Here the machinery returns a different finite answer depending on what looks like pure bookkeeping, which feels strictly worse.

None of the standard moves seems to touch it. Truncating at N flips and letting N grow gives partial sums that oscillate and settle toward −ln 2, but I can't tell whether taking that limit is a theorem or a shrug; when I raised this on a forum, someone told me to just use the limit of finite truncations, which is precisely the move that conditional convergence is supposed to forbid.

So, questions. Does this configuration — an infinite-outcome lottery whose expectation is conditionally convergent, so that its value depends on an arbitrary ordering — have an established name? I know it isn't St. Petersburg proper, and I have a secondhand sense that the two-envelope paradox hides a conditionally convergent series somewhere in its switching argument; I'd like to know whether that's right and how the three relate. Is there a settled treatment: a principled answer to what the game is worth, or a proof that it has no value at all? I've heard vaguely that some people propose looking at long-run average payoff over many plays in cases where expectations misbehave, and I can't work out whether that rescues this game or deepens the trouble. If none of this has an established home, I'd still want the carving rather than a coinage and a wave: what exactly is the phenomenon, what is it not (I keep almost confusing it with a mere failure of invariance under re-description, and I don't think those are the same thing), and where does it sit relative to the familiar paradoxes of infinite expectation?
