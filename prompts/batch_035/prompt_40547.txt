I'm writing a novel in which a fabric merchant in Ottoman Damascus is told by a fortune-teller — one with a documented habit of being right, specifically about his own choices — that she has seen his death, and that he will die in Aleppo. This is, of course, the old decision-theory puzzle dressed in different clothes, but I'm writing it as a novel rather than as a counterexample, which means the scene where he decides whether to stay or flee has to carry real emotional weight, and I'd like to know where the weight actually sits before I write it.

Give me your credence that fleeing to Aleppo would extend his life by even a single day, assuming her predictions about his own future choices come true roughly four times in five. Then decompose the number: how much rides on whether her vision is a fixed fact about the world versus a report of what he was already going to do; on whether hearing the prophecy changes what it prophesies; on whether, if her accuracy is genuine, any act available to him can beat it at all. Assign a rough probability to each component, show how they combine, and tell me which component the final figure is most sensitive to.

Then commit, because I have to: does he flee or stay, and does he do it in hope or in resignation? I want a definite answer with your reasoning behind it, not a survey of positions — if I disagree, I'll push back, but I need something concrete to push against.
