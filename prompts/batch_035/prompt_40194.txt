I'm a software engineer with no genetics or stats training beyond what leaks into ML blog posts, and I've spent the last month in a Slack war with my coworker Dave about heritability — the number you get from twin and sibling studies. We keep talking past each other, so last week I sat down and wrote out my actual gut reactions to a bunch of claims people make about that number, and I've realised the verdicts are the only thing I'm sure of. I want to know if there's a single principle underneath them, or whether I'm just vibes-matching. Here are the verdicts:

(1) "Human eye colour is almost entirely genetic" — fine. (2) "Height is around 80% heritable in the Netherlands, so the fact that the Dutch shot up over the last century and a half can't have been about nutrition or disease" — not fine, and I get mocked for this one because it sounds like I'm denying the 80%. (3) PKU: a disorder caused by a variant in one gene, so about as heritable as anything gets, and yet a dietary intervention completely prevents the harm it used to cause — fine, and to me it obviously shows "heritable" doesn't mean "fixed", though Dave thinks I'm sneaking something in. (4) "If IQ is 50% heritable, then half of any given kid's intelligence comes from genes and half from environment" — not fine, though I couldn't explain to Dave why without hand-waving about variance vs. slices of a person. (5) Take one tomato genotype, clone it, grow the cuttings in two fields with differnt soil: within each field there's no genetic variation so heritability is zero, yet the between-field difference is entirely environmental — I'm comfortable with this, but it makes Dave throw up his hands. (6) Those classic old experiments where rats were selectively bred to be good or bad at running a maze, and then in very enriched or very deprived environments the bred-in difference basically vanished — I think that's fine as a demonstration that a genetic difference can be conditional on the environment in a way a single heritability figure hides, but I'm honestly not sure I could defend that against (1). (7) The one I keep going back and forth on, and this is where I need the most help: a trait can be highly heritable within each of two populations while the difference between those populations is entirely environmental — I want to say that's fine, but I also have a gut verdict lurking that if a trait is measured near 100% heritable in a modern well-fed population, then attributing group gaps in it to environment feels like fighting the data. Those two verdicts seem to pull against each other and I can't tell if one has to go.

So: is there one account of what heritability actually is — I suspect something about it being a ratio of variances within a particular population in a particular range of environments, but I've never seen it written out carefully — that gets all seven verdicts, or nearly? If it exists, I want it stated precisely enough to apply mechanically, with the clause of the principle that handles each verdict, and then I want it run on Dave's favourite claim, which is that since schizophrenia is 60-80% heritable, funding searches for environmental causes is mostly a waste — something about that still smells wrong to me but I can't say from first principles whether the number itself supports him or not. Also please tell me which of my verdicts the principle forces me to drop, since I suspect at least one of (6) and (7) is in trouble, and predict what the principle says about a case I haven't listed: what should happen to the measured heritability of, say, eyesight as an environment becomes universally good vs. wildly variable.
