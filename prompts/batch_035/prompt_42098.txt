I write about science and ideas for a living, and the thing I keep circling back to is drug scheduling. Under the US Controlled Substances Act the whole edifice turns on two criteria: potential for abuse, and whether a drug has any currently accepted medical use. Cannabis and MDMA sit in Schedule I — no accepted medical use — while cocaine and methamphetamine are only Schedule II, because those do have accepted uses. Alcohol and tobacco aren't in the system at all. And here's the part I can't get past: the same molecule, same pharmacology, same dangers, can move from Schedule I to Schedule II purely because medicine decided it has a use. Nothing about the substance changed. Meanwhile, the famous Nutt rankings in The Lancet concluded alcohol was the most harmful drug overall, above heroin and crack, and leagues ahead of cannabis or LSD. So help me think this through: what theory of harm does the scheduling practice actually presuppose? Is "danger" being treated as partly constituted by medical and legal recognition rather than intrinsic to the substance? Does leaving alcohol and tobacco out require some view like 'harm only counts when it flows from abuse of a non-established substance,' or is the practice compatible with several theories of harm and is really just historical accretion dressed up as a ranking? And if we rebuilt scheduling around an actual harm function, what would have to change — would alcohol end up scheduled, or would the entire idea of ranking drugs this way collapse into something else entirely? I'd also love to know where the practice is underdetermined, ie which parts genuinely depend on the theory of harm and which could be justified under almost any view. Sorry for the rambling, this has been bugging me for ages.
