I am a novelist currently drafting a book set in London in the early 1960s, at the height of the quarrel between Lord Devlin and Professor H. L. A. Hart over whether the criminal law may punish private acts simply because a majority of the public finds them immoral — the dispute that grew out of the Wolfenden Committee's recommendation that homosexual acts between consenting adults be decriminalized. My narrator is a barrister named Aldous Fenn, and I want him to deliver, in a dinner-party scene, the argument by analogy on which Devlin's side came to rest: society is not held together by force but by a shared moral fabric, invisible and interlocking like the weave of cloth or the rivets of a ship's hull, and since no one can say in advance which thread or rivet is load-bearing, a society is entitled — in something like self-defence — to suppress even private immorality, lest the whole thing quietly come apart. My editor is delighted by this passage and wants Fenn's speech to win the evening, but my own instinct, as someone who spends his working hours inventing seamless fictions, is that an analogy that sounds load-bearing may be merely ornamental, and I would like an honest audit before I decide how the scene plays out.

Please give me the audit Fenn's dinner companions never got. Set out explicitly what maps onto what in the fabric-or-rivets picture: which parts of collective life are the threads, who or what is the weaver, what counts as a tear as opposed to ordinary wear, and what role the idea of structural collapse is playing. Then identify where the mapping breaks — noting, for instance, that societies have abolished slavery, tolerated religious dissent, and liberalized divorce without dissolving, which suggests that the fabric undergoes wholesale re-weaving rather than catastrophic unravelling. Distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the legal question of enforcing morals from the ones that, however interesting, do no real work. Then tell me whether Devlin's legal-moralist conclusion survives once the fabric picture is in ruins — whether there is a repaired version of the argument that does not lean on it — and, conversely, whether Hart's own counter-argument, with its appeal to society's capacity to survive changes in its moral code, quietly depends on an analogy of its own that deserves the same scrutiny. I intend to close my chapter with whichever side has the argument rather than the better simile, so please be as hard on my narrator as you would be on Devlin.
