Conservation law protects named biological entities: a species appears on an endangered list, and everything downstream — funding, habitat designation, penalties for harming individuals — attaches to the list entry. Two features of this arrangement are philosophically puzzling. First, taxonomy is unsettled and keeps moving: when taxonomists split one widely distributed species into two, a population with no special status yesterday can instantly become a critically endangered species, and when they lump two rare species into one, protection can evaporate, all without a single animal dying or being born. Second, hybrids generally fall outside protection altogether: a rare species interbreeding with a common relative produces offspring that count as neither, and in some programs animals are removed or even culled for being hybrids, precisely to keep the protected entity pure. What view of what a species is does this body of law and practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit ontology — is it treating species as historical individuals with a continuity and a fate of their own, as natural kinds with essences that hybridization adulterates, or merely as bookkeeping units whose boundaries the law has to fix somewhere, even arbitrarily? Then tell me whether the practice strictly requires one of these views or is compatible with several, and what a legal regime built on the rival position — that species boundaries are conventional, so that a hybrid is no less valuable than a purebred animal — would have to change, beyond simply extending the list of protected entities. As you go, say how confident you are in your claims, and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of. Write in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter.
