I am a novelist, at the outline stage of a multi-generational work whose later chapters I cannot yet see clearly. The story will span roughly a century, and a structural question has begun to press on me: should the present be treated, within the fiction, as a singular moment in history, or as one corridor among many? I want to answer that question honestly rather than by temperament, and so I turn to a claim advanced in recent philosophy under the name of the "hinge of history" hypothesis: roughly, that people alive now occupy the most influential time there has ever been — that the long-run future will be settled, in large part, by what is done or left undone in the coming decades. As I understand it, the claim typically rests on three premises: that if humanity survives this period, the future could be vastly longer and richer than the past; that existential risks are unusually concentrated in a present "time of perils," after which they will either subside or take us with them; and that decisions taken now — about technology, institutions, and values — can shape which future arrives, perhaps by fixing a pattern later generations cannot revise.

I confess the idea tempts me as a novelist; a character who believes her generation is the hinge of history is a compelling figure. But I also recall Tolstoy, who devoted much of the epilogue to War and Peace to mocking the habit of explaining vast outcomes by the choices of a few men in a narrow window, and I wonder whether the hinge hypothesis is, at bottom, the great-man theory of history promoted to the scale of centuries.

Write an essay that weighs the case for and against the hypothesis and reaches a verdict. Please address at least the following threads, and add others as you see fit:

1. The premise structure. How much of the hypothesis's plausibility rests on the three premises above, and where each could fail — for instance, if the future conditional on survival is not especially long; if existential risk remains roughly constant for many centuries rather than falling after a brief peril; or if present choices diffuse rather than compound.

2. The "every century is a hinge" worry. People in the past could have argued, with equal confidence, that their moment was decisive — the printing press, the first nuclear weapons. If apparent hinges are historically common, we should distrust our own conviction that ours is the real one. Assess this symmetry argument and its limits.

3. The anthropic tangle. If the future holds vastly more people than the past, should we be surprised to find ourselves so early? Does that surprise count for or against the hinge hypothesis, or does it cut no ice at all?

4. Lock-in and its skeptics. Some hold that values or institutions, once fixed by powerful technology, become effectively permanent — making ours the last generation with a genuine choice. Others doubt that any lock-in survives the centuries. Which view is more credible, and how much does the hypothesis depend on it?

5. The Tolstoyan objection. Is the hinge hypothesis, as I suspect, the great-man theory scaled up — or can a defender articulate a principled difference between "this century matters" and "this man matters"?

Reach a conclusion and defend it. A qualified verdict — that this century is unusually consequential without being uniquely so — is acceptable if that is where the arguments genuinely land, but do not end on a shrug. Then close by telling me what developments over the next fifty years would most shake your verdict; that, more than anything, will tell me how my later chapters should treat the present.

No citations or bibliographies are needed — reason from the considerations as I have set them out.
