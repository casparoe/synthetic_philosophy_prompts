I spent thirty-nine years in physical chemistry, most of them measuring the microwave and infrared spectra of transient gas-phase molecules, and I came to philosophy of physics only in retirement. During my working life I used the quantum state the way a joiner uses a level: constantly, competently, and without once asking what it is. Now that I finally ask, I find the field divided into two camps I'll label, following the usual terminology, psi-ontic (the quantum state is itself a feature of the world, as an electromagnetic field is) and psi-epistemic (it represents an agent's information or expectations, as a probability distribution does). Both camps contain serious people, and my impression is that each spends much of its energy answering a slightly different question than the one a laboratory scientist actually wants answered.

Before I take sides, I would like the specification first, and that is what I am asking you to write: the desiderata that any adequate account of the status of the quantum state must satisfy. I want to fix the purpose explicitly, since adequacy is relative to it: I want an account good enough to guide a working scientist's stance toward the state in daily practice -- good enough, that is, to tell me whether to treat it as something out there in the molecule, or as bookkeeping about the molecule. Not a merely consistent interpretation; not a resolution of everything.

From my own practice I have some candidate requirements, and I want you to assess each -- is it a genuine desideratum, or a prejudice of one camp dressed up as a demand -- alongside any further requirements you think the list needs:

1. Replication without communication. Two laboratories on different continents, following the same published preparation procedure, with different apparatus, different backgrounds of information, and no communication, can produce systems whose quantum states are close enough to interfere coherently when combined. Whatever the state is, an account must explain what in the world underwrites this portability, or explain why no underwriting is needed.

2. The Born question. If the state is knowledge, the Born-rule probabilities are probabilities *of what*? An epistemic reading appears to owe us an ontology over which those probabilities range -- some underlying state of affairs -- or an argument for why no such ontology is required and what the probabilities then mean.

3. Surplus structure. The state carries phases and relative amplitudes that no classical probability table carries, and in computations of, say, a benzene ring's absorption the details of that structure do apparent causal work in predicting what the spectrometer records. An epistemic account owes an explanation of this surplus: why, if the state is merely someone's information, its fine structure tracks the world so faithfully.

4. Consistency with what is already established. The account must coexist with Bell-type results -- the underlying picture, whatever it is, cannot be local -- and with the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem, which as I understand it shows that a model treating the state as mere information about an underlying physical state, together with the assumption that independently prepared systems have independent physical states, contradicts quantum theory's predictions. I take that theorem to threaten a joint unsatisfiability claim, and that is the part I most want to see addressed: which desiderata on the final list are jointly unsatisfiable, which leg of such conflicts should be dropped, and at what cost.

5. Dual character. My own hunch, offered for your scrutiny, is that the state may be like thermodynamic entropy: a quantity that is simultaneously a physical thing, measurable by calorimeters and governing engines, and an information-like thing, whose value depends on the coarse-graining of the observer. If that analogy is apt, perhaps an adequate account must make the state capable of this dual character -- both out there and agent-relative -- without collapsing into one side. Or perhaps this is sentiment rather than a requirement; tell me honestly.

For each requirement on your final list, give the case or consideration that motivates it; mark which are non-negotiable in principle and which are preferences of particular camps masquerading as demands; flag any subset that is jointly unsatisfiable, and say so plainly if the list itself then amounts to a result; and identify which single requirement does the most to exclude the live candidates. If you think a requirement of mine is confused or question-begging, strike it from the list and say why -- I would rather have a shorter list that stands up than a longer one that flatters me.

Finally, a brief scorecard only, a few lines each, of how an Everett-style view, a Bohmian view (where the state is real but perhaps more like a law than a thing), a QBist or other neo-Copenhagen view, and a hidden-variable epistemic view fare against the list -- the weight should sit on the criteria and their motivating cases, not the scoring, and please do not append any reading lists or references; I want your reasoning, not a bibliography.
