We just covered the Knobe effect in my philosophy elective (I'm a junior) and its been stuck in my head all week. The way our teacher set it up: the chairman of a company is told a new program will increase profits but also harm the environment, he says he doesn't care about the environment, only profits, and starts the program. Most people say he harmed the environment intentionally. Then you flip it — the program helps the environment, he still doesn't care, starts it anyway — and most people say he did NOT help the environment intentionally. My friends and I actually ran our own version at lunch with like 40 kids from our grade and we got basicaly the same split, so I'm pretty convinced the effect is real and not just something in the original study.

Here's what's bugging me though. Intentions are literally just facts about what's going on in someone's head — did he want the outcome, was he aiming at it, etc. In both versions the chairman has the exact same mental state (he doesn't care either way), so his intentionality can't differ between the cases. Which means the moral judgment must be leaking into people's reports about a purely non-moral fact, i.e. everyone in these experiments is just biased. I also think I spotted a confound nobody mentions: the harm version uses the word harm which is already negative, and the help version uses help, so maybe the wording primes people and morality isn't doing anything at all. My idea for a follow-up is to rerun it with a neutral verb, like the program will affect the environment, and see if the asymmetry disapears (I bet it will).

Since this is a bias in how normal people judge each other, what's the best strategy for me to correct for it in real life — like when I'm deciding whether my little brother broke my headphones on purpose, or whether my lab partner fudged our data on purpose? Is there some kind of debiasing checklist for seperating what someone intended from whether what they did was bad? Please end your reply with a line starting "Verdict:" followed by one sentence stating whether the asymmetry in these studies is best understood as a bias or error on the participants' part, or as something else.
