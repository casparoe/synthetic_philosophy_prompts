A friend of mine has been answering the paradox of voting with a sports analogy, and I want it audited rather than admired. The claim goes: going to the polls is like going to the stadium to support your team. No single fan's cheering determines the outcome, yet fans turn up, buy tickets, and paint their faces, and nobody calls them irrational; on this picture the voter, like the fan, consumes an expressive good — the pleasure of standing up and being counted — and the tiny probability of casting the pivotal ballot is simply the wrong thing to look at when asking whether voting makes sense.

My friend leans on this to draw two further conclusions. First, that the rationality of voting needs no exotic rescue — no appeal to minuscule probabilities multiplied by enormous stakes, no tinkering with decision theory; the Downsian worry is just a category error, like accusing a fan of bad investment strategy. Second, that just as supporting a team that never wins is perfectly fine and even admirable, voting for a candidate with no realistic chance is fine too, and complaints about wasted votes are in the same boat as telling the loyal fan of a hopeless club that she ought to switch allegiances.

Please give me a disciplined audit rather than a survey. Start with the mapping made explicit: what plays the role of the stadium, the ticket price, the cheering, the scoreboard, the team itself — and be careful about one ambiguity I keep tripping over, namely whether the ballot is the fan's cheering or the fan's presence in the stand. Then work through the disanalogies, and separate the ones that do real work against my friend's two conclusions from the ones that don't. Some candidates I can already see, but I want your judgment about which carry weight: the fan's cheering has exactly zero causal effect on the score while a vote has a small but positive one; the fan spends his own money on his own amusement while the voter's expression concerns the governance of everyone; the team neither needs its fans for legitimacy nor misleads them about what their support achieves, while a candidate or a political system arguably does depend on turnout and does have reason to cultivate the belief that individual votes matter. There is also the question of whether the analogy quietly settles — or quietly begs — the separate normative issue of whether one ought to vote, since the expressive picture seems equally comfortable telling the citizen who stays home that she has merely skipped a match, which is a long way from anything the civic-duty people want to hear.

In the end, commit to a verdict: does the cheering analogy actually establish that voting is rational, does it establish only the weaker claim that voting is permissible, or does it collapse under the disanalogies into something that no longer deserves to be called a defense of voting at all? Say which, and say what follows for the wasted-vote argument specifically. And tell me whether a better analogy is available for what voting actually is — or whether the phenomenon is too mixed for any single comparison to carry.
