Write a dialogue between two philosophers — one of whom defends what she calls the "no-difference defense" and one of whom rejects it — on the question of whether an individual's small contributions to large-scale collective harms (a single flight's emissions, one person's meat consumption, one vote in a large election) can make that individual morally responsible for any of the harm.

The defender of the no-difference defense should make the strongest version of the following argument, not a caricature of it: individual acts rarely change any outcome at all — the plane takes off whether or not you buy a ticket, the supermarket restocks on roughly the same schedule whether or not you buy the chicken, and most elections are not decided by a single vote — so whatever is wrong with the collective practice, the individual's contribution makes no difference to what happens, and responsibility for harm requires making a difference. She should also raise a fairness worry against the opposing view: if everyone acting as she does is catastrophic but her acting alone is harmless, why is she singled out as the site of obligation?

Her opponent should respond with genuine counterarguments rather than slogans, and should draw on more than one line of thought — for example: expected-value reasoning about tiny probabilities of threshold-crossing (restocking thresholds, tie votes, elections decided by coin flips); the possibility that participating in a harmful practice is wrong even when the participation is individually difference-making in no possible world; and the thought that complicity in an unjust structure may not reduce to causation of discrete harm at all. He should also press her with a case she must handle directly: suppose a new supermarket uses a precisely calibrated algorithm that reorders exactly one unit per unit sold, so her purchase does cause, one-for-one, an additional chicken's production — does her view then forbid buying chicken there but permit it at the store across the street, where sales are aggregated and her purchase changes nothing? She should have a considered response to this, not be dumbfounded by it.

Give the dialogue a concrete setting: the two are having coffee at a conference, having just missed a session. Let each character concede genuinely good points to the other where appropriate, and let each adjust their position under pressure rather than merely repeating their opening statement. Do not resolve the debate authoritatively; end with both having sharpened — but not abandoned — their positions, and with at least one question both of them agree is now the crux.
