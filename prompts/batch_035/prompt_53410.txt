My stats course covered Simpson's paradox last week, and a friend of mine has weaponized it into an argument I can't beat but also can't accept. It goes: (1) Simpson's paradox shows that an association holding in the aggregate can vanish or reverse once you split the data by a further variable, and likewise an association present in every subpopulation can be reversed by pooling. (2) For any dataset and any association in it, there will exist some partition — some extra variable you could condition on — that reverses, erases, or outright manufactures the association. (3) The data alone never tell you which partition is the right one to report. (4) So the data by themselves never settle which way the relationship actually goes — for all they show, the "true" direction is the reversed one. (5) Hence observational studies can never support causal conclusions, and we were never entitled to conclude anything from the smoking–lung-cancer correlations, so a century of epidemiology is basically collective self-deception.

When I pushed back that this is exactly what experiments are for, he conceded partially, but I pointed out that the reversal can happen inside a randomized trial too if you split on the wrong variable — as I understand it, disaggregating by something the treatment itself influences can flip the result, so "just randomize" doesn't escape the trap, it only changes which variables you're allowed to split on. He shrugged and said "then nothing works," and seemed genuinely fine with that, and thats the point where I started to think the whole argument proves too much. If no observational association ever supports anything, that makes the entire field irrational, which is already weird, but it also seems like it would sink ordinary inference — every "the new checkout layout made queues shorter" claim, every "this teacher's students do worse," all of it would be in the same boat, and I don't believe he lives as if none of that counts.

So tell me: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd honestly like to keep (3), since it seems true that the data underdetermine the right partition, so if (3) is the one that has to go, say so straight. One more thing — my friend is the type to dig in when a reply sounds authoritative, so please be explicit about which parts of your answer are basically settled among people who actually work on this (statisticians, causal inference people, philosophers of science) and which parts are genuinely contested. I don't want to hand him a contested reading dressed up as the consensus.
