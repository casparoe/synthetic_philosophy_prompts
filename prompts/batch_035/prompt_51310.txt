My flatmate is midway through a masters in formal epistemology, and she has baited me with an argument about van Fraassen's Reflection principle that I cannot get out of my head. For those who, like me three weeks ago, have not met it: Reflection says that if you are certain that your future self, at some later time t, will assign credence r to a proposition A, then your present credence in A should be r. It is meant to be a requirement binding a single person across time — you defer in advance to your own future opinion, because anything else leaves you open to a diachronic Dutch book, in which a bookie who knows nothing except your own current and foreseeable dispositions extracts a guaranteed profit from you by trading with you at different times. So far, so plausible; I even found it elegant. Then she constructed the following, and I want to know exactly where it goes wrong, because I refuse to accept where it ends up.

(1) Reflection holds without exception: whenever I am certain that at t my credence in A will be r, my present credence in A should be r. (2) The case for Reflection is the diachronic Dutch book argument, which is structurally identical to the synchronic Dutch book argument for the probability axioms: violate either, and a bookie can guarantee himself a profit from you. (3) In my diary I have an appointment next Friday at which I will be hypnotised (a demonstration a friend is running — suspend disbelief) into complete confidence that the spare vase on my windowsill is full of water; I have just emptied and dried the vase and can see that it is empty. (4) I am certain that next Friday my credence that the vase is full will be 1. (5) By (1) and (4), my present credence that the vase is full should be 1 — while looking into the empty vase. (6) The same holds for any belief I can foresee acquiring through fatigue, drunkenness, propaganda, or plain error, provided I am certain I will acquire it: Reflection obliges me to pre-adopt all of them now. (7) And since what makes my future self worth deferring to, on the Dutch book story, is only that its opinions are locked in and hence exploitable if ignored, the same deference should extend to anyone whose opinions I am certain of — so I should adopt, right now, the credences of any known fanatic whose views happen to be on record. (8) A principle with consequences (5)–(7) is unacceptable. (9) So Reflection is false. (10) Worse, she says: since the diachronic book is structurally identical to the synchronic one, its collapse takes probabilistic coherence down with it — there is no Dutch book route to the probability axioms at all.

I can see roughly where I want to dig in — I would gladly lose (1) if it came to that, and I am certainly not conceding (10) without a fight — but every time I try to reject a step I find I am rejecting it in one case and wanting to keep it in another. Rejecting (1) outright seems to cost me the whole idea that a person's opinions should cohere across time, which was the thing I found elegant; rejecting only the hypnotist version of (1) seems ad hoc unless I can say what distinguishes a future opinion I should defer to in advance from one I should not — and I notice I cannot say it without mentioning how the future opinion was formed, which the Dutch book rationale in (2) never does and, she insists, cannot do without ceasing to be a Dutch book rationale. My flatmate thinks the real action is in (7), and that the vase is a distraction; I think the vase is the point and (7) is a separate, cruder argument bolted on. Settle this for us: name the step to reject, explain why it looked fine, and price the alternatives — including whether anything survives of the idea that rational opinion is unified across time once your diagnosis is granted.
