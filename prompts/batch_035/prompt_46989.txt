Over the holidays I got into an argument with my brother-in-law, who has been deep in ancient-alien material for a few months — the usual stuff about precision-cut granite, aligned monuments, gaps in the archaeological record. At one point he showed me photos of a drill core and said mainstream archaeology has no explanation, so the door is open. I ended the argument the way I've seen online skeptics end every argument about Atlantis, ancient astronauts, and re-dated pyramids: with the line that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. It's usually credited to Carl Sagan, and I dimly remember it's supposed to go back further than him. The line worked — he stopped — but walking away I realized I couldn't state what it actually claims, and I'd like to be able to, because I'm not sure it's doing what I think it's doing.

For context on where I'm coming from: I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, though I work with probabilistic systems and I'm comfortable with the idea that a hypothesis you assign a tiny prior needs a lot of evidence before its posterior is worth taking seriously. That framing makes the slogan look almost like arithmetic. But when I replay the conversation, my brother-in-law wasn't claiming aliens were probable — he was claiming there's a specific piece of physical evidence the standard account doesn't cover, and the slogan let me decline to look at it. So I suspect the line contains several different theses of very different quality, and the one I invoked may not be the defensible one. For instance: does "extraordinary" describe the claim's prior improbability, or its distance from what we already accept? Does "extraordinary evidence" mean more evidence of the ordinary kind, evidence of a qualitatively better kind, or evidence whose strength is somehow scaled to the weirdness of the claim? And is the underlying principle a banal truth of Bayesian updating, a substantive and contested claim about how much to trust testimony regarding the strange, or something that quietly begs the question — perhaps even a rule that lets you dismiss a claim without ever examining the specific evidence offered?

I'd like you to take the slogan apart for me. Lay out the distinct precise readings it could be expressing, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, and for each one tell me: whether it's trivial, substantive but defensible, or actually false or circular; and, crucially, whether it would have supported the move I made, which was to refuse to engage with a particular piece of evidence on the grounds that the conclusion it points toward is too far out. If one of the readings genuinely licenses that refusal, I want to know which and why; if the defensible readings all require me to go look at the drill core after all, I'd rather know that before the next family dinner. Feel free to note which reading the people who repeat the slogan in these contexts most likely have in mind, since I doubt most of them have separated the versions either.
