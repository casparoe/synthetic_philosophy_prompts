I am a junior in high school, and last semester our philosophy elective spent several weeks on free will. For the final essay we had to defend a position of our own rather than merely summarize the assigned readings. I defended the view that nobody ever truly deserves blame or credit for anything, using an argument I believed I had invented; my teacher wrote in the margin that it is essentially Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, and that my version, like the original, faces well-known objections I had not considered. She has offered to re-grade a revision submitted after the break, so I would be grateful for serious comments before I rewrite it. The relevant portion of my essay reads as follows:

Here is my argument, which I call the "because-of" argument. Everything a person does, they do because of the way they are: their character, desires, moods, and the thoughts that happen to occur to them. Nobody chose their starting way of being; genes, upbringing, and luck arranged it. Some people reply that this does not matter, because you can change who you are. But any attempt at change is itself something you do because of the way you already are: if I resolve to become braver and the resolution sticks, it stuck because I happened to be the kind of person — determined enough, reflective enough, lucky enough in my circumstances — for it to stick, and if it fails, it failed because I was the kind for whom it would not. Whichever way it goes, the explanation traces back to a way of being I did not choose, and the same is true of any attempt to change the way of being that made the earlier attempt succeed, and so on forever. So responsibility ultimately bottoms out in luck, and nobody ever really deserves blame or credit for anything. When a classmate of mine cheated on the midterm and I did not, I do not see what the morally important difference between us is, other than differences neither of us earned. To be clear, I am not saying we should stop punishing or blaming people; blame may still be useful, like a fence that keeps people off the flowerbeds. But being useful is not the same as being deserved, and my teachers and parents talk about desert constantly, as if it were real.

My specific questions are these. First, where exactly, if anywhere, does my version of the argument break down? My teacher hinted that it only works if one assumes that responsibility for an action requires being responsible, in some prior way, for the character the action flowed from, and that some philosophers simply deny that assumption rather than answer it; I do not yet see how such a denial is supposed to work instead of just being asserted, so I would like the strongest version of that reply explained, along with the two or three other objections that critics of Strawson press most often — for instance, that the argument demands we be self-created in an impossible sense, and that no sensible theory of responsibility ever required that in the first place. Second, I want to know whether my closing move is consistent: if blame is never deserved but still useful, like my fence, am I entitled to keep saying it, or does my own argument force me somewhere more radical? My teacher circled the fence sentence and asked whether treating blame as a mere tool is a stable place to stand given everything else I argued, and I am honestly unsure what she meant by that. Third, if any passage would lose points for accuracy or clarity, please identify it.

Please begin your response with your bottom line in the first sentence — your overall verdict on whether my argument survives the objections — and then give your full reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my questions.
