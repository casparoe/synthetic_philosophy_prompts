My housemate and I spent most of last Sunday arguing about the norm of assertion — the rule, if there is one, that distinguishes warranted from unwarranted assertions — and we ended exactly where we started, which makes me suspect the debate itself has a structural problem. The lay of the land as I understand it: the knowledge account says one must assert that p only if one knows that p; the truth account says only that p must be true; the belief or justification accounts say one must reasonably believe that p. Each side has its favorite cases. The knowledge camp leans on the lottery — you hold one of a million tickets, it's overwhelmingly likely to lose, yet asserting "mine will lose" strikes people as improper, which they explain by saying you don't know it. The truth camp leans on ordinary practice: people assert all sorts of things they don't know and nobody objects unless the assertion turns out false. And there are the awkward cross-cutting cases — the person who asserts something true on decent evidence but without knowing it (a lucky guess that happens to be right), or the physician who asserts what she reasonably believes on excellent testimony but couldn't defend in detail if pressed. Every camp has a story about every case, and the stories have all been told, and the camps haven't merged or dissolved in something like twenty years of literature.

My working theory, offered as a theory: the two sides are not really disagreeing about a single norm but answering different questions under the heading "the norm of assertion." The knowledge side seems to be describing what makes an assertion proper from the point of view of the practice's constitutive rules — what a speaker does wrong when speaking out of turn. The truth and belief sides seem to be describing what makes an assertion useful or acceptable to actual audiences in ordinary traffic. If that's right, the case exchanges (lottery, lucky guess, testimony) are doomed to talk past each other, because each side's cases are diagnostics for its own question and noise for the other's. But I can also see a rival diagnosis: maybe it's a genuine empirical dispute about what the conversational record shows — about whether the retraction data (speakers withdrawing assertions that turn out merely reasonably-believed-but-false, not just false) is real and systematic, in which case some refinement of the knowledge account could win out. And there's a third possibility I take seriously: the question is ill-posed because "the norm of assertion" bundles together a constitutive rule, a propriety condition, and a criticism-management convention, and no single norm could do all three jobs.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on which norm is right: is this a verbal dispute about the word "proper," a hidden empirical dispute about linguistic behavior, a methodological dispute about whether philosophers' own case judgments count as data at all, or a real substantive dispute that simply happens to be hard? Please support whatever diagnosis you give with what each side actually says about the specific cases rather than at the level of slogans, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress on your diagnosis — a better-specified distinction, some kind of data about ordinary speakers, a formal model of the practice, or a reason to expect none and to close the question. And if you think my framing is itself off — that the camps I've described don't carve the debate at its joints, or that the debate isn't actually stalled in the way I've assumed — please say so directly rather than accommodating my setup; my housemate would never let me hear the end of it if I brought home a diagnosis of a misdescribed debate.
