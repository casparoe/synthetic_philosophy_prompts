I write literary fiction, and I have become puzzled by an apparent inconsistency in what critics, editors, and workshop norms demand of characters facing terrible choices. When a protagonist of mine sacrifices one hostage to save five, reviewers insist that she be haunted afterwards — that she feel she has done wrong, not merely that something terrible happened through her — and they credit the book with moral seriousness partly on that basis; the tragic mode, as we teach it, seems to require that some guilt be unavoidable. Yet when I wrote a character who could not have foreseen a fatal accident, the same readers found her years of guilt self-indulgent and wanted her to move on, and when a character acted under coercion, they accepted without complaint that he be excused entirely. So the practice demands a residue of guilt after a justified wrong but rejects it after an innocent harm. What theory of moral dilemmas does this practice presuppose — that obligations can genuinely conflict, so that one can do wrong while doing everything right, or some distinction between being the agent of a wrong and merely being causally implicated in a harm? Reconstruct the implicit view, say whether the practice genuinely needs it or is compatible with a rival view on which the right choice leaves only regret and never guilt, and tell me what my fiction would have to look like if I wrote on that rival assumption instead — whether tragedy could survive it, and what the norms would have to give up.
