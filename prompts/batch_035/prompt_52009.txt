So I teach a survey course on democratic theory, and every spring I end the term with the epistemic democracy unit — Condorcet, wisdom of crowds, that whole cluster. I've been putting together my notes and a TA of mine looked them over and said half of what I assert in one week undercuts what I assert in another. I've stared at it and honestly can't tell if she's right or just being clever, so here's the package, tell me where it breaks.

(1) The Condorcet jury theorem is correct as math and gives majority rule a real epistemic vindication: if each voter is a bit better than a coin flip at picking the right answer, and votes are independent, then a majority of a large electorate is right with probability approaching one.

(2) I'm not bothered that no actual electorate satisfies those assumptions. Physics gets along fine with frictionless planes and ideal gasses; idealization is just how modeling works, and complaining that voters aren't independent is like complaining that intro students can't name a frictionless plane.

(3) That said, where the independence assumption fails it fails in a particularly nasty way. Voters share media environments, social circles, background narratives — their errors aren't independent noise, they're correlated, they pile up in the same direction. And unlike friction, which we know pushes against the motion, with correlated voter error we typically have no idea which way the bias runs.

(4) I defer to expert majorities without apology. If nine out of ten climatologists agree, I believe it, and I tell students they should too. (Yes, I'm aware experts share training, journals, funding, and a whole prestige economy, so they're at least as correlated as ordinary voters. I still defer.)

(5) I also find the "miracle of aggregation" story pretty convincing: most individual voters are basically clueless about policy, but their cluelessness is random, it cancels out at scale, and the electorate as a whole gets it right most of the time anyway.

(6) And one more, more of a gut-level commitment: when a lone serious person dissents from an otherwise unanimous expert consensus, that should count for more than one vote. One stubborn contrarian with real credentials going against the field makes me think the consensus might be an artifact of the field, not a discovery by it. The jury theorem, as far as I can tell, says the exact opposite — every additional vote for the consensus is further evidence it's right.

Which of these actually conflict, which only look like they do, and how bad is each? Rank the tensions by severity, and for each one tell me what would have to give and what giving it up costs me elsewhere in the set. If some pair seems to be in tension but dissolves on inspection, I want to know why it dissolves, not just that it does. I genuinely don't know which one I'd surrender first, so don't assume I'm protecting any of them.
