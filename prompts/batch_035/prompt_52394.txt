I'm taking a graduate seminar on population ethics, and a good deal of it has been given over to longtermism — the view that positively shaping the very long-run future is the overriding moral priority of our time, since the number of people who could exist in the centuries and millennia to come overwhelmingly exceeds the number alive today. Several of my classmates have adopted this outlook with something close to fervor: one of them has reorganized his entire career around reducing existential risk, and a visiting speaker dismissed critics of the view as guilty of a kind of temporal parochialism. I found much of this persuasive until fairly recently.

Last term, in a different course, I read Nick Bostrom's simulation argument, and I am now having trouble seeing how the two positions fit together. Bostrom's argument shows that we are almost certainly inhabitants of an ancestor-simulation run by our own posthuman descendants, which means that the far future the longtermists keep trying to secure is nothing more than software executing on a machine in someone's basement. If the simulators tire of the project and switch it off, the trillions of lives whose interests are supposed to dominate our moral deliberation will simply never exist, regardless of anything we do; and if they leave the program running, they will presumably do so whatever we do. Either way, our choices appear incapable of affecting whether the far future happens at all. It seems to follow that the money and talent pouring into existential-risk work is being spent, quite literally, on the upkeep of other people's computer files. When I put this to my classmate, he conceded that the simulation argument had made him somewhat less of a fanatic about the far future, though he could not say why, which only strengthened my suspicion that the whole longtermist outlook has been quietly refuted and its adherents simply have not noticed.

Assuming I have the argument right, why do the major longtermist organizations continue to fundraise and recruit as though the far future were a real thing whose existence hangs on our choices? More importantly, is my classmate's reaction the reasonable one — should learning that we are simulated make us more relaxed about what happens down here, since none of it was ever going to be real anyway? Or is there something to the opposite thought, that the simulation conclusion, if it went through, ought to make us more rather than less concerned about what happens inside the simulation, and if so, what exactly turns on that?
