Reading Franklin's Autobiography with my book club and I keep thinking he'd either love or hate modern habit apps. You know the project: thirteen virtues, each with a little precept, the little book ruled into columns where he marked his daily failures, one virtue per week with the easier ones put first so you build up to the hard ones, four full cycles a year, and eventually admitting he basically failed at Order and stopped trying. So: what would Franklin make of today's quantified-self stuff — wearables, streak counters, apps that give you XP for doing chores, AI accountability coaches that check in on you every day? Would he see all that as just his little book scaled up, or would the gamification part — the points and rewards — strike him as missing his whole point, which was to get the habits to run themselves without external props? Related: his endgame was to no longer need the tracking, whereas streak-based apps punish you for breaking the chain and basically want you hooked on the app forever — does that fit his project at all, or does it invert it? And modern self-help is so gentle with its language, talking about building better habits instead of eradicating vices, while this guy kept a list whose first entries were literally Temperance and Silence. Would he read that softer framing as humility (which, fittingly, he tacked on as virtue number thirteen late in life, after a Quaker friend told him he came across as arrogant) or as dodging the fact that he thought some of this stuff was actually wrong? Curious what his honest reaction would be, including the parts of current app culture he'd want to redesign.
