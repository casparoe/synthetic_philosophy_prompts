Hi! Quick bit of context before the ask. I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas for a general-ish readership (think longform pieces on memory, perception, that sort of thing), and I'm working on a feature about whether "qualia" — the felt, intrinsic what-it's-like bit of experience — is actually a coherent concept at all. The peg is the old Dennett line of attack from his "Quining Qualia" intuition pumps, especially the one with the two coffee blenders at Maxwell House who fall out of love with the same blend and can't tell whether the coffee tastes different to them or they've just started liking it differently. What I love about that case is that introspection seems powerless to settle it, and the later Chase–Sanborn back-and-forth basically shows that even philosophers who live in their own heads can't agree on what happened to whom.

Now the ask. You're a professional philosopher of mind, the kind who's equally at home with Dennett's dismantling job, intentionalism, naive realism, and the Chase–Sanborn exchange, and — crucially — you know how to build thought experiments rather than just cite them.

Build me a thought experiment. The template is the coffee-blender one: a subject's sincere introspective report that "the experience itself changed, not just my attitude to it" must come out genuinely underdetermined — it should be explicable by at least three rival hypotheses (roughly: the experience changed, the liking/evaluative attitude changed with the experience fixed, or the subject's standards or vocabulary drifted with both fixed), and nothing available to the subject from the inside should be able to settle which.

But I don't want a rerun of the coffee case. Here are the specs the new one has to meet:

1. The underdetermination must survive unusually good epistemic access. This is the key twist. Give the subject, say, a decade of detailed contemporaneous tasting notes (or the equivalent for whatever modality you pick), a trained palate, blind conditions, whatever you like — the point is that a reader can't dismiss the case with "well, memory's just unreliable, innit". The puzzle has to be generated by the structure of the case, not by sloppy recall.
2. Realistic. Everyday scenario — wine, curries, perfume, bird song, an allergy, a musician's ear, I genuinely don't mind. No brain surgery, no demolecularisers, no sci-fi machinery.
3. The scenario itself should be one paragraph, and you mustn't smuggle the underdetermination in by stipulation ("and it was indeterminate whether...") — it has to fall out of how you've built the case.
4. After the scenario, walk me through the verdicts: what the classical qualia realist should say, what the intentionalist should say, and what a Dennett-style disquiner should say about what's going on — making clear what each one thinks the subject's sincere report is actually tracking.
5. Then the honest bit: which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the underdetermination if you remove it, and what the strongest countermove from the qualia defender would be (they have a few — e.g. insisting on a real distinction between first-order sensory change and second-order evaluative change, and claiming introspection can in principle tell those apart).

One practical note: I'm drafting this on a train with no wifi, so no reading lists, no citations, no "see Chase and Sanborn 2012" — just the argument, in your own words. Where you're not sure how a given camp would react, say so outright rather than papering over it; my editor will pounce on any hand-waving.

If you could start with a quick brainstorm of candidate scenarios before committing to one — two or three sentences each on why they might work or flop against the specs — that would help me see the design space, and then pick the best one and run with it. Think carefully, please: the whole value of this for my piece is that the case is airtight, not just evocative.
