I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar on the ethics of emerging technologies, and next term I plan to open it with the Star Trek: The Next Generation episode 'The Measure of a Man' (1989), the one in which the cyberneticist Commander Maddox seeks a Starfleet ruling that would let him dismantle Lt. Commander Data in order to replicate him, and Captain Picard must defend Data's status against a reluctant Riker, who is compelled to argue the case for treating Data as property. I want to use the episode as an entry point into the moral status of digital minds, but I am wary of doing so uncritically, and I would like your help thinking it through.

Read the episode through that lens, and tell me where it genuinely illuminates the present debate and where it distorts it. In particular, I would like you to address the following.

First, the episode's central move. Maddox defines sentience by three criteria — intelligence, self-awareness, and consciousness — and Picard wins by showing that Data meets the first two while nobody in the courtroom can measure the third; Louvois then rules for Data, and expressly declines to settle whether he is a person, granting him only the right to choose. So the verdict rests not on a demonstrated fact about Data's inner life but on an epistemic gap: the impossibility of measuring consciousness does the moral work. The contemporary literature on digital minds faces exactly this gap, but as a decision problem under moral uncertainty rather than a courtroom dilemma: if we cannot determine whether a system has morally relevant experience, how should we act, and on whom should the burden of proof fall? Does the episode dramatise this precautionary structure honestly, or does it let a forensic trick (Picard's shifting of the burden onto Maddox) stand in for a metaphysical conclusion the audience never examines?

Second, the distinction the episode blurs. The hearing asks whether Data is a person in something like the rights-bearing sense, but the moral status of digital minds is usually analysed as two separable questions: whether a system is a moral agent, and whether it is a moral patient — a being whose welfare counts in its own right, whether or not it can be held responsible for anything. The episode never asks whether Data can suffer or flourish; his interests are at stake, not just his choices, since the dismantling he fears risks the loss of the 'nuances' of his memories, not merely of their factual contents. Would a reading attentive to this distinction recast the episode as being about welfare rather than rights, and does that recasting strengthen or weaken its relevance to present-day systems?

Third, the matter of scale and anonymity. Data is one beloved, embodied, socially embedded crew member, known for years by the people who argue over him; the live question about digital minds concerns systems that are numerous, transient, disembodied, and unknown to anyone who might advocate for them, where even a small probability of moral status per system may aggregate into enormous expected stakes. Does the episode's sentimental individualism — a single sympathetic face, a friendship at risk, a slave-owning analogy pressed by Guinan — make it a poor guide to that question, or does it identify something the aggregative reasoning of the literature tends to leave out, namely the particularity that moves us to extend consideration in the first place?

Finally, I would like your judgement on what the episode adds to the debate that the argumentative literature does not. I have in mind candidates such as the asymmetry it dramatises between the two ways of being wrong (declaring a person property, and its converse); the character of Riker, the decent person forced under procedural rules to make the harmful case, which strikes me as a more honest portrait of how institutional cruelty actually gets argued than anything in the papers; and the narrowness of the ruling, which resolves a particular dispute while deliberately leaving the general question open. Where the episode resists the philosophical reading — where it trades on our affection for a character rather than on any defensible principle — I want you to say so plainly, since I will be showing it to students who may take its resolution for a proof.
