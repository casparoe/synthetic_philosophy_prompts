I write about science and ideas for a general audience, and a recurring problem in my copy is the color phi phenomenon — the old Kolers-style apparent-motion result where a red dot flashes at one position, a green dot flashes a moment later at another, and viewers report seeing a single dot moving smoothly across and changing color somewhere in between, even though nothing was ever shown at the in-between location. When I explain it, I and most of my colleagues reach for the same analogy: the newsroom. The brain, on this picture, is an editor who files the finished story after the fact — complete with invented details and backdated timestamps — and conscious experience is the published version. Dennett's Orwellian-versus-Stalinesque framing seems to live inside this analogy rather comfortably: either the copy desk inserts the color change before publication, so experience is doctored in advance of being had (the Stalinesque show trial), or the first, unedited version really was experienced and the archive was rewritten afterward, with the originals destroyed (the Orwellian retouching). Dennett's further claim is that once you take the editing picture seriously enough, the distinction between these two collapses, because there is no single screening room where the "first run" is shown and no privileged moment of publication.

I would like an audit of the newsroom analogy before I lean on it again. Please give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the raw feed, the copy desk, the deadline, the archive, the reader, the moment of publication — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. In particular, I want to know what the analogy quietly imports that the brain lacks: a distinction between the reporter who saw the event and the editor who writes it up, a finished record that exists prior to editing, and, crucially, an editor who could in principle be asked what actually happened. Then tell me which disanalogies matter for the conclusion I keep using the analogy to support — that there is no fact of the matter about when a subject became conscious of the color change, and that first-person reports are marketing copy rather than testimony — and which are incidental. Does that skeptical conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does it depend on the very moment of publication the analogy was supposed to be doing without? And if the newsroom fails, is there a better analogy for what the color phi result shows about the timing of experience, or is the moral that no single analogy should be carrying this much weight?
