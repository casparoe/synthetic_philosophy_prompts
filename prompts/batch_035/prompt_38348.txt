I work on systems that must reason under deductive limitation: they have to form graded beliefs about mathematical claims they cannot settle in the time available — whether a given Π₁ sentence is true, whether a piece of code halts, whether a search will find a counterexample — and act on those beliefs before any proof arrives. Before I wade back into the literature on logical uncertainty, I want the specification settled first. Write me the desiderata: what any adequate assignment of probabilities to the closed sentences of a formal theory (say, arithmetic) must deliver, with the motivating case behind each requirement.

Let me suggest the candidates I have in mind, and treat the list as yours to amend, prune, or reorganise: deference to the deductive process where a proof or refutation is in hand; non-dogmatism about statements the theory leaves undecided; inductive learnability — that credences pick up empirical-looking patterns in mathematical data (frequencies of true instances of an open formula, the reliability of cheap heuristics like spot-checking or symmetry arguments); inexploitability — that no efficiently computable trading strategy can extract unbounded value from the credences, in the market metaphor where credences are prices; sensible behaviour under self-reference, where sentences encode claims about the very credences assigned to them, and where the Löbian and procrastination-style obstructions live; an account of the arrival of a proof, given that a theorem of a theory one already trusts carries no new information in the ordinary sense; and finally a stance on the standard of adequacy itself — whether the desiderata are demands on an ideal, possibly uncomputable, agent, or on something a bounded reasoner can approximate, and how much that choice quietly rewrites the rest of the list.

Two things I especially want flagged. First, which pairs or larger subsets of the desiderata are jointly unsatisfiable — my suspicion is that full deference to a sound deductive process plus non-dogmatism plus conditioning on newly derived implications runs into Gödelian trouble, and that learnability of every learnable pattern at feasible computational cost is too much to ask, but I want this argued rather than asserted. Second, which single desideratum does the most exclusionary work: does it knock out the Gaifman-style approach of putting a prior over complete consistent extensions of the theory, and what does it do to the Garrabrant-style market construction, with its traders and prices over time, as a candidate rather than merely as an existence proof? Then score those two contenders briefly against your list, and say where you would look for a theory that does better.
