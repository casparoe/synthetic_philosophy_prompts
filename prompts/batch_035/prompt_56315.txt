I run a small ethics blog (mostly everyday stuff — gym contracts, subscription cancellations, that kind of thing), and I want to build a "which theory of consent do you actually hold?" quiz as the next post. The trick I'm going for: the quiz should sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which labels they like. So no question can just ask "does deception invalidate consent?" — every question has to be a concrete case where the real positions come apart, and the answer options have to be plain descriptions of verdicts, never theory vocabulary.

Here are the cases I want in (add one or two of your own if something important is missing):

1. A surgeon tells a patient a procedure is "routine" when it's actually experimental with a much higher complication rate. The patient agrees. It goes fine.
2. A woman sleeps with a man who has taken off his wedding ring and told her he's single.
3. A trial participant signs up after the researcher explains randomization so badly that he walks away thinking he'll personally get whichever treatment turns out best for him.
4. A patient consents to a "body scan" without knowing its actual purpose is to check whether she carries a gene for a disease she's said she doesn't want to know about.
5. A slightly tipsy person — coherent, not staggering, maybe two glasses past the driving limit — signs a gym membership with a nasty cancellation clause she would've spotted sober.
6. A mugger: your money or your life.
7. A landlord tells a tenant the rent is doubling next month, pay up or move out — perfectly legal, but she'll be much worse off either way.
8. A coach tells an athlete "sleep with me or you're off the team." He genuinely does get to pick the team.
9. Someone donates a kidney after being told the complication risk is 1% when it's really 3%.
10. A mature-for-her-age 14-year-old refuses a blood transfusion for religious reasons; the hospital wants to override her.

For each case, offer 2–4 options phrased as verdicts, like: "nothing wrong here," "she agreed, but she was still wronged," or "her agreement never counted — it's as if she never consented at all." That last split is the thing I care most about and want the quiz to actually measure: the difference between thinking a defective consent means the act was non-consensual, and thinking the consent was real but something else went wrong. Readers who pick "agreed but wronged" on some cases and "not consent at all" on others should get caught by the scoring key, and I want the key to say what that mixed pattern amounts to.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto the actual positions, described by content: the view that only deception about the act itself (what's being done to you) invalidates, while deception about risks, consequences, or surrounding facts merely wrongs you; the rival "decision-relevant" view where any deception a reasonable person would care about undoes the consent; the consent-as-waiver camp and its trouble with mistakes; on the coercion cases, the position where threats invalidate because the threatener has no right to do the thing threatened while offers don't because they only add options — plus its rivals that struggle with the landlord and the coach; and on the last two cases, people who treat competence as a threshold versus people who treat it as a matter of degree.

Two extra asks. First, tell me which pairs of positions no set of cases like this can really seperate — where two views will give identical answers on every case I listed and I'd just be pretending the quiz can tell them apart. Second, flag if some combination of answers is one that no actual position takes, and give me your honest guess as to why: is the combination incoherent, or just undefended because nobody got around to it?
