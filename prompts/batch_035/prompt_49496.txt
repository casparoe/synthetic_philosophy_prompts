I'm a graduate student writing a philosophy-of-science paper on Lakatos, and I want a disciplined case study rather than a polemic. Take the string theory controversy that crystallized in the mid-2000s — the standoff between string theorists and their critics, culminating in Lee Smolin's The Trouble with Physics and Peter Woit's Not Even Wrong, both published in 2006 — and analyze it as a Lakatosian episode. Start with the mapping, done carefully: what exactly should count as the hard core of the string theory research programme (is it stringy unification itself, or something weaker, like the claim that quantum gravity is to be found by quantizing extended objects in a supersymmetric framework?), what its protective belt has absorbed over the decades (compactification of the extra dimensions, the flux landscape and moduli stabilization, the adjustable supersymmetry-breaking scale), and what the positive and negative heuristics have actually instructed researchers to do. Then apply the criteria honestly: over which window of time, and by Lakatos's lights, is the programme progressive or degenerating? Make sure you take seriously the two results that complicate the critics' story: Steven Weinberg's 1987 anthropic reasoning about the cosmological constant, which after the 1998 discovery of dark energy looked like a successful prediction of a small but nonzero value; and the mirror-symmetry computations of rational-curve counts on the quintic threefold, which mathematicians later proved correct — a novel 'fact' confirmed by an outside discipline, a category Lakatos's apparatus arguably does not contain.

Then tell me where the theory breaks. I'm aware that Lakatos is forgiving by design — he allowed that a scientist may rationally stick with a degenerating programme for a long time, and his verdicts are only ever retrospective — so I want to know what, if anything, in his methodology could have adjudicated the dispute at the time it was being fought, or whether the critics were borrowing a stringency that the methodology does not actually supply. Note also that Lakatosian rivalry presupposes two programmes competing for the same facts, whereas string theory's would-be rivals, like loop quantum gravity, never addressed the same questions or the same mathematical terrain; does degeneration talk become unmoored when there is no live competitor over the shared evidence, and if so, what does that say about how the mid-2000s debate could have been rational? Finally, run the analysis in the other direction and tell me what the episode shows about Lakatos: does the string case vindicate his tolerance for programmes that spend decades producing mathematics rather than experimental predictions, strain it, or expose a genuine gap — the absence of any category for confirmation routed through another discipline, and for predictive success that consists in narrowing a range of possible values rather than pinning one down? If you think some other framework handles this episode better than Lakatos does, say which and defend the claim, but only after you've shown me exactly where his apparatus snaps under the load.
