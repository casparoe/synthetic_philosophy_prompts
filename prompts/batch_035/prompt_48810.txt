i teach an upper-level decision theory seminar, 12 students, 90 min session. we just finished subjective expected utility and dutch book arguments, next week is ambiguity. i want to design an in-class experiment where the students are the subjects and they walk straight into the ellberg-style result themselves, before i ever say the word ambiguity or mention any of the literature.

rough shape i'm imagining: three betting conditions. one urn they know has exactly 50 red and 50 black balls. one urn where the red/black split is unknown (they can see its sealed and that i'm not peeking). and then a third condition where instead of drawing balls they're betting on outcomes of some trivia/sports/weather thing -- except i secretly gave half of them a "thanks for participating, here's some background reading" packet a week ago and nothing to the other half. so we get the classic known-vs-unknown comparison but also a test of whether people still shy away from the unknown when its their own area of competence on the line. i half-remember a result that ambiguity aversion basically evaporates when people feel knowledgable about the domain, even when the odds are objectively the same -- build the activity around testing that too, but don't worry about the citation, i don't need it.

no real money obviously, so figure out a stakes currency (candy, extra credit points, whatever) and make the payoff scheme lopsided enough that indifference is not the comfortable answer. also partway through i want a second phase where they have to sell their already-placed bet to another student and we watch what prices actually clear at -- my hunch is the premium people demand to take an ambiguous bet off someone's hands is a much cleaner measure of the aversion than their initial picks are, so the game should surface that.

then a proper debrief: map specific moments back onto the formal machinery they just learned -- where exactly the revealed preferences violate the axioms, what the sure-thing principle has to do with the pattern we're seeing, why the dutch book story they loved last week doesn't obviously come to the rescue here. and tell me honestly what will predictbly go wrong the first time i run it (one student already knows the paradox and blabs, the cheat-sheet half turns out to be terrible at judging what they know, everyone just picks whatever their neighbor picked) plus what a classroom version with fake stakes fundamentally can't reproduce about the thing we're studying.
