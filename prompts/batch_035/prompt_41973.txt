I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy writing a dissertation chapter against the intention condition in the doctrine of double effect — the Thomist principle that foreseeing a harm as a side effect of pursuing a good can be permissible while intending that same harm is not, whatever the numbers. My problem is that the stock cases put the doctrine on the side of common intuition: everyone prefers the diverted trolley to the organ harvest. For the chapter's centerpiece I want the case in the opposite direction — one in which the doctrine, applied honestly and without hand-waving, obliges an agent to impose more harm on innocent people than even a blunt act consequentialist would.

You are a professional moral philosopher with deep expertise in the doctrine of double effect and the literature on intended versus foreseen harm, from Foot and Thomson through Kamm and Scanlon. Think carefully, and before committing, work through at least three candidate scenarios in your head; but do not show me the scratch work. Begin the response with the finished scenario itself, in the first paragraph, with no preamble and no restatement of this brief.

Specifications the case must meet:

- One realistic setting — emergency management, industrial safety, public infrastructure, or the like. No trolleys, no transplant surgeons, no bombing campaigns, no demons, no perfect predictors.
- The agent faces exactly two options, and each saves the same number of people.
- In the first option, a small number of innocents die, and their deaths are causally necessary for the rescue: were they somehow to survive, the rescue fails. The agent genuinely aims at their deaths — sets the mechanism that kills them — rather than merely using their location, property, or bodies as an obstacle.
- In the second option, a larger number of innocents die, but their deaths contribute nothing: the rescue would stand unchanged if, by luck, all of them survived.
- The numbers must be far enough apart that any view that simply counts deaths selects the first option.
- Both options must be doings, not omissions; the victims must be equally innocent, equally unidentified strangers to the agent; the time pressure and the agent's epistemic situation must be the same in both.
- Proportionality must not dissolve the case: the good at stake must be large enough that even the second option's larger death toll passes an ordinary proportionality test.
- The case must generate these verdicts from the theories; it must not stipulate or rig them.

After the scenario, open the analysis with a short verdict table — theory, and which option it requires — then the reasoning for each of: act consequentialism; the classical doctrine of double effect; a means-based constraint of the sort Thomson later endorsed, on which what forbids the transplant is using a person as a means rather than intending harm as such; and the doctrine read through Kamm's distinction between harming someone as a causal means and harming them as a mere means. Identify precisely which structural feature of the case is doing the work, and say what happens to it if that feature is removed — in particular, what if the means-deaths stop being necessary, or stop being aimed at. Then give the doctrine's defender their two strongest lines of response: reframing what the agent really intends in the first option, or accepting the verdict and defending it victim-by-victim; assess which is stronger and why. Close with an honest note on whether the case is a fair test or whether it quietly rigs things against the doctrine by making the means-option strange in some independent, morally loaded way — and if it does, tell me, because a referee will.
