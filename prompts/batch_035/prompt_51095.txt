quick one that matters a lot for my teaching next term. I'm running a second-year seminar on theories of justice, about 16 students, one 90-minute slot, and next month we're on luck egalitarianism -- dworkin, the brute luck / option luck line, the expensive tastes stuff. I'm done lecturing at them about it. I want a game where they have to *run* the view instead of hearing about it.

rough shape I'm imagining: everyone gets a starting bundle partly by luck (cards or dice, doesn't matter), then makes a couple of choices with actual payoff consequences, and then -- this is the important bit -- the group has to decide who gets topped up from a collective pot, and they have to write the top-up rules themselves. the trick is that the only evidence they'll have about *why* someone ended up poor is whatever they can reconstruct about that person's card draw versus their choices, so they should run into the brute/option luck line rather than being told where it is.

two specific things to build in. first, at least one case where somebody's costly 'choice' is a religiously or culturally mandated practice -- like a player who spends chips observing a dietary rule or has to buy a substitute for something the game gives everyone else free -- so the room has to fight about whether that counts as an option for luck-egalitarian purposes or something the view owes an answer to and doesn't have. second, a moment where the player ending up worst off is demonstrably there because of their own recorded decision, so someone has to actually say out loud "the scheme gives them nothing," and feel how bad that is to say. that's the harshness/abandonment objection (anderson's line) and I want it to land emotionally, not just be in their notes.

then a debrief that maps moments of the game onto the distinction, onto the expensive tastes debate, and onto what the exercise structurally can't show -- dworkin's hypothetical insurance idea, actual desperation, adaptive preference stuff, whatever you think the biggest gaps are. and be honest with me about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it.

constraints: keep it cheap, cards and chips and paper, no software. also please don't have anyone literally play "the disabled student" -- I don't want one kid made to embody a disadvantage for 90 minutes. make endowments abstract things like puzzle-solving speed or number of practice attempts before a scored task instead. no lecture up front, they shouldn't know the name of the view until the debrief.
