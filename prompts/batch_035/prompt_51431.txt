Grading term papers has resurfaced an old worry about Machiavelli that one student has sharpened into an argument that seems to prove too much, and I can't see where it slips. (1) In chapter 25 of The Prince, fortune is the arbiter of about half our affairs and leaves the rest to us; since fortune is fickle, the man who suits his mode of proceeding to the times will prosper, and this is supposed to ground the advice to be impetuous rather than cautious. (2) In Discourses III.9 Machiavelli adds that men cannot really vary their natures: Fabius' dilatory caution was exactly right against Hannibal and would have been fatal in other circumstances, and Pope Julius II succeeded only because his impulsive temperament happened to fit the times, with Machiavelli conceding that had the times demanded patience, Julius would have been ruined. (3) But whether the times call for audacity or for patience is itself fortune's department, not the agent's. (4) So virtù is identifiable only in hindsight: it names whichever fixed disposition happened to fit, and at the moment of decision there's no difference in kind between the virtuous prince and the lucky one. (5) Then the famous claim that virtù can oppose fortune -- build the dikes before the river floods -- is empty, and the practical teaching reduces to advice to have been lucky. (6) Yet Machiavelli clearly takes his own examples to show something real, and I want to keep (6). So which step do I give up? And a bonus question: are chapter 25 and Discourses III.9 actually in tension, or is there a reading on which they fit together?
