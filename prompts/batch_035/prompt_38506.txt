I am a novelist three years into my third book, and before I hand the manuscript to my editor I want an audit of the beliefs I have been writing under, because I have begun to suspect they do not all fit together. I came to philosophy late, through rereading the James–Clifford exchange during the long period when my second novel was quietly dying in the marketplace, and I find that I have ended up holding an uneasy mixture of the two men's positions. Let me lay out my commitments plainly. (a) I accept the Cliffordian rule in its full generality: believing anything on insufficient evidence is an intellectual wrong, not merely a prudential mistake, and it applies to flattering beliefs about my own work as much as to religious or political convictions. (b) I nonetheless believe, and must believe in order to keep writing, that the novel I am finishing is good and will find its readers — the evidence before me (two commercial failures, one hostile early reader, my own six a.m. certainty that every sentence is false) does not support this, and I have never managed to hold the belief and assess the evidence at the same time. (c) I hold that this belief is causally load-bearing: if I stopped believing the book was good I would stop writing it, so the belief partly brings about the fact that would make it true, in something like the way James says trust between people does. (d) At the same time, I hold, against what I take to be James's picture, that belief is never under direct voluntary control — on a bad morning I cannot simply decide to believe the book is good; the most I can do is manage my exposure, which in practice means rereading the one chapter I trust, keeping the early reader's letter in a drawer, and not looking at my last book's sales figures. (e) I think what makes a belief worth having is its truth, so that my belief in the book's goodness would be worthless if the book were not in fact good, however indispensable it was in getting the book written. (f) Finally, I think that while I am actually writing I believe in my characters in a quite literal sense — at the desk I believe they exist, have histories extending beyond the page, and act independently of me — even though away from the desk I believe no such thing and never have.

Tell me whether this set hangs together. Which of the commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the cheapest way to restore consistency? I am especially interested in whether (d) quietly changes what the Clifford–James dispute amounts to in my case: if I cannot will my beliefs at all, I am no longer sure whose rule I am following or breaking when I reread the good chapter and leave the letter in the drawer — those indirect maneuvers may be either the only form the will to believe can take for a creature like me, or exactly the managed self-deception Clifford was writing against, and I cannot tell which. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give and what giving it would cost, practically, for someone who has to be back at the desk tomorrow morning.
