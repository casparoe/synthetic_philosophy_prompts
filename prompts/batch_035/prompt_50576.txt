I'm in a history of philosophy course this semester and we just spent two weeks on Émilie du Châtelet, mostly on her late essay on happiness (the Discours sur le bonheur). We read it alongside her life story, so the context that stuck with me: her claim that happiness is not luck but a kind of discipline or choice, her argument that we actually need our illusions and shouldn't tear them down, her love of gambling and study as examples of the passions that keep life vivid, and especially her point that glory and ambition are the best cure for women whose loves go wrong, since love makes women miserable far more often than it makes them happy. My professor is big on reading her as an optimist about the malleability of the self -- happiness as something you can train, like she trained herself into mathematics at midnight with freezing ink.

For my final paper we're allowed to do a "reception" style question, and I want to do: what would du Châtelet actually say about the modern happiness industry? I mean the whole apparatus that came after her time -- positive psychology as a field, the research on hedonic adaptation and set points, mindfulness apps that promise trainable contentment, governments (like with national wellbeing indexes) putting life satisfaction on the dashboard next to GDP. My study partner thinks she'd be thrilled: she basically anticipated the idea that happiness is a skill you work at, so a society that measures it and engineers for it is her program implemented at scale. I'm skeptical. Her illusions are supposed to be grand, self-chosen, almost heroic -- the ambition for glory, the passion for a demanding project -- and I can't picture her being satisfied by an app nudging you toward contentment, or by a statisticians' happiness that's basically the absence of misery. And the "trainable self" part cuts both ways: if happiness is trainable, who's doing the training, you or the app?

So please write me a draft of her likely position, arguing from her actual views (the necessity of illusions, the role of the passions, glory as the woman's remedy, her own biography of study, love, and losing at cards) to what she'd make of positive psychology, hedonic set-point research, and state-led wellbeing measurement -- and, as a bonus section, whether she'd see social media as a new theater of glory for women or as exactly the cheap counterfeit of glory that her account would predict makes people miserable. Feel free to be speculative, but ground each claim in something she actually held or lived. It doesn't have to side with me, but it should take my worry seriously. Maybe 900-1200 words?
