I've been keeping a list of my own judgements about which of my reactions are "just preferences" versus "biases I should try to correct," and I want to know if there's one principle behind them or whether I'm hopelessely inconsistent. Here's the list, with my gut verdicts:

1. I prefer vanilla over chocolate. Preference, obviously, nothing to correct.
2. I find licorice disgusting — not just don't-prefer, actively gross. Feels different from #1 even though it's the same sense modality, but I still wouldn't call it a bias.
3. I think my mother's cooking is the best in the world. I know it's just because I grew up with it — mere exposure, if I'd been raised in a different kitchen I'd have some other "best." Somehow I still file it as a preference and feel no pressure to correct it.
4. I prefer hanging out with people who went to colleges like mine. Same causal story as #3, but this one I DO want to call a bias, and I've actually tried to correct it.
5. When I see a face that's clearly had a lot of cosmetic work done, my gut says "that looks worse," and when I see an untouched aging face it says "fine, distinguished even." I've started to suspect the first reaction is a bias about what faces are supposed to do (age invisibly, apparantly) rather than a preference.
6. I love the music I discovered at 15 more than any music since. Everyone says it's just the critical period talking, but nobody is bothered by it the way they'd be bothered by #4. Neither am I, really.
7. I think bullfighting is wrong. I know the people who enjoy it were raised somewhere that enjoys it, exactly parallel to my licorice disgust or my mom's-cooking conviction. But I do NOT want to file this one as a preference or a bias — it feels like it's tracking something about the practice, even though the causal story behind my judgment is the same as behind #1.
8. Hiring a friend's kid over a better-qualified stranger because it's your friend's kid: bias, obviously.
9. Spending more on your own kid's tutoring than donating the same money toward a stranger's kid's tutoring: feels like... an allowed preference? Partiality that's fine? But the psychological machinery in 8 and 9 looks awfully similar (favoring near-and-dear), and I have a clear verdict for one and a fuzzy verdict for the other.
10. Someone tells me they think humility is a virtue mainly because they were raised in a culture that prizes humility. If their whole reason is "that's how I was raised," I'd say they hold it dogmatically — but I hold my humility conviction the exact same way, and I don't think it's dogmatic in my case. This might be #7 in different clothes.

So: is there a principle that sorts these the way I sort them? My hunch is the answer hinges on whether "bias" should be defined by the judgment's causal etiology (produced by mere exposure, parochial upbringing, or self-interest rather than by contact with the thing's actual merits), or by its correctability, or just by its domain (taste vs. belief). But I can't get any of the three to do all the work: etiology misclassifies #7, correctability misclassifies #3 (I could correct it, don't want to, and that seems fine), and domain seems to assume the answer. And if the principle ends up being "anything whose causal origin I can see gets downgraded to bias," then #7 is in serious trouble, which I'd hate.

So tell me which verdicts have to go, and if they can all be saved, what the saving costs in complexity — a principle with six ad hoc clauses isn't obviously better than just admitting I'm inconsistent. Then run it on two cases I haven't listed: (a) a wine snob whose entire palate was formed by which bottles happened to be expensive when they were learning, and (b) someone whose refusal to eat dog meat comes entirely from having grown up with dogs as pets — and for (b) say whether you think they should try to correct it. Please also be upfront about where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what consideration would flip you to a different verdict, because I suspect my whole list is steered by which conclusions I emotionally want, and I'd rather know that than get a tidy answer.
