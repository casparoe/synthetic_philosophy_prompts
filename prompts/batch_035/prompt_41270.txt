So I'm a novelist, and for the last year and a half I've been researching a book set in Ceaușescu's Romania, centered on Decree 770 — the 1966 law that made abortion essentially illegal and pulled contraception off the shelves, on the theory that the country needed to grow from 20 million people to 30. Quick facts in case they're fuzzy for you: birthrates nearly doubled overnight, women were monitored monthly by gynecologists, illegal abortions became a leading killer of pregnant women, and — this is the part my novel lives in — tens of thousands of kids ended up warehoused in state orphanages, most of them not even actual orphans, just children their parents couldn't afford. The whole thing only came fully into western view after the 1989 revolution, when the decree was repealed within weeks.

My problem is with my editor, honestly. She keeps saying the novel is "about" whether the policy was wrong, and I think that's the wrong question — everyone agrees it was definately a disaster, that's not a novel, that's a verdict. The question I keep circling in my notes is weirder. Population ethics has this thing people call the procreation asymmetry — roughly, that it's bad to bring into existence a life that will go badly, but there's no corresponding duty to bring into existence lives that will go well. And Ceaușescu's regime ran on the exact opposite premise: more Romanians was a good in itself, and the welfare of any particular Romanian was an afterthought at best.

What I want from you is an analysis of the whole episode through the asymmetry. And I want you to be disciplined about the mapping, because I keep fudging it in my notes: whose choices are the "procreation decisions" here — the state compelling, the parents conceiving under coercion, or is the state not a procreator at all, so the asymmetry is just silent on it? What does the theory say about each, and where does it genuinely not speak? (I have a suspicion the non-identity thing — that those particular children wouldn't have existed at all without the decree — makes the asymmetry do strange work here, since the alternative for them wasn't a better life but no life, but I can't tell if I'm reasoning about that correctly.)

And then the other direction, because it has to run both ways or it's useless to me: what does the episode show about the asymmetry itself? The orphanage half of the story obviously vindicates the "don't create suffering lives" side. But here's what nags me — if the orphanages had been humane, if those kids had grown up okay, would the asymmetry have anything at all to say against the decree? Because if the answer is no, then what the asymmetry condemns Ceaușescu for is the conditions, not the natalism — the same policy with better-funded orphanages comes out morally clean. That's a conclusion I'd rather my novel earned than just asserted, so tell me straight whether it follows or whether I'm missing something.

Also, no bibliography or reading-list stuff — I'm fine on sources, I've read enough for three novels. I want the argument, and I especially want you to flag every place where you'd be forcing the theory to say something it doesn't actually say about my book.
