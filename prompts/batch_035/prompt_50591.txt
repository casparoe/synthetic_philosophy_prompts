I'm writing a paper on whether tiny harms can ever sum to something worse than a single grave harm, and before I wade into the back-and-forth arguments, I want the specification: what would a theory of the aggregation of small harms have to get right in order to count as adequate?

To fix ideas, take the standard stylized case: a dust speck lodging briefly in the eye of each of an enormous number of people (make the number absurdly large, so that no appeal to physical impossibility can do the work), versus fifty years of torture for one person. Also fixed for purposes of the spec: the small harms in question are genuine but negligible harms — a stubbed toe, a dull ache, a moment of irritation — not mere neutral sensations; if you think that framing already smuggles in an answer, build your worry into a requirement rather than a footnote.

Write the desiderata: the cases the theory must handle (concentrated versus dispersed harm with the same total; small harms accumulated over one person's lifetime versus spread thinly across persons; the run from one dust speck to a lifetime of them with no single step crossing a visible threshold; harms sitting just below and just above whatever line the theory draws); the structural commitments it must either respect or openly reject (transitivity of the better-than relation; additivity across persons; continuity between adjacent magnitudes of harm); the distinctions it cannot collapse (intensity versus duration of pain; the number of sufferers as a moral quantity versus a mere arithmetical one; aggregation within a life versus aggregation across lives); and the constraints from ordinary practice it must either satisfy or explain away (why we don't spend unbounded resources eliminating small harms one at a time; why we seem to treat a thousand mosquito bites on one person differently from one bite on each of a thousand people, if in fact we do).

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it — a requirement with no motivating case is a camp prejudice and should be flagged as such rather than passed off as a constraint. Then say which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are demanded only by particular camps, and, most importantly, whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable: I have a suspicion the list itself can be turned into a theorem of its own, and that's the part I most want to see even if you can't prove it rigorously. Finish, briefly, with how three natural contenders fare against your list — a straightforward additive view, a threshold or lexical view on which harms below some intensity cannot be summed across persons to outweigh one above it, and a view that gives up transitivity — and identify the single requirement that does the most work to exclude candidates.

One thing I need throughout: keep separate what is settled among people who actually work on aggregation and population ethics from what is genuinely contested. Do not present the rejection of transitivity, or the assumption of additivity, or the insistence on continuity as the consensus when any of them is a live dispute; wherever the field itself is divided, mark the requirement accordingly.
