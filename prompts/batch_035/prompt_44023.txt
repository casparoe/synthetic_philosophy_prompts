I'm on the classification working group for a national psychiatric association's diagnostic manual (think a smaller cousin of the DSM, we put out a new edition every few years). This cycle a vocal group of researchers is pushing us to add "maladaptive daydreaming" as an official diagnosis — you might have heard of it, it's been floating around the literature and reddit for a decade or so: people who spend hours a day in extremely vivid fantasy worlds they feel partially unable to control, to the point their jobs and relationships suffer. It's not in any major manual yet, but there are patient communities with tens of thousands of members who already use the label, self-diagnose, and say that having a name for their experience was what finally made them seek help.

The committee is split three ways and I've been tasked with drafting the memo we'll vote on. One faction wants full inclusion as a disorder (they point at the suicide-risk and disability data, and note that without a code nothing gets funded or treated). A second wants what our manual calls "condition meriting further study" — listed in an appendix, researchable, but not billable. The third wants it rejected outright: they worry we'd be pathologizing intense imagination, pathologizing coping, and basically rubber-stamping a category that was born on internet forums rather than in clinics. The chair also keeps bringing up Ian Hacking's stuff about looping effects — his point that classifying people changes the people classified, that once "maladaptive daydreamer" is an official identity people will organize their lives and symptoms around it, and the category will drift from whatever we thought we were defining. She half thinks that's a reason to reject it, half thinks it's just a fact of life for every diagnosis we've ever added and not a special objection here.

Draft the decision memo for me please: recommend one of the three options (or a variant if you think one is actually better, but say why), give the reasoning the other members could genuinely engage with, and make sure the strongest version of the losing side's argument gets a fair shake rather than a paragraph of throat-clearing before we dismiss it. Take the looping-effects worry seriously either way — don't treat it as a philosophical curiosity, but also don't let it do all the work, since if it counted against every diagnosis we'd have no manual at all. Also add a short closing section on what would change your mind: what kind of evidence or argument over the next revision cycle should make us regret this call, and where you think the memo's own reasoning is weakest. Keep it to something I can actually circulate — maybe 800 words. (And forgive any typos above, I'm typing this between sessions.)
