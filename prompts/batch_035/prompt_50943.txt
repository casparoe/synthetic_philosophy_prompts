My aunt has served as the archivist of our county historical society for thirty-one years, and I've been helping her inventory the accession backlog before she retires, which has gotten me wondering what principle actually governs what she takes in. The volunteers believe they can predict her decisions, but I've started to suspect they're predicting her with the wrong rule. Her record, as far as I can reconstruct it: she accessioned a bundle of letters exchanged between two farm wives between the wars, though only fragments survive and half the pages are water-stained; she accessioned a folder of tenant letters from the 1968 rent dispute, though the letters are furious, barely literate, and, by her own account, of no consequence to anyone who writes histories; and she accessioned a set of oral history interviews recorded in 2005 with Black families displaced when the highway was cut through their neighborhood in 1962, though the recordings are of poor quality and the memories more than forty years old. On the other side, she has declined the meticulously kept ledgers of the shipping firm that dominated the town from 1870 to 1930 — the best-condition records ever offered to the society, and the volunteers still mourn them for the charts alone; the complete minute books of the chamber of commerce; and the official commissioned portraits of every mayor since 1890, along with the programs from their inaugural ceremonies.

Three offers are on her desk for the spring accession meeting, and the volunteers are already arguing about what she will do. First, the diaries of Eleanor Ashford, matriarch of the mill family that was the county's largest employer from the 1890s to the 1930s: thirty years of near-daily entries, and — unusually for a woman of her station — sustained attention to her cook, laundress, and housemaid, whose names appear nowhere else in the society's holdings. Second, the ledgers and shareholder minute books of the Colored Farmers' Cooperative store, kept between 1912 and 1934 as carefully as the shipping firm's books, and documenting Black commercial life in a county whose holdings on the subject are otherwise nearly empty. Third, the internal case files of the county welfare office from the 1950s: institutional files, compiled by caseworkers about poor families, and containing what is apparently the only contemporary account of those families' circumstances that exists anywhere.

Predict her verdict on each of the three. What I'd most like from you, though, is not the verdicts but the reasoning underneath them: what rule best explains her entire record, which other rules explain that record just as well, and where those rival rules come apart on the three offers. If none of the three offers separates the candidate rules cleanly, tell me what a fourth offer would have to look like to do it. For what it's worth, she is fond of remarking that the ledgers always survive; it is the people who don't — though I can't tell whether that is the key to her decisions or just something she likes to say.
