so, I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on has a POV character with pain asymbolia — the real thing, where people (usually after certain brain lesions, sometimes with other neurological stuff going on) say they feel pain, can rate its intensity just fine, but are simply... not bothered by it. They'll tell you the injection hurt, accurately, and then not pull away, not ask for anything, sometimes even laugh at the stimulus. I've done my homewrok on this, so don't point me at reading lists or case reports — I know the shape of the literature well enough, and I'm not asking you to fetch anything.

What I actually need to decide, as a writer, is this: when my character burns her hand, is she suffering? I've been quietly assuming that the badness of pain just is the feeling — that there's nothing more to pain's being bad for you than how it feels from the inside. If that's right, then my character either (a) still feels what we would feel, and so suffers and merely doesn't show it, or (b) the feeling itself is partly gone, since most accounts split pain into a sensory side and an affective/evaluative side, and maybe the affective side was part of the feel all along. This matters for every scene I write from inside her head.

So give me a number: your credence that pain's badness comes apart from its felt character in asymbolia — that a person can feel something with roughly the sensory character of our pain and yet have nothing bad happening for her, not merely fail to react to a bad thing. Decompose it. I'd expect terms at least along these lines, plus whatever else you think belongs: your probability that the affective component is constitutive of pain's phenomenal character rather than a separate attitude taken toward the sensation; your probability that asymbolics' self-reports are reliable — they have little motive to fake indifference, but there's the classic worry that you can't introspect the absence of a feeling you've lost; whether 'badness' even attaches to experiences themselves, or to the wanting, and the thwarting of wanting, instead. One line of justification per term, then combine them into the final credence.

Then tell me which term you're least confident in and what would move it most. And if your honest conclusion is that a novelist shouldn't expect philosophy to settle this — fine, say that, but give me the number anyway, and tell me which way you'd write her in the meantime.
