I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Bayesian epistemology and decision theory — twelve students, one seventy-five-minute session. Design an in-class game built around Good's theorem: the result that a rational agent with a stable prior and preferences over final outcomes can never be made worse off, in expectation, by costlessly acquiring information relevant to her decision — the result usually taught alongside the principle of total evidence. My worry is that students nod along to the proof (draw the tree, take the expectation of the conditional expectations, watch it simplify to the prior expectation) while never feeling how strange the theorem is, because their everyday intuitions scream that information can be bad for you: ignorance is bliss, bad news before a decision you can't influence only ruins your week, and no one wants the biopsy result before the vacation either. I want the game to first make them feel the theorem's force and then make them feel exactly where their intuitions of bad information live.

Here is the game skeleton I'd like you to develop and refine (adjust the numbers as you see fit, but keep the three-phase arc). Base phase: each round a card is drawn from one of two decks — deck A runs heavily red, deck B heavily blue — with the deck chosen by a hidden coin flip, and students bet red or blue at odds calibrated so that the bet is exactly fair given no information. Before betting, each student individually and privately chooses whether to accept a free, known-accuracy "witness" signal about which deck is in play; accepting costs nothing. Before any cards are drawn, elicit predictions: how many students think that, over the session, the ones who always take the free signal will do worse on average than the ones who never do? Record the votes on the board. Twist phase: introduce rounds that mutate one ingredient at a time — a signal that reveals something about the prize pool rather than the deck (so the students' effective preferences over outcomes shift with the news), a signal that tells you whether the current round will even be counted in your final score (second-order information, about the game rather than within it), and a paired round in which your counterpart can observe whether or not you chose to peek before the two of you bet against each other. Debrief: map each twist onto the precise assumption of Good's theorem it breaks — which ones violate the fixed-preferences-over-outcomes condition by making utility depend on the information state itself, which one introduces agents who condition on your learning, and whether second-order information is a genuine counterexample or dissolves once the decision tree is drawn in full. The debrief should also include one fully worked numerical example, small enough to do on the board, showing the inequality doing its work.

Deliverables: complete rules, a materials list cheap enough for a graduate-student budget, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a point-based payoff scheme with no real money (points redeemable for something small — your choice). Then two honest notes. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: deck preparation errors, students peeking in the twist rounds purely because peeking is fun (which is itself a utility consequence the theorem never models — flag this), and the near-certainty that in a finite session the always-peekers will sometimes lose to the never-peekers by luck, which threatens to teach the opposite lesson. Second, tell me plainly which feature of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply in the debrief — I suspect it is the gap between ex ante expectation and any single finite run, and I want your judgment on whether anything short of pooling every student's draw across all rounds can make the expectation visible. Please write the whole thing in American English.
