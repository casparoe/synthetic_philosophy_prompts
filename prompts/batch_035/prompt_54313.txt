I started leading walks for a rambling club last spring, after two decades of only ever following other people, and responsibility for other people's safety on open hillside has turned what I assumed was a settled matter — that knowing something is better than merely believing something true — into something I find I can no longer cash out. I gather this is an old puzzle: Plato has Meno press Socrates on why a man who knows the way to Larissa should guide travelers any better than one whose correct opinion gets them there just the same. Rather than appeal to authority, though, I want to work it out through my own cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures my verdicts on them.

Last October I brought a group down off the ridge ahead of a squall by taking the left fork at a junction I had never crossed, on nothing but a hunch — and the left fork was in fact the quick, sheltered descent, so what I believed was true the whole way down. Verdict: I didn't know it, but my belief did the job exactly as knowledge would have, and I cannot find any respect in which the group or I was worse off that day than if I had known. Contrast my co-leader, who has walked that hill in all weathers and would have chosen the same fork: I feel she would have been in a better state than I was, yet every reason I reach for — prudence, justification, being entitled to the belief — sounds like it matters for the future, not for that afternoon. Third case: one of our regulars navigates by a private rule of thumb that works on our local hills only because every wrong branch there doubles back into the same valley; on those hills his true beliefs seem to me worth exactly as much as the expert's, since his instructions have never once misled us. But drop him onto any other range and the method collapses, so I would never hand him the map elsewhere; verdict: whatever extra value knowledge has, it lives in its travelling. The pair I cannot reconcile is the first verdict with the second: if my lucky belief did everything knowledge would have done on the day, then either there is a value in the present state of knowing that shows up nowhere in outcomes — in which case, what is it, and do I even want it? — or there is no such value, in which case my second verdict has to go and I must admit that at the fork, hunch and knowledge were equally good. A friend of mine presses the skeptical side bluntly: if all you want from a belief is that it be true, then once it is true that want is fully satisfied, and nothing about knowing can add to it, any more than a cup of coffee is improved by the existence of a machine that could also make different coffee tomorrow.

Could you give me a principle — or a short list of candidates — about when and why knowledge is worth more than true belief, stated precisely enough to test? For each clause, please identify the case of mine it is there to save. Then tell me what the principle predicts in two cases I have not mentioned: a navigator whose hunches are right not by ordinary luck but because of some feature of the world she has never identified, so that she is reliably right on every hill without being able to say why; and a walker who once genuinely knew the terrain but has, through no fault of her own, lost the ability to say why, though the belief and its truth remain. Finally, be explicit about which of my verdicts your favored principle cannot accommodate, and tell me what consideration would most likely change your mind — I would rather know where the reasoning is weakest than receive it as settled.
