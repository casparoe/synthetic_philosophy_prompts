I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar — 12 students, one 75-minute session — and we're about to start the causal exclusion argument. Design an in-class game in which the students run into the exclusion problem themselves rather than hearing me state it. The core mechanic I have in mind: some event occurs (pick a concrete one, e.g., someone's arm going up because they wanted to hail a cab), and competing teams representing different levels of description — neurophysiological, lower-level physical, psychological/intentional — have to stake claims to being *the* cause, while the rules allow only one causal-credit token to be awarded for the event. Include role assignments, materials, a payoff scheme that doesn't use real money, and at least one rule-change in a second round that lets teams try escape routes (e.g., claiming their event just is the other team's event, or petitioning to award two tokens). Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the argument's moving parts: the causal closure of the physical, the exclusion premise, and why nobody wanted the overdetermination option — and a note on which part of Kim's argument the game genuinely cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply afterwards (e.g., the event/property distinction, or why closure is supposed to be a priori). The students will not have read Kim yet; they should meet the problem before they meet the name. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it.
