Every few years I teach our department's seminar on philosophy of technology, and every few years I end up arguing with the same senior colleague about whether artifacts are political in the strong sense. She's a fan of the classic "do artifacts have politics" line of thought, and over the years she's issued enough verdicts that I've started keeping a tally. Here's her record so far:

- Moses's low overpasses on the Long Island parkways: political. (Yes, I know that story has been challenged pretty hard in the literature; she knows about the challenges and buys the story anyway.)
- The mechanical tomato harvester, as it was actually deployed in California agriculture: political.
- Rooftop solar, contrasted with big centralized generation: political.
- QWERTY: not political. A dumb standard, sure, but no arrangement of power comes with it.
- The ordinary hammer, and presumably the ordinary screwdriver: not political.

Next month she's guest-visiting the seminar and the assigned case is the shipping container — the standard steel box, plus the cranes and ports that got built around it, as deployed from the 1960s onward. I'd like to walk in with a prediction instead of a shrug. My hunch is this is the first case in the series where the rules she might be running actually diverge: the box looks like a plain efficiency device, no group excluded by design, but look what it did to the dock unions and to where things get manufactured.

Can you help me think it through? Please structure your reply in these sections, in this order:

1. "The candidate rules" (~200 words): Lay out the rules that would each make her whole track record come out consistent. I can see at least two — roughly "political iff the design itself settles a power question and would settle it in almost any society you dropped it into" versus "political iff, in its actual setting, it shifted power between groups, whatever the mechanism" — but state them cleanly and mention any others I'm missing.

2. "What each rule says about the box" (~150 words): Apply each candidate rule to the container case and tell me where they diverge, if they do.

3. "My prediction and why" (~150 words): Your best guess at her verdit, which rule you think she's really running, and what in her track record supports that guess over the alternatives.

4. "The tiebreaker" (~100 words): If the container verdict wouldn't distingusih the rules — or to stress-test the prediction — what case should I raise next in the seminar to force the issue?

Keep it conversational, dont worry about formality, and no reading lists — I want the reasoning, not a bibliography.
