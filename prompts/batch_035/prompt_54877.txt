I serve as a trustee on the investment committee of a mid-sized charitable endowment, and our deliberations have settled into a familiar standoff. One faction insists that everything be quantified: probability distributions over twenty-year outcomes, Monte Carlo simulations of spending rates, expected returns specified to two decimal places. The other faction responds that beyond a certain horizon this is theater — that we are facing genuine uncertainty about which our numbers tell us nothing, and that pretending otherwise imports a false precision into decisions that are really judgments of confidence. The disagreement has become almost theological, and I suspect it is an old one wearing modern dress.

My suspicion comes from some reading I've been doing on Keynes's work on probability. I gather that in his 1921 Treatise on Probability he treats probability as a logical relation between a conclusion and the evidence for it, but also introduces a distinct notion he calls the weight of argument — the completeness of the evidence on which a probability rests — and that these two can come apart: new evidence might raise the probability of some conclusion while leaving us, in a sense, worse off, because the total body of relevant evidence has become less adequate or less complete relative to what we now know we don't know. Later, in his economic writings, he seems to have leaned harder on this: in a 1937 piece clarifying and defending the General Theory, he distinguishes probabilities we can calculate from propositions about the distant future about which, as I understand his paraphrasable point, there is no scientific basis for forming calculable probabilities at all — so that for decisions like ours, about building a new factory or (in our case) committing capital for decades, the relevant attitude is a matter of confidence and convention rather than expectation in the mathematical sense. I'm also aware that our committee members keep invoking Knight's risk/uncertainty distinction as though it were the same thing, and I'd like to know whether Keynes's version and Knight's actually pull in the same direction, or whether the differences matter for practice.

What I would like from you is the most important implications of Keynes's treatment of probability and uncertainty — including the weight of argument, which our current debates never touch — for how a committee like ours should actually operate. Concretely: which kinds of questions should we insist on quantifying, which should we deliberately insulate from quantification, and does the distinction suggest any specific procedures (about how we record confidence, how we treat thin evidence, when we should demand more information before acting at all)? Please do not simply lay out what the quantifying faction and the uncertainty faction would each say and leave the choice to me: take a definite position on whether our probabilistic modeling is genuinely useful at our horizons, or whether it should be demoted to a supporting role, and defend that position with reasons, including your considered view on whether the probability-versus-uncertainty carving has real practical bite or is, in the end, mostly a matter of rhetoric.
