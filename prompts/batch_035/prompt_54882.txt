I am writing a novel set in Edinburgh in the 1770s, whose central character is a freethinking physician given to quoting Hume at moderate clergymen across the dinner table. In one scene I want him to demolish a minister's a priori proof of a necessary Being, and the natural passage for him to wield is Cleanthes's counterblast in Part IX of Hume's Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, delivered just after Demea has sketched his simple and sublime argument a priori. The passage, which I have copied out carefully, reads as follows: I shall begin with observing, that there is an evident absurdity in pretending to demonstrate a matter of fact, or to prove it by any arguments a priori. Nothing is demonstrable, unless the contrary implies a contradiction. Nothing, that is distinctly conceivable, implies a contradiction. Whatever we conceive as existent, we can also conceive as non-existent. There is no being, therefore, whose non-existence implies a contradiction. Consequently there is no being, whose existence is demonstrable. I propose this argument as entirely decisive, and am willing to rest the whole controversy upon it.

On the page it reads like a tidy chain of syllogistic steps, but the more I stare at it the less sure I am which links are doing the work, and I cannot have my physician brandish an argument I only half understand myself. My confusion centres on the middle steps. What exactly is "distinctly conceivable" supposed to mean here — is it the ability to picture something's absence, the failure to find a contradiction in a full description of its non-existence, or something stronger still? And does the argument not need a suppressed premise, running from my failing to conceive a contradiction to there being no contradiction, before the conclusion about demonstrability can follow? There is also a dramatic irony I want to get right: Cleanthes is the one character in the Dialogues who is religious on other grounds, and he follows the passage by inviting the material universe itself to apply for the office of necessary Being, so I suspect the argument is aimed less at piety than at Demea's specific proof. Finally, I have gathered that Hume elsewhere qualified his reliance on conceivability when it made trouble for his own account of space and the vacuum, which suggests he did not regard the principle as an unbreakable instrument.

Please help me understand the passage well enough to put it in a character's mouth. Reconstruct the argument as an explicit sequence of numbered premises and inferences, then say plainly which premise carries the weight and which, if any, begs the question against Demea. I want a committed reading rather than a survey: settle on what Cleanthes most plausibly means by conceivability in this passage, judge whether the argument succeeds on that reading, and tell me whether the verdict would change under the stronger reading. Please write your answer in British English, and do not hedge — my physician is a confident man, and I should like to know exactly how confident he is entitled to be.
