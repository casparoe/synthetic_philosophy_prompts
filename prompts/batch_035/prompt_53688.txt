I teach first-year agency and free will, and it winds me up that Buridan's ass and sphexishness always get lumped together as "funny cases where deciding goes wrong", because to me they fail in opposite directions. The ass starves because nothing distinguishes the haystacks — the procedure for choosing needs a difference that isn't there. The digger wasp drags its prey to the burrow, pops in to check, and if you nudge the prey a few inches while she's inside, she'll repeat the entire routine from the start, apparently forever — the proceedure can't register that nothing has changed. One is a breakdown from below (no grounds for picking), the other from above (no capacity to stop re-running the check). A student of mine swears the ass is just sphexishness in mirror image: both agents, she says, are stuck executing a rule outside the conditions it was built for. Is she right? If not, what do the two cases actually show differently about choice — one being about needing reasons to pick, the other about needing the flexibility to break off a routine? And which of them, in your view, cuts deeper against the thought that choosing well is simply a matter of running the right procedure? Take an actual stance, please — I've had quite enough of "both raise fascinating questions" this term.
