I teach an introductory political philosophy course, and the last unit of the semester covers conceptions of liberty: negative liberty as noninterference, positive liberty as self-mastery, and the republican or neo-Roman alternative, on which freedom means not being subject to another's arbitrary power — living at someone's mercy — whether or not they ever actually interfere. My students grasp the negative/positive distinction easily enough, but the republican view catches them off guard. The example that finally lands for them is the classic one of the slave with a kind master: an enslaved person whose owner never actually commands, restricts, or punishes her, and whose life is therefore free of interference in the ordinary sense — and who is nonetheless unfree, because everything she has she has at his sufferance. He simply hasn't happened to interfere yet. My co-teacher thinks ending a syllabus unit with a poem is pretentious, so I would like you to help me prove her wrong.

Write a sonnet in the voice of, or centered on, such a person — someone living under a benevolent master, or a modern analogue if you prefer (an employee under a manager with unchecked discretion, a citizen under a ruler who merely refrains), though I'd rather the analogy not be labored. Use the first two quatrains to build the picture of unhindered daily life: no orders given, no doors locked, the master genial, everything permitted. The volta should be the turn in which the speaker realizes that the source of her unfreedom is not anything that has been done to her but the standing fact that anything could be — that the master's restraint is a matter of his will and mood rather than her status, so that her liberty is his gift, revocable at any moment and for any reason or none. The closing couplet should crystallize the republican point: that freedom is not the absence of obstruction but the security of not living at another's arbitrary pleasure, and that the kind master is a master still. Please keep the diction clear enough for first-year students — the poem should carry the idea, not name-drop it — but exact enough that someone who actually works on republicanism and nondomination would find it right rather than merely gestural. Iambic pentameter if you can manage it without straining, and please avoid the word "freedom" itself in the couplet; make the couplet earn the distinction rather than announce it.
