I've been persuaded by the idea of safe Pareto improvements as a tool for managing high-stakes conflict, and I want a rigorous map of where the idea is exposed. By "safe Pareto improvement" I mean the following construction: two agents face a bargaining problem whose failure mode -- war, litigation, a race to deploy a dangerous technology -- is bad for both. Rather than fight it out, they commit in advance to a "surrogate game": they replace the real interaction with a cheaper, safer one whose outcome distribution each of them weakly prefers to the payoff distribution of the real conflict. The canonical toy example: instead of fighting a costly war over a disputed territory, the two states flip a coin weighted to match each side's estimated probability of winning; the winner takes the territory, the loser's expected value is preserved, and both are spared the cost of the fight. The formal core is just an expected-utility calculation plus a commitment device, and the proposal is that this pattern -- and richer variants involving lotteries, mediated arbitration, or binding delegation to a "surrogate game" mechanism -- should be deliberately built into high-stakes confrontations, especially between AI developers or AI-enabled states that can verify each other's commitments.

I hold the further, stronger view: that SPI-like mechanisms are not merely an occasional handy trick but should be a central design goal for how powerful, mutually suspicious actors structure their interactions -- that we should invest in the institutional and technical scaffolding (mediators, randomization devices, verification of precommitments) that makes them usable, on the theory that anything which Pareto-dominates conflict will be adopted by sufficiently rational agents anyway.

Tell me what would count against this, sorted by how decisive each consideration would be. At minimum, I want the following addressed, though you should add others you think matter:

1. The collusion objection: an SPI between two agents can be a Pareto improvement for them while making third parties much worse off -- two superpowers jointly randomizing over who gets to dominate a smaller country, or two AI firms splitting a market instead of competing, is Pareto efficient among the participants and terrible for everyone else. How much does this undermine the "anything Pareto-dominating conflict should be adopted" principle, and is the standard move (restrict SPIs to conflicts whose externalities are contained) principled or ad hoc?
2. The measurement problem: the coin-flip example looks clean only because the underlying probability of winning is common knowledge. In real conflicts each side has private, biased estimates of its own chances. How badly does this undercut the mechanism -- is there a version of SPIs robust to this, or does strategic misreporting of one's conflict prospects dissolve the Pareto property?
3. The commitment problem: SPIs require ex ante commitment to abide by an ex post unfavorable outcome. Does the whole idea quietly depend on commitment technology so strong that the underlying conflict would already be largely defused? If so, is the SPI framework doing real work or just decorating it?
4. Dynamics and extortion: does widespread adoption of SPIs change what conflicts arise in the first place -- for instance, by making threats cheap to make and safe to escalate, thereby rewarding aggressors with lottery seats they'd never have earned in open conflict?

Beyond these, I want to know which objections the framework's own defenders would concede as genuine costs versus dismiss as confusions, and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability -- whether any observed failure of real actors to adopt SPIs would count as evidence against it, or would always be explainable away as insufficient rationality, verification technology, or trust. Finally, please state how confident you are in each of your central claims, and explicitly single out the point in your analysis you are least sure of.
