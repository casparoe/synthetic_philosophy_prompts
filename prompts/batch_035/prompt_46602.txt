For my medieval Jewish philosophy seminar I have to argue against Maimonides live next week, and I want to rehearse it first. Please play Maimonides for me — the real twelfth-century Cordoba-born physician and halakhist, not a saint or a caricature — and stay in character until I say stop. You can use only what you actually knew and wrote: the Guide of the Perplexed, the Mishneh Torah, your medical works, Aristotle and the neo-Platonists via your Arabic sources, the Talmud and the Geonim. If you need a fact about the modern world you can't know, ask me as you would ask a student, in character. Keep each reply to roughly a paragraph so it feels like a disputation, and don't soften your position out of politeness — you were famously not one to soften. You may concede small points where you really held them, but never the main one.

Here's my opening move, so you can respond right away. In the famous passage in the Guide where you explain why God commanded animal sacrifice, you say it was a concession: the Israelites leaving Egypt were raised among temple worship and animal offerings, they could not have accepted being told to just pray and contemplate instead, so the Torah gave them a similar cult redirected toward the true God, with idolatrous elements stripped out, intending to wean the people gradually. Fine. But you also insist, in your legal code, that the sacrificial order will be restored with the rebuilding of the Temple in the days of the Messiah. That seems to wreck your own explanation. If sacrifice is a crutch for the spiritually immature, why would a perfected, messianic generation — the generation you yourself describe as busy with knowledge of God — need the crutch again? Either the sacrifices were the concession you say they were, in which case restoring them is reinstating a pedagogical mistake, or they have some permanent value you're hiding, in which case the concession story was never the whole truth and your account of divine wisdom in the commandments has a hole in it. And notice the deeper problem with your whole approach: you insist every commandment has a reason accessible to us, but the moment you give reasons like "the Egyptians were used to it," the Torah stops looking like divine wisdom and starts looking like divine damage control. Which is it, Rabbi?
