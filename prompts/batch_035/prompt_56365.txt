I teach a small evening seminar in philosophy of mind to support myself between novels, and I've just finished reading through the take-home midterm essays. One of them concerns a text I know well — the "Prelude... Ant Fugue" dialogue from Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, in which Achilles, the Tortoise, and an Anteater discuss whether an ant colony, taken as a whole, could think, and in which the Anteater describes his odd friendship with a colony he calls Aunt Hillary even while he eats her ants by the thousands. The midterm question I set was the following:

"In the 'Prelude... Ant Fugue' dialogue, the characters suggest a layered picture in which individual ants are like the low-level, unintelligent components of a brain, and in which certain organized clusters of ants, active at the colony level, play a role analogous to the higher-level entities out of which thought is built. Explain this picture carefully, making clear what is being mapped onto what. Then evaluate the dialogue's central analogy: what exactly is supposed to make the colony a candidate for having a mind, and what objections does the analogy face? In your answer, distinguish claims about the components' ignorance from claims about the whole's intelligence."

One of my students, whose essay I quote below in full, wrote:

"The dialogue's picture is that an ant colony can be understood on two levels. On the lower level, the signal level, each individual ant is very stupid — it follows simple chemical trails and has no idea what the colony is doing. On the higher level, the symbol level, the colony as a whole can have beliefs, intentions, and even a kind of personality, as shown by Aunt Hillary, whom the Anteater describes as an intelligent, talkative friend. The mapping Hofstadter intends is: individual ants are like letters or symbols, and clusters of ants working together are like neurons firing, so that a message spelled out in ants is like a neural activation pattern. The analogy works because of substrate independence: since intelligence doesn't depend on what the parts are made of, only on how they are organized, any system of many communicating parts — an ant colony, a traffic system, a market — is conscious to some degree. The main objection the dialogue considers is that ants are too small and too dumb to think, and the reply is that neurons are equally dumb, yet brains think. The deeper problem, which Hofstadter acknowledges, is that consciousness requires a very specific neural architecture, so although the analogy is suggestive, it ultimately shows that colonies like Aunt Hillary's cannot literally have minds, since they lack the right kind of wiring."

I would like a point-by-point assessment of this answer. Please give me your response as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion before or after the list. At least one item should state an overall grade and briefly justify it; the others should each identify something specific in the answer — a correct point that deserves credit, a conflation of levels, a misattribution, or an inference that does not follow — and say precisely what is right or wrong about it.
