A friend in my philosophy of physics seminar has an argument he presents as nothing more than Bell's theorem plus two innocuous steps, and I cannot find where it goes wrong, though the conclusion seems to prove far too much. Here it is as he gives it.

(1) Bell's theorem: no theory that is local in Bell's sense — the joint probability of the two outcomes, conditional on the complete state of the pair, factorizes into a product each factor of which depends only on the setting at its own wing — can reproduce the quantum predictions for spin measurements on an entangled pair.
(2) Bell's locality condition is simply the probabilistic translation of the relativistic ban on faster-than-light influences: if nothing can influence an event at spacelike separation, then the probability of an outcome here cannot depend on the setting chosen, or the outcome obtained, there.
(3) Quantum mechanics itself reproduces the quantum predictions; they are, trivially, its predictions.
(4) So by Bell's theorem, quantum mechanics itself is not local in Bell's sense.
(5) A failure of Bell-locality just is a dependence of the probability of an outcome on something in the spacelike-separated region — on the setting chosen there or on the outcome obtained there.
(6) Whatever a probability depends on, it is influenced by. If the probability of what happens here depends on what is chosen or what occurs there, then what happens here is influenced by what is chosen or occurs there, faster than light.
(7) So quantum mechanics itself describes faster-than-light influences, in every run of every entanglement experiment.
(8) And since nearly every system in the universe is entangled with something, these influences are not occasional exotica: special relativity's causal structure is false more or less everywhere.

My friend takes (8) to be the real lesson of Bell — that the experiments refute relativity's causal structure, not locality, and that physicists have simply been too timid to say so. I can't accept that, but I also can't isolate the faulty step, because each of (2) through (6) looks acceptable on its own. The step I would most like to keep is (2), and the conclusion I cannot swallow is (8). Tell me which step or steps I should reject and why those steps looked fine, and then price the alternatives: what does rejecting each candidate cost, and what positions are available to someone who refuses to say that relativity's causal structure fails everywhere? I would especially like to know whether the argument really is Bell's theorem plus innocuous steps, or whether something substantial has been slipped in along the way — and if so, exactly where.
