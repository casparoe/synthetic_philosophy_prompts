Our department's value-theory workshop has hosted, for six years now, a standing argument between two factions. The comparativists hold that when a choice is genuinely hard — the life of a touring cellist against the life of a city lawyer, a hiring decision between two candidates excellent in unrelated ways — the options are still comparable: they stand in a fourth relation, neither better, worse, nor equal, but on a par, a positive tie that makes choosing for reasons possible rather than arbitrary. The incomparabilists hold that hard choices instead reveal gaps: sometimes no option is better and the options are not equal either, and there is no substantive positive relation at all; what makes the choice rational is not comparison but the fallback structure of practical reason — you may pick either option so long as the choice fits your will, your commitment, what you can live behind. Both factions accept the small-improvement argument as fatal to the simple equality reading; both deny that hard choices are coin flips; both insist we often choose for reasons in them. Where they part ways is over whether the fourth relation is real or a renaming of the gap, whether the phenomenon is better explained by vagueness or by the sensitivity of comparisons to contextual considerations, and whether the inference from "neither is better" to "there is a positive tie" is licensed or a sleight of hand.

Draft the joint statement these two factions could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration conducted over a full academic year. The document should contain: (i) a numbered section of agreed claims about the data — the existence of hard choices, the failure of the better/worse/equal trichotomy to cover them, what the small-improvement argument does and does not establish, and what rational choice is like in hard cases — each claim worded so that both factions can put their names to it; (ii) a disagreements table, with every residual disagreement phrased so that the faction it describes would accept it as a fair statement of its own view, not a caricature; (iii) the cruxes — the subquestions such that settling one would move at least one faction — including at least these three: the chaining test, where one option is nudged upward by steps so small that each adjacent pair stays hard, until the endpoints plainly compare, and the question of which faction's machinery better explains why the chain doesn't transfer comparability downward; whether the fourth relation can be given an informative analysis or must be taken as primitive, and whether primitiveness here is a defect or an honest stopping point; and whether the mere fact that we deliberate and sometimes find reasons in hard cases is itself evidence of comparability; (iv) for each crux, the argument, test case, or thought experiment both factions agree would bear on it, with each faction's prediction written down in advance; and (v) an explicit verdict on which parts of the residual dispute are substantive and which are merely verbal — in particular, whether the two factions disagree about the relation between the options or only about what the word "comparable" should be reserved for.

The joint statement itself must remain neutral, but after it, add a final section signed by no one in which you break the symmetry. State which crux you judge most likely to move either faction, whose recorded prediction on it you would bet on and why, and where you think the winning faction's position is weakest. Do not present the two factions as equally matched everywhere: where you think one side's argument is plainly stronger, commit to that judgment and defend it.
