I fact-check claims for a mid-size newsroom, and lately most of our fights aren't about whether things are false but about statements that are technically true and feel like lies anyway. Like a politician saying crime doubled in the city on his opponent's watch when it doubled off a fifty-year low and is still lower than it was a decade ago. Half my desk says "it's literally true, we can't call it misleading," the other half wants to rate it misleading, and our rating system has no honest place for any of this. What I'd like is an actual procedure we can run, not vibes.

I remember enough from college philosophy to know the relevant ideas live somewhere around Grice -- that we assume speakers are being as informative as needed, relevant, and not oddly worded, and that a speaker can exploit those assumptions to get an audience to believe something false without ever asserting it. That's exactly the politician's trick, right? He says the true thing, we do the math to get the false thing, and he never technically lied. But I don't know how to turn that into something two fact-checkers can apply in the twenty minutes we have before publication, and I don't want a procedure that's secretly just our politics.

So please build me the procedure. It should say: what questions to ask, in what order; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; where the procedure stops and hands off to the editor because the remaining question is a judgment call no checklist can make (I assume "would a normal audience take away the false thing" is one of those). It should tell us what to do when the misleading takeaway is true for part of the audience and not another part, and it should be honest about the misfire modes -- like when a statement is clumsy rather than sneaky, or when calling it "misleading" makes us the story.

Then run it on three claims. One is the crime-doubling example above. Second: a governor announcing that her new program "has already served ten thousand families," where the program mainly rebranded services that already existed and the number includes everyone who walked past an information table. Third: a statement from a politician our editorial board endorsed, one where I have a feeling your procedure is going to come back with "misleading" and it will cost us -- I want to see whether the procedure actually binds us or quietly bends when it's our guy. And finish with what the procedure can't settle at all, so I know which arguments we're still going to have. Keep it practical -- my deadline is real.
