I serve on the interpretive planning committee of a national history museum, and we are finalizing the main gallery label for our new permanent exhibit on the Great Irish Famine of 1845–1852. The committee has deadlocked and I have been asked to circulate a written recommendation before next month's meeting. Draft that decision memo for me.

The dispute is over how the label should characterize the event's causes. One faction, backed by our diaspora donors, wants the label to call the Famine a genocide and to foreground the food exports that continued throughout the crisis. A second faction, led by our senior historians, insists that the label must present the Famine primarily as a natural disaster — the potato blight — compounded by policy failures, and argues that importing a postwar legal category like genocide into an 1840s context is anachronistic moralizing that our peers would treat as unserious. A third faction wants a middle path: famine as a "man-made catastrophe," language they point to in recent debates over whether Bengal in 1943 and the Great Leap Forward should be classified as crimes rather than tragedies.

The memo should not simply split the difference. It should recommend specific label language we can vote on, and ground the recommendation in the substantive question underneath the wording: is famine best understood as an event that befalls a population, or as a political fact about who retained the entitlement to eat while others did not? Amartya Sen's classic argument that starvation reflects a collapse of people's entitlements rather than a shortage of food is central here, as is his observation that famines can occur without any overall decline in food supply, and his further point that famines are comparatively easy to prevent in countries with a free press and electoral accountability. The memo should also confront the omission/commission problem directly: much of the case for culpability in Ireland rests on things the government declined to do — halting exports, continuing relief — rather than on affirmative acts, and the memo must say whether that distinction can carry the moral weight the genocide faction places on it.

As exhibit material, we have a letter from April 1846 in which Charles Trevelyan, the Treasury official overseeing relief, wrote that measures must proceed with as little disturbance as possible of the ordinary course of private trade, which must ever be the chief resource for the subsistence of the people, but that the people must not, under any circumstances, be allowed to starve. A million people died. The memo should address what we should do with a document like this in a label: whether it is evidence of intent, evidence of self-deception, or something the exhibit should simply let visitors sit with.

Finally, give the strongest honest treatment of the losing side, whichever side loses: the committee's decision will be publicly scrutinized, and the memo needs to show we understood the best argument against us, not merely the most convenient one. The memo should be short enough to be read before the meeting — roughly 1,000 words — and should end with a recommendation framed so that each committee member can see exactly what they are voting for or against.
