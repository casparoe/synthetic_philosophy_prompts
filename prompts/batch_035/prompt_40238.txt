I'm on the sentencing advisory board for my region, and at every meeting the chair falls back on the same picture: committing a crime is like incurring a debt, and punishment is the debtor paying it back. The point of the sentence is to settle the account — once the offender has "paid their debt to society," the ledger is clean and we should ask nothing further of them. He uses this to argue two things at once: that the sentence must be proportionate to the offense, in roughly the way a repayment should match the sum borrowed (which he takes to support retributivism against the consequentialist view that severity should just be set at whatever level best deters future crime), and that after a sentence is served, lingering legal disabilities — employment restrictions, loss of voting rights — are illegitimate, since you can't keep collecting on a debt that has already been repaid.

I'd like a careful audit of this analogy. Please take the role of a professional philosopher of law and think through the mapping explicitly before evaluating it. Specifically:

1. Set out the correspondence table: what plays the role of the creditor, the debtor, the principal, the interest, the contract, and the discharge of the obligation? Note that the chair's picture equivocates between two creditors — society and the victim — and say whether that ambiguity matters for anything he concludes.

2. Identify where the mapping breaks. Some candidate disanalogies to weigh: debts are normally incurred voluntarily, through contract or at least through accepted borrowing, while crimes are not consented to by the "creditor" at all; debts are commensurable in a common currency, while harms and suffered punishment arguably are not; debts are transferable and can be paid by third parties, whereas we think it matters that the guilty person serves the sentence personally; a debt's magnitude is fixed at the time of the loan, whereas the harm of a crime may depend on the victim's circumstances; and there is no lender whose resources are diminished in a way that repayment restores. For each disanalogy you take seriously, say whether it is fatal for the chair's purposes or merely cosmetic — a disanalogy in a feature the argument never needed.

3. Say what the analogy quietly imports. In particular, whether the ledger/balance imagery presupposes the retributivist's core claim — that there is a moral equilibrium that wrongdoing disturbs and suffering restores — rather than arguing for it, and whether proportionality really falls out of the debt picture any more determinately than it does from deterrence theory.

4. Tell me whether each of the chair's two conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table: does the proportionality claim stand on its own if the debt framing collapses, and is the anti-collateral-consequences argument actually strengthened or weakened by noting that real-world lenders do report defaults to credit bureaus for years after discharge?

5. If there is a better analogy available to the retributivist — for instance, punishment as forfeiture of an unfair advantage, or as an apology rendered in the currency of hard treatment — sketch it briefly and say what it does better and where it too strains.

Finally, please be candid about the epistemic status of your verdict: state which single consideration, if it failed or were reversed, would most likely change your overall assessment of the analogy's soundness, and identify the step in your reasoning where you think an intelligent critic of your audit would press hardest. If you find yourself genuinely torn on some point — for example, on whether consent-based disanalogies matter for state punishment, given that no one consents to legal liability generally — say so rather than papering over it.
