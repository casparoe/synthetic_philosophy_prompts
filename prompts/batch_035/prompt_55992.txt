I have been teaching Ian Hacking's work on psychiatric classification and what he calls looping effects of human kinds, and I have caught myself grading my own examples by gut rather than by any principle. Let me lay out my verdicts and ask whether anything captures them.

When homosexuality was listed in the DSM as a disorder, I think the classification helped organise an identity around itself, including a counter-movement that eventually forced the category's removal; that, I say, is a clear loop. When Victorian statisticians began counting 'the unemployed' rather than 'the idle poor', people came to understand themselves as unemployed and to organise politically, and I count that as a loop too. When a child is labelled 'gifted' — not a diagnosis at all — the label changes how teachers treat her and how she comes to think of herself, so I want to count it as well. But when a psychiatrist labels someone a psychopath, I do not think the classification changes the person one bit; a psychopath, if the label fits, seems supremely indifferent to it, so no loop. Actuarial risk scores applied silently by insurers or border agencies to people who never learn of them strike me as non-looping as well, even though they certainly change how the classified are treated. And I am genuinely torn about the widening of the autism spectrum: it has plainly altered how a great many people describe their lives and find one another, yet I am reluctant to say the classification made them autistic in anything like the way the unemployment statistics arguably made a class.

Is there a principle that separates the looping classifications from the indifferent ones and fits all of this? It will have to say something about whether the classified must know of the classification and respond to it, whether the change must reach the underlying condition or may be confined to self-conception and social treatment, and where that leaves my verdict on the autism spectrum, which seems to want it both ways. Please state the principle (or a small set of candidates) precisely, tell me which verdicts of mine cannot be saved and why, and say what the resulting account predicts for cases I have not mentioned — for instance a mooted diagnosis for daydreaming, sluggish schoolchildren, or an employer's classification of workers as 'burnout risks'. Please answer in British English.
