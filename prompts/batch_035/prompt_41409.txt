I am a high school student who has been programming in Python for about four years — small games, some data projects, nothing professional — and I have no formal philosophy background. Recently, while reading a philosophy forum, I came across Leibniz's principle that no two distinct things can share all of their properties, apparently called the identity of indiscernibles, together with a counterexample by Max Black: a universe containing nothing but two exactly resembling spheres, arranged so that the whole universe is completely symmetric, so that no property, not even a relational one, distinguishes one sphere from the other. As I understand it, Black's point is that in such a universe we would still say there are two spheres rather than one, which contradicts Leibniz's principle.

This struck me because it looks almost exactly like something I deal with constantly in programming. In Python, two objects can pass every equality check — same type, same fields, same values — and still fail the identity check: `a == b` can be True while `a is b` is False. Value equality and object identity are separate operators, and programmers use both without any sense of paradox. My instinct, honestly, is that Black's spheres are just a case of `a == b` but not `a is b`, and that the philosophers are making a fuss over a distinction every programmer applies daily without ceremony.

I would like you to test that instinct properly. Be faithful rather than charitable: if some part of the philosophical debate has to be distorted to fit my terms, distort it and tell me, rather than quietly reinterpreting it.
