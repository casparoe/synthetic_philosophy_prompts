You are an expert epistemologist, specializing in the value problem for knowledge, and I am asking you to review a set of position statements before I present them at a departmental workshop on epistemic value. Please begin by briefly restating what each of my commitments commits me to, brainstorm the possible pairwise and larger interactions among them, and only then deliver a structured consistency audit.

Here are my commitments as they will appear on the handout:

(1) The Meno problem is a genuine demand, not a confusion to be dissolved: knowledge must be more valuable than mere true belief, and the surplus value must be non-instrumental — it must survive even when the truth of the belief is held fixed. I regard deflationary answers on which knowledge has merely derivative or conditional value as evasions.

(2) Anti-luck epistemology is on the right track: knowledge is, roughly, safe true belief — a true belief that could not easily have been false — where safety is a modal, externalist matter of how things stand in nearby possibilities, not something the subject can verify from the inside.

(3) The only things of final epistemic value are true belief and understanding. Everything else we prize epistemically — reliability, justification insofar as it amounts to reliability, evidence insofar as it is a truth-conducive means — is of merely instrumental value.

(4) Understanding is neither necessary nor sufficient for knowledge. Small children and non-human animals can know things they do not understand, and the two can come apart in both directions.

(5) I am strongly drawn to the credit view: knowledge is apt belief, a true belief that is true because of the subject's cognitive abilities, in the way a bullseye is an achievement when it results from an archer's skill rather than a gust of wind. This view seems to me to hold out the best hope of explaining why knowledge is more valuable than true belief.

My question: does this package hang together? Please identify which pairs or subsets of my commitments are genuinely in tension and which merely appear to be; for each genuine tension, explain what generates it, what would have to give, and what the cheapest repair would cost me philosophically; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and be concrete about the kinds of cases where it matters (testimony, environmental luck, skilled performance that succeeds anyway). Do not lecture me on the background literature — assume I know it. What I need is the audit.
