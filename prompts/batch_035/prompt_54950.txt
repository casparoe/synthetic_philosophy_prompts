The instructor wants each of us to assign a mark out of 15 and to justify it with point-by-point comments, and she has warned us that the answers were written under time pressure and that several of the mistakes are of a kind that is easy to miss if you read quickly. Before I submit my grading, I would like a second opinion on how to mark it fairly and what the comments should focus on.

The exam question read:

The units-of-selection debate asks whether natural selection acts primarily on genes, on individual organisms, on groups, or on some other biological entity. (a) What conditions must an entity satisfy for natural selection to act on it at all? (b) State the main considerations in favour of treating the gene as the unit of selection, and the principal objections to that view. (c) Using either kin selection or group selection as your illustration, explain what turns on the choice of unit — why does it matter, in practice, which entity we treat as the unit of selection? (15 marks)

Here is the answer I have been assigned to grade:

A. For selection to act on an entity at all, three conditions must hold, as Lewontin pointed out: the entities must vary, the variation must be heritable, and the varying traits must affect the entities' reproductive success. Since genes, organisms, and populations can each in principle meet these conditions, the real question is not whether selection at several levels is possible, but which level does most of the actual work in evolution.

B. The case for the gene begins with the observation that genes are essentially the only entities that persist across generations: organisms are temporary, but a gene can survive through thousands of bodies. Dawkins generalized this into a distinction between replicators (the entities that get copied) and interactors (the entities that do the struggling for existence, namely organisms), and since the replicators are the things that last, they are the true units of selection. Moreover, every adaptation is encoded in genes, so all selection is ultimately selection among genes: describing selection as acting on organisms or groups is just a convenient bookkeeping choice, a matter of which statistics one prefers to use. The genic view also explains phenomena that the organismic view cannot. Segregation distorters such as the t-allele in mice spread even though they reduce the fertility of the individuals carrying them, which makes sense only if selection is operating below the level of the organism. The main objection on the other side is that genes are not directly exposed to selection, since selection sees whole phenotypes rather than lengths of DNA. But this objection is weak: a gene's phenotypic effects are still its effects, so the replicator framework handles it easily. I conclude that the gene's-eye view settles the debate.

C. Take kin selection. Hamilton's rule says that an altruistic trait spreads when rB > C, where r is the proportion of genes the two individuals share; a full sibling shares half of them, on average. So sacrificing for a sibling is favoured whenever the benefit to the sibling is more than twice the cost to the actor. This shows that what looks like altruism is really just genes helping copies of themselves: apparent sacrifice at the organism level is self-interest at the genic level. It follows that there is no genuine altruism in nature, and the idea of group selection is thereby made redundant. Wynne-Edwards thought that birds restrained their breeding for the good of the population, but Williams subsequently proved that adaptations can never evolve for the good of the group, so explanations of that kind are not merely rare but impossible in principle.
