In my philosophy of mind course we've just finished the extended mind thesis — the Clark and Chalmers idea that when an external tool plays the same functional role in cognition as a biological organ, it counts as part of the mind itself, with Otto's notebook standing in for the memory Inga carries in her head. Our final seminar session closes with poems instead of response papers, and I'd like to write a sestina from Otto's point of view in which the form does the argumentative work rather than decorating it. Specifically: choose six end-words, three belonging to Otto's biological side (his eyes, his head, his forgetting) and three to the notebook's side (pages, ink, entries), and let the sestina's fixed rotation shuffle them across the stanzas so that by the final stanza a reader can no longer sort the end-words into "inner" and "outer" without turning back — which is exactly what the parity principle demands. The envoi should end on one word from each side joined in a single line doing a single piece of cognitive work, so that the closing line enacts the thesis instead of stating it. The poem should remain faithful to the actual case — Otto's condition, his writing down the museum's address, consulting the notebook on the way — and must not slide into the reading the thesis opposes, where the notebook is merely a convenient tool Otto happens to use. Someone in my seminar who knows the paper should be able to reconstruct the parity argument from the poem's structure alone; someone who doesn't should still find it a moving poem about a man, his failing memory, and the book that keeps him whole.
