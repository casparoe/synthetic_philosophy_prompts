I want the distinct precise theses laid out as a menu, roughly: a providence claim where something (God? history itself?) is steering events; a bare empirical trend claim that things have on average gotten more just over recorded history and will keep doing so; a selection-effect claim where surviving moral progress gets remembered and backlash gets forgotten; a self-fulfilling version where believing it makes activists persist and that's what does the bending; and a purely rhetorical reading where it motivates without asserting anything at all. For each reading, tell me whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or just false, and — this is the part I actually care about — which of those readings would support my friend's use of it, i.e., which ones justify relaxing rather than working. Also I keep snagging on this: even if the arc bends, bends toward *what*? Who gets to say what counts as justice when we're checking whether history curved the right way, and is that a fatal circularity or just normal normative disagreement? One more thing: please be upfront about which parts of this are more or less settled among people who study history and progress for a living and which parts are genuinely contested — I don't want one particular philosopher's theory handed to me as if it were the consensus. A list I can bring back to my friend would be perfect. (Also if a couple of the readings are basically the same idea in different clothes, just say so instead of padding.)
