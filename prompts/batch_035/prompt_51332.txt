I'm on the academic integrity council at a mid-sized state university, and we've got a genuinely contested case coming up Thursday. Our engineering school wants to phase out proctored, in-class exams in favor of unproctored take-homes with a signed honor pledge, on the grounds that (a) remote proctoring software is invasive and glitchy, penalizing students with bad internet or ADHD tics, and (b) with AI tools everywhere, trying to police take-home work has become an arms race we're losing anyway, so we should pivot to trust and assessment design. The CS department is dug in hard against it: they say if you remove detection and enforcement, you're handing every student the ring of Gyges, and Glaucon's challenge basically writes itself — people only act justly when they can be seen and punished; make injustice invisible and consequence-free and the "just" students turn out to be the ones who were just never given a good enough offer. A philosophy colleague of mine on the council keeps pushing back with a wrinkle I half understand but want the memo to take seriously: she says the pledge isn't (only) a detection-free enforcement substitute but something like an identity cue — signing your name to being an honest person changes what the act means to you, which is supposed to do motivational work that neither surveillance nor deterrence captures. The CS folks also claim, more cynically, that the engineering school's real motive is that proctoring costs money and their students complain.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting. It needs a concrete bottom line — approve the switch, reject it, or approve with conditions (and if conditions, what they actually are, not vague "safeguards") — plus reasoning the losing side could in principle respond to, not just a restatement of our values. Please engage the Glaucon challenge head-on rather than assuming it's obviously wrong: if the council's position amounts to "we trust our students," the memo has to say why that trust isn't just naivety given that most of us would, at minimum, keep the ring in a drawer. And give the identity/pledge idea an honest shake — either it can carry real weight, or it can't, and the memo should say which and why. Keep it to something the council will actually read, like 800 words.
