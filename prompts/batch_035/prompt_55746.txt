I would like your help identifying a concept, and I would rather have careful carving than a quick label. Please take on the role of an expert working at the intersection of formal epistemology, decision theory, and the economics of collective belief formation, and think the question through before answering: lay out the candidate concepts you considered, argue for the best fit, and only then draw the boundaries.

Here is the pattern I keep running into.

Two restaurants sit across the street from one another. One has a queue. I join the queue, and so, as far as I can tell, did everyone ahead of me — not because we each independently investigated both kitchens, but because each of us took the crowd's choice as evidence about which one is good. The queue is thus largely evidence about queues. The same shape shows up at work: my team adopted a tool because another team did, and they are sharp; that team adopted it because a well-known engineer wrote it up after trying it once. Nobody in the chain has more than a single direct encounter with the thing, but by the time the decision reaches me it arrives as three independent endorsements. The same two or three early experiences get re-counted at every stage as though they were fresh evidence, and the crowd's confidence compounds while the underlying evidence stays the same size.

Three features of this bother me. First, each individual step seems defensible: audit any one person's reasoning in isolation and it holds up — nobody made an arithmetic error, and nobody caved under social pressure. The failure exists only at the level of the whole chain. It is a mistake with no author. Second, every so often the whole thing runs backwards: one respected figure visibly defies the crowd, and within months everyone's reasons point the new way, with the same confidence they had in the old way. Confidence appears to be conserved while direction flips — the building is slow and the collapse is instant. Third, and this may be the heart of it, everyone in the chain treats correlated evidence as if it were independent: a single piece of information, passed along hand to hand, gets counted once per hand it passes through.

The nearest things I know of are groupthink and the bandwagon effect, but both seem to name a psychological failure — people caving to pressure or lazily copying. In what I am seeing, the individuals appear to be reasoning correctly: given what each can observe, following the crowd is the smart move. I have also heard the phrase "wisdom of crowds," but that seems to be about averaging independent guesses, and my phenomenon is precisely one where the guesses are not independent. So my questions are these:

1. Does the core configuration — locally rational at every step, collectively bad at the level of the whole, with no individual error to point to — have an established name?
2. Is there a name for the version where the conformity is produced by the arrangement of who can observe whom, rather than by anyone's psychology? That distinction matters to me, and I suspect the field has a way of marking it.
3. Does the reversal part — the fragility, the flip on a dime with confidence intact — have its own name, or is it treated as a footnote to the main phenomenon?
4. If any part of this is unnamed, coin a term for it, define it, and give a couple of boundary cases. In particular: does it still count when people go along to be safe — reputationally — rather than because they have inferred anything? I suspect that is a different animal with the same footprint, and I would like to know whether you agree and on what grounds.

I am not looking for a reading list or a bibliography. If the configuration has a standard name, give me the name, but what I actually want is the account underneath it: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are.
