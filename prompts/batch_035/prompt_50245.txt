Retired physicist here -- mostly solid state, so I spent forty years treating finite chunks of copper as if they were infinite, since a mole of atoms is close enough to infinity for thermodynamics to come out clean. Lately I've been reading philosophy of math, and the fight over actual infinities has grabbed me: Hilbert's hotel, Cantor's counting, the finitists who want none of it. What I can't figure out is which parts of this fight actually matter and which are people arguing over words.

Here's my scientist's confusion, for instance. My old trade used infinities as approximation bookkeeping -- the thermodynamic limit, renormalization in field theory where you basically subtract an infinity from another infinity and call the leftovers physics. That seems totally different from the philosopher's question of whether an infinite totality can "really exist" all at once. But every book I pick up smushes the two together, and I suspect that smushing is where alot of the pseudo-problems come from. Meanwhile there might be real questions hiding in there too -- whether the potential/actual distinction from Aristotle does any work once you have Cantor, whether physics could ever in principle detect an actual infinity rather than just use one as shorthand, and whether supertasks (Zeno plus a lamp switch) are serious or just party tricks.

So here's what I want: rank the open questions in this actual-infinity business by which ones deserve the effort and which are overrated. I'm 71, so think of the budget as one retiree with a stack of library books and maybe a decade of clear thinking -- I can't learn forcing or proof theory from scratch, so weight tractability honestly. Tell me not just the list but the reason for the order: what makes one question upstream of another, and which popular debate you'd tell me to just skip because its answer wouldn't change anything downstream.

Give me exactly five items, numbered, a sentence or two each -- no intro or conclusion, just the five, with the justification baked into each one.
