For the past two years of retirement — I spent my working life in experimental condensed-matter physics — I have been reading philosophy of science in earnest, and I have now written up my position on scientific truthlikeness for the reading group I belong to. Before circulating it, I would like an audit of its internal consistency. Here is the position, as I hold it:

1. I am a scientific realist of the no-miracles stripe: the predictive success of our mature theories is best explained by their being approximately true, and this is a non-negotiable starting point for me.

2. "Approximately true" is to be understood as truthlikeness, or verisimilitude: an objective, graded comparison between a theory's content and the whole truth about the world. A theory is closer to the truth the more of the true consequences it carries and the fewer false ones it carries. I take the comparison to be a matter of the theory's consequences, not of extraneous virtues such as heuristic fruitfulness, mathematical elegance, or how easily the next generation of scientists corrects it.

3. Truthlikeness verdicts must be invariant under reformulation. Whether Newtonian mechanics is closer to the truth than its rivals cannot depend on whether we write it in the language of forces and accelerations or in the Lagrangian formalism, and it cannot depend on how we choose to define our variables. Throughout my career we moved between formulations of the same theory without ever supposing that we had thereby altered its relationship to reality, and I regard that practice as correct.

4. Newtonian mechanics is strictly false, and yet it is much closer to the truth than Descartes' vortex theory or Aristotle's account of motion. I regard this comparative judgment as secure — it is the paradigm case of theoretical progress — and it is what makes the history of mechanics a history of improvement rather than a mere succession of fashions.

5. The target of the comparison in (2), the whole truth about the physical world, is itself a body of exceptionless, precise laws. Call it the true theory. All truthlikeness comparisons are, ultimately, comparisons of a false theory against the true theory in this sense.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs stand in genuine tension and how badly; which tensions only look real and dissolve on inspection; and, for each genuine tension, what would have to give and what the revision would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I ask for a committed answer: where you judge that one of my commitments must go, name it and defend that verdict, rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me. If it helps you to bring in specific results from the literature on verisimilitude, do so in your own words, but I want the audit to stand on the argument, not on appeals to authority.
