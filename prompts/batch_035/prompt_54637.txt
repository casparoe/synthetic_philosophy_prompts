I've been defending lexical threshold negative utilitarianism in my reading group, mostly because everyone else there is a garden-variety hedonist and someone has to. Quick version of the view: there's a severity threshold T such that no amount or duration of happiness can outweigh any suffering at or above T, but below T pleasure and pain trade off against each other the ordinary utilitarian way. So a world with lots of scraped knees and hangnails plus plenty of happiness is fine, even good — the threshold is exactly what keeps this kind of NU from having to endorse painlessly sterilizing the universe.

My friend claims he can show the threshold doesn't actually do that, using the view's own machinery:

(1) In comparisons between certain outcomes, LTNU prefers a happy world containing only sub-threshold pains over an empty world — that's the entire point of having a threshold rather than plain NU.
(2) Choices under risk are settled by applying the axiology via expected value: you rank lotteries by the expectation of the value of their outcomes.
(3) Since no amount of happiness compensates any above-T suffering in a certain-outcome comparison, no probability-weighted amount of happiness can compensate a nonzero probability of above-T suffering in a risky choice either — whatever p is, the slice of the gamble where the risk materializes is lexically worse, and adding happy slices elsewhere can't fix that.
(4) So any option with any nonzero chance of above-T suffering is worse than any option with zero chance, however tiny the chance and however drab the zero-risk option.
(5) Every way of keeping sentient beings around — going for a run, having kids, just existing another day — carries some nonzero chance of above-T suffering, since T sits somewhere in the moderate-to-severe range and pain intensity is more or less continuous; you can always be unlucky enough to cross it, even by a hair.
(6) Painless annihilation of all sentient life guarantees zero above-T suffering forever.
(7) Therefore LTNU recommends painless annihilation over any continued existance — the exact bullet the threshold was built to dodge. Or, if you flinch at that, the view still makes us live like terrified actuaries, refusing every picnic because there's a 0.0001% chance someone gets a migraine at 1.01 times T.

He insists this works no matter where you put the threshold, even a very severe one, because p never has to be large.

Something feels off to me between (3) and (4) — like he's treating the threshold as assigning infinite negative weight instead of just imposing a different ordering — but I can't pin down what the licensed move is for a threshold view if expected value isn't it. And if (2) is the culprit, what do threshold people even use for choices under risk? For what it's worth, I'm pretty attached to (1), and honestly somewhat attached to (5) too — the part about being able to be unlucky enough to cross any line seems just true. So: which premise do I give up, why did it look fine, and what does giving it up cost me? If the answer is give up (2), I need to hear what replaces it. If the answer is give up (4), I want to know how that's not just sneaking compensability for severe suffering back in through the probabilities.
