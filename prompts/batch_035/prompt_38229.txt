I am grading midterm exams for an undergraduate course on the philosophy of death and would like a second opinion on one of the answers. The exam was closed-book and in-class, with roughly twenty minutes per question. The question the student was answering read as follows: "State the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the person who dies. Then present Nagel's deprivation reply. Finally, explain how Nagel answers Lucretius' symmetry argument — the argument that our attitude toward the span of time before our birth should match our attitude toward the time after our death, since in each case we do not exist."

The student wrote:

"Epicurus held that death is nothing to us. His argument is that while we exist, death is not present, and when death is present, we no longer exist; so death never coexists with the subject it is supposed to harm, and there is no one left for it to be bad for. This appears in his Letter to Menoeceus. Epicurus also argued there that we should not fear posthumous nonexistence because we already endured an equally long nonexistence before birth: the eternity before we were born caused us no distress, and by symmetry the eternity after our death should not either.

Nagel replies that death is not bad because of any positive feature it has — no pain, no dreadful experience awaits the dead — but because it deprives us of goods we would otherwise have had. He supports this with cases of unexperienced harms: the man betrayed by his friends and ridiculed behind his back is harmed even if he never finds out, and the intelligent adult reduced by brain injury to the mental state of a cared-for infant has suffered a great misfortune even though the infant-like creature that remains is not unhappy. These show that a thing can be bad for you without your being aware of it, which is what the Epicurean argument denied.

As for Lucretius, Nagel's answer is that the time at which death makes us worse off is indeterminate. Just as betrayal and other posthumous events are not located at any precise moment yet are still harms, Nagel says it may be an indeterminate matter when death makes us worse off, even though it clearly does so. So the Lucretian challenge dissolves: the question of when death is bad for you has no determinate answer, but that does not stop death from being bad for you."

Please assign a grade (a letter grade or a score out of 100) and give specific comments justifying it. I want a point-by-point assessment: exactly what the student has right, where the answer goes wrong, and how serious each error is — not a general impression.
