I teach political philosophy, mostly transitional justice stuff — truth commissions, amnesty, that whole world — and I have a puzzle about a friend's track record that I want to think out loud about with you.

Quick background. My friend from grad school spent years on the amnesty committee of a truth commission abroad (I'll leave the country out, it doesn't matter — the setup is copied pretty shamelessly from the South African one). The test the committee formally applied was the familiar three-parter: full disclosure of the facts, the act associated with a political objective, and proportionality between the act and that objective. But of course the interesting question is what she actually did with that test, because her recorded votes are what I've got, and I think I can see two or three different decision rules hiding in them. Here's her voting record on the big cases:

1. A young cadre in the liberation movement planted a landmine on a road used essentially only by military patrols; two soldiers killed. Full disclosure, said the operation was approved by his command. She voted to grant.
2. An operative from the same movement bombed a bank branch after hours to fund the movement but admitted skimming a cut for himself — disclosed everything, including the skim. She voted to deny.
3. A state interrogator who beat detainees during questioning, short of what anyone would call torture, full disclosure, said he believed he was implementing counterinsurgency policy and named his superiors. She voted to grant.
4. A movement commander who ordered the killing of a local informant, disclosed everything, but the man had only passed on low-level gossip and the movement's standing orders actually forbade unauthorized executions. She voted to deny.
5. A three-person fringe group nobody had ever heard of firebombed an empty government records office at night, no casualties, purely political motive, complete disclosure. She voted to deny — this one surprised people at the time.

Now two new applications are coming up and I want to predict her votes before I hear them.

Case A: a mid-level member of the main movement who put a bomb in a busy café near a government district. Nine civilians dead. He discloses everything, has no personal stake whatsoever, and insists the attack was the movement's openly declared strategy of making the country ungovernable — which is true. Case B: a man who killed a neighbor who had informed on his cell and gotten two comrades killed — but who also, years earlier, had an affair with the man's wife. He acted alone, off the books, but discloses everything and the victim really was a serious informant.

So: what does she do in each case? Tell me the candidate rules that fit her first five votes, which rule you'd bet on and why, and — this is the part I actually care about — design the next hypothetical application that would best discriminate between the rules you've identified, since her committee hears a fresh batch every quarter and I want to know what to watch for.

And look, if you think her record is just too short and messy for any of this, that I'm reading signal into noise, or that I've misunderstood how the South African test was actually meant to work — tell me that straight instead of manufacturing a tidy principle to please me. She's a caseworker, not a philosopher, and maybe her votes don't cash out into a rule at all.
