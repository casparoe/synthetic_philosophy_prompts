I teach an upper-level seminar on formal epistemology — twelve students, one 90-minute session, a whiteboard, and no software beyond cards, envelopes, and colored chips. Next week we begin imprecise probabilities, and before the students read a word of the literature I want them to feel why anyone would represent a credence as a set of probability functions rather than a single number. Design the session for me.

The activity should have three phases, with choices committed to paper before any discussion. First, an Ellsberg-style betting phase: one urn with a known color composition and one whose composition is unknown, students individually choosing among bets drawn from each, with small real stakes or a token currency so the choices cost something. Second, a phase that separates indifference from something weaker: offer students the chance to buy the ambiguous bet for a modest price and, separately, to sell it for a modest price, so they can discover that they refuse both trades while still being forced to rank the options on paper — the point being that any single sharp probability, even one hedged toward one-half, licenses taking one side of the market or the other, whereas a set of probabilities can leave both sides unattractive without the options being tied. Third, a dilation phase: partway through, reveal a draw from a third urn whose composition is correlated with the ambiguous one in such a way that, for every probability in the student's set, learning the outcome of the draw widens the range for the target event rather than narrowing it. I want them to feel the strangeness of paying not to observe something offered for free, and to work out what that reaction implies about whether their state is really a single number.

The debrief should then reconstruct, from the recorded choices, whether the class's revealed preferences are jointly rationalizable by any one probability assignment, and if not, exactly where the pattern of refusals breaks the sure-thing principle or invites a money pump. It should map each phase onto a piece of the formal apparatus — the set-of-probabilities representation, the difference between imprecision and merely not knowing one's own precise credence, and dilation — without becoming a lecture. It should close by saying honestly which feature of the imprecise view the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply myself; my own candidate is the normative question of why imprecision is preferable to a precise but unknown value, but tell me if there is something worse.

Please include a timeline with minute allocations for the three phases, the exact materials and how the ambiguous urn is physically managed so students can trust that no one knows its composition, and a note on whether the class should be told the setup's name before or only after. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students pricing ambiguity as ordinary risk, the majority drifting toward the sharp-credence answer because classrooms reward decisiveness, or whatever else you foresee — and how I should handle each failure in the moment.
