As a professional epistemologist, help me diagnose a debate I've been immersed in for the past year and can no longer see clearly. In my department's reading group we have worked through the peer disagreement literature — the conciliationist camp, which holds that discovering an equally intelligent, equally informed colleague disagrees with you should substantially reduce your confidence in your own view, and the steadfast camp, which denies this, holding that you may reasonably retain your credence on the strength of your own first-order reasoning. My observation is that the debate has effectively stalled: everyone knows the stock cases and counterexamples, the increasingly baroque definitions of "peer" and "reliability tie," and the back-and-forth about whether the conciliationist's own confidence in conciliationism is self-undermining — yet no one in the literature or in my group has budged in years. I have two competing hypotheses about what is really going on. The first is that the entire dispute is parasitic on the hidden question of whether there is a unique rational response to a body of evidence: if permissivism about rational strength is true, then two peers can disagree without either being irrational, and the conciliationist demand collapses; if uniqueness is true, steadfastness looks like special pleading. On this hypothesis, the peer disagreement debate is just the uniqueness debate wearing a costume. My second hypothesis is that the dispute turns on a structural feature of higher-order evidence itself: perhaps evidence about the reliability of your own reasoning cannot be treated as ordinary evidence to be weighed alongside the rest, because the weighing is done by the very faculty the evidence impugns — so that each camp is implicitly taking a stance on a question they cannot coherently address. Please think carefully about which of these diagnoses, if either, is correct, or whether some third account fits better: is this a substantive epistemological disagreement, a verbal one about the word "peer," or two camps answering different questions — one about what to believe, one about what to do — under a single heading? Support your diagnosis with what each side actually says, explain why the standard arguments fail to move anyone given that diagnosis, and tell me honestly whether anything — a new distinction, a formal model, a change in the question asked — could constitute genuine progress here, or whether the impasse reflects a bedrock difference that argument cannot reach.
