My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the grandfather paradox, and I came away convinced that her side of the debate has a fatalism problem nobody in the room could solve. Here is my position, and I would like someone to either refute it or tell me precisely where it goes wrong. If I travel back to 1920, then the mere fact that I arrive there already entails that my grandfather survives my visit: I exist, so nothing I do in the past can prevent my birth. Every attempt I make — buying the gun, learning his schedule, waiting outside his office — is guaranteed to fail before I make it. In ordinary life, whether a determined person succeeds at a task is an open question; for the time traveler it is closed in advance. That looks like a textbook case of fatalism about my actions, and it seems to apply to everything I do in the past, not just the murder attempts: whether I catch a particular train in 1920 is just as fixed as whether the gun jams. My friend kept retreating to the line that I can still try, and fail for perfectly ordinary reasons — a jammed gun, a missed bus, a change of heart. But that seems to miss the point entirely: in the ordinary case *some* attempts fail and others succeed, whereas for the traveler *every* world-threatening attempt must fail, and that difference has to be grounded in something. If it is grounded in Novikov-style self-consistency, then self-consistency is doing real causal work — constraining, selecting, steering — and that is exactly the fatalism I am worried about. If it is not doing causal work, then it is just the trivial observation that inconsistent histories do not happen, and I cannot see how the trivial observation explains why my gun jams on the one occasion when history needs it to. So here are my questions, in order. First, is the defender of backward time travel committed to fatalism about the traveler's actions in the past, or only to the weaker claim that the traveler's actions are counterfactually dependent on the future they produce? Second, is there a genuine distinction between "you cannot change the past" and "you cannot act freely in the past," or is that a distinction without a difference? Third, and most important to me: does the self-consistency constraint explain why particular attempts fail, or does it merely forbid the success of attempts without saying anything about which failures occur — and if the latter, doesn't the standard resolution quietly concede that the failures are either selected by something fatalistic or left unexplained? I should say up front that I have heard the version where the traveler simply decides, upon reflection, not to kill anyone, and I do not find it comforting: a decision that was always going to be made is not obviously a free one. Please answer in American English, and do not simply repeat the usual consolation that "you can try and fail for ordinary reasons" — I want to know whether that answer dissolves the fatalism worry or merely renames it.
