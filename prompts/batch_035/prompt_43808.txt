I work on uncertainty quantification for ML systems — ensembles, calibration curves, Brier scores, correlated errors, the whole bag — and my partner is a philosophy PhD student who keeps coming home with stories about the peer disagreement literature. She's tried to explain the lay of the land: conciliationism, which she describes as the view that when you find out an epistemic peer disagrees with you, you should move your credence toward theirs, roughly splitting the difference; and steadfastness, the view that you're allowed to just keep your original view because your own reasoning is already something you've conditioned on. I nodded along but honestly I don't think I understood either position until I started mapping them onto my day job, and I suspect my mapping is quietly smuggling in assumptions. So here's what I want: restate the whole debate in the vocabulary I actually use — ensemble members, aggregation weights, independent vs. correlated errors, calibration, what you condition on, distillation, maybe federated averaging — and do it honestly, not as a loose analogy but as a real translation. Don't pre-translate her positions into something convenient and call it fidelity.

Two things I keep noticing, and I want you to tell me whether they're insights or artifacts of my vocabulary. First: when two of my ensemble members disagree, I read the spread as an uncertainty estimate — I never treat one member's output as evidence against another member, because I know their errors are correlated (shared training data, shared architecture, shared priors), and averaging over correlated members is exactly where naive pooling goes wrong. Does the philosophical debate have a counterpart to the correlated-errors problem, or does it just assume peers are independent samples of the truth? Second: in federated learning you literally average model weights across nodes that have seen different data, and I joked to her that conciliationism is just federated averaging for humans. She said no, because in her literature there's a weird asymmetry — the agent whose credence it is can't treat their own belief as just one more model in the ensemble to be averaged, since doing anything with the average presupposes trusting your own assessment of the weights. That sounds like it might be the real thing my vocabulary can't say, since I always aggregate from the outside, as a spectator who isn't one of the models.

So give me the full residue report: for conciliationism and for steadfastness separately, what translates cleanly, what survives only with distortion, and what has no expression at all in terms of calibration and ensembling. Then take a stance on the residue itself — is it there because my vocabulary is missing something (and if so, what, concretely), or because the philosophical debate is less substantive than it looks once you see it as a question about how to aggregate credences? If it helps, there's apparently a middle position where you defer to peers only if they arrived at their view the right way — that sounds to me like weighting ensemble members by validated skill on held-out data, and I'd like to know if that analogy holds up or falls apart somewhere specific.

One more wrinkle she raised that I can't get out of my head: apparently there's an objection that conciliationism is self-undermining, because if you apply it to the disagreement about conciliationism itself, the view tells its own opponents to moderate, and something about that is supposed to be embarrassing for the view. In my world, an aggregation rule that does badly when applied to arguments about which aggregation rule to use just... doesn't come up, we pick rules based on benchmarks. Is that a difference in subject matter or is the self-undermining thing actually expressible in my vocabulary and I'm just not seeing how?
