At a dinner party, two friends of mine — Marco, who works in behavioral economics, and Selin, a philosophy graduate student — spent most of the evening arguing about whether weakness of will genuinely exists. I followed enough to know that neither was being foolish, but not enough to referee, so I am bringing the exchange here. It unfolded in four moves:

(1) Marco opened by claiming that akrasia is a philosophical illusion. The dieter who eats the cake at midnight simply prefers, at that moment, eating it to not eating it; behavior reveals preference, and calling the preference "weak" is just moralizing. Aristotle and Davidson, he said, were chasing a ghost.

(2) Selin replied that the akratic agent is not someone whose preferences changed between Tuesday and Thursday, but someone acting against a judgment she still holds at the moment of action: she judges, then and there, that not eating is better all things considered, and eats anyway. Marco answered that this "all things considered" judgment is merely an echo of her noon self, and the noon self has no authority over the midnight self; there is no fact of the matter about which preference is really hers.

(3) Selin then brought in hyperbolic discounting. When someone compares a smaller-sooner reward with a larger-later one, her preference can lawfully reverse as both draw near in time — the sooner good looms disproportionately at short range. So the noon self and the midnight self are not two arbitrary opinions; there is a systematic bias that operates exactly where weakness of will has always been observed, which is why "weakness" is a fact rather than a sneer. Marco countered that this dissolves akrasia rather than explaining it: if the discount curve is part of her, the midnight choice maximizes her own value function and is rational by construction; she has curved preferences, not a divided will.

(4) For her final move, Selin invoked the idea of personal rules, associated with Ainslie's work: because each choice sets a precedent, the agent faces her future selves as partners in a repeated game, and the midnight lapse is not bad maximizing but defection in a kind of prisoner's dilemma she had arranged with herself — and that, she said, is the sense in which the long-term judgment is really hers. Marco closed by arguing that if so, irrationality collapses into imprudence: the rule-breaker is punished by her own future distrust, so the lesson is just that treaty-breaking costs you, which is ordinary prudence, and "weakness of will" adds nothing but a moralizing tone.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say who is right at each step and to what degree, note where either of them concedes more than they need to, and identify what, if anything, both are missing.
