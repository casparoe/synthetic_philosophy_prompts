I am a retired chemist who has lately taken up philosophy, and I now sit on the environmental advisory committee of a lakeside town. An invasive mussel is spreading through our lake; if nothing is done, it will almost certainly clog the water-intake pipes within a decade and smother the native invertebrates. A company has proposed applying a new biocide: effective in short trials, but with sparse long-term ecotoxicology, so that no one can say what it does to the lake's ecosystem over twenty years. Half the committee insists we must not introduce an untested chemical into the drinking-water supply — better safe than sorry — while the other half says exactly the same about letting the mussels spread, since that too is a change we will not be able to reverse. Each side accuses the other of recklessness.

Please answer as a professional philosopher specializing in the ethics of risk and decision-making under uncertainty. Think carefully, and begin by briefly brainstorming the distinct questions buried in this dispute before committing to a line of argument. In particular: is the "better safe than sorry" position even coherent as a decision rule here, given that declining to act is itself a choice with foreseeable consequences; does the symmetry between the two kinds of risk ever genuinely break, and what breaks it — the magnitude of the harm, its reversibility, our relative uncertainty about each outcome, or something else; and how should a committee of non-experts, who cannot verify the biocide trials themselves, weigh expert testimony against the unknown-unknowns the experts cannot testify about? Conclude by telling me, plainly, what you would say if you held one vote at our next meeting.
