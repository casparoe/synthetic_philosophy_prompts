I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a feature about a large cross-cultural psychology project whose teams in different countries each follow their local research-ethics norms — what counts as informed consent, how much deception of subjects is tolerable, whether consent comes from the participant or from community elders — while the project as a whole publishes a single set of findings under a single banner. When I pressed a methodologist on whether that arrangement was coherent, she waved the worry off with "when in Rome, do as the Romans do," and the phrase has been bothering me since, because I can't tell which of its possible meanings was doing the work.

Take that proverb and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be expressing when it is used to settle an ethical question rather than a travel itinerary: as a bare report that moral standards vary from place to place; as a claim about who is epistemically positioned to criticize practices they didn't grow up with; as a norm of tolerance or restraint; as a full metaethical thesis on which local standards are what make moral claims true or false; and anything else that belongs on the list. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, substantive but defensible, or simply false; flag what is settled versus genuinely contested among philosophers who work on relativism, and don't present a contested position as the consensus; indicate which reading people who toss the line off in conversation most likely have in mind; and, most important, say whether each reading would actually support letting the research teams follow their local norms instead of one protocol. Lead with your bottom line, in the first sentence, about whether the proverb on its best reading sustains the methodologist's dismissal; then give the menu of readings with verdicts. No preamble and no restating of my question.
