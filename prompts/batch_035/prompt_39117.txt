I read a pop history book last year with a chapter on Max Weber, and I've basically been quoting it at people ever since. From memory, the chapter went something like this: Weber showed that capitalism was caused by religion. Before the Reformation, work was just what you did to survive, and making money beyond your needs was frowned on as greed. Luther changed all that by inventing the idea of the calling -- your daily work is itself a duty to God -- and that's the spirit of capitalism in a sentance. The Calvinists took it further. They believed in predestination, so nobody could know whether they were saved, and this made them anxious. Their pastors told them you can't earn salvation, but you can look for evidence, and the best evidence is success in your calling: if your business thrives, that's a sign you're one of the elect. So believers worked constantly, saved everything, reinvested instead of enjoying life, and that disciplined pursuit of profit for its own sake is what Weber called the spirit of capitalism, and it's what launched modern capitalism -- hence Protestant countries got rich first while Catholic ones lagged behind. The book closes by saying this settled the argument with Marx: ideas and culture, not material conditions, drive history.

I've repeated chunks of this and it mostly sounds right to me, but a friend who did her degree in sociology said the book "isn't really telling you what Weber claimed", though she couldnt spell out why either, and honestly neither can I. So go through it piece by piece: what would a Weber scholar nod along to, what's a simplification they'd grumble about but let slide, and what is actually wrong? I'm most unsure about the mechanism bit -- "success in business as evidence you're elect" sounds like a caricature when I say it out loud, but I don't know what the real story is or whether the difference matters for the thesis. Also the ending: does the big conclusion -- Weber proved culture causes economics, case closed against Marx -- follow from whatever accurate core survives your audit, or is the book cashing in a modest claim for a huge one? For what it's worth I'm inclined to think there's something to the culture story, I just want to know where the book oversold it.
