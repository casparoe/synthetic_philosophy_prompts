You're a professional philosopher who's spent years mediating between warring camps in philosophy of mind and philosophy of cognitive science, and you've been asked to run an adversarial collaboration between two researchers who've been arguing past each other for over a decade.

Camp one: a committed extended-mind theorist in the Clark and Chalmers tradition. Cognition ain't in the head, they say — if an external resource functions the way internal memory does (reliably available, automatically endorsed, easily accessed), then it literally is part of the agent's cognitive system, on par with biological memory. Otto and his notebook, and all that.

Camp two: an internalist, more in the spirit of Rupert or Adams and Aizawa. External tools are at best input to cognition and scaffolding for it, but the cognitive system proper is the brain (or at most the whole organism). The parity principle, they charge, is either trivial or proves way too much — anything conveniently available starts counting as part of your mind, and you get "cognitive bloat."

Here's the twist that makes this collaboration non-standard: rather than rehashing Otto, the two parties have agreed to center their disagreement on a hard concrete case, and I want you to use it throughout. The case: a woman in her early 60s with early-stage dementia whose memory runs largely through a shared digital system — a notes app and calendar co-maintained with her adult daughter. The daughter doesn't just transcribe; she curates, edits, prunes, and occasionally adds gentle reminders her mother never asked for. The extended-mind theorist wants to say large parts of this system count as the woman's memory. The internalist has a specific worry here that goes beyond generic bloat: if the daughter is editing the content, and the mother can't distinguish her own past contributions from her daughter's insertions, then whose memory is this? Maybe the extended-mind theorist has to say the daughter is now partly constitutive of the mother's mind — and the theorist is visibly uneasy about that, while the internalist thinks this unease is the whole ballgame.

Please start with a short brainstorming pass where you think out loud about what each camp would actually insist on in this case, then produce the collaboration's joint statement:

1. A numbered section of claims both parties can sign. These should be substantive — about reliable access, about what the curatorial editing does to the system, about what would count as memory vs. reminder vs. someone else's memory — not vacuous pablum.
2. A disagreements table. Each disagreement must be phrased so that its own side would accept the wording. If one of the disagreements turns out to be merely verbal (e.g., they might agree on all the facts but differ only on whether the word "cognitive" applies), say which one and why.
3. The cruxes — the subquestions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect one crux is whether the daughter's curation breaks the kind of integration the parity story needs, and another is whether constitutive vs. merely causal contributions to behavior can be drawn cleanly at all, but find whatever the real ones are.
4. For each crux, what test, thought experiment, or argument both sides would agree bears on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance. Feel free to invent concrete variations on the mother-daughter case (e.g., what if the daughter only ever transcribes verbatim? what if the mother reviews and re-endorses everything weekly? what if the app's autocomplete is doing the curating instead of the daughter?) — both parties should be able to say in advance which way each variation pushes and why, so the predictions can actually clash.

Don't turn this into a survey of the literature or pad it with citations — I want the actual joint document, not a reading list. And keep it honest: if some crux is the kind of thing neither a test nor an argument could settle, both parties should be made to admit that in the document rather than pretend otherwise. Take your time and think carefully, this is the kind of drafting where sloppy formulations make both parties refuse to sign.
