I'm giving the opening remarks — ten minutes, at the start of a small two-day workshop on debate-based approaches to AI safety. The room is split: about half ML alignment researchers who run debate experiments, and half philosophers of language and informal logicians who signed up mostly to find out whether any of it makes sense. I don't want a welcome speech; I want to use the slot to take a position, and I've settled on what it is. I need you to write the speech that argues it.

The position: debate — two adversarial models arguing in front of a human judge, on the assumption that truth has the advantage — is advertised as a route to truth, but the most it can deliver is a concentration of verification effort. Debate works when it makes the judge's task cheap, and it fails on obfuscated arguments: cases where the honest decomposition of an argument is long and full of steps no human can feasibly check, while a dishonest decomposition can be made to look short and clean, so the judge's verdict ends up tracking the shape of the argument rather than its soundness. The speech must defend my claim that this is not a patchable edge case but the load-bearing failure: obfuscation gets easier as models get stronger in exactly the judge-checkable style the whole scheme relies on, so the field should stop trying to patch debate for obfuscated cases and start measuring the judge's task directly — for instance, as the length of the shortest verifiable certificate the honest side would have to produce.

Constraints: the ML half of the room should leave persuaded that this is an actionable research problem and not philosophical grousing, and the philosophy half will pounce on anything that quietly conflates truth with verifiability, so the speech has to meet that objection head-on rather than route around it.
