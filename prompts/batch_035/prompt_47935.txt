I'm a PhD student in origins-of-life chemistry, and for an upcoming group meeting I've been asked to write down my working position on what counts as life so the others can stress-test it. Here is what I've drafted; before I present it, I want to know whether it actually hangs together:

(1) I favor a functional, cluster-based definition: something is alive insofar as it has enough of a cluster of capacities -- metabolism, self-maintenance, reproduction with heredity, responsiveness to the environment -- with no single capacity strictly necessary.
(2) Viruses are alive. Giant viruses settled this for me: they carry hundreds of genes, some of them for translational machinery that most viruses lack, and they unquestionably form evolving lineages.
(3) Sterile organisms -- a mule, a worker ant -- are fully alive, and their aliveness is not somehow borrowed from their fertile relatives.
(4) Whether an individual thing is alive supervenes on that individual's own intrinsic organization. Facts about ancestors, descendants, or the population it belongs to -- including evolvability -- are not part of what makes this particular organism alive.
(5) Digital artificial life -- self-replicating, mutating, competing programs of the Tierra sort -- is not alive, however impressive; simulated organisms are not organisms.
(6) The NASA working definition of life as "a self-sustained chemical system capable of undergoing Darwinian evolution" is, broadly, on the right track.

I genuinely don't know where the problems are, and I'm not protecting any one of the six. Please go through them pair by pair and tell me which pairs are in real tension and how badly, which only look like they conflict but dissolve on inspection, and for each genuine tension what the cheapest repair is and what that repair costs elsewhere. Structure your response as follows, in this order: (i) "My view, restated" -- two or three sentences, so I can check you've understood it; (ii) "Genuine tensions" -- ordered from most to least severe, roughly 150 words each, each ending with the cheapest fix and its cost; (iii) "Tensions that dissolve" -- the pairs that look problematic but are not, with brief reasons; (iv) "One revision to make" -- a short closing section naming the single change that would clean up the most, and why.
