I'm an undergraduate taking a philosophy of biology course, and we've now spent three weeks on the definition of life. Rather than keep circling the standard positions, I've been recording my own verdicts on borderline cases in the hope that they add up to something. So far they don't, and I'd like to work out the principle behind them, or find out which of them has to go.

Here are the verdicts. A bacterium is alive, obviously. A mule, sterile though it is, is alive, and so is a worker honeybee that will never reproduce in any sense. A HeLa cell in culture: alive. A human red blood cell: I waffle, but I lean toward alive. A virus in its extracellular state: not alive, though when a mimivirus is inside its amoeba and running the cell's machinery, it feels closer to alive than a flu virion ever gets. A prion: not alive. A self-replicating RNA system in a test tube, one that mutates and undergoes selection with no cell and no metabolism: not alive, even though it evolves. A forest fire: not alive, despite growing, spreading, and consuming. A bacterial endospore that has sat dormant and metabolically inert for ten thousand years: alive. A transmissible cancer cell, like the one behind the devil facial tumor: alive. A von Neumann-style self-reproducing machine that gathers its own raw materials and improves its own design by trial and error: not alive, though this is the case I'm least sure about.

Two tensions seem built into this list. First, I count the mule and the endospore as alive even though neither reproduces or metabolizes, which seems to defeat both the metabolic criterion and the evolutionary criterion taken on their own; yet I also refuse to count the evolving RNA replicator as alive, which defeats the evolutionary criterion even where it is satisfied completely. This matters because the NASA working definition, which treats life as a chemical system that sustains itself and undergoes Darwinian evolution, looks like it can't be the whole story if my verdicts are even roughly right. Second, the red blood cell and the extracellular virion look structurally similar — both are more or less inert, both require a larger system to do anything, both are doomed on their own — and yet I say one is alive and the other is not, without being able to say what makes the difference.

My tentative principle is something like: an entity is alive if and only if it is, or is a functioning part of, a self-maintaining system whose continued existence depends on its own organized activity. That gets the mule and the HeLa cell, and maybe the endospore if dormancy counts as an activity the spore is organized around. But I can see it either counting the virion as a functioning part of the infected cell or not, and I can't tell what decides that — which makes me suspect the principle is doing less work than it appears to.

So here is what I'd like. Is there a single account that delivers all, or nearly all, of my verdicts? If so, state it precisely enough to apply mechanically, tell me which verdict motivates each clause, and say what it predicts about cases I haven't mentioned — in particular, a synthetic minimal cell whose genome was written from scratch and transplanted into a shell stripped of its own contents, and a self-maintaining lipid vesicle carrying replicating RNA that nevertheless does not evolve. If no single account saves everything, tell me which of my verdicts to abandon and why those rather than the others. And please be explicit about where the account lands on the two cases I genuinely can't settle: the red blood cell, and the self-reproducing machine.
