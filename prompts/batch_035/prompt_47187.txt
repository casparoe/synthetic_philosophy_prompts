I teach a graduate seminar on digital ethics and am writing up my position on the metaphysics and ethics of virtual worlds for a symposium. Before I circulate the paper, I want an audit of whether my package of commitments is jointly tenable, because I can no longer see the set from the outside. Here is roughly where I stand:

(1) Virtual objects are not real. When my character picks up a sword in a game, there is no sword anywhere — no more than there is a detective at a fictional Baker Street address. I take the fictionalist line against the sort of digital realism Chalmers defends in Reality+, on which virtual objects are genuine entities realized in the computational substrate. At best, the sword is a shared fiction, however vividly rendered.

(2) Virtual harms are real harms. The well-known incidents of virtual assault and harassment in online worlds — from early text-based communities to immersive VR — wrong and harm the victim, full stop. What matters is that a real person is having an experience that is really theirs, and no ontological status of the virtual objects involved diminishes that.

(3) Taking someone's in-game assets — by compromising their account, or by exploiting a scam within the game — is genuine theft, not merely cheating or a terms-of-service violation. I want to say that something of value that was really theirs has really been taken from them, in the sense that matters to why theft is wrong. And I want this to hold even for items with no market value and no sentimental story beyond the time invested.

(4) I am, however, committed to a demanding principle about property: ownership and theft presuppose an owned thing, and value must attach to something that exists. No res, no theft of a res. I am strongly inclined to say that one cannot steal a fiction from someone — at most, one can interfere with their engagement with it.

(5) I also hold that virtual worlds contain genuine communities, artworks, and institutions: the guild, the player-built cathedral, the in-game economy are real social entities, on a par with money, borders, and universities. I take this to sit comfortably with the rest of my picture, since social entities are constructed anyway.

(6) Finally, I insist that the wrong of virtual harassment is located entirely in the interaction between persons — in what one person does to another's experience — and is not mediated by any virtual object or virtual event.

Please audit the set rather than lecture me on the literature. Tell me which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and, for each real tension, what would have to give and what that concession would cost me elsewhere in the set — I do not want to give up a commitment that secretly props up two others. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting a flat list, and tell me which single repair would clean up the most. Two requests on how to frame the answer: first, separate what is settled among people who work on these questions from what is genuinely contested — for instance, do not present any one analysis of virtual objects, or of virtual harm, as the consensus when the field is in fact split; and second, where my commitments pull against each other, say so plainly rather than harmonizing them on my behalf, since a paper that papers over the fault lines will not survive the symposium.
