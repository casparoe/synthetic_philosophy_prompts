Write a satirical operational policy document — roughly 700 words, in numbered sections, in the dry bureaucratic voice of a hospital's Office of Ethics and Emergency Planning — for a hospital that has fully adopted anti-aggregationism in triage and resource allocation, on the model of Taurek's position that the number of lives at stake should not, as such, count in deciding whom to save. The piece is a straight-faced reductio: it should follow the view faithfully into situations where the reader begins to feel the pull of the thought that numbers must count somehow, without ever stating that thought, since the document is written from inside the position.

The humor must come from the actual philosophical content rather than from invented absurdities, and the policy should include, at minimum, provisions for the following:

1. The coin flip as the canonical procedure when claims are equal, including the annual calibration audit of the coin, the rules for who may flip (a party with no stake in the outcome), and a grievance procedure for patients who lose the flip — one that does not, of course, aggregate their grievances.
2. An appendix on unequal claims: when a clinician's personal relationship to a patient (the "friendship provision") is grounds for weighting the coin, and how the hospital squares this with its formal commitment to every patient's equal claim not to die.
3. A prohibition on summing patient outcomes, which quietly dismantles the surrounding institution: the mass-casualty plan that can no longer plan, the epidemiology department that can no longer count, the quality dashboard that may no longer report "deaths this quarter" (nothing happened to anyone that adds up with anything that happened to anyone else), and the actuarial tables the hospital can no longer purchase insurance against.
4. Each patient's death treated as a loss that matters to that patient — with the corollary that no one else's stake in a patient's survival, whether a dependent child's or an entire transplant waiting list's, may be put on the scale either, since those interests would themselves need to be aggregated to be weighed.
5. A formal denunciation, in the organ-allocation section, of the transplant committee's old habit of preferring arrangements in which one donor's death saves five recipients, the committee having confused something that is good for five people with something five times as good.

Constraints: stay deadpan throughout — no winking narrator, no exclamation points, no asides revealing the author's distance from the policy. A specialist reader should come away convinced that the author understands the position (the coin's fairness, the refusal to sum losses across persons, the thought that what happens to David is bad for David but not a number in anyone else's ledger) and also recognizes, without being told, the standard pressures on it: the sense that five deaths differ from one, the contractualist question of what each patient could reasonably reject, and the worry that refusing to aggregate is not neutral between the many and the few but a decision for the few. End with a short closing paragraph from the hospital director expressing serene satisfaction with the policy's first-year results, the humor of which must consist entirely in what the director declines to count.
