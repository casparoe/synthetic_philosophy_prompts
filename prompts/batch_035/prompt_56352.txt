I am preparing a seminar unit called "Schopenhauer at the Keyboard of a Generative Model," and I need a prediction exercise rather than an essay. Assume Schopenhauer's mature system is fixed exactly as published in The World as Will and Representation, but that he has been granted a month of supervised access to three modern systems: a statistical model trained only on digitized scores that predicts the next note and can thereby compose entire movements; a real-time engine that generates unending adaptive music keyed to a listener's heart rate, breath, and reported mood; and a completion tool that finishes any fragment convincingly in the style of any composer in its corpus. He is told in full technical detail how these systems work, he can test them on nothing but music, and he learns nothing else about the modern world. The unit's question is which parts of his aesthetics survive the encounter intact, which bend, and which shatter.

Please respond with a numbered list of exactly five claims — no introduction, no conclusion, no prefatory sentence of any kind. Each item should be one or two sentences long and should state, flatly and assertively, a position Schopenhauer himself would commit himself to, not a guess about his probable reaction. Each claim must answer exactly one of the following five questions, in this order:

1. A model that has never striven, suffered, or wanted anything produces an adagio that moves listeners to tears. Does this confirm, refute, or force a revision of his doctrine that music alone among the arts is not a copy of the Ideas but a direct image of the Will itself?

2. His aesthetics draws a sharp line between music that wells up from the inner being of genius and the light, agreeable music composed to flatter the listener. If a machine can produce both and trained listeners cannot reliably tell them apart, does that distinction collapse — and does his image of the composer as a clear mirror of the world extend to a mirror made of nothing but statistics?

3. He treated music as a rare deliverance from the tyranny of willing. Does an infinite personalized stream engineered to soothe anxiety on demand count as that deliverance made ubiquitous, or as the Will's cleverest device yet for colonizing the one art that was never properly its servant?

4. Given that machines now generate music without limit and at no cost, does he revise his ranking of music above all the other arts, or does he argue the ranking is untouched because it never depended on how music came to exist?

5. He suggested that the composer reveals the inner nature of the world in a language his own reason does not understand, something like a sleepwalker. Does a next-note model, which likewise assembles what moves us without understanding anything, complete that picture — such that the machine composes from the Will more literally than any human ever did — or does the analogy fail somewhere he would insist on?

Anchor every claim in his actual doctrines — the metaphysics of Will and representation, his account of genius, his theory of aesthetic contemplation — rather than in generic Romantic aesthetics, and do not cite section numbers or secondary literature; I want reasoning from the system, not scholarship. If two of his commitments genuinely collide somewhere along the way, let the relevant claim say which one he would allow to break.
