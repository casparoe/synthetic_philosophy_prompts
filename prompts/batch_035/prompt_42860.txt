I teach a large introduction to ethics course, and in two weeks I have a single fifty-minute lecture, plus a follow-up discussion section run by my TAs, devoted to the question of whether sentience comes in degrees and what follows for moral status if it does. The students will have already covered utilitarianism and the standard arguments for extending moral concern beyond humans, but they will arrive with a picture in which the world divides cleanly into beings that matter and things that do not, and they will treat the line's exact location as the whole interesting question — fish? insects? nematodes? — rather than the line's very existence. I want the session to dislodge that picture, but I have learned from past attempts that if I simply assert "sentience is a matter of degree" as a premise, half the class takes it as a stipulation and the other half hears it as a vague gesture of sympathy rather than a metaphysical claim that itself needs content. So I am asking for a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself: what has to be established first, and in what order, before the gradation thesis can even be evaluated — for instance, I suspect the students need the distinction between sentience as a capacity for subjective experience and mere nociception or behavioral responsiveness before anything else, and I suspect the distinction between the intensity of an experience and the breadth of a creature's welfare range has to be separated out somewhere, but I am not confident about which should come first or how many of these distinctions one lecture can bear.

Second, I want your judgment on the carrying example. My instinct is to build the whole lecture around a comparison set — a honeybee, a fish, a human infant, and a thermostat — worked through in that order, because the thermostat is where the functionalists in the room (I have a few who took phil of mind as a module last year and will insist that heat-sensing is a rudimentary form of detecting a stimulus) get either productively confused or merely dismissed, and I cannot decide whether the thermostat belongs at the beginning, where it forces the question of what we are even measuring, or at the end, where it serves as a reductio. Tell me what the carrying example must contain and what it must not, whether my comparison set works or should be replaced, and where in the sequence the thermostat should enter and what job it should do.

Third, the misconceptions. I can predict some: that degrees of sentience straightforwardly track phylogenetic proximity to humans; that neuron count is a proxy for moral status; that if moral status comes in degrees, then the wrongness of killing must also come in degrees in the same proportion, which conflates quite different questions; and the functionalist slide toward attributing minimal sentience to any system with a feedback loop. But I want a fuller map of what students will actually believe or come to believe, in the order they will arrive at it, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than left to fester until the discussion section. Finally, tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level — the hard problem, the details of measuring welfare ranges, whatever else you judge — and how to say so without the students feeling shortchanged; and give me one question I can pose at the end of the lecture, answerable in a sentence, that would reveal whether they have genuinely replaced the threshold picture with a gradient one rather than merely learned to repeat the phrase.
