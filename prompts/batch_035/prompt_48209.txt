I'm on the board of a 46-unit housing co-op, and my unofficial job has become handling exception requests. We have a thick house-rules doc — quiet hours from 10pm, no subletting, guests capped at 14 nights a year, shared workshop has to be booked through the app, no storage in the bike room, residential use only, etc. — and basically every month somebody writes in asking to be excused from one of them for a special reason. The board is split between the "rules are rules" people and the "be reasonable" people, and we have no principled way to split the difference, so it mostly comes down to whoever argues loudest at the meeting.

Years ago I read a bit of Kant, and the thing that stuck was the universalizability idea: before acting on a maxim, ask whether you could will it as a universal law — the classic case being that lying promises only work *because* most people keep their promises, so the liar is relying on the very practice he's undermining. It struck me that a lot of our exception requests are exactly like that. Some exceptions only work because most people don't ask for them: one member wanted to park in the loading zone all of December because relatives visit for the holidays — fine when it's just her, a disaster if everyone with holiday guests did the same. And then other exceptions seem genuinely harmless even if everyone did them, and maybe those are the ones we should grant.

So here's the ask: turn universalizability into a procedure the board can actually run, in maybe fifteen minutes per request, with no philosophy background assumed. Things I think it needs:

- A way to write the request down as a policy statement ("maxim"?) at the right level of detail. I know this is where it gets gamed — "anyone in my situation may..." where "my situation" is described so narrowly it's just me. Give us a rule of thumb for when a description is too gerrymandered to be the thing we universalize, since that seems to be where half our arguments actually live.
- A clean split between exceptions that make the rule self-defeating if granted to everyone (everyone books the workshop "by exception" and the booking system stops meaning anything) and ones that don't defeat it but that we still couldn't all want at once (everyone sublets their unit and the co-op quietly stops being a co-op). If Kant people have names for these two failure modes, give them to us in plain English but don't make us use the jargon.
- The points where the test is silent and it just has to go to a straight values vote, marked as such — I don't want the procedure pretending to settle questions it can't.
- An honest section on where it will misfire. I can already tell it'll say no to sympathetic cases and yes to obnoxious ones, and I'd rather the doc admits that up front than have the board discover it mid-meeting and junk the whole thing.

Then run it on three real-ish requests from the past year: (1) the December loading-zone one above; (2) a member who wants to run a small home-bakery business out of her unit in violation of the residential-use clause, arguing it's low-impact and she'd be delighted if everyone did it; (3) a member asking to be exempt from the chore rotation because he travels most weeks, who proposes buying out his share instead of doing chores — he's genuinely not trying to free-ride, he just can't be there.

One more thing: tell me how confident you are in the reading of Kant you're using. I gather there's live disagreement about what the universalizability test is even supposed to rule out — whether the problem is supposed to be some kind of contradiction in the universalized rule itself, or a contradiction in what you can rationally will alongside your other ends — and I want you to flag which of those interpretive choices actually change what my board should do versus which are academic. And please single out the one step of the procedure you're least sure of, so I know where to keep a human override no matter what the checklist says.
