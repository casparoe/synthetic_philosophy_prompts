Every year the evolutionary psychology unit in my philosophy of science course goes off the rails in the same way, and this time I want to head it off at the pass. The pattern is this: a student (or occasionally a colleague, honestly) deploys the famous image of the mind as a Swiss Army knife — a bundle of special-purpose gadgets, a blade for cheater detection, one for face recognition, one for mate choice, and so on — and then immediately draws a conclusion from it: that there's no such thing as domain-general reasoning, that "learning" conceived as a single all-purpose faculty is a fiction, and that a bunch of cognitive science and education research is therefore chasing something that doesn't exist. Meanwhile a second student fires back with Gould's old taunt that the whole field is just-so stories, in the Kipling sense — clever tales invented after the fact to fit whatever outcome we observe, unfalsifiable by construction. And then they trade these two slogans at each other for the rest of the seminar while I stand there like a referee.

What I'd like is a template for auditing the first move, since it's the one that does the most work. Can you lay out the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the knife as a whole, the individual blades, the red casing that holds them together, the user who decides which blade to open when, and the manufacturer? Then show where the mapping breaks. My own hunch is that the analogy quietly imports things that cut against the very conclusion it's being used to support: a Swiss Army knife is assembled by a designer who already knows the tasks in advance, each gadget is useless until a user decides when to deploy it — which sounds awfully like domain-general cognition, not an alternative to it — and nothing in the knife changes in response to use. But I could be wrong about which of these disanalogies actually matter for the intended conclusion versus which are just noise, and that's exactly the distinction I want the students to internalize: an analogy can fail in ten places and still carry its conclusion, or fail in one place and lose it.

And then two follow-ups. First, the Kipling side: does the just-so-story analogy prove too much — it would seem to indict any historical science, geology and cosmology included — or is there a version of it that targets something specific to adaptationist reasoning, like the absence of a design standard that separates a genuine functional explanation from a plausible one? Second, the question I actually care about: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that there's no domain-general reasoning survive at all, or was the knife carrying it the whole time? And if the knife fails as a picture, is there a better analogy for a massively modular mind — a factory floor, a golf bag, a phone running separate apps — and, crucially, does the better analogy license the same strong conclusion or only some weaker one? If you can walk me through this with the correspondence table and a clear verdict on which disanalogies are load-bearing, I'll steal it for my seminar prep.
