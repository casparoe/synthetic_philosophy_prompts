I'm a backend engineer and I sit on our company's architecture review board, which is effectively run by one principal engineer, Dana, whose rulings have decided every big infrastructure bet we've made in the last six years. I'm trying to predict how she'll rule on a proposal coming up next month, and I'd like your help thinking it through. Here's the track record. In 2020 she approved our monolith-to-microservices migration even though the first year was brutal — her stated reason was that it was the only proposal on the table that would let us onboard two new regional teams without a scheduling free-for-all, and in fact it later enabled integrations nobody had planned for. In 2023 she rejected rewriting our billing service in Rust: the claimed performance gains had already been achieved by profiling and fixing hot paths, so the rewrite would just relocate functionality we already had. In 2024 she killed our move to an active-active multi-region database after initially supporting it, noting that every problem it had solved was a problem the deployment itself had created, patched with a growing pile of one-off fixes — custom conflict-resolution rules per table, a manual reconciliation job, and so on. And she kept our homegrown feature-flag system over a more mature vendor product, on the grounds that it had correctly anticipated three needs (per-team permissions, gradual rollouts keyed to customer tier, and one I forget) before anyone asked for them.

The new proposal is to replace our homegrown job scheduler with a "next-gen internal orchestration platform" built by the same team that built the feature-flag system. The pilot is currently at feature parity minus two features, and it has already needed three one-off patches for incidents in the pilot environment — each fixed with a bespoke workaround rather than a general mechanism. The team's pitch is that once the platform matures, product teams will be able to define cross-service workflows in one place instead of chaining jobs with glue scripts, something nobody has formally requested but that two teams have admittedly hacked around ad hoc. For what it's worth, a colleague of mine who did a philosophy PhD before engineering says Dana's pattern reminds him of Lakatos on scientific research programmes — something about progressive versus degenerating programmes, novel predictions, and ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses bolted onto a protective belt — though I only half-followed him.

So: predict Dana's verdict on the orchestration platform, and walk me through the reasoning. In particular, I want you to identify the candidate rules that fit her past rulings equally well (the Lakatos-style rule my colleague hinted at, plus whatever simpler rules — cost-benefit, "never fund rewrites," "back teams with good track records" — also fit the record), say what each predicts for this case and where they diverge, give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and propose the further proposal or pilot milestone that would most cleanly tell the candidate rules apart. If the honest answer is that the track record underdetermines the prediction, say so and tell me what principle Dana would be committing herself to with each possible ruling. Assume no access to our internal documents — reason from the record as I've described it, and flag anything about the new case I should go find out before the meeting.
