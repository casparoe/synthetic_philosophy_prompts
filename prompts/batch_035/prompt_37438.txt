You are a philosopher of statistics who has been hired to facilitate an adversarial collaboration between two camps who have been arguing past each other about Simpson's paradox for years. Please think carefully and systematically about this task before drafting, and begin with some private brainstorming about what each camp genuinely believes, where their formulations of the other camp have been unfair, and which of their disagreements might turn out to be verbal.

The two camps:

1. The causal resolutionists hold that Simpson's paradox is no paradox at all. Once you know the causal structure behind the data, the correct decision is fully determined; and without causal assumptions, no quantity computed from the data settles anything. The reversal is an artefact of treating associations as if they could be read causally, and the interesting question it raises is where the causal knowledge comes from — not whether it is needed.

2. The statistical formalists hold that the phenomenon is entirely domesticable within probability theory plus assumptions about sampling and exchangeability. The decision-relevant question, they say, is which groups are exchangeable with the patient in front of you, and that is a judgement about symmetry in the data-generating process, not a commitment to any causal metaphysics. They suspect the causal camp is dressing a familiar statistical insight in novel vocabulary.

To make things concrete, both camps have been given the same hypothetical trial, in which a drug was offered to 165 patients. Among the 60 women, 10 took the drug and 9 of them recovered (90%), while of the 50 who did not, 40 recovered (80%). Among the 105 men, 100 took the drug and 70 recovered (70%), while of the 5 who did not, only 1 recovered (20%). Aggregated over all patients, the drug group recovered at 79/110 (about 71.8%) and the control group at 41/55 (about 74.5%): the drug appears better for women, better for men, and worse overall. The camps also discussed a second scenario with the identical table, except that the partitioning variable is not sex but a blood-pressure reading taken three weeks into treatment, where background knowledge says the drug lowers blood pressure in most patients and that normal blood pressure strongly aids recovery.

Your task is to draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a good-faith collaboration. Please include:

- A numbered section of agreed claims — including about the arithmetic itself, about what Simpson's reversal does and does not show about probability theory, and about whether either camp is entitled to claim that the other's method gets the wrong answer in the two scenarios above (if both agree on the right decision in each scenario, say so; if they do not, that belongs in the disagreements section, not here).
- A disagreements table giving, for each residual disagreement, a formulation of the causal resolutionist's position they would accept as their own, a parallel formulation for the statistical formalist, and one sentence on what turns on it.
- A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. I suspect one crux is whether the extra assumptions needed beyond the data (both camps seem to concede some are needed) must be causal in kind, or whether weaker symmetry assumptions suffice; but identify the others yourself. For each crux, give a concrete test, argument, or case that both camps have agreed in advance would bear on it, and record each camp's prediction about what that test would show — for instance, what each camp predicts would happen to the reversal under a properly randomised replication, or what each says about a third scenario in which the partitioning variable is pre-treatment but is itself affected by whether a patient chose the drug. If you find that some part of the dispute is merely verbal — for example, about whether the word "causal" marks a genuine kind of assumption or a dialect in which ordinary statistical assumptions are described — say which part and why.

Please write in British English throughout, at the level of rigour of a professional statistician and philosopher of science, without citations or references to specific papers.
