At a public philosophy workshop on the future of work and leisure, two camps have been arguing for years, largely past each other. The first camp — call them the work-valorists — hold that demanding, absorbing work is a constitutive good of a human life: a principal source of meaning, skill, social contribution, and self-respect, in a lineage running from the Protestant work ethic that Weber analyzed through to contemporary defenses of career-centered lives. The second camp — the leisure-valorists — hold that modern societies have pathologically overvalued work: they point to France's 35-hour week as a live institutional alternative rather than a utopian fantasy, to karoshi (death by overwork) as evidence of what a work-valorizing culture licenses at its extreme, and to Keynes's 1930 prediction that his grandchildren's generation would work around fifteen hours a week as a benchmark that rich societies have failed to meet without offering a good reason. The workshop organizers have commissioned an adversarial collaboration between the two camps, and you are to draft its joint statement.

The statement should contain the following:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — at least six — that both camps could sign. These should include claims about facts both sides accept, stated in wording neither would object to: e.g., about the broad trajectory of working hours since the industrial revolution; about the documented health effects of chronic overwork; about how much discretion different kinds of workers actually enjoy; and about the awkward pattern, for both camps, that as societies get richer it is disproportionately the well-paid, well-educated minority whose hours fall.

2. A table of disagreements, each row phrased in wording the camp holding that view would accept as fair. Avoid slanted characterizations on either side: the leisure camp should not be described as advocating idleness, nor the work camp as rationalizing exploitation or drudgery.

3. The cruxes: sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. I suspect at least one concerns whether working hours are driven more by cultural valuation or by structural economic dynamics that neither camp's preferred policies touch; another may concern how to weigh time for people who do not experience their jobs as meaningful (do the work-valorists' claims about work's dignity apply to work that the worker herself finds meaningless?); a third may concern whether the goods the work camp values in work could survive a large, deliberate reduction of hours, or whether they are tied to work being central rather than peripheral to a life. Identify the cruxes as you actually find them, not as I have guessed them.

4. For each crux, a concrete case, comparison, or argument that both camps agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction about that case recorded in advance. Prefer comparisons the camps can assess without new data: the divergent trajectories of annual hours in France, Japan, and the United States since roughly 1980; the contrasting fates of hour-reduction policies for low-wage versus high-wage workers; the observation of what people actually do with time gained; and thought experiments such as a society in which hours are cut in half while the character of jobs is held fixed.

5. A closing section identifying which parts of the dispute, if any, both camps agree are verbal — for instance, whether it turns partly on what is meant by leisure itself (rest versus free activity versus cultivated idleness), or on whether work must mean paid employment — and why those parts are verbal rather than substantive.

Both camps are well-informed and intellectually honest: they will not invent empirical findings, and neither will sign a claim that stretches the evidence. Your task is the drafting, not the verdict: produce a document a sympathetic reader from either camp would accept as accurately representing her side, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than papered over.
