I'd like a specification of what any purported law of history would have to deliver, so that debates about whether history admits laws can proceed case by case rather than by slogan. By a law of history I mean, following Popper's target in The Poverty of Historicism, a law of historical development: a regularity strong enough to ground either explanation of large-scale social change or prediction beyond the very short run — the sort of thing Spengler's civilizational cycle or the Marxist succession of modes of production claim to be, and which contemporary projects like Turchin's cliodynamics are sometimes said to be inching toward. I am not asking for another rehearsal of Popper's attack; I am asking for the requirements list that attack gestures at, made explicit and evaluated on its own merits.

The desiderata should be organized around three purposes, since adequacy is relative to purpose: a law of history good enough for explanation, one good enough for prediction, and one good enough for policy guidance — and you should say where the requirements differ across the three. Among the requirements you should at least consider: testability given that historical sciences cannot run controlled experiments (is consilience across independent cases a genuine substitute?); robustness to the reflexivity problem Popper raised — the fact that a believed law of history can be self-fulfilling or self-defeating in a way a law of physics cannot, so that the law's own publication may be one of the causes that falsifies or sustains it; insensitivity to the novelty objection — the claim that each historical situation is unique because the stock of knowledge changes, which threatens the very idea of a repeatable historical setup; and a boundary condition distinguishing genuine laws from extrapolated trends, which Popper thought historicists systematically conflated.

For each requirement, give the motivating case or argument, mark it as non-negotiable or as a prejudice of one camp, and identify which single requirement excludes the most actual candidates. Finally, the part I most want: say whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, whether the reflexivity and testability requirements together rule out prediction-grade laws of history in principle, or whether that incompatibility is only contingent on current social science. A table for the requirements themselves is fine; keep the joint-satisfiability discussion in prose.
