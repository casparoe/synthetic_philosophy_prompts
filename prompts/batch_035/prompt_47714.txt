I've come to hold the scale argument for insect welfare: even if the probability that insects are sentient is very low, their numbers are so enormous that the expected moral stakes dwarf those of almost any other cause, so insect welfare deserves serious research attention and perhaps real resources. Tell me what would count against this view. I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd be, and I want them of several kinds: empirical findings about insect nervous systems and behavior that should drive the probability down to the point where even 10^18 instances don't help; structural objections to the expected-value reasoning itself, such as whether minuscule probabilities of moral status license fanatic or Pascalian conclusions and whether we should instead act on something like a threshold of credence before counting a being morally; and "proves too much" worries -- if the argument works for insects, does it also work for bacteria, plants, or silicon-based systems in a way that reveals something has gone wrong? Also flag what the view depends on but rarely states: assumptions in population ethics about aggregation and about whether small negative states can sum at all. Distinguish what a committed proponent would concede as counting against them from what they would dismiss, and give an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated by qualifications -- and if it has, whether that's a defect for a view of this kind or just what moral decision-making under deep uncertainty looks like.
