A friend who audited a semester of metaethics recently tried to explain the Frege–Geach problem to me, and I'd like to check whether I've been misinformed before I repeat any of it. Here is her summary, as I wrote it down at the time:

"Expressivism is the view that moral sentences don't describe facts but instead express attitudes — saying that lying is wrong is basically a sophisticated way of booing lying. The Frege–Geach problem is that this can't make sense of moral disagreement: if one person boos lying and another hoorays it, they aren't disagreeing about anything, any more than two fans cheering opposite teams at a match are disagreeing about a fact. Peter Geach pointed this out in the 1960s, borrowing a point from Frege about how the meaning of a sentence stays fixed across different contexts, and it's widely agreed that this finished off expressivism — which is why nobody works on it anymore."

Something about this feels off. I have the impression that Simon Blackburn and Allan Gibbard were expressivists who wrote at length in response to exactly this problem, which sits awkwardly with her claim that nobody works on it anymore. But she was confident, the booing analogy is vivid, and I don't know the literature well enough to say where she goes wrong, or even whether she does.
