I am a retired physical chemist who has lately been reading in the philosophy of well-being, and I would value a careful audit of a passage I am not equipped to evaluate myself. A popular book on the science of happiness that I picked up recently — no author I had heard of — dismisses Nozick's experience machine in a single paragraph, which I will reproduce in substance:

"Robert Nozick asked us to imagine a machine that would give us any pleasurable experience we could want: a life of stimulating conversation, real achievement, and love, all delivered as pure sensation, with no way to tell it from reality. Since only a fool would choose to plug into such a machine, he concluded that pleasure is not the only thing that matters, and by extension that every theory of well-being which locates value in the mind rather than the world stands refuted. But the experiment has since been run on actual subjects: when people are told they have been in the machine all along and are offered the chance to unplug into what they are told is real life, most of them prefer to stay plugged in. So their earlier refusal was mere status quo bias, and Kahneman's work on the remembering self had already shown that well-being is nothing but a retrospective narrative anyway. The thought experiment is empirically dead, and the hedonists were right all along."

Something about this paragraph strikes me as too tidy, but I cannot locate the seams. As a scientist I spent my career operationalizing quantities that resist direct measurement, so I came to the book half hoping it would vindicate locating well-being in states of the person — which is precisely why I distrust my own favorable reaction to it. Would you go through the passage more or less claim by claim and tell me which statements a careful philosopher of well-being would accept, which are common simplifications, which conflate distinct positions that the literature keeps apart, and which are simply wrong? In particular I would be grateful for your judgment on four points: (a) whether the paragraph is right that Nozick's argument is aimed at every theory that locates well-being in mental states, or only at hedonism in a stricter sense, and whether it matters that the summary describes the machine as delivering "pleasurable" experiences when the thought experiment was set up more broadly; (b) whether the described unplugging result actually establishes that the original refusal was bias rather than evidence, and whether the same bias story could not, with equal justice, be told about the subjects who stay plugged in; (c) whether the appeal to Kahneman's distinction between the remembering self and the experiencing self is being made to do work it was never designed to do; and (d) whether the final sentence — that the experiment is empirically dead and hedonism stands vindicated — follows from anything accurate in the preceding claims. If parts of the paragraph are garbled but a defensible version of the point survives the corrections, please say what that defensible version is, so that I know what to keep and what to discard before recommending the book to my reading group.
