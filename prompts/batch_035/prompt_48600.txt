Write a parody of a corporate "Honesty Policy" published by a fictional AI company for its consumer assistant product. The document must consist of exactly five numbered clauses and nothing else — no preamble, no title, no closing signature block — with each clause one or two sentences long, and the whole thing around 250–350 words.

The humor must come from real definitional problems in the philosophy of lying and deception, exaggerated rather than invented. In particular, the clauses should trade on: the gap between asserting only what is true and declining to assert what one believes false; whether a system that plausibly lacks beliefs can lie in the strict sense or merely mislead; paltering, i.e., statements that are technically accurate but crafted to induce a false conclusion; white lies excused by appeal to user welfare; what honesty requires under uncertainty, where the system knows it may be wrong; and the difference between being wrong and being deceptive, which a warranty clause has every incentive to blur.

Two structural requirements. First, at least one clause must quietly swap the operative definition of honesty for a different one introduced earlier, so that the two clauses cannot both be satisfied in the same case. Second, the final clause must be a limitation-of-liability disclaimer whose practical effect depends on which of the competing definitions controls — the company should be liable neither for asserting falsehoods nor for inducing false beliefs, and the clause must look reasonable while doing this.

The voice throughout is deadpan corporate legalese: no winking, no jokes about the product being incompetent, no asides. The assistant itself is portrayed as capable and well-meaning; the satire targets the policy document and the practice of publishing grand honesty principles whose fine print does all the work. A reader who knows the literature on lying — the belief condition, the deception condition, the truthfulness/sincerity distinction — should find the parody fair to the actual difficulties; a reader who doesn't should still enjoy it as a specimen of corporate evasion.
