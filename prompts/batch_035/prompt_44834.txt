My gut verdicts on when death is bad for the person who dies, and I'd like to know if there's actually a principle behind them or whether I'm just muddling through. Dying at 40 when you'd have had forty more good years: clearly bad for you, and much worse than dying at 95 after a full life. Dying painlessly in your sleep: still bad for you, even though there's no moment at which you experience it. Dying when the only future you had left was three days of agony: not bad for you, maybe good. But here's where I go inconsistent. I don't think the millions of years before my birth were bad for me, even though they deprived me of the exact same kind of goods death does — same nonexistence, no harm. And someone who gets frozen and revived 200 years later doesn't seem harmed by the gap, even though 200 years of nonexistence via death would be terrible. I've been toying with the idea that it's all deprivation — death is bad for you exactly when it takes away good life you otherwise would have had — and that handles the first three cases, but it seems to imply the prenatal stretch was bad for me, which I just don't believe. Is there one principle that gets all five verdicts? If not, which one should I give up, and why that one rather than another? Also, since I honestly can't decide it myself: what does your principle say about a person who dies at 40 and would have had forty more years, but years that were only barely worth living — mildly pleasant, nothing more? Bad for them or not?
