I'm writing a seminar paper on personal identity and want to stress-test a particular reading of Dennett's account of the self. By that account — the one he develops in Consciousness Explained — the self is not an organ or an inner spectator but something more like a center of gravity: an abstract posit that interpreters place in a person in order to make sense of the person's behavior, just as physicists posit a center of mass without expecting to find a little object sitting there. On this picture, a self is the protagonist of the narrative the person's brain (and their social world) spins, and its persistence over time works like the identity of a character across a long serialized novel: it can tolerate gaps, reinterpretations, even outright retcons, without the character being lost. What interests me is that this seems to decouple two things that ordinary views of personal identity run together: the smoothness of a life story and the accuracy of its links to what actually happened.

Here is what I would like you to build: a thought experiment, set in an ordinary clinical context (no brain transplants, fission, teleporters, or uploading), no more than a paragraph long, in which Dennett's narrative-gravity account delivers the verdict that the person persisted — one and the same self throughout — while a standard memory-based psychological-continuity account is driven to deny persistence, or at least to strain badly. My template, which you should adapt or replace if you can do better: some kind of acquired confabulatory condition, in which a person's true autobiographical past has become largely inaccessible, but the narrative faculty fills the gap seamlessly, so that the life story remains continuous and coherent while its causal and mnemonic anchoring in real events is largely severed. Whatever scenario you choose, two constraints are non-negotiable. First, the verdicts must fall out of the theories themselves rather than being stipulated: you may not just assert that the person is or is not the same, and you may not rig the case by giving the continuity theorist an absurdly demanding requirement (photographic recall, perfect correspondence to fact) that no actual defender would accept. Second, the case should not lean on the person's social environment papering over the rupture; it should be a case about the person, not about whether their family plays along.

After presenting the scenario, do the following in order. Walk through each theory's reasoning to its verdict, showing where the two come apart. Identify precisely which feature of the case is doing the work — presumably something about whether narrative coherence can be preserved while accuracy and causal ancestry are lost — and then say what happens to the case if you remove that feature (does it collapse into an ordinary, boring amnesia case where both theories agree?). Then give the continuity theorist their best attack: I would expect them to argue either that narrative identity, once accuracy is optional, cannot distinguish persistence from replacement, or that Dennett himself never intended the fiction of autobiography to extend this far, so the case exploits an artifact of the separation between narrative and memory rather than a genuine disagreement. Address both lines, and finish by telling me whether you think the narrative theorist has a principled response — in particular, whether sameness of character across a retcon is a principled identity verdict or a metaphor stretched past its breaking point, and which of the two is really load-bearing in Dennett's account: the coherence of the narrative or its causal ancestry. If you think the specification I've given is not jointly satisfiable in the direction I want, say so and explain what gives.
