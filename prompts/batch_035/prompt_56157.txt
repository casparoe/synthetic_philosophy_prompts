I should mention upfront that I spent my career not in a philosophy department but in an analytical chemistry laboratory: thirty-one years of mass spectrometry, a good deal of it devoted to measuring hydrogen and oxygen isotope ratios in natural waters, so that I can no longer hear the word water without thinking of a mixture rather than a substance. Since retiring I have been auditing philosophy lectures at the university, and I have offered to moderate the interview series at our lifelong-learning institute, whose audience is sharp but mostly non-technical. Next month's guest is a philosopher known for defending Putnam-style semantic externalism, the view that what my words mean depends partly on facts about my environment rather than on what is in my head. He is fond of the Twin Earth scenario, and fond of pointing out that in his story the chemist is the hero: the ordinary speaker who cannot distinguish water from the look-alike liquid still manages to refer to water because she silently defers to people like me. I have been asked to prepare the questions, and I would like your help drafting eight to ten of them — questions that go beyond having him restate the thought experiment, anticipate his most likely reply, and include a follow-up keyed to it.

I want at least one question on each of the following. First, division of linguistic labor: my profession is flattered by being made the custodian of the term, but I would like to press him on the fact that chemists themselves have not always known what water is — before isotopes of hydrogen were identified, no chemist on Earth knew that water was an isotopic mixture, so it looks as though the extension of the word changed while every speaker's beliefs and deference relations stayed exactly the same. Second, the condition he actually uses, the same-liquid relation: Earth water is not a pure microstructure but a variable blend of H2O, HDO, and H2O18, differing measurably between a glacier and a tropical river, and I would like to know whether his underlying-structure criterion is a single formula, a cluster, or a similarity metric — and, if he grants that kinds tolerate some microphysical variation, how he blocks the slide toward the kind of disjunctive, gerrymandered kind his own tradition treats as pathological. Third, what survives for the individual speaker: if my duplicate on Twin Earth and I are molecule-for-molecule identical, yet his word tracks the wrong liquid, I want to ask what content remains that is shared between us, and what his view says a person actually knows when she sincerely says water is good for you. Fourth, a continuity puzzle in the spirit of the slow-switching cases: if the water on Earth were replaced, glass by glass over a decade, by the look-alike liquid, I would like him to say at what point my word changed its meaning, given that nothing inside me changed and no one noticed. If you have room for further questions, I would welcome one pressing on the fact that ordinary usage and expert usage disagree at the edges — seawater, melted ice, homeopathically dilute solutions — and whose verdict he takes to fix the extension when they do.

Please write the questions so that they could be asked aloud to a mixed audience: technically precise where needed, since the guest and I can handle the chemistry, but framed so that a retired schoolteacher in the third row can follow what is at stake. And please keep my own skepticism out of the wording — I want questions that give him a fair chance to answer, not a chemist's complaint dressed up as an inquiry.
