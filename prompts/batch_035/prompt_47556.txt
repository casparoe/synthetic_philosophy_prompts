My wife, who is an archaeologist working on the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, and I spent most of last night's dinner arguing about something, and I have been unable to construct a satisfying reply since. I would like you to help me. Please take on the role of a philosopher of history with a serious interest in historiography and the epistemology of retrospective judgment, and think carefully before committing to a diagnosis — start by brainstorming several places the argument might go wrong, evaluate each, and only then tell me which you actually reject and why.

Her argument runs as follows. (1) Every judgment that a past society "collapsed" is made after the fact, by observers who already know how things turned out. (2) Hindsight bias is a systematic tendency of such observers: once an outcome is known, it comes to seem inevitable, and the antecedent conditions are reconstructed as a chain pointing toward it all along. (3) The narrative fallacy compounds this: we impose a story-shape — rise, hubris, fall — on sequences that no one living through them experienced as a story at all. (4) A judgment produced by processes like these cannot count as knowledge, whatever its truth. (5) Therefore we are never justified in asserting that any society collapsed; at best we can say that it changed, as all societies are always changing. (6) A concept with no justified applications should be retired. (7) So we should stop saying that the southern lowland Maya, the Mycenaean palatial system, or Rapa Nui "collapsed."

She also pressed a supporting point I found genuinely uncomfortable. Much of the evidence for Bronze Age "collapse," she says, is elite evidence: burned palaces, the cessation of Linear B administration, the interruption of long-distance exchange in copper and tin, the disappearance of the diplomatic correspondence we happen to be able to read. Rural settlement in many regions shows far more continuity than the palace record suggests, and the revisionist work on Rapa Nui has made a similar point about the standard ecocide story. So, she says, what we call a collapse is often the collapse of our window — of literate, monumental, institutionally visible strata — rather than of a society, and the very selection effects that make the past visible to us also make its endings look like endings.

When I objected that her argument proves too much — that the same reasoning would retire "flourished," "declined," "recovered," and indeed every retrospective evaluative term we apply to history, since all retrospective judgment has the same structure — she shrugged and said that was fine, maybe we should be that austere.

I don't want to be that austere. For my part, I would very much like to keep some version of (2) and (3), since hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy are real and clearly distort historiography; I also don't want to deny her point about elite visibility. But I don't accept (7), and I especially don't accept the austerity her shrug commits her to. So: which premise or inference should I reject, what does rejecting it cost, and how much of her argument survives the diagnosis? In particular, I want to know whether there is a principled line between "hindsight-biased narratives distort our judgments about the past" and "we are never entitled to those judgments" — or whether the interesting action is instead in redefining "collapse" as something operational and non-narrative, say a sustained drop in regional population or in political centralization, that does not depend on anyone's story-shape. If that move works, does it also answer her point about our window, or does the window problem survive as an independent objection? Please price the alternatives honestly, including the ones I would rather not pay.
