I'm a sixth-form student writing an extended project on the law of evidence, and I've got stuck on something that seems less legal than philosophical, so I hope you can help me untangle it. In common-law courts, a witness is generally barred from repeating what somebody else said out of court — the hearsay rule — on the grounds that the original speaker cannot be cross-examined, was not under oath, and cannot be observed by the jury whilst speaking. Yet the rule is full of holes: excited utterances blurted out under the stress of a startling event, dying declarations, routine business records, and confessions against the speaker's own interest are all admissible. Meanwhile, the jury is perfectly free to believe a first-hand eyewitness whom nobody has checked for a track record of accuracy, even though mistaken eyewitness identification is a leading cause of wrongful convictions. Parliament has even relaxed the hearsay rule in England and Wales in recent decades, admitting second-hand statements in various situations where the courts judge them dependable.

The philosophical connection, as my supervisor sketched it, is to the debate over testimony between reductionists and anti-reductionists. My (possibly shaky) understanding is this: the reductionist holds that a hearer is justified in believing what they are told only if they possess independent positive reasons — a general track record of the speaker's reliability, or background evidence that testimony is usually true — whereas the anti-reductionist holds that testimony is a basic source of justification, like perception or memory, so that in the absence of special defeaters one is entitled to believe what one is told without any prior checking. What I would like is a reconstruction of the implicit epistemology of the hearsay rule and its exceptions. Does the core rule presuppose a reductionist picture, in which hearsay is worthless until validated by something other than the speaker's word, while the treatment of ordinary eyewitnesses presupposes an anti-reductionist entitlement to believe a present, sworn witness unless something specific discredits them? Or can one theory cover both?

I'd also like you to be disciplined about which bits of the practice strictly presuppose which views, as opposed to merely sitting comfortably with them. It strikes me that the exceptions point in different directions: the business-records exception seems justified on straight reliability grounds, which sounds reductionist, whilst the dying declaration seems to rest on an old psychological assumption that people facing death do not lie — a very different kind of guarantee. Finally, tell me what would actually have to change in courtroom practice if the law were rewritten first on a thoroughly reductionist epistemology and then on a thoroughly anti-reductionist one: which parts of the current rules would survive each regime, and does the overall practice come out coherent, or does it reveal a legal system operating with two incompatible theories of when we may believe what we are told?
