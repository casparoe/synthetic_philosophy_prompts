A friend of mine who's really into the antinatalism stuff cornered me at a party and I've been chewing on his argument for a week now. He's trying to show that Benatar's asymmetry, which he actually endorses, drags you to a conclusion even he flinches from, and I cannot find where it slips. It goes like this:

(1) Benatar's asymmetry: for anyone, coming into existence is a harm — their life taken as a whole is worse for them than never existing — while never existing is never a harm. He stresses this is a claim about whole lives, not about moments, and that it holds no matter how pleasant the life is.

(2) A life is a temporal whole built out of segments, and whether a life, or any stretch of one, is good or bad for the person is just the balance of bads against goods inside it. My friend says this isn't some extra assumption he's smuggling in — it's how anyone has to reason to call one life better or worse than another at all, Benatar included.

(3) Now take someone alive at time t and compare two futures: continuing past t, or stopping at t — dying painlessly at t, no pain in the picture. If continuing were better for them than stopping, the post-t segments would have to be net good, contributing more good than bad. But then those same segments, stacked on top of the earlier ones, would push the whole life toward the good side of the line. And by (1) no whole life ever gets to the good side, no matter how pleasant. So no continuation is ever better than stopping.

(4) Therefore for anyone, at any moment, a painless death right then is at least as good for them as going on — dying is never worse for you, and usually better.

(5) That's the pro-mortalist conclusion, and my friend backs off right here — but he says that's just him lacking the courage of the asymmetry, not a flaw in the reasoning.

I'm never accepting (5), and I'd honestly rather keep (1) — I find the asymmetry pretty persuasive and I don't want to be pushed off it just to dodge a bullet. I feel like the problem is somewhere between (3) and (4) — the move from "the whole life is a harm" to "no continuation beats stopping" — but I can't say exactly what's wrong with it, and when I pushed back he asked me which premise I'm rejecting, becuase (2) is either true or the entire practice of comparing lives collapses. That shut me up.

So: where does this go wrong? Tell me which step to reject, and what rejecting it costs me — if rejecting (3) means lives have some value as wholes that isn't just the sum of their parts, my friend will say that's exactly what he was demanding all along, so I want to know what I'm paying for it. Also, please tell me how confident you actually are in your diagnosis, and single out the step you're least sure about — I'd much rather get a maybe here and maybe there than a confident wrong answer.
