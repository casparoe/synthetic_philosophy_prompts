So I may have made a mistake at our end-of-semester drinks. Our methods professor is on medical leave for the fall, and after a couple of beers I agreed to cover the opening session of his graduate seminar — the one on what actually counts as an explanation in history. I'm a third-year PhD student, I've run survey sections before, but never this. The syllabus frames the session around the old covering-law idea (Hempel's: explaining an event means subsuming it under a general law, the way you'd explain a pendulum) and the pushback that this just isn't what historians do — they explain by tracing a path through a sequence of events where each step makes the next intelligible, and the explanation is the whole path, plus the nagging sense that at several forks it could plausibly have gone the other way. His notes for the session literally just say "make them feel the gap," which is a mood, not a lesson plan.

The audience is incoming MA students, mostly historians plus a couple from sociology, no real philosophy background. My worry is that they'll split into two camps that both miss the point: half will find covering-law obviously right because "science is the gold standard, history should aspire to it," and half will find it obviously silly because "nobody thinks there are laws of history," and neither camp will have noticed that the interesting question is what the narrative people think they're doing when they explain something without laws. I don't want the philosophy-of-history survey lecture; I want them leaving able to open a page of real historical writing and say something about what kind of explanatory work a given paragraph is doing. Don't explain the debate to me, I know it well enough — I need the pedagogy.

Give me the plan as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no introduction or conclusion: (1) what to establish first and why it has to come before anything else, (2) the one concrete case that should carry the whole session and what it must have and lack, (3) the misconception I should expect from this audience and the exact point in the sequence to head it off, (4) what to openly refuse to cover at this level and say so, and (5) the single question I can ask at the end that would show me whether they actually got it.
