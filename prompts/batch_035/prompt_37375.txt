In my ethics class we were discussing acts that are good but not required — a soldier throwing himself on a grenade to save his squad, someone donating a kidney to a stranger — and my teacher called these supererogatory. To explain the category she used the analogy of an exam: duties are the required questions, supererogatory acts are extra credit. You are obligated to answer the required questions; extra credit is praiseworthy if you do it, but skipping it costs you nothing.

After class, a friend used this to argue that someone who declines to donate a kidney to a stranger has done nothing wrong at all — after all, it was extra credit, and no one is penalized for skipping extra credit. Something about that move bothers me, but I cannot articulate exactly where it goes wrong, or whether it goes wrong at all.

Please audit the analogy. First, set out explicitly what maps onto what: the required questions, the extra credit, the passing grade, the grader, the total number of points available. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and — this is the part I care about most — separate the disanalogies that matter for my friend's conclusion from the ones that don't. I can already see the analogy isn't perfect: there is no literal grader, and moral life isn't divided into discrete questions with fixed point values. What I want to know is whether those imperfections are load-bearing. Does "optional" in the exam sense really mean the same thing as "permissible but not required" in the moral sense? Does the analogy quietly import something like a fixed ceiling on what morality can demand, or the idea that moral worth is scored by an external examiner rather than owed to particular people who are standing in front of you? And does my friend's conclusion — that declining costs nothing, morally speaking — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole way?
