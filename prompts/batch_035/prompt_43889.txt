At a reading-group session on formal learning theory, two of my doctoral students fell out over whether Solomonoff induction vindicates Occam's razor, and I should like an external adjudication of the exchange.

Marcus maintained that the vindication is essentially complete: the invariance theorem shows that Kolmogorov complexity is independent of one's choice of universal machine up to an additive constant, so simplicity is not a artefact of description language, and the razor rests on firm mathematical ground.

Devi replied that the constant can be arbitrarily large, so for any two rival hypotheses of comparable length one can choose a universal machine that reverses their ordering; the invariance is thus a merely asymptotic nicety which tells us nothing about the finite comparisons the razor is actually invoked to make.

Marcus answered that this misconceives the result's role: Solomonoff induction does not rank hypotheses once and for all but forms a mixture over all computable hypotheses with weights decaying exponentially in complexity, and the resulting predictor can be proved to incur only bounded expected error against any computable environment; the constant affects how long the bound takes to bite, not whether it does.

Devi granted the convergence theorem but objected that an agent who is right only eventually is right only in the limit, which is no epistemic improvement on ordinary induction; and she added that since Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable, the razor as Solomonoff defines it can never in fact be applied.

Marcus countered that uncomputability does not render a notion useless, since theory choice may be guided by approximations to it, and that Devi's complaint about the limit, if sound, would sink induction generally, so it can hardly count against this one specific proposal.

Neither would yield. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, saying who is right about which specific point and what, if anything, both parties are missing, and close with a final line of the form

Verdict: <one sentence naming who is more right and why>.
