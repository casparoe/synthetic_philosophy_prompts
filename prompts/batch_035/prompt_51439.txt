The topic was whether AI safety via debate can supervise models on tasks beyond the judge's own competence. Ana's opening position: debate can never give you oversight above the judge's ceiling. If the judge can't already tell a true answer from a persuasive false one, then on the questions where the judge is lost, the better rhetorician wins — so all we get is oversight laundering, not extra oversight, and a debate-trained system is just a persuasion-optimized system.

Ben replied that she's conflating generating with verifying. A judge who can't solve a hard math problem can still check a single cited step once a debater points to it. So debate converts one global verification task the judge can't do into a bunch of local ones the judge can, and that's the whole trick — it's why debate is supposed to beat naive weaker-model supervision on exactly these tasks.

Ana came back at him: fine for transparent failures, but the failures that worry her aren't local. Think of omission — if the strongest strategy for one debater is to leave out a consideration, the judge can't check a step that doesn't exist, and noticing the omission requires knowing what questions to ask, which is precisely the competence the judge lacks. In her view this is where debate quietly collapses back into the judge supervising only what the judge allready understands.

Ben's last word was that this is what training the debaters is for. A debater that wins by omission gets beaten the moment its opponent raises the omitted point, so debate-RL penalizes leaving things out, and meanwhile the judge only needs the weaker skill of checking whether a rebuttal is consistent with what was already claimed. He compared it to grading a proof you couldn't have come up with yourself: you can still tell when the later steps contradict the earlier ones.
