My book club spent last month reading a philosopher named Bernard Williams, and while most of it went over my head, one small example has refused to leave me alone. He imagines a man whose wife and a stranger are both drowning: the man can save only one, and he saves his wife. Williams doesn't dispute the choice — he thinks it's obviously the right one — but he's bothered by the demand that the man be able to justify it in the right vocabulary, as though the rescue only becomes properly moral once it's been brought under some impartial rule or calculation, and as though the husband should have had that justification available as the thing that actually moved him. His famous remark is that if the man needed to run the extra thought — the counting of outcomes, the principle that covers the case — to make his action right, that would be one thought too many. What hooked me even more was the same author's related complaint elsewhere, that a moral outlook which makes you weigh every commitment, including the people and projects that make your life your own, on a single impartial ledger ends up treating your own life as something that just passes through you, a stranger's schedule you happen to be carrying out.

Here is what I would like. Write a poem, somewhere between twelve and sixteen lines, no fixed rhyme scheme or meter, that lives entirely inside the moment of the rescue. It should be concrete and physical — the water, the cold, the arm, the loved face — and it should give no reasons at all, because the man isn't reasoning; the poem's whole body should show someone acting out of what he is, not out of what he has concluded. Then the final line should be the one thought too many: a correct, even true, justification of what the poem has just shown, phrased the way a theory or a tribunal would accept it — the impartial bookkeeping, the principle that makes the choice defensible to anyone — arriving only after the rescue is already over. The last line should feel bolted onto a poem that had already ended: a reader should be able to delete it and feel the poem improve, and yet the line must not be false, because that is the point — the trouble isn't that the justification is wrong but that it is one thought too many, a receipt handed over for a gift. Someone who knows this corner of Williams should see exactly what the last line is doing; someone who has never heard of him should simply feel the deflation, the way a gesture of love goes dead the moment it starts explaining itself. If you can, let the register shift at the end too — the body of the poem in the language of hands and water, the final line suddenly abstract, as though it had wandered in from a different, cleaner room.
