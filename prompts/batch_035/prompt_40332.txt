I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar (mostly juniors and seniors, a couple of grad students auditing) and I'm writing the take-home final. The unit they struggle with every single year is epiphenomenalism — specifically the way it seems to saw off the branch it's sitting on: if qualia are causally idle, then whatever caused me to say "I'm having a vivid red experience" was a wholly physical chain, and the experience itself did nothing to bring that utterance about. So what makes my utterance a report of the experience rather than a lucky guess? One of my TAs calls this the self-undermining problem, and the other insists it's just the mind-body problem wearing a costume, and honestly I want a question that forces students to adjudicate between those two takes rather than just paraphase the textbook.

Here's the question I want you to write: it should turn on whether the epiphenomenalist can say anything non-ad-hoc about the reliability of phenomenal judgment — not "how do the judgments arise," since students can hand-wave that with causal closure, but "why are they so often right." A good version might have students consider a twin scenario in which the psychophysical laws are scrambled, so that the physical state that here accompanies an experience of red accompanies an experience of green there, and then ask whether our epiphenalist is entitled to claim our judgments track anything at all — and if they appeal to some harmony or correspondence built into the laws, whether thats a principled commitment or a patched-up rescue. It has to be unanswerable by reciting labels ("the paradox of phenomenal judgment," "zombie duplicates"): I want the real crux to be whether the appeal to lawlike harmony does genuine explanatory work or just restates the problem in its Sunday best.
