(1) indicative conditionals — "if the match gets moved to Tuesday, I'll come" — express genuine propositions: they're true or false, you can believe or disbelieve them, and crucially they can sit inside other sentences, so nested stuff like "if (if the match is moved I'll come), then book two tickets" is perfectly meaningful, and you can conditionalise on a conditional or take its probability like anything else in the algebra. (2) Adams basically had the right story about probabilities: the probability of "if A then B" just is P(B|A) — I take this to be the single most data-driven insight in the whole literature, since people's bets on conditionals track conditional probability, not the probability of ¬A∨B.
