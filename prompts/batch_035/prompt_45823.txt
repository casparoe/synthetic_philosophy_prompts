As one of the two student representatives on my school district's curriculum and technology committee, I have to vote next month on a pilot proposed by our computer science department, and I would like your help drafting the written opinion I will circulate beforehand. The pilot would replace six weeks of the usual CS elective — lectures on search procedures, writing rules, and debugging — with a different exercise: each student team receives the same small learning program, a blank child machine with no rules installed in it, and is graded on how it educates that program: which behaviors they reward, when they correct and when they let a mistake stand, and what they do when the machine hits on something clever they never intended. The teacher's rationale is an old one from the founding literature of the field: if machines can be brought to competence the way children supposedly are — by correction and encouragement rather than by having explicit rules loaded in advance — then the best way to show students how intelligence works is to put them on the teacher's side of the desk, and the best way to show them how they themselves were taught is to have them teach. Some committee members are enthusiastic; others object that six weeks spent raising a toy program teaches programming culture but not computer science, that grading students on their training regime rewards intuition for the machine rather than mastery of content, and that the analogy between bringing up a child and training a program is inspirational rhetoric rather than a reason. Please draft the opinion I will circulate: it should recommend one concrete course — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and defend it in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, written the way one would write a long and careful letter. In the reasoning I want an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the side that loses, not a token acknowledgment of it. I also want the memo to engage the philosophical question the pilot actually turns on: whether teaching is the transfer of explicit rules or the shaping of dispositions through correction, and whether the difference between educating a child and training a machine is a difference of degree, of kind, or merely of sentiment. I am honestly undecided, so I would rather the memo took a position I could defend under questioning from either side of the committee than one written to make everyone comfortable before the vote.
