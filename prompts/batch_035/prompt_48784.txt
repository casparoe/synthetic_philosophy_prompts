I'm taking an advanced game theory course, and next week I'm presenting on axiomatic bargaining — specifically the contrast between the Nash solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution. The textbook treatment of the monotonicity versus independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives tradeoff is abstract, so I want to build a concrete centerpiece case for my presentation, and I'd like your help constructing it to specifications. The case should demonstrate that the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution can reward a bargainer for destroying possibilities *before* negotiations even begin, while the Nash solution leaves that bargainer no better off for having done so — ideally worse off.

Specifications for the scenario:

1. Realistic setting, one paragraph. Two bargainers — a labor negotiation, a partnership dissolution, a salary negotiation, something like that. The "destruction" must be an ordinary, credible act (a signed contract, an irreversible sale, debt taken on in advance), not a perfect commitment device or a paradoxical act of self-binding.
2. The destruction must cut both ways. It has to strictly lower the *opponent's* utopian payoff, but it must also sacrifice some of the destroyer's own best outcomes — I don't want the manipulation to be free self-improvement. The bargainer pays a real price and should still come out ahead under Kalai–Smorodinsky, which is what makes the case unsettling rather than obvious.
3. Hand computation. Please specify payoffs (a piecewise-linear Pareto frontier with two segments is fine, or anything else I can compute by hand) and exhibit the disagreement point and each player's utopian point, then compute both solutions before and after the destruction. The manipulator's Kalai–Smorodinsky payoff must strictly rise even though the feasible set has shrunk; the Nash payoff must not rise. I want the numbers on a slide, so keep the arithmetic clean.
4. After the scenario and computation, tell me which axiom is doing the work, and be explicit that this is the price Kalai–Smorodinsky pays for individual monotonicity. Then sketch the mirror case in which Nash fails monotonicity — a bargainer whose opportunities expand but whose Nash payoff fails to improve — so my presentation doesn't read as a one-sided hit job on the equal-concessions view.
5. Stress tests. First, what happens to the Nash verdict if the destroyed agreements had included the original Nash point — does the manipulation start to pay under Nash too, and does that weaken or strengthen my case? Second, what if the destroyed set lies off the Pareto frontier, so that no one's utopian payoff changes — confirm both solutions are unmoved, since that shows it is the utopian point, not destruction as such, that drives the result. Third, give me the strongest reply a defender of Kalai–Smorodinsky could make — for instance, that utopian points encode the parties' reasonable aspirations and that bargaining power legitimately includes pre-commitments, or that the destruction should properly count as changing the disagreement point as well — and tell me which of these replies actually has teeth.

One last thing: I'm not asking you to stipulate the verdicts and call it a thought experiment. The numbers have to generate the conclusions from the two solutions' own definitions, so that a skeptical classmate who recomputes everything from scratch gets the same answer. That's the difference between a case that works and a case that just asserts what I wanted to show.
