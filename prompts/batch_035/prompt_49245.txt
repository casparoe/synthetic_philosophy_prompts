I'm a staff attorney for the sentencing commission of a mid-sized state. Our legislature has just enacted a statute requiring judges to impose sentences that are "no more severe than the offender's desert" while also "taking account of public safety, deterrence, and rehabilitation" — that is, they have legislated a hybrid of retributivism and consequentialism without saying anything about how the two halves interact, and the commission is directed to issue practical guidance within six months. Our judges sentence roughly thirty felony cases a week, have no philosophical training, and have at most fifteen minutes of deliberation beyond the presentence report in the ordinary case. The commission has provisionally settled on limiting retributivism as the skeleton: desert sets a range rather than a point, and consequentialist considerations may move a sentence only within that range, never past its edges. My job is to turn that skeleton into something a judge can actually run.

Please draft the decision procedure. It should do the following:

1. Give the judge a small, ordered set of questions that converts the theory into steps, with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer at each step — including how a judge is supposed to reason about the width of the desert range, not just its existence, since a wide range is nearly no constraint at all and a narrow one is nearly a mandatory sentence.
2. Say explicitly what the judge should do when the answers are unavailable or fuzzy, since desert does not come with a number attached and the empirical evidence on deterrence at the level of the individual case is almost always worse than it looks.
3. Mark the points at which the procedure necessarily hands the decision over to judgment, and explain why those particular points cannot be proceduralized — as opposed to points that merely haven't been proceduralized yet.
4. Be honest about the failure modes we should expect: judges smuggling consequentialist aggravation in as "desert"; deterrence arguments doing work their evidence cannot support; and the risk that publishing a clean-looking flowchart creates the appearance that the hard value questions have been answered when they have merely been relocated.

Then run the procedure on three cases, showing its output at each step:

(a) A 22-year-old first offender with no prior record, convicted of armed robbery in which no one was physically hurt, with strong family and employment ties and an assessed low risk of reoffending — the case where leniency pressure comes from both halves of the statute and the procedure must still say something.

(b) A 61-year-old woman with no prior convictions, convicted of embezzling from her employer over a decade in order to cover a grandchild's medical debt, where the prosecution argues that general deterrence demands prison because embezzlement by trusted employees is chronically under-prosecuted and lenient sentences invite more of it — a case where the desert range and the deterrence argument pull in opposite directions.

(c) A twice-convicted burglar whose habitual-offender exposure puts him near the statutory ceiling at exactly the moment the legislature is under public pressure about a burglary wave — the case where the desert ceiling and the deterrence push are in direct conflict. This is the one where the procedure will likely give an answer the commissioners will not like, or expose that the statute's two commands cannot both be honored, and I need that surfaced rather than smoothed over.

Finally, include a short section addressed to the commission itself rather than to the judges: what the procedure cannot do, which kinds of cases it will systematically mishandle (offender ignorance of the law, strict liability offenses, desert for attempts versus completed harms), and whether there are circumstances under which a published procedure of this kind makes sentencing worse than honest, explicit disagreement about the values would be.
