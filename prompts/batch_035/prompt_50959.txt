I'm taking a moral philosophy course this semester and we just spent two weeks on Williams vs utilitarianism, including the integrity objection and the Jim and the Indians case. But the thing that keeps coming up and never gets unpacked is the "one thought too many" line. Our professor has basically adopted it as a general-purpose move at this point: last week someone asked whether a doctor who treats her own child first when triage says the numbers point elsewhere acts permissibly, and he just sort of smiled and said she'd be having one thought too many if she paused to check, and that was the end of it. It shut the discussion down rather than opening it, and I left not actually knowing what had been claimed.

So here's what I want: a menu of the distinct precise things this slogan could be asserting. As far as I can tell, at least these readings are live:

1. A psychological claim about ideal deliberation: the loving husband who thinks "and it's permissible that I save her" has a redundant or distorted item in his deliberation, like someone pausing mid-act to confirm the act is grammatically well-formed.

2. An evaluative claim about the man's character or the quality of his love: a person whose affection needs moral scaffolding to operate loves less well, so to speak, and the thought is evidence of this defect.

3. A claim specifically about the *order* of considerations: maybe the thought is fine but only in its place — as a later reflection on what happened, or as something the agent could produce if challenged, but not as a premise.

4. A purely adversarial claim about impartialist theories: that any theory on which the thought is required, rather than merely permitted, stands refuted.

And maybe there are readings I haven't seen. For each reading, tell me whether it's plausible, whether it's doing any real work or is just a restatement of something boring, and — this is the part I actually care about — whether it supports the way my professor uses it, i.e. as a verdict on what the agent ought to *do* or *avoid doing* in the moment. Because it seems to me like readings 1 and 2, even if true, don't obviously settle any normative question: the thought being ugly or surplus doesn't immediately mean the act performed *because* of it is wrong, or that the agent must not have it.

Two more wrinkles if you have thoughts on them. First, is the slogan parasitic on the fact that his partial concern tracks something already permissible? Like, if the man's deep attachment were to saving his own skin, or to members of his political tribe only, the "one thought too many" line seems to lose all its force — in fact you'd want him to have that extra thought. So maybe what looks like a point about thought is really a smuggled-in claim about when partiality is legitimate, and the slogan is hiding the interesting part. Second, our TA claimed the line is connected to the integrity objection, because both say morality alienates you from your projects/attachments, but I can't tell if it's the same argument in miniature or a genuinely separate point about deliberative structure.

Also: does the slogan apply to the wife? If she later learns he saved her because he calculated that the numbers made it permissible, does she have a complaint, and is it the same one Williams is gesturing at? That might be a decent test case for sorting the readings — if the readings disagree about what her complaint would be (or whether she has one at all), that helps me see which one people are actually using. Lay it all out as a list I can bring to office hours.
