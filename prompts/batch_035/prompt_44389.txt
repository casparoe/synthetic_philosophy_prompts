I'm a PhD student in machine learning, and for the past two years our safety reading group has been stuck in the same argument about prosaic alignment — whether systems built from current techniques, large models trained by imitation and then refined with human feedback, can be made genuinely safe without waiting for some new conceptual breakthrough. One side holds that aligning today's models is the same problem at a smaller scale: the feedback methods, red-teaming practices, and interpretability tools we build now are load-bearing and will carry over, with refinements, to whatever systems matter later. The other side holds that current work is at best rehearsal: that it mostly teaches us to shape visible behavior rather than the underlying dispositions of the system, and that what will eventually matter — when models are capable enough that we cannot check their work — will require either new theory or techniques that look nothing like present ones. Everyone in the group knows both positions, and no one has moved an inch. My own working hypothesis is that the two sides aren't answering the same question: one is answering what we should do now, under uncertainty, given that current techniques are the only game in town, while the other is answering what would suffice, in principle, for the systems we will eventually face. But I suspect that isn't the whole story, since there also seems to be a buried empirical wager about whether the systems that matter will be continuous extrapolations of today's or qualitatively different, which nobody can settle at present. Diagnose the disagreement for me: is it verbal, is it two questions under one heading, is it a hidden bet about the technology's trajectory, or is it a genuine substantive dispute about the transferability of current methods? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually claims, not in a survey of the field. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a conceptual distinction, a demonstration that some current technique does or does not transfer to a harder setting, or an argument that no progress should be expected until the systems themselves change.
