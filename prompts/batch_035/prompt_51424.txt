I teach an upper-level seminar on social epistemology — "Who Gets Believed," roughly — and I'm putting together the take-home final. The students have all read the Fricker material and can recite testimonial vs. hermeneutical injustice in their sleep, and honestly past versions of my exam have rewarded exactly that recitation, so this year I want a question they can't sleepwalk through. I want one essay question built around a specific case, and I have a sketch of it, though feel free to polish the details if something's off.

Here's the case I want to use. Dr. Reyes works nights in an understaffed ER in a region hit hard by the opioid epidemic. Over years she's noticed (correctly, as it happens — the base rates are real and well-documented) that patients from a particular demographic present with dental pain at a rate far above what would be predicted by actual pathology, and opioid-seeking is common. She doesn't hate this demographic, isn't conscious of any animus, and her discounting policy genuinely raises her expected accuracy — if you forced her to use the policy on every future patient, she'd be right more often than a colleague who didn't. Tonight a patient from that demographic comes in with genuine, testable, severe dental pain, and Reyes discounts his report, he gets delayed treatment, and a molar abscess turns into something much worse. My question for the students has to get at whether this is testimonial injustice on Fricker's account — which seems to require a prejudice-driven credibility deficit, and it's not clear a merely statistically accurate generalization counts as prejudice — and, if it isn't injustice but only "error," whether that classification does any real work or is just bookkeeping. I'd also like the question to push on whether it matters that Reyes is a lone doctor versus the discount being baked into the triage software her hospital uses (she just clicks "apply guidelines"), since some of my sharper students will want to say the algorithmic version feels worse even though it seems *less* prejudiced, and I want to see if they can say why that intuition tracks anything real or is just technophobia.

Please write me the essay question itself (worded so the crux is explicit), a model answer of about 600-700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 built around five must-hit points — with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how students typically fumble each one. The marking scheme should make clear what separates an A answer from a B answer: the B answer, say, correctly applies the labels and notices the tension, but the A answer has to take an actual position on whether accuracy-tracking discounting can be unjust *and* defend it against the obvious reply (that calling accurate inferences unjust stretches the concept past usefulness), not just report that "some say X, others say Y." One more thing: don't let the model answer lean on any reading beyond the standard Fricker stuff — the students only read the core text and a couple of overview pieces, so if the answer gestures at outside literature it should be by describing the argument, not name-dropping citations I didn't assign.
