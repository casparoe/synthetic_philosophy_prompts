I'm trying to pin down where my credence on the common prior assumption actually lives, and I'd like yours with the reasoning laid bare. State the proposition this way: whenever two ideal agents disagree about the probability of some event while their rationality and their common knowledge of each other's opinions is itself common knowledge between them, at least one of them is failing to respond correctly to evidence — that is, there is no disagreement that can be legitimately traced to priors alone. My credence in this proposition is roughly 0.35, and I honestly can't tell whether the number is doing real work or is just a mood.

Give me your number, but I care far more about the decomposition. Consider, at minimum, terms along these lines:

1. The probability that Harsanyi's modelling argument — that heterogeneous priors can always be reinterpreted as private information about a state of the world with a single underlying prior — gives the assumption normative and not merely formal force. It is one thing to show the two formalisms are equivalent; it is another to show that an agent who declines the reinterpretation is irrational.
2. The probability that Aumann-style agreement results carry the load: whether they show that rational agents cannot agree to disagree, or only that they cannot both know they disagree — and whether that gap matters for the proposition as stated.
3. The probability that priors are even well-defined for agents facing logical or self-locating uncertainty, where it is arguably impossible for agents in different epistemic positions to share anything deserving the name of a prior.
4. The probability that the assumption survives transplanting to artificial agents. Consider a language-model-based agent whose prior is whatever its training distribution has baked in — it neither chose it, can introspect it, nor separate it from its posteriors. If the common prior assumption is supposed to be a constraint on rational agents as such, does it apply to an agent like that, or does this regime reveal that the assumption was always a modelling convention about how we write down games, rather than a truth about rationality?
