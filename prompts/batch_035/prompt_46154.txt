I am a novelist by trade — the long, close-third-person sort of novelist, where a narrator hovers just behind one character's shoulder for three hundred pages — and I have arrived, by way of a craft problem, at what I gather philosophers have been arguing about since Russell published "On Denoting" in 1905. I would like you to meet me on my ground rather than yours: restate the theory of descriptions in the working vocabulary of fiction-writing, and then tell me honestly what does not survive the translation.

The craft problem is this. In a novel, nearly every sentence of the form "the X did Y" is chosen by a narrator, and it carries information about two things at once: the world of the story and the state of mind of the character through whom we are seeing it. When I write, in a passage focalized through my protagonist, "the woman by the window was watching him," I am not asserting that exactly one woman by a window exists and is watching him — I may intend that the protagonist wrongly believes it, that the narration is infected by his paranoia, or that the reader is meant to spot what he misses. My editors and I talk about this constantly, in terms like the following: which facts of the story-world the narration has "established" for the reader; whether a description belongs to the narrator's voice or has slipped into free indirect discourse, so that it reads as the character's description, not the book's; whether a character has been "introduced" (and so can be referred to by name) or merely "glimpsed" (and so must be reached through a description anchored in what the focalizing character knows). We also live comfortably with names that have no bearers at all: Anna Karenina does not exist, has never existed, and yet the name does everything a name does — track a character across eight hundred pages, support anaphora, carry a title. Nobody in my profession finds this alarming, which makes me suspect we are either missing something or have already absorbed the answer without noticing.

So here is the assignment. Take Russell's analysis — a sentence like "the present king of France is bald" comes out as a conjunction of claims: that at least one thing is the present king of France, that at most one thing is, and that whatever is, is bald — and restate it, piece by piece, in my vocabulary. What, in narrative terms, is the existence claim doing? Is the uniqueness clause anything more than what we would call narrative economy — the reader can identify exactly one candidate from among what the text has established? Is Strawson's rejoinder, that such a sentence presupposes rather than asserts existence, simply a description of what every competent novelist does with a definite description on page one of a chapter: treat the antecedent setup as given, and exploit its failure deliberately (the reveal that no one was by the window at all) as one of the oldest tricks in the repertoire? And what of Russell's deeper motivation, that a genuine name, unlike a description, must be backed by something — acquaintance, a bearer, a direct line to the world? Fiction appears to flatly refute this, since its names work with no bearers whatsoever; I would like to know whether that is a real counterexample, an edge case the theory was never meant to cover, or evidence that the theory was covertly about a different question, one about how a sentence latches onto the world, which fiction simply declines to pose.

Then give me the residue report I gather your discipline reserves for occasions like this. Which parts of the theory of descriptions translate cleanly into craft talk, which parts survive only by distortion — smuggled in under a narrative-sounding name that quietly keeps the logical structure intact — and which parts have no expression at all in my vocabulary? And in your judgment, does the residue indict my vocabulary, which after all evolved to move readers rather than to carve truths, or does it indict the theory, which announced itself as a general account of denoting phrases and then, on this evidence, turns out to be a general account of denoting phrases in books that report on a world? I should say that while I have read a fair amount of criticism and narrative theory, I have no training in logic, so any formalism you need should be translated along with everything else — I am asking for one translation, not two.

Write it as you would write to a serious colleague from another field: I will follow a difficult argument, but I will notice if you quietly replace Russell's claims with claims my vocabulary finds comfortable, and I will notice if the residue report turns into a lecture on why my trade is unserious. I am interested in where the two vocabularies genuinely meet and where they only appear to.
