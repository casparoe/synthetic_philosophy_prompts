I have just started a job in the compliance unit of a central government benefits agency, and my team leader invokes the proverb 'trust, but verify' constantly — most recently to defend a new scheme of spot checks and data-matching that treats every claimant as presumptively suspect. The slogan clearly sounded reasonable when it governed nuclear arms treaties, so I cannot dismiss it outright, but I want to know exactly what it commits me to before I help administer it. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses those three words could be expressing — e.g. trust as a defeasible default rather than a conclusion, trust in persons versus trust in institutions or records, verification as a supplement to trust versus a precondition for it, and the reading on which verification is itself only possible against a background of trust (in auditors, data custodians, and procedures)? For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or actually false, and, crucially, which if any of the readings would justify casting a dragnet over claimants who have done nothing to lose our trust, as opposed to one-off inspections of a hostile foreign power. Please answer in British English, and give me the readings as a numbered list I can bring to a team meeting.
