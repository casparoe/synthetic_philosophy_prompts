Something I can't stop chewing on: central banks publish probabilities about the future and then talk about the future as if they can't attach numbers to it. So the Fed puts out the dot plot every quarter, the Bank of England wraps its inflation forecast in a fan chart where the darker bands are supposed to be more likely than the lighter ones, and then you watch the press conference and the chair refuses to put a probability on anything — the most you get is that the risks are skewed to the upside, or that the committee stands ready to act if needed. Meanwhile I've been reading about Keynes, and it seems like his whole thing, from the Treatise on Probability right through the famous chapter about the state of long-term expectation, was that this is exactly backwards: that the stuff that actually matters — the price of copper twenty years out, whether there's a war, the rate of interest a couple decades hence — is precisely the stuff you can't treat as a draw from a distribution, and that a probability fan gives you a feeling of precision while ignoring that the future is being created by other people's animal spirits, not pulled from an urn.

So heres my question. What view of probability does the fan-chart-and-dot-plot apparatus actually presuppose? Is it treating next year's GDP as if it were a roulette wheel — measurable risk rather than radical uncertainty, in the Knight-ish sense Keynes would have insisted on? And then the part I really want your read on: the verbal hedging. Is saying the risks are skewed more Keynesian than the fan chart itself — an ordinal, non-numerical comparison of the sort he went out of his way to defend even in the Treatise, where he argued probabilities might not be comparable by numbers at all — or is it just mush that gets read as numbers anyway? Basically I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory behind this whole practice: which parts of it are genuinely committed to probability being numerical, which parts could survive on a more Keynesian view of uncertainty, and where the practice is underdetermined — that is, consistent with several different theories of probability such that it doesn't strictly need any one of them. One more wrinkle: I've read that the Bank calibrates the width of its fans partly off its own past forecast errors, which seems like it could be doing a lot of work here. What does calibrating to your own past misses presuppose about the future resembling the past, and would Keynes have accepted that as a response to his objection or waved it off?
