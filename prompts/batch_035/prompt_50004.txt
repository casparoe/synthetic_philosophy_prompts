For about a year now I have been turning over, entirely on my own and without any training in philosophy, a puzzle about what makes a theory scientific, and last night a television documentary crystallised it in a way I cannot evaluate myself. I would be grateful for a careful audit, written as continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, just flowing paragraphs in the manner of a letter — and in British English.

Here is what the documentary said, as faithfully as I can reproduce it. Karl Popper, the philosopher who proposed that a theory counts as science only if some possible observation could refute it, turned his own criterion on Darwin's theory of natural selection and found that it failed. Since the fittest are by definition the ones that survive, the theory says only that whatever survives, survives — a tautology with no empirical content, and therefore, on Popper's criterion, not science at all but a kind of faith. The programme placed Darwinism in the same bucket as astrology and Freudian psychoanalysis, noted that this verdict became a gift to creationists, who quoted it for decades, and reported that late in life Popper changed his mind and conceded that natural selection is testable after all — so that the whole affair is now remembered as a great philosopher's single embarrassing blunder, and the tautology objection died with his retraction.

Three things about this summary nag at me, though I cannot pin down why. First, even in the documentary's own telling, Popper seemed to be making a subtler point than Darwinism is nonsense: it mentioned that he called the theory a metaphysical research programme, and I have no idea whether, in his vocabulary, that is a slur or a compliment. He may have thought metaphysical ideas could be fertile rather than empty, in which case putting Darwinism next to astrology would misdescribe his view twice over. Second, the circularity seems to me to depend on how one reads the word fittest. If fitness simply means having left the most offspring, then the survival of the fittest slogan is empty; but if it means having the traits suited to that environment, it looks like a prediction that could come out wrong, and I cannot tell whether Popper was targeting the first reading only, or believed the second quietly collapses into the first. Third, and this is my own thought, which may be naive: it seems obvious that a principle can be nearly empty on its own yet generate testable claims once you add specifics. Animals inherit traits from their parents, combined with this environment punishes slow seed-eaters, yields something checkable, in the way that nature obeys laws says nothing until you name a law. Does that rescue the theory by Popper's own lights, or would he reply that I have merely moved the emptiness somewhere else?

Please go through the documentary's claims one by one, telling me which parts a philosopher of science would accept, which are simplifications a professional would wince at, and which are outright wrong, and then say whether its closing verdict — retraction, closure, one-off blunder — follows from the accurate remainder. I am inclined to think Popper was onto something real about the familiar slogan, but I do not want to hold that view on the strength of a programme that may have flattened him.
