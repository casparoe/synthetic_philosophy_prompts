I teach a intro critical-thinking course, about 25 second-years, one 75-minute session, and the department wont let us use real money, so it's points only. I want to run a game that makes the Dutch book argument feel like something you discover rather than a theorem you get told.

Phase 1 I can mostly design myself: students privately post buying and selling prices for a handful of claims about a shuffled deck — next card is a spade, next card is red given I've already burned a black one, both of the next two are spades, and so on. Then a few volunteer bookies each pick a victim, get shown that victim's full price list, and try to construct a package of bets the victim would accept at their own posted prices that guarantees the bookie a profit however the cards come out. One rule I want enforced: the exploitation has to be built out of the victim's actual numbers. Nobody gets to just announce "you were inconsistent" and dock points — the loss has to come out of the student's own posted prices, otherwise the game proves nothing. Debrief maps each accepted bet in the book onto a specific violation (betting on and against the same claim, prices that don't sum to one, whatever turns up in the room).

The part I actually need help with is phase 2, and it's the point of the whole session. I want them to run into a diachronic Dutch book: a student whose prices are internally coherent at every single moment — no bookie can touch them at any one time-slice — but who updates on evidence in a non-Bayesian way (I was thinking base-rate neglect, maybe a medical-test-style scenario or more cards), and gets pumped across time: bookie buys cheap before the evidence, sells dear after, or trades against the pair of old and new prices. My problem is making this happen live in the room instead of reconstructed on the whiteboard afterwards. The bookie needs to see both price lists and feel out the trade within the session, and the victim needs to actually feel the hit in their point total. If that genuinely can't be done in 75 minutes, say so and design the honest fallback version — but dont pretend the fallback is the same thing as the live version.

Then the debrief has to do something I find hard, and I want you to take it seriously: distinguish what the two phases prove. Phase 1, fine — if you'll trade at your posted prices, incoherent prices are just irrational. Phase 2 is supposed to show you must update by conditionalization, but as I understand it this is exactly where the Dutch book argument is weakest. There's an old line of objection that diachronic books show nothing at all, roughly: your future prices are a different agent's problem, or the bookie's ability to pump you presupposes you'll be incoherent rather than revealing it the way the static case does. Present that side fairly in the debrief. Say what phase 2 legitimately shows about the students' updating, then say exactly where the step from "exploitable across time" to "irrational" is contestable, and why the same doubt can't be turned against phase 1.

Constraints: one instructor, no TA, materials from a stationery shop, printable score sheets are fine. And give me the honest failure report — students posting huge gaps so nothing ever trades, bookies just accepting every bet instead of constructing a book, phase 2 eating the last 20 minutes. Predict what will go wrong the first time I run it and how to fix or absorb each one.
