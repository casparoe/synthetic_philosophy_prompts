I coordinate release review at a mid-sized AI research organization. Roughly forty times a quarter, a team asks for approval to publish something — a paper, an open-source model checkpoint, a dataset, a demonstration video, a technical report — and the question of who gets to say yes has become acute. We have repeatedly had the following experience: an individual team is enthusiastic about a release, several colleagues in other groups privately have reservations, and the release goes ahead because no single person besides the releasing team had either the standing or the obligation to block it. A colleague pointed me to the literature on the unilateralist's curse, particularly Bostrom, Douglas, and Sandberg's 2016 paper in Social Epistemology and their proposed principle of conformity, and I think the diagnosis fits us. But I've also noticed that in some of our incidents the opposite failure occurred: something that should have been released — an innocuous dataset, a paper whose value was time-sensitive — sat unreviewed for months because responsibility was so diffuse that nobody felt authorized to be the one who pushed it through. So the procedure I need has to guard against two opposite tendencies, not just one.

Please write a decision procedure my review board can actually run on each request. The constraints are these: the board has five members plus me, none of whom is a domain expert in more than one area, so most reviews are done by intelligent non-specialists reading a two-page summary; the default clock is five business days, after which some current releases proceed by default and others stall, depending on which inbox they land in — the procedure should make that default uniform and explicit; and every final decision must have a named human signatory, because we cannot have a policy that releases or blocks things anonymously.

The procedure should be grounded in what the unilateralist's curse literature actually establishes and where its remedies break down, and I want the translation to be honest about that. In particular, I want you to address, in whatever way the procedure handles them, the following points, which are the places I foresee the theory failing us in practice:

1. The curse assumes dispersal of opinion around some true or best view of whether an action is beneficial or harmful, with each actor's estimate being noisy around it. Our reviewers are not independent draws: they attend the same meetings, share the same safety training, and read the same preprint each morning. What does the recommended remedy — deference to the group's median or majority view — actually buy when the errors are correlated?
2. The principle of conformity tells an agent to defer to the group rather than act alone. But for us the inaction side has its own costs: a delay of three months on a paper can mean being scooped, and sometimes a delay is itself the harm (a report that would have helped responders during an ongoing incident is useless six weeks later). The procedure needs a principled way to price delay, not a vague balancing test.
3. Most of the literature imagines a bounded set of actors who can each unilaterally trigger the action. In our situation the set is often open: if we sit on a result, a rival lab may publish the same technique next month anyway, which weakens the case for blocking but does not obviously reduce to "so never block." Tell me whether and how the procedure can use that fact without it becoming an all-purpose excuse.

For each step of the procedure, state: the question to be answered, what counts as a sufficient answer, what facts versus value judgments it turns on, where it terminates in a clear yes or no, and where it must hand the decision to a named person because the residual question is one of values. Then run it on three cases in full:

Case A. A team wants to release a fine-tuned version of one of our checkpoints that substantially improves its ability to write persuasive political messaging. Internal opinions are genuinely dispersed: two reviewers rate the dual-use risk as negligible on the theory that the capability already exists elsewhere, two rate it as serious, and one is unsure. The team argues that our releasing it changes nothing about the world because a replica will exist within months.

Case B. A paper describing a new inference-efficiency method that would cut the cost of running large models by roughly half. One security reviewer notes that the same method also defeats a watermarking scheme that our deployment partner currently relies on, though the partner could migrate given a ninety-day notice. I will be candid: this paper matters to me personally, I am an author on it, and I want it released. If the procedure blocks me or forces a delay that costs me the priority claim, I need it to say so anyway — this is the case where I most need to know whether the procedure resists the person who wrote it.

Case C. A public-interest organization that is not answerable to us has obtained a dataset we declined to release last year, on the grounds that it contained re-identifiable records, and they intend to publish it in two weeks. We are being asked to comment, and some board members want to publicly endorse the release, others to publicly oppose it. Here we cannot control the action at all; show me what, if anything, the procedure says when the unilateralist is someone else and our only lever is speech.

Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do: the cases it will misfire on, the inputs it silently guesses at, and the ways it can be gamed by a team that learns how it works. I would rather know its failure modes now than discover them in a post-mortem.
