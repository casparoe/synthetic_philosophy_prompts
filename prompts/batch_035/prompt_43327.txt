My brother has basiaclly been living inside a VR game for the last two years and it finally blew up into a real argument at Thanksgiving, and I need someone to sort out who was right. Quick background: he hurt his back, went remote for work, got a headset to do rehab exercises, and now he works his job in the morning and spends every other waking hour in this MMO where he's a guild leader, runs an in-game shop, and is "married" to another player (a real person, lives in Ohio, they've never met). He's 31. He actually eats okay and showers now, but he told me flat out that the game is the best part of his life and if he could be in it 24/7 he would be.

The fight was between my cousin Maya (philosophy major) and my friend Dev, who stayed after dinner. I'll try and give the exchange fairly:

(1) Maya said he's basically volunteered for Nozick's experience machine -- everything good in his life now happens inside a machine, and Nozick's point was that people refuse to plug in because we want to actually do things and be a certain kind of person and have contact with reality, so a life of machine-given experiences, however pleasant, can't be good for you.

(2) Dev said that's not even the same thing. The experience machine is a lie machine -- it feeds you fake experiences of doing things, while my brother genuinely IS doing things: coordinating with real people, building real skill, his guildmates and wife are actual humans. So whatever intuition we have about the machine doesn't transfer. And anyway, he said, the "everyone would refuse" intuition is junk: if a scientist told you you've been in an experience machine this whole time, and that your "real" life is the one with the bad back and the loneliness, most people would say don't unplug me -- which suggests what we care about is keeping the life we're attached to, not contact with reality.

(3) Maya conceded the shared-game point but said the game is still engineered -- the whole thing is tuned by a company to maximize retention, the achievements are a calibrated drip-feed, and the "best part of my life" feeling is manufactured engagement the same way a casino manufactures excitement. If a casino made you feel heroic all day, you wouldn't say you were flourishing.

(4) Dev said by that logic nothing counts, since gyms and jobs and dating apps are all engineered too. And on the two big theories of wellbeing -- hedonism and desire satisfaction -- his life is going great: he reports being happy, and his desires (the guild, the shop, the marriage) are actually satisfied, not just simulated. Maya's the one who has to produce some secret third thing that's missing.

(5) Maya said his desires don't count because the game grew them. Two years ago he wanted a career and a partner in the actual world; now he wants sim-shaped things because the sim is the only thing around to want. That's adaptive preference -- sour grapes with a login screen. Dev answered that his "normal" desires were manufactured too, by our parents, by LinkedIn, by an ex who left, and Maya never treats THOSE as suspect, only the game-shaped ones -- which is just snobbery about where a desire comes from.

Neither of them would budge and I honestly couldn't referee it. Please go through the five moves and say who's right about what -- not just who wins overall. Then tell me if there's anything both of them are missing. And if you have a view: does any of this actually tell me whether to be worried about my brother or whether I should just leave him alone?
