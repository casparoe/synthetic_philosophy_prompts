I serve on the charter commission of a mid-sized city where mayoral elections routinely draw four or five serious candidates, and our last two winners took roughly 35 and 38 percent of the vote respectively. The commission must recommend one of three options to the council and, eventually, to the voters: retain our first-past-the-post rule, adopt instant-runoff (ranked-choice) voting, or adopt approval voting. The debate has come to be conducted largely through Arrow's impossibility theorem, in ways I suspect are muddled but that I am not confident I can correct on the fly. Draft the written opinion I will circulate before our final hearing.

Four lines of argument are in play, and the memo should engage each seriously rather than waving it away:

1. Our commissioner with a mathematics background argues that Arrow's theorem shows every ranked-ballot method is arbitrary: any social welfare function satisfying unrestricted domain, Pareto, and non-dictatorship must violate independence of irrelevant alternatives, so instant-runoff belongs to the doomed class from the start. He concludes there is no principled basis for preferring it to plurality, and that reform merely trades one arbitrary rule for another with added ballot complexity and voter confusion.

2. A second commissioner counters that approval voting escapes the theorem outright, because Arrow's framework assumes only ordinal information — voters can rank candidates but cannot express intensity — while approval ballots carry cardinal information. On her view, the theorem is simply inapplicable to approval voting, which is therefore the rational choice.

3. Our most stubborn commissioner raises what he calls the regress problem: the commission itself will choose among the three options by simple majority vote, and he claims Arrow's theorem applies to that meta-vote just as it applies to any other aggregation of ranked preferences — so our choice of voting system will be made by a voting system the theorem has already convicted. He concludes that the entire exercise of choosing a better rule is confused, and that plurality should be retained by default.

4. Finally, a commissioner who formerly lived in Maine points to the 2018 election in Maine's second congressional district: the incumbent led by about a point on first preferences but lost after the trailing candidates' ballots were redistributed, and a federal court upheld the result against a challenge to the method itself. She presents this both as evidence that ranked-choice voting works as advertised — it produces a majority winner — and, when pressed, as evidence that it discards or devalues first choices. The memo should be honest about which reading the episode actually supports, and about what it does not show.

The deliverable is a formal commission memo with a clear recommendation among the three options, but its central task is conceptual triage: what Arrow's theorem actually establishes about this decision, what it leaves open, and where each of the four arguments above goes right and wrong. In particular, the memo should address whether the ordinal-versus-cardinal escape claimed for approval voting is genuine or merely relocates the problem (I am aware the Gibbard-Satterthwaite result about strategic manipulation is often said to bite harder in practice than independence of irrelevant alternatives does, and I want the memo to take a position on that); whether the regress argument is a real difficulty or a category mistake; and whether known failures of instant-runoff, such as nonmonotonicity, should figure in the recommendation. Where the mathematics underdetermines the choice — as I suspect it does — the memo should say so plainly and rest the recommendation on grounds it names explicitly, rather than dressing up a value judgment as a theorem.
