I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been assigned a piece pegged to the claim, associated with Benedict Anderson and Eric Hobsbawm, that nations are much younger than their members believe: that most mass national identities are creatures of the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, assembled out of print capitalism, vernacular standardization, compulsory schooling, conscription, censuses, and outright invented traditions like clan tartans and imperial pageantry, rather than the continuation of immemorial ethnic communities. My editor is at roughly 0.7 on the strong version of this claim; I suspect I'm below that, but I don't trust my number. Before I write a word, give me yours. Specifically: your credence that more than half of today's European nation-states had no mass national identity before about 1750, where a mass national identity means, in Anderson's sense, ordinary people imagining horizontal kinship with millions of strangers they will never meet, and not merely elites or literate classes claiming one on their behalf. Then decompose the number into the sub-questions it actually rides on: how much depends on how we count mass participation and whether elite self-identification is evidence for it; how much on whether print and vernacular standardization were necessary conditions or only accelerants; how much on whether pre-modern protonational attachments, such as religious confession, dynastic loyalty, or regional dialect identity, should count as national in some weaker sense, as the ethnosymbolist critics of the modernist school insist; and how much on the counterfactual of whether industrialization and urbanization alone, absent deliberate state-building and myth-making, would eventually have produced nations anyway. Assign a rough probability to each term with a line of justification, show me how they combine into your final credence, and tell me which term the number is most sensitive to and what sort of evidence would move it most — by which I mean evidence a historian could actually turn up: parish records, language-shift data, conscription rolls, literacy rates, whatever bears on it. And please write it as continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; I want to read it the way I'd read a long, careful email from a source I trust.
