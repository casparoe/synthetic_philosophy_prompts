The exam question was: "The socialist calculation debate is often said to involve two distinct objections to replacing markets with planning — one about calculation and one about knowledge. Explain the difference between the objection raised by Mises and the objection raised by Hayek, and explain how Lange's proposal in On the Economic Theory of Socialism responds to each. Which objection does Lange's proposal handle better, and why?"

Here is the student's answer:

"Mises's 1920 essay argued that socialism fails because the knowledge needed to run an economy is dispersed among millions of people and much of it is tacit — knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place that no central authority could ever collect. Hayek developed the same argument in more detail in his 1945 article, using the example of tin: when tin becomes scarcer, its price rises, and users of tin can infer from the rise exactly why it became scarcer, which lets them adjust without a planner telling them to. The calculation objection is the older and simpler one: without private ownership of the means of production there are no markets for producer goods, hence no genuine prices for them, only arbitrary marks set by officials. Since rational choice requires comparing alternatives in a common unit, and money prices are that unit, socialism cannot allocate capital goods rationally.

Lange's proposal answers both objections at once. He keeps markets for consumer goods and labour, and the Central Planning Board sets prices for producer goods, instructing managers to produce where price equals marginal cost. If a price produces a shortage, the Board raises it; if a surplus, it lowers it; repeat until markets clear. Because the Board only needs to observe that shortages exist, not why, it never has to gather the dispersed tacit knowledge, so the knowledge problem is dissolved. And because the trial-and-error process converges on the same prices a competitive market would have found, the calculation problem is solved too: there are real prices for capital goods after all, just ones the Board announces rather than discovers. Mises had assumed the socialists would have to solve the whole system of simultaneous equations, which is impractical, so Lange showed that the market has no unique epistemic role here.

Which objection does Lange handle better? The calculation one. On the knowledge side, prices under his scheme are announced, so whatever information they carry has already passed through the Board, whereas the point of Hayek's tin example is that a market price aggregates knowledge that nobody individually possesses. So Lange answers Mises but not Hayek, and the debate reveals that the knowledge objection was the deeper one all along."
