You're a professional political philosopher with a serious interest in theories of justice and egalitarianism -- think hard, start by brainstorming before you write your answer. Here's my situation. A few years ago I read Sen's 1979 Tanner lecture (the one that launched the whole "equality of what?" framing) and I came away convinced that the capabilities approach is basically right: egalitarians should care about distributing people's real freedoms to do and be the things they have reason to value, not their subjective welfare (which is distorted by adaptive preferences -- the oppressed contentment problem) and not just their resource holdings (which ignore that people convert the same resources into very different lives depending on health, disability, social norms, etc.). I also find Nussbaum's version persuasive in outline: a list of central human capabilities that functions as a benchmark for what a minimally just society owes people, regardless of culture.

So this is a genuine question, not an invitation to cheerlead: what would actually count against the capabilities approach? I want a map of where it's exposed, ranked from fatal (if such a thing exists) down to merely awkward. To be clear about the kind of answer I want:

1. What would its own defenders concede counts against it, versus what only critics say? I'm thinking of things like: the notorious measurement/index problem (whose ranking of capabilities, and how do you aggregate a plural, incommensurable set into anything usable for policy?), the paternalism worry about Nussbaum's list, the objection from the other side that capabilities are too demanding an egalitarian metric (Dworkin-style luck egalitarians arguing that paying for someone's expensive tastes is different from paying for their disability -- where does a capability metric draw that line, and is its answer stable?).
2. Are there cases the view genuinely cannot describe, or collapses into a rival? E.g., if you handle the adaptive-preferences problem by asking what the person "would have reason to value" under some idealized conditions, at what point have you smuggled in a functioning-based or objective-list theory and given up on the "it's her freedom, not her achievement" selling point? Similarly, if the distinction between capability and functioning is doing anti-paternalist work, does it survive cases where the valuable thing seems to be the actual doing (nourishment, literacy) rather than the option?
3. What does it predict that its rivals don't -- what would have to be observed, in the world or in how institutions actually operate, for it to come out worse than resourcism or a suitably-idealized welfarism? And be honest: has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability over the decades (vague about the list, vague about weights, vague about the cutoff between enough and not enough), and if so, is that a fatal problem for a view of this kind, or is it the normal state of a mid-level framework that's meant to guide comparative judgments rather than generate a welfare function?
