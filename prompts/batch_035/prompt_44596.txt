I'm a software engineer with no background in philosophy, and I lead a platform team of about forty engineers spread across three time zones. A recurring source of pain for us is what I'd casually call "but I told everyone" failures: a deprecation gets announced in a Slack channel, the deadline passes, and it turns out that some team never saw it, or saw it but assumed it applied to someone else, or assumed that we knew they hadn't seen it and would therefore give them an extension. We keep arguing about whether "posting an announcement" is sufficient notice or whether we need mandatory acknowledgments, design review sign-offs, response-required emails, and so on, and the disagreement always feels vibes-based. Someone recently told me that epistemic game theory has an actual literature on this kind of thing — the difference between everyone knowing a fact and it being common knowledge, some parable about generals or an electronic mail game where communication can never quite establish certainty, models where everyone assumes everyone else reasons a bounded number of steps — and I'd like that body of theory turned into something my team can actually use.

Please act as a professional game theorist with a specialty in epistemic logic and common knowledge, who has spent time translating these ideas for engineering organizations. Before writing the procedure, do some structured brainstorming about which results and distinctions from the literature actually bear on coordinated action under imperfect communication, and which are mathematical curiosities that shouldn't survive contact with a chat app. Then write a decision procedure my team could run whenever we're about to rely on other teams having internalized some piece of information that affects a coordinated action — a migration deadline, a new on-call escalation path, a change to a shared interface, a freeze period. The procedure should consist of ordered questions to ask, criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, an explicit point at which it concludes "posting the announcement is enough," and an explicit point at which it concludes "you need forced acknowledgment, not just broadcast" — plus a marked zone where the question stops being about facts at all and is a judgment call about how much friction the org will tolerate, which a human has to own. Be honest about the ways the procedure will misfire.

Finally, run the procedure on three concrete cases from my world: (1) announcing in a widely-followed channel that a legacy internal endpoint will be removed in two weeks; (2) a shift handoff between two on-call engineers where the incoming engineer must not just receive the handoff notes but know that the outgoing engineer knows the notes were received; and (3) a quarter-long migration where the deadline only works if every downstream team knows that every other downstream team knows the deadline, since the whole plan collapses if even one team delays. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer my team will find annoyingly bureaucratic, and explain why the theory nonetheless supports it. Please think carefully and take your time — I care much more about getting the reasoning right than about the length of the answer.
