I'm a graduate student in a philosophy department where I work mainly on social epistemology but have been taking Sanskrit for three years, and I've been given a ninety-minute slot in our epistemology reading group to introduce the Nyāya treatment of śabda -- testimony or, more precisely, the knowledge arising from a trustworthy speaker's words -- as a distinct pramāṇa. The group is about a dozen analytic epistemologists. They know the contemporary testimony literature cold: they've followed the long-running fight over whether testimonial warrant reduces to perception, memory, and induction or is basic in its own right, and several of them have strong commitments on it. They know nothing about Indian philosophy except vague impressions that it is "wisdom literature." My own line, which I want the session to build toward without announcing it too early, is that the standard gloss -- Nyāya as the ancient Indian anti-reductionists, since both sides count testimony as a basic source -- is subtly wrong, and interestingly wrong: the pramāṇa framework isn't carving the pie anywhere near where the reductionism debate assumes, so the apparent convergence hides a real disagreement about what the question is.

I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself. Work out the order the pieces have to come in -- I suspect I can't even start with śabda, that I have to first get across what it means to enumerate pramāṇas at all, with each source having its own definition, conditions, and characteristic operation, and that this is a different intellectual project from listing "ways of knowing" in the casual Western sense -- and tell me whether that suspicion is right and what depends on what. Then help me pick the carrying example: I'm torn between the very mundane case of learning from a reliable passerby that the mangoes at a particular stall are ripe, and the classic one about someone in southern India who has never left the lowlands coming to know that the Himalayas are snow-capped; say which better does the work and what the example must have and must lack. I also want a map of the misconceptions this audience will predictably form and where in the sequence to head each off -- my guesses are that they will assimilate the pramāṇa list to a reliabilist taxonomy, that they will read the trustworthy-speaker condition as their own familiar "no defeaters plus reliability" story and miss that it includes the speaker's intention to convey truth rather than mislead, and that they will hear "scripture" when I say śabda and conclude the whole framework is epistemology in the service of theology, but correct me if you see worse ones. Tell me what to openly refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud -- I assume the Buddhist reductionist opponents who argued śabda collapses into inference deserve more than a sentence, but I can't do them justice in ninety minutes alongside everything else, so advise me on whether to cut them entirely or set them up as the cliffhanger. Finally, give me one question I can ask at the end that would reveal whether they've actually understood what a pramāṇa is, rather than just filed Nyāya under "testimony, basically anti-reductionist, very old."
