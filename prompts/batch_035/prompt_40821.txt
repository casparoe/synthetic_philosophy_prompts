Quick context for why I'm asking: I'm a grad student TA-ing a philosophy of physics course, and next week's section is on Newton's bucket and the whole substantivalism vs. relationism fight. The bucket is famously the relationist's nightmare — the water climbs the sides even when there's nothing left nearby for it to be rotating relative to, so Newton concludes the rotation must be with respect to absolute space itself. Fine. But every year some student asks "is there a case where it goes the other way, where the relationist wins?" and I just mumble something about Mach and move on. This year I want an actual answer prepared.

So here's what I want from you: construct a thought experiment where the relationist gets the better of the substantivalist — the mirror image of the bucket. My rough sketch, which you should improve or replace: imagine a world in which everything — every star, every planet, every speck of dust — rotates together, in lockstep, about some common axis. Relative to each other, nothing moves at all; the complete relational description (all the distances, all relative velocities, the whole history of them) is identical to that of the non-rotating world. Now put a Newton-style bucket in that world, at rest relative to everything else, and ask: does the water climb? The substantivalist, treating inertial frames as fixed by absolute space, seems committed to answering yes — the bucket rotates relative to space even if not relative to any body. The Machian relationist answers no — and I gather general relativity actually sides with them here, something about a rotating mass distribution dragging the inertial frames along with it (frame dragging, i think it's called). But I want this worked out properly, not hand-waved.

Constraints: the scenario itself should fit in one paragraph; the physics must be the kind a physicist would actually accept (Newtonian mechanics where that's fine, and where you need relativistic effects, use the real ones GR predicts for rotating masses, not invented forces); and — this is the important one — the substantivalist has to lose because of how the physics works out, not because the setup quietly stipulates absolute space out of existence. If the verdict is baked in rather than derived, my students will smell it a mile off. After the scenario, walk through what each side predicts and why, tell me which feature of the case is doing the work (my guess is it's whether inertial structure is fixed independently of the matter distribution, but correct me if it's something else), and finish with the strongest comeback a substantivalist could mount — I've heard there are more "sophisticated" versions of substantivalism that were built to survive exactly this style of argument, and I'd like to know whether they genuinely survive it or just dodge the question.
