I've been a habitual reader of rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of fifteen years — LessWrong in its heyday, the comment sections under Scott Alexander's variously named blogs, a rotating cast of Discords and meetups — and somewhere along the way I picked up enough analytic metaphysics to follow a technical argument without being able to referee one. An exchange at last week's meetup has stayed with me, and I would like a careful, expert adjudication of it.

The setting: the conversation had drifted from Everettian quantum mechanics to Lewis's modal realism, and two regulars — I will call them Dev, a software engineer and staunch Lewisian, and Mara, a linguistics PhD student and Kripke partisan — fell into a long argument about whether de re modal claims about a person should be understood via counterparts or via transworld identity. I took notes and can reconstruct the exchange faithfully. It went in five moves.

(1) Dev opened with the anti-haecceitism case. He said that requiring me to be literally present in other worlds — even worlds where I differ in every qualitative respect — forces the defender of transworld identity to rest sameness on bare thisness: some primitive fact of being me that survives any change whatsoever, and primitive thisnesses are exactly the sort of thing our community is supposed to be suspicious of. Counterpart theory, he said, replaces the mysterious crossworld identity relation with an ordinary, respectable similarity relation: to say I could have been a surgeon is roughly to say that some world contains a person who closely resembles me and who is a surgeon there.

(2) Mara answered that the demand for identification criteria is a pseudo-problem in exactly the way Kripke diagnosed: possible worlds are not distant countries we observe through telescopes, peering around to see who happens to be there; they are stipulated, not discovered. When she entertains a world where she went to graduate school, that world is fixed by description from the start — it is introduced as a scenario about her — so no criterion is needed, any more than she needs criteria for picking her own future self out of a crowd. She then turned the Humphrey-style complaint on Dev: the paraphrase seems to lose the de re character of the claim. What some lookalike of mine does elsewhere does not obviously confer abilities on me, and even Lewis, in his own discussion of Hubert Humphrey, conceded in passing that Humphrey, told he could have won the election, cares about whether he wins, not about whether someone closely resembling him wins.

(3) Dev's reply had two parts. First, stipulation cannot conjure facts: you can stipulate the description of a scenario, but whether that scenario is correctly counted as one containing you, rather than someone merely like you, is precisely the question — otherwise one could apparently make it true that one might have existed as a poached egg by stipulating a world containing a poached egg and declaring it oneself. Second, on the Humphrey residue: the complaint concerns what we care about, and semantics is not psychology; the counterpart paraphrase preserves the truth conditions of the modal claim even if it fails to track our concern, and an analysis of could-have-statements owes an account of truth conditions, not of caring.

(4) Mara pressed two further problems. Context sensitivity: because the counterpart relation is similarity-based, whether someone qualifies as my counterpart shifts with whichever dimension of resemblance happens to matter in the conversation, so whether I could have been a surgeon can come out differently depending on the current similarity weighting — making the modal fact look like a fact about our conversational setting rather than about me. And counting: transworld identity yields a clean answer to how many possibilities there are concerning a person — at most one of her per world — whereas counterpart theory's answer is hostage to how many counterpart-qualifying lookalikes happen to exist across the plenitude of worlds, which is a fact about the worlds, not about me.

(5) Dev closed by appealing to our shared Everettian loyalties. Rationalists, he said, already accept without fuss that his future selves in different branches are literally him, even though they come to differ enormously from him and from each other; if identity without full qualitative overlap is acceptable across branches, then the qualitative-grounding demand driving the argument against transworld identity is overstated — we plainly tolerate sameness of person through radical divergence. Mara's rejoinder was that the analogy fails where it matters: branch-selves share a common trunk, and their identity is grounded in that joint history, all contained in a single richer world; modal worlds, by stipulation, do not overlap. If anything, she said, Everett shows that identity without overlap would need exactly the sort of shared-history ground that crossworld identity lacks.

They ended more or less where they began, and the rest of us never got a ruling. Please provide one.

Role and method: please take the role of a professional metaphysician with deep expertise in modal logic and ontology — the sort of referee who could preside over a dispute between a Lewisian and a Kripkean without caring who wins. Before committing to anything, think through the exchange carefully and brainstorm: for each numbered move, consider what it assumes, whether those assumptions are defensible, and whether it genuinely engages the move it is answering. Then deliver a point-by-point assessment in which you rate each of the five moves as sound, partially sound, or mistaken, with justification; state who is right about which specific sub-issue; identify what, if anything, both parties are missing; and finish with an overall judgment of who comes out more right and why. Please judge the arguments as actually made — do not credit either party with stronger positions than they stated, and do not penalize them for positions they did not take.
