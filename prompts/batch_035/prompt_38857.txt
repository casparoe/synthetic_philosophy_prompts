My flatmate and I have been going round about Nuremberg for weeks and she just produced an argument I can't get out of, except I'm sure it can't be right because it proves way too much. Here it is, roughly as she states it:

(1) A criminal tribunal is legitimate only if the rules it uses to condemn the accused would, in principle, also condemn the accusers, had the accusers done the same things.
(2) At Nuremberg and Tokyo, the accusers had done the same kinds of things: the British and Americans had firebombed cities full of civilians, the Soviet judge sat on the bench having invaded Poland and fought an undeclared war on Finland, and the Soviets had massacred some twenty thousand Polish officers in the Katyn forest and then spent the Nuremberg proceedings trying to pin that one on the Germans.
(3) The tribunals never applied their rules to the accusers -- not a single Allied or Soviet act was even charged.
(4) So the tribunals failed the condition in (1) and were illegitimate.
(5) But every criminal court in history fails that same condition: domestic courts try the state's subjects and never the state itself, and every postwar court tries only the vanquished.
(6) So no criminal court has ever been legitimate.
(7) So Nuremberg and Tokyo weren't specially tainted -- a trial run by the winners is just what a trial is.

The kicker is she actually likes (7). She's a bit of a legal realist and says tribunals have always been politics in robes, and Nuremberg at least used good rules even if enforcement was selective, which she thinks is the best you can ask for. I don't want to bite that bullet, but I also think the victors' justice complaint against these tribunals is basically right -- the one-side-of-the-dock thing isn't a cosmetic defect. So either I give up the complaint or I concede that no court anywhere was ever legitimate, and I don't like either.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? My instinct is that something slips between (1) and (5) -- maybe "in principle" is doing different work in the two premises -- but I can't pin it down. I'd also like to know whether the retroactivity complaint (crimes against peace being, arguably, invented after the fact, Kellogg-Briand notwithstanding) is a separate problem or whether it can be folded into her symmetry argument, and whether folding it in makes her case stronger or actually worse. And when you answer, can you keep straight for me what's actually settled here -- the historical stuff about what was charged, Justice Pal's dissent at Tokyo, what happened with Katyn -- versus what's genuinely contested among people who study this? I don't want to come back at her waving some contested realist take as if it were the consensus view.
