so this happened at dinner friday and I still can't decide who was right. my roommate Sam has gotten really into this position he found online called tranquilism — the idea that the only thing that's intrinsically good is the absence of craving and suffering, everything else is either instrumental or just doesn't count. he brought it up and our other roommate Jordan went after it. it went like four rounds and I want to say upfront both of them were making decent points, this wasn't one of those fights where someone's obviously out of their depth.

round one: Sam said tranquilism implies a quiet life of simple contentment is exactly as good as a life where you cure malaria, since what matters is only whether craving is absent, not whether anything else gets achieved. Jordan said that's just negative utilitarianism reheated, and negative utilitarianism has the famous bullet it has to bite: if nothing matters except suffering's absence, then painlessly ending all life would be the best possible outcome, no more suffering ever, and if you won't bite that bullet the view is dead on arrival. so by his lights Sam either has to accept world-termination as optimal or give up tranquilism.

round two: Sam said no, that's a category error — negative utilitarianism is an aggregative morality, it's about summing up suffering across all beings and telling you what the world should look like. tranquilism as he understands it is a view about the shape of a good life for a person: it says for each of us the only intrinsic good is the absence of craving. it doesn't tell you to go rearrange the universe, it tells you what to aim at in your own case. Jordan said fine, but then it's basically just Epicurus with a new coat of paint, and anyway the word "only" is doing way too much work — when a craving ends and you feel relief, that relief is pleasant, it feels good. so the view doesn't actually exclude pleasure, it just refuses to use the word, which is a labeling choice not a philosophy.

round three: Sam came back with what he called his trump card, and honestly this is the part I keep chewing on. he said maybe every pleasurable state just IS the cessation of some discomfort or lack — hunger, boredom, longing, restlessness — so pleasure isn't being smuggled in under the name of relief, rather there was never any separate thing called pleasure to smuggle in. it's not that tranquilism values relief and calls it pleasure, it's that what we call pleasure is what the stopping of lack feels like from the inside. Jordan said that's an empirical claim about introspection and he thinks it's just false — listening to a song you love doesn't feel like any discomfort stopping, there's no itch it's scratching — and anyway even granting it, Sam keeps planning hiking trips and looking forward to things, which is textbook craving about the future, so he doesn't live like any of this is true. Sam said the trips are how he quiets the restlessness, so no contradiction, and Jordan made a face like that answer was somehow worse than a contradiction.

they finally turned to me and said settle it, and I couldn't. please go through the whole exchange move by move and tell me who's right about which specific point — like, is the negative-utilitarianism objection really a hit, or did it miss the target Sam actually claims to be aiming at? does Jordan's "that's just pleasure under another name" point defeat the view or just rename the debate? and is Sam's trump card doing what he thinks it does — is it a substantive claim, and if it were true would it actually support tranquilism? also please tell me what, if anything, both of them managed to miss entirely across all four rounds, because I suspect there's something about the difference between a theory of value and a theory of wellbeing (or something like that) that neither ever put his finger on. who's more right overall?
