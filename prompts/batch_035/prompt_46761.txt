For the past two years, my brother-in-law and I have been arguing about whether it can ever be right to impose a serious burden on a few people in order to give small benefits to a great many others — for instance, whether a city may make one street unbearably noisy for its residents so that half a million commuters each save four minutes a day. He is a thoroughgoing utilitarian: he thinks well-being can be added up across people, like the contents of separate bank accounts poured into one pot, and that once the adding is done, whose well-being it was doesn't count for anything further. I have come to believe this misses something about how separate people are: nobody experiences the total, the commuters' four minutes don't pile up into anything the residents of the noisy street can be compensated from, and I suspect that treating the sum as what matters is a kind of optical illusion created by arithmetic. We have reached the stage where we mostly repeat ourselves, so I would like you to draft the document we should have produced long ago: the joint statement of a good-faith collaboration between the two of us, written so that each of us would actually put his name to it.

I have no philosophy training — everything I know about this comes from thinking it through on my own and arguing at family dinners — so please keep technical vocabulary to a minimum and explain any term you do introduce.

The document should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered list of claims we can both sign. Include claims about the cases themselves: not just the noise case but also one where the arithmetic runs the other way (a large cost to one person prevented by small contributions from many), and one where the many small benefits add up to something enormous. Also include whatever we can agree about what a theory of these decisions is even supposed to do.

2. A precise statement of each remaining disagreement, phrased so that each of us would accept the wording as a fair statement of his own view — not so slanted that he would refuse to sign it, and not so vague that we could both sign it while still disagreeing.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us. I have a suspicion about one crux and want you to test whether I am right. He says that a single life is just a series of moments, related to each other exactly the way separate lives are related, so that if I accept that a bad year of my own can be outweighed by good years — and I do accept this — then I have already accepted the pot for a small container and am merely haggling about the size of the container. I feel there is a real difference — the moments of one life are connected in a way that lives are not — but I cannot make the difference precise, and I worry that my feeling is just sentimentality about the word "life." State whether this is a genuine crux, give the strongest version of each side's position on it, and identify any other cruxes you find, including ones neither of us has noticed.

4. For each crux, at least one concrete case, argument, or thought experiment that we would both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case written down in advance — so that we could actually run the test and see whose prediction holds.

5. A verdict, to the extent one is possible, on whether any part of our dispute is merely verbal: if some of it turns on using words like "one person's good" or "aggregation" differently rather than on genuine disagreement, say which part and why.

You are not being asked to decide who is right. You are being asked to find formulations that are neither empty nor rigged, and to make the residue between us exact.
