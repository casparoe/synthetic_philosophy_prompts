The exam question was:

Explain Kripke's interpretation of Wittgenstein on rule-following (as presented in Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language). Your answer should (a) reconstruct the skeptical paradox about meaning, using the example of a mathematical function such as addition, (b) explain the distinction between a "straight" solution to the paradox and a "skeptical" solution, and (c) reconstruct and evaluate at least one candidate straight solution that Kripke considers, explaining why he rejects it.

The student's response:

Wittgenstein's rule-following paradox, as Kripke presents it, concerns how any finite number of past uses of a word or symbol is compatible with indefinitely many future uses. Consider someone who has computed "57 + 68" many times before but has never previously added numbers larger than 57. When asked to compute "68 + 57," the skeptic suggests that this person may have always meant a different function, call it "quus," which agrees with addition for all smaller numbers but returns 5 when both arguments exceed 57. Nothing in the student's past behavior or mental history determines that they meant plus rather than quus, so there is no fact about what they meant, and their future answer is not right or wrong in any robust sense.

Kripke's own solution to this paradox is dispositionalism: what it is to mean addition is to be disposed, when presented with a pair of numbers, to give the answer that agrees with addition. This handles the quus problem because we are simply not disposed to answer "5" when adding large numbers. A skeptical solution, by contrast, is one that admits the skeptic is right that there is no fact of the matter, but offers a non-factual replacement: assertions of meaning are justified by whether they fall under communal rules of assertion, and the community's practice of endorsing certain answers and correcting deviant ones is what gives judgments about meaning their role. Kripke opts for the skeptical solution on behalf of Wittgenstein, since Wittgenstein held that meaning is use, and the community's use settles the matter. Straight solutions try to identify a fact that refutes the skeptic, and they fail because any candidate fact, such as a mental image or a formula, would itself admit of multiple interpretations and thus be subject to a regress.

In evaluating: the dispositionalist solution seems basically right, and Kripke should have accepted it rather than retreating to the skeptical solution. Its main problem is finitude — our dispositions for enormous numbers are undefined or unstable — but that problem could be fixed by idealizing the dispositions. The skeptical solution, meanwhile, threatens relativism: if the community's standards define correctness, then a community of quus-followers would be right by their lights, which seems to abandon the objectivity of arithmetic.
