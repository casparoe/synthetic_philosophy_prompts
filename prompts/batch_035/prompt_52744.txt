I've been asked to write up my views on what the Athenian assembly's canonical disasters — Mytilene, Sicily, and the trial of Socrates — actually teach about institutional design, and before I write the piece I want a consistency audit of the position I've drifted into. Here are its six planks.

(1) The Mytilene debate of 427 BCE is the strongest single piece of evidence we possess for democratic self-correction. The Assembly voted to execute the adult male population of Mytilene; a second debate was held the very next day, the massacre was rescinded, and Diodotus carried the reversal without appealing to justice at all — he argued that killing the Mytileneans was simply not in Athens' interest. The system heard an argument and processed it. It is true that the rescue depended on the first trireme being slow and a faster one being sent in pursuit, so luck played a part, and I've never settled in my own mind whether the credit belongs to the institution or to the luck.

(2) The Sicilian expedition of 415 BCE shows the same institution at its worst. Nicias spoke candidly in the hope of deterring the enterprise, and the Assembly responded by giving him more than he asked for. Inflated requirements produced the largest force Athens had ever sent out, while the many citizens who privately doubted the whole venture stayed silent. The lesson I draw is that mass meetings reward confident projection and punish candour.

(3) The trial of Socrates in 399 BCE was not a failure of democracy as such. He was prosecuted, defended himself, and was convicted by a jury of some five hundred citizens under the procedures the city used for everything else. A large jury in almost any regime could have convicted a man who spent decades telling the most influential young men of the city that their fathers' virtues were worthless. So I resist counting the trial as an indictment of majority rule in particular.

(4) Since Mytilene and Sicily happened under the same constitution with opposite outcomes, the operative variable is not constitutional machinery but the condition of public deliberation at a given moment — war fever, demagoguery, the safety or otherwise of dissent. Constitutions do not have a dial for the sobriety of the moment, so the institutional lessons of these episodes are more limited than people usually claim.

(5) That said, I favour copying the one structural feature that saved Mytilene: irreversibility is the enemy. Modern assemblies should have mandatory cooling-off periods before irreversible decisions, plus small review panels chosen by lot and given the time, information, and immunity from crowd pressure that the full Assembly lacked.

(6) I remain a democrat in the end: the Assembly's decisions were legitimate regardless of whether they were wise, because legitimacy does not track wisdom.

What I want from you is an audit, not a history lecture — assume I know the cases and the standard scholarship, and do not re-tell them at length. Identify which pairs among the six planks are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say precisely what each side would have to give up and what the revision costs elsewhere in the set. For each merely apparent tension, show why it dissolves. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I genuinely do not know where the inconsistency lies, if it lies anywhere, and I have no plank I am protecting or looking to discard — so please do not infer any such preference. Please write your answer in British English.
