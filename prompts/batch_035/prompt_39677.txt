So I got into this huge argument with my friend Sam at dinner last week. He's basically a utilitarian, always saying stuff like "if the numbers add up you do it, doesn't matter whose happiness it is." I kept pushing back with something I couldn't quite put my finger on: like, say you can make 20 people slightly better off by totally screwing over one person, and the 20 small gains add up to more than the one big loss — Sam says do it, obviously. And something about that feels wrong to me in a way that isn't just me being bad at math. His comeback is that I'm just squeamish about arithmetic. Later I found out my feeling actually has a name, the "separateness of persons" objection — roughly, people aren't like regions of one big super-person whose pleasure and pain you can pour into a single pot. Each person has their own life and their own losses, and nobody else's gain cancels out my loss. If I stub my toe, your nice meal doesn't make me feel better. The pains and gains don't pool across people, they only pool within one life.

Anyway, since then I've been collecting the replies utilitarians give to this, and I want to know which of them actually work, because some of them feel like they're answering a slightly different question. Here's my list with the names I made up for them: (1) the equal concern reply — utilitarianism respects persons *precisely* by counting each one exactly the same, one unit each, no favorites, so it's actually the alternatives that treat people unequally; (2) the shuffle reply — if you didn't know which of the people involved you'd end up being, you'd still want the total maximized, so an impartial chooser would sign up for the rule ahead of time; (3) the everyday trade reply — we already accept this constantly, taxes make one guy worse off so alot of people are a bit better off, and nobody calls that tyranny, so my intuition proves too much; (4) the edge case reply — in real life the numbers almost never line up the way my hypothetical does, so the objection is about freak cases, and a theory shouldn't be judged by freak cases; (5) the "you owe us a better one" reply — until I can say how much weight one person's big loss gets versus many small gains, I haven't proposed anything, I've just complained, and any rule I'd accept instead faces the exact same question at the margin anyway.

Rank these five replies by how well they actually answer the objection, not by how popular or famous they are. Give me a table with these columns: "reply" (use my names so I can follow), "score" (1–10 for how much sting it takes out), "what it gets right" (one sentence), "where it falls short" (one sentence), and "would it move someone who has the intuition strongly" (yes / maybe / no). Then below the table a short paragraph saying which reply, or combo of replies, comes closest to a full answer, and whether the objection still stands in some form once you've absorbed all five. And please take the objection seriously — don't just hand Sam the win because his math is tidy.
