My roommate and I keep having this running argument about when its ok for science to just make stuff up, and I've gotten to where I can mostly predict her verdicts but the new case we just hit actually stumps me, so I want your read.

Her track record so far, as best I can reconstruct it:

- Physics treating gas molecules like tiny perfectly elastic billiard balls that never interact: totally fine.
- Econ models where every person is a perfectly rational emotionless calculator: also fine, as long as the predictions come out roughly right. She actually goes further here — she says ridiculous assumptions are almost a feature, like if your theory still predicts well while assuming something insane, that proves its capturing something real.
- The Hardy-Weinberg thing from genetics, where you assume an infinite population and random mating and so on: fine (she brought that up, don't ask me how it came up).
- A climate model where the scientists hand-tuned some parameter until it matched the historical temperature record: NOT fine. She called that cheating, which confused me at the time because it "predicts" the past perfectly well — although I realize now that's a weird thing to call a prediction.
- A traffic model where cars are dots that all want the same speed and never crash: fine.
- A psychology study that quietly dropped the data points that didn't fit: not fine, obviously, though she seemed more worked up about this one than the climate one.

Now the new case. There are these AI weather models now that are just giant neural nets trained on decades of past weather, no explicit physics in them, and they apparently beat the traditional physics-based models at actual forecasting. Nobody can really explain WHY they work or point to the mechanism — there's no "assumptions" in there at all, just a huge pile of fitted parameters.

So: what's her verdict gonna be? Here's my problem. I can see at least two rules she might be running. One is something like "assumptions can be as false as you like, but you can't fit stuff to the data to force agreement" — that accepts the billiard balls and rejects the hand-tuned parameter, but it would have to reject the neural net, since that's nothing BUT fitted parameters. Another is something like "idealizing = legitimately simplifying away messy detail, versus fudging = inserting stuff you know is wrong just to make it fit" — and I genuinely can't tell what that rule says about something with no assumptions at all. And honestly the first rule might be too strong anyway, since economists estimate coefficients from data all the time and she's never complained about that.

Give me your best guess at her verdict, what rule (or rules) you think actually fits her whole track record, where the rules you come up with diverge on this case, and — this is the part I care most about — what further case I could throw at her that would actually settle which rule she's running. Like would the hand-tuning still count as cheating if a computer did the tuning automatically over ten thousand parameters instead of a scientist doing it by hand? I have no idea and it's bugging me.
