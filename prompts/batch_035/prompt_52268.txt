I build ML systems for a living — mostly prediction models, no philosophy background beyond stuff I half-remember from a podcast. Last weekend I got into a really long argument with my old college friend who's now doing a philosophy PhD and is a huge Richard Rorty fan. His claim was that modern ML basically vindicates Rorty's whole thing: our models don't mirror reality, they're just tools that happen to work for our purposes, and when they stop working we don't discover that they were false, we just build better tools. He kept bringing up Rorty's book Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature and his famous line about truth being what your peers let you get away with saying, and at some point he said the fact that we keep retraining and re-benchmarking our models, instead of checking them against reality-as-it-is-in-itself, is exactly the antirepresentationalist point. I argued back that we literally call our models' internal states representations, and that evaluation on held-out data sure feels like being answerable to something outside our own vocabulary — but honestly I was wingin it and I think he noticed.

So here's the ask: restate Rorty's antirepresentationalism — rejection of truth as correspondence, knowledge and justification as social practices, vocabularies as tools, incommensurability between them, whatever else is actually central to his position — in the vocabulary I actually use day to day: training objectives, loss functions, generalization, benchmarks, distribution shift, latent features, the difference between a model that fits its training set and one that works in deployment. I want a systematic translation, not vibes. Then the residue report: which parts of his view map cleanly, which ones had to be distorted to fit, and which can't be expressed in my vocabulary at all — and for that last part, your view on whether the failure is my vocabulary missing something or his claim being less clear than it looked. The bit I most want you to take seriously is the word representation itself. In my field it has a precise technical meaning (features the model learns because the loss rewards them), and I genuinely can't tell whether Rorty's claim that language doesn't represent survives contact with that, or whether translating his view through my field quietly changes the subject to something he wasn't talking about. And then the verdict on my friend's conclusion: does ML practice really show there's nothing more to say about a model being correct than that it currently passes our tests, or was he stretching the analogy past what it can carry?

Two more things. First, if some part of Rorty is contested among people who actually study him, say so instead of papering over it — I'd rather know where you're on thin ice than get a smooth answer. Second, I want you to flag where this translation is most likely to go wrong and what would make you change your verdict. I'm specifically suspicious that phrases like the world pushes back, which we use constantly in ML, might be doing exactly the job Rorty denies the world can do — and if so I'd like to know whether that's a real problem for translating him into my vocabulary or just a coincidence of wording.
