In six weeks I present at a workshop on the Fischer controversy, some sixty years after the 1961 book that started it. My thesis is that both sides of that old fight were answering the wrong question. Article 231 of the Versailles Treaty, insofar as it asserted anything, asserted that Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage — and it failed, I want to argue, because it hung a nation's responsibility on the causal chain of July 1914, exactly where structuralist historians can multiply contributing causes until the blame dissolves into a system nobody authored. The responsibility I want to defend is real but second-order: it is incurred not by causing the war but by what a nation does afterward with the deed — in Germany's case, by the decades-long campaign to deny any German role, the treatment of the war-guilt clause as a slander to be repudiated rather than a claim to be answered, and the fury that greeted Fischer's documents when they were finally published. Blame the response, not the war.

The audience is about fifteen people. Most are historians of the Kaiserreich from the structuralist, Bielefeld tradition, who hold that causal talk about a nation acting is a category mistake and that responsibility-talk belongs to moral philosophy, not historiography; one is a Fischer loyalist who thinks my second-order move quietly lets the 1914 leadership off the hook; two are philosophers of collective responsibility who will attack the very idea of a nation incurring anything. Prepare my Q&A sheet accordingly: the objections in the order they are likely to arrive, phrased in the idiom of whoever will raise them; each ranked by how much damage it could do if badly answered; each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft; plus the one question I am hoping nobody asks. I would also like a candid assessment of which objection, if any, my thesis cannot survive.
