I've been reading LessWrong and the blogs around it since the Sequences were still trickling out, and over the years I've ended up squarely on Dennett's side of the debate about the timing of experience. The case that persuaded me is the one he built with Marcel Kinsbourne in the early 1990s around the color phi phenomenon: two stationary spots of different colors, flashed in sequence, are experienced as a single spot that moves smoothly across the gap and changes color midway — even though nothing in the world ever moved or changed. The puzzle isn't the illusion itself but the timing. Subjects can report where and when the color changed, and the report can be shaped by information that only became available after the change was supposedly experienced. The classic framing then asks: did the subject first have a plain experience of two static spots that was subsequently edited in memory (the Orwellian version, the record rewritten after the fact), or did the brain's editing happen before any experience was fixed, so that the unedited experience never occurred at all (the Stalinesque version, the history airbrushed before it entered the record)? Dennett's move, as I understand it, is not to pick a side but to deny that there is a well-defined fact of the matter here: the assumption that there must be a determinate moment at which the content of consciousness gets fixed is what he calls the Cartesian Theater, and his Multiple Drafts model is supposed to show that nothing in the phenomena or the neurophysiology requires such a theater to make sense of timing effects like phi, the cutaneous rabbit, and the postdictive results catalogued since.

I hold this dissolutionist view fairly confidently — I think the theater is a genuine conceptual trap and that the standard railings against it (that it denies consciousness, that it can't accommodate qualia, that it's behaviorism in a trench coat) miss the point. But a load-bearing belief that has never been stress-tested is just a belief I happen to hold, so I want to stress-test it. Tell me what would count against it. Concretely: what empirical findings, if they turned up, would put pressure on the claim that there is no fact of the matter about when an experience's content is fixed? Would a refined Libet-style result, or the discovery of a precise neural stage such that downstream interventions count as memory edits while upstream ones count as content-determining inputs, force the view to retreat — or would it absorb them as more of the same drafts-and-revisions machinery? Which objections would Dennett and his allies themselves concede as counting against the view, which do they reinterpret away, and is the reinterpretation principled or ad hoc? Please rank the defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and end with an honest verdict: has this view been qualified to the point where no possible discovery about the brain could count against it — and if so, is that fatal for a view of this kind, or is it just what a successful dissolution of a confused question is supposed to look like? Along the way, tell me whether the strongest complaint on the other side — that saying there's no fact of the matter quietly makes the timing of experience answerable to nothing at all, and thereby concedes more than it admits — is a fair strike or a failure to grasp the position. I'd rather come out shaken than reassured, so argue as though you actually want to move me.
