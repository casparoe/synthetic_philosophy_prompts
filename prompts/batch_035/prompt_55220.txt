For most of my career I was a structural biochemist — thirty-odd years of protein folding, crystallography, and teaching — and I have only lately, in retirement, begun reading philosophy of biology seriously. Something keeps snagging me, and I would appreciate a careful audit rather than the usual hand-waving.

At a public lecture last month, the speaker — a lively fellow promoting a book on the origins of life — leaned hard on the claim that the genome is literally a computer program: DNA is written in a four-letter alphabet, arranged into words (codons) that are read in order, spelling out instructions that the cellular machinery executes. He extended the metaphor with relish: ribosomes as compilers or 3D printers, promoters as entry points, gene regulation as conditional branching, mutation as copying errors, and repair enzymes as version control. He is far from alone; one finds prominent scientists and technologists remarking that DNA is like a very sophisticated piece of software, and whole popular books built on the picture. And of course the same analogy does heavier work elsewhere: in debates about intelligent design, the inference runs that code implies coder — that whenever we find a functioning code, in every case we know of, there is a mind behind it, so the burden falls on the naturalist. In my own field I also watch the metaphor do quieter work: gene-editing companies speak of "debugging" the genome, and journals run headlines about "reprogramming" cells, as though the cell were a machine awaiting our instructions rather than a perpetually self-maintaining chemical system.

I have my own suspicions about where the analogy strains — the genetic code proper is just the codon-to-amino-acid cipher, a lookup table, while the "program" language seems to be about something else entirely, namely the developmental and regulatory role of the whole genome, and conflating the two may be where much of the trouble starts. But I want this done systematically rather than by anecdote.

Please lay out the audit as follows. First, a correspondence table: what plays the role of the program, the data, the hardware, the compiler or interpreter, the programmer, the documentation, the runtime environment, and the output. Be explicit about which parts of the cell-genome system are being assigned to which roles, since I suspect the metaphor is internally inconsistent — different users assign the ribosome, the cell, and the organism to different slots depending on what they need it to do. Second, the disanalogies: where does the mapping break, and which breaks matter for which of the three uses above? I am particularly interested in the features the analogy quietly imports — that software is designed, that code and execution are separable, that a program runs identically on any suitable hardware, that there is a specification the program is meant to meet — and in whether the target system lacks them. Third, the verdict: for each of the three uses (origins-of-life storytelling, the design inference, and the rhetoric of "editing" and "reprogramming"), does the conclusion the analogy was recruited to support survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole way? Fourth, if the metaphor is more burden than help, propose a better one — I have seen recipes, blueprints, and even a "self-executing chemistry" offered as replacements — and say what each alternative preserves and what it gives up, including the pedagogical cost of abandoning language that has evidently made molecular biology legible to the public.
