At the small effective-giving fund where I work, we recently spent two meetings arguing over whether to renew a grant to a well-evaluated deworming program or redirect the money to a highly speculative research effort that, if it succeeded, could save enormously more lives, but with a probability nobody can honestly pin down. When several of us expressed discomfort with the long-shot option, a colleague shut the discussion down by invoking the rationalist slogan 'shut up and multiply', as if the arithmetic itself settled the matter. I have a feeling that phrase is doing several different jobs at once, and that only some of them were legitimate in that room, but I lack the vocabulary to sort them out. Could you lay out, as a structured menu I can bring back to our next meeting, the distinct precise theses that 'shut up and multiply' might actually be asserting? I would want you to cover at least the following candidates, though I suspect there are others: (1) the near-trivial reading that one should do the expected-value calculation before deciding, whatever one ultimately does with it; (2) the psychological claim that humans systematically underweight large benefits at small probabilities or fail to scale with the number of beneficiaries, and that the slogan is a corrective against that bias; (3) the normative claim that once utilities and probabilities are on the table, the expected-value verdict is decisive, so that hesitation itself is irrational; (4) the stronger metaphysical or decision-theoretic commitment that risk aversion is never rational, as opposed to being represented innocuously as concavity of the utility function; and (5) whatever reading generates the fanatic result that for any nonzero probability of a sufficiently vast payoff, the gamble dominates any certain modest benefit. For each reading, please say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or false, what assumptions it needs about interpersonal aggregation and diminishing returns, and, crucially, whether it would actually have supported my colleague's move of ending the debate rather than merely starting one. I would also appreciate a brief note on whether the readings that make the slogan forceful in our funding context are the same ones that make it forceful in individual life-and-death gambles, or whether the slogan quietly equivocates between the two.
