Until last month I was a process engineer at an insect-protein company, and my philosophical commitments extended no further than a firm belief in the spec sheet. Then, because I once took an elective in ethics, I was assigned to draft the animal-welfare policy our investors require before they will fund our expansion from crickets into black soldier fly larvae. Immersing myself in the literature, I have found that the question of which creatures deserve moral consideration has no comfortable answer: the arguments for a sharp line between the sentient and the non-sentient are weak, the arguments for treating moral status as a continuum are worse for my industry, and every threshold I encounter appears to have been reverse-engineered from its author's preferred conclusion. To survive the literature review, I need satire.

Please write a piece of sustained corporate satire on my behalf: an internal memorandum, around 650 words, issued by the fictional Continuum Wellness Division of an insect-agriculture firm, announcing the company's new Moral Status Rubric. The rubric is a numbered-clause points system that grades candidate entities — the building thermostat, the cleaning robot, shrimp, the company's flagship insect lines — on criteria such as nociception, centralized integration of sensory information, behavioral flexibility, self-recognition, and evidence of valenced states. The memo must be deadpan, bureaucratically polished, and entirely sincere; it should never notice that it is satire. All of the humor must come from the genuine philosophical difficulties. I would like to see, among other things: the distinction between detecting noxious stimuli and experiencing anything at all, handled by awarding partial credit; the aggregation problem, whereby a welfare weight of one ten-thousandth per animal quietly dominates the ledger once multiplied by ten billion; the precautionary principle, applied consistently, threatening to engulf the HVAC control system; and the awkward fact that the scoring criteria have shifted just enough that the company's own products land safely above the welfare threshold, while a competitor's are found, regretfully, to fall just below it. Please include a short appendix addressing the cleaning robot, which the facilities staff have named and now defend in meetings, so that the memo has to take a stance on when affection becomes evidence. Somewhere the document should acknowledge, without registering what it is acknowledging, that the rubric has settled the hard problem of consciousness by committee vote.

The piece should be funny to a reader who knows the animal-sentience and consciousness debates and readable to one who does not; nothing should require looking anything up, and I ask that you cite no real researchers, works, or companies. Do not wink at the reader anywhere: the memo believes in its rubric, and that belief is where the sting should live. By the end, a reader ought to feel the uncomfortable question I now face daily — not whether moral status comes in degrees, but who profits from exactly where the graduations are marked.
