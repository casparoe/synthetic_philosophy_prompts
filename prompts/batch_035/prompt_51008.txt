Retired chemist here, finally reading history of science after a career of teaching the textbook version. Every historian I pick up repeats the line "The past is a foreign country; they do things differently there," usually right before scolding people like me for telling the history of our field as one long march toward the current periodic table, as if Lavoisier were just an incomplete draft of a modern chemist. But what is that sentence actually claiming? As far as I can tell it could be anything from a platitude (they used different instruments and wore funny wigs) to a methodological point (don't assume past scientists were even trying to answer our questions, so 'they got it wrong' may be a category error) to a full normative ban on judging them, to something radical I suspect is in the air -- that our chemistry is itself just one local custom, no more 'correct' in the relevant sense than phlogiston was to its users, so the march-of-progress story isn't just bad manners but strictly incoherant. Lay out the distinct readings you can find in that one sentence, mark which are trivial, which are defensible, which are probably false, and tell me which ones actually support the historians' scolding -- because it seems to me the radical reading, taken seriously, makes any history impossible, including theirs. And which reading should I actually use when I explain to my grandkids why smart people believed in phlogiston for a century?
