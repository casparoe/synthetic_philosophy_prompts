A friend of mine does integrated pest management consulting for restaurants and warehouses, and after a few beers he got me started on something I can't stop chewing on. The way we treat pest deaths is bizarrely uneven. In a lot of countries you could theoretically be prosecuted for torturing a rat — cruelty laws cover vertebrate pests even when they're vermin — and there have been real campaigns and even bans around things like glue boards for rodents, precisely because the deaths are considered inhumane. Meanwhile the same societies run mosquito fogging programs and sell cans of spray that kill thousands of insects, and nobody asks how the ants or the wasps experience it. Look at how a new insecticide actually gets evaluated: it has to kill the target efficiently, it gets tested for residues dangerous to humans, and for harms to bees and aquatic life (but basically as pollinators and ecosystem members, not as individuals having a bad time), and that's the whole assessment. Even "organic" or "natural" pest control products get their status from how the substance was sourced, not from how it kills. So the death of the target insect is the one thing the entire edifice is silent on.

My question is what this whole practice presupposes, philosophically. Is it committed to the factual bet that insects can't suffer (which seems increasingly shaky as nociception research on flies and bees piles up), or to a moral claim that even if they do suffer it doesn't count below some cognitive cutoff, or to something more like a role-based view where having the status of "pest" strips you of any standing whatsoever? Or maybe it's not a theory of moral status at all but something about which kinds of harm we find visually disturbing — the glue board produces a body you can watch struggle, the spray doesn't. Can you reconstruct the implicit view, and tell me which of these readings the practice strictly requires versus which it merely sits comfortably with? I'd especially like to know whether the humane-rodent part and the don't-care-about-insects part even rest on consistent foundations, or whether the whole thing is philosophically underdetermined such that several rival theories would each license the pattern. And then flip it around: if we swapped in a view where insect welfare genuinely counts, what would have to change concretely — killing-method restrictions like the ones rodents get, choosing the fastest-killing rather than lowest-residue compound, some kind of welfare assessment step in pesticide approval?

Please be upfront about how much of your reconstruction you'd actually stand behind versus where you're reading tea leaves, and single out the part you're least confident in — I'm fine with a "here's where the evidence runs out" answer, I just want to know where that is. Also if you think my framing of the inconsistency is off, e.g. that the vertebrate/insect line isn't really where the action is, say so rather than running with my setup.
