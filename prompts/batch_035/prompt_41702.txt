I manage the sensory panel at a mid-size specialty coffee roastery — eight trained cuppers, weekly calibration cuppings, and ad-hoc panels whenever a supplier changes, a customer complains, or we sign off on a blend reformulation. Increasingly I realize my core job is a philosophical problem wearing an apron. When a panelist who has scored our flagship blend's body as a 7 for two years suddenly scores it a 5, I have to decide whether the coffee changed or the taster did — and when she insists, from the inside, that she can tell the difference between the coffee tasting different and her merely judging it differently, I have no way to check that claim, only her word. This is, I gather, exactly the bind Dennett stages in Quining Qualia with his coffee drinker who moves from cream and sugar to black, and his wine expert whose once-loved vintage now strikes him as cloying: the taster's introspective report seems unable, by itself, to settle whether the change is in the object, the percept, or the reaction to an unchanged percept — and the deeper suggestion is that the very idea of a determinate answer may rest on treating qualia as more well-defined posits than they are.

Please turn that cluster of ideas into a decision procedure my lab can actually run, written so a shift lead with no philosophy background can follow it. I need: an ordered list of questions to ask about any reported difference (whose report, over what interval, against what reference, with what blind controls, corroborated by whom), what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and the specific outputs — accept as a product change, treat as panelist drift and recalibrate, split the panel and retrain, or declare the difference undecidable from within and say what external evidence would settle it. Mark clearly the points where the procedure cannot eliminate a judgment call, and say so plainly rather than disguising it as measurement. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) our shelf-life study, where the same six panelists scored the same lot at month zero and month eight, and the staleness scores rose for three of them but not the rest; (2) our most senior cupper — the one whose palate we treat as the reference standard — returns from a national competition and starts scoring our flagship as thin, while the rest of the panel scores it unchanged; and (3) the head roaster wants to approve a new lot from a new farm that he swears tastes identical to the lot it replaces, and two of our newest panelists, hired last month, score it as brighter. One of these cases should produce an answer I won't like — probably the one about our senior cupper or about what our calibration history is worth — and I want you to give that answer anyway. Finish by stating honestly what the procedure cannot do: which distinctions it renders genuinely undecidable, where a cupper's from-the-inside confidence will simply have to be overruled or deferred to as a matter of policy rather than knowledge, and where the philosophical point that the object/subject split may be partly a posit shows up as a permanent hole in the flowchart rather than a step in it.
