I run a four-person forecasting team at a mid-size insurance company. Every friday we put out estimates together — how many claims a big storm will produce, whether a reinsurance partner renews, that kind of thing — and whenever two of us land on noticeably different numbers we hash it out on a 45-minute call that mostly goes nowhere. Someone told me about Aumann's agreement theorem, the idea that two honest rational people with the same priors who know each other's estimates shouldn't be able to agree to disagree, and I want that turned into an actual procedure my team can run on a disagreement: steps in what order (independent estimates first? swap numbers before reasons? swap reasons then re-estimate?), what counts as having actually shared the relevant information versus just restating positions louder, when the procedure gets to declare "you two have different priors, that's the whole difference, stop" and when it should keep pushing, and what to do when someone's best evidence is something they can't share (client-confidential, or a source they promised anonymity) and all they can give is the upshot. Be honest that the theorem's assumptions don't hold for us — we're not Bayesians, we definitely don't have common priors, and 45 minutes isn't convergence — so this thing is at best Aumann-flavored, and say which parts are doing real work and which are theater. Then run it on three real-ish cases: (a) me and a colleague both read the same regulator report and still differ by 15% on expected payouts; (b) a disagreement where my teammate's best evidence is a confidential conversation with a broker she can only describe the gist of; (c) two forecasters who have gone back and forth three times over a month on one renewal question and are still 20 points apart — what does persistent disagreement like that actually mean, and is there a point where the procedure treats it as a red flag about one of them rather than just "we disagree"? And flag where this becomes a judgment call no procedure can settle.
