I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar this term on personal identity, and my study partner and I have gotten into the habit of running thought experiments past each other before each Tuesday session. Over the semester she has built up a track record of verdicts on mind-transfer cases, and for the final session our instructor is having us each predict the other's verdict on a new case he has circulated, with our prediction sheets due before we discuss it. I've been keeping notes on her record, and I can't settle on a prediction. Her verdicts so far:

- If her neurons are replaced one at a time, over roughly five years, by silicon chips that take over each neuron's input-output role at the moment it is removed, while she goes on living normally: she survives, and remains conscious throughout.
- The same procedure, confined to her left hemisphere over two years: she survives.
- If every organ except her brain were replaced with artificial parts: she survives, obviously.
- If her brain is destructively scanned and an exact emulation of her is then run: the emulation is fully conscious, but she does not survive — a new person comes into existence who happens to be psychologically continuous with her.
- If the scan is non-destructive: she survives in the flesh, and the digital copy is a new person.
- A teletransporter that destructively scans her and reconstructs her elsewhere: no survival.
- A patient whose brain activity was deliberately and completely suspended for a full day of hypothermic surgery and then restarted: she survives.

She insists her verdicts have nothing to do with consciousness — she is entirely willing to grant that the emulation and the copy are conscious — and says the question is always whether the later system is the same continuing one as the earlier one. That formula hasn't helped me, because I can hear at least three different ideas inside it, and I can't tell which she means.

The new case we have to predict on: over a single weekend, under general anesthesia, each of her neurons is removed one at a time and replaced by a chip that is not installed in her skull but sits in a server rack across campus, connected by a radio link with sub-millisecond latency. At no moment is any of her brain's function interrupted; the last biological neuron is retired on Sunday night; on Monday she wakes up with no biological brain in her head at all. What will she say — survival or no survival?

My uncertainty is about what, in her eyes, did the work in the earlier cases: the unbroken causal handoff from each dying neuron to its successor, the sheer slowness of the change relative to ordinary cellular turnover, or the fact that everything so far happened in place, inside her skull. The new case seems designed to pull these apart — the timescale collapses, the hardware leaves her head, but the handoffs never miss a beat — and depending on which of those I take to be her real criterion, I get opposite predictions.

Please don't hand me a menu of candidate rules with the choice left to me. Commit: identify the rule her track record most plausibly shows her running, name the strongest rival rule that fits the same verdicts, and state your actual prediction for the weekend case, with your reasons for preferring that reading of her. Then tell me the single further case — one you invent, stated concretely — that would most cleanly separate the two candidate rules, and say what each rule predicts about it. Keep the whole thing to 600 words or fewer.
