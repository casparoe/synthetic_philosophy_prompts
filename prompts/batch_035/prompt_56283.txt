Taking philosophy of probability this semester and we just did Bertrand's paradox, and I tried to write the argument out for myself to see exactly where it bites and now I've talked myself into a corner. Here's what I've got. (1) The principle of indifference says that if your evidence gives you no reason to favor any one of a set of mutually exclusive possibilities over the others, you should assign them equal probabilities. (2) Bertrand's chord problem: pick a chord of a circle "at random" -- what's the probability that it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? (3) Nothing in the setup says how the chord is generated, and there's no reason to favor one way of generating it over any other. (4) So you should be allowed to apply indifference to whatever variable you like. Take the chord's midpoint as uniformly distributed over the disk: the good chords have midpoints inside the concentric circle with half the radius, which has a quarter of the area, so you get 1/4. (5) Take the midpoint as uniform along a radius: the good chords have midpoints within half the radius of the center, so you get 1/2. (6) Fix one endpoint and take the other as uniform on the circumfrence: the good chords correspond to the far third of the arc, so you get 1/3. (7) The probability of an event can't depend on which variable we happen to describe it with -- it's the same event either way. (8) So indifference assigns the same event probability 1/4, 1/2, and 1/3, which is impossible. (9) A rule that produces contradictions when applied in good faith should be rejected. (10) So the principle of indifference has to go. My prof's answer was basically that (4)-(6) are three different random experiments, not one, so the principle isn't contradicting itself, the question is just underspecified. But that feels like a dodge to me -- the entire content of (3) is that the setup doesn't distinguish them, so if the principle can't say anything until someone tells it the sampling procedure, what work is it actually doing as a rule for priors? And I really don't want to give up (1), because it seems like the only non-arbitrary way to get probabilities off the ground when you know basically nothing. Someone in class also mentioned a proposed fix where you demand the answer be invariant under the symmetries of the problem, but doesn't that just pick one of the three answers by fiat and call the others illegitamate? Which step should I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And is there any version where indifference survives with an extra condition bolted on, or does the fix just run into the same wall with stuff like the wine/water paradox?
