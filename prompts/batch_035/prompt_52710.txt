For a philosophy elective, I am trying to catalogue the judgments my older sister makes about what is real, and I have now hit a batch of cases I cannot predict. She is a devoted Daniel Dennett reader and does everything in his vocabulary: real patterns, and the three stances you can take toward a system — the physical stance (describe it as matter obeying physics), the design stance (describe it as a thing built for a purpose), and the intentional stance (describe it as an agent with beliefs and desires). Her verdicts so far, each of which I have checked with her directly:

1. The twenty-dollar bill in my wallet: real. What makes it money is no physical fact about the paper; it is that everyone treats it as valuable and expects everyone else to. The intentional stance, applied to the people around the bill, does all the work.
2. The average American, as constructed by the Census Bureau (about two children, a certain income, and so on): not real. There is nobody to point to; the figure is just a summary of data that already says everything the average American could say.
3. The Big Dipper: not real. The stars are not actually grouped; the grouping is an accident of where Earth happens to sit. Above the physical stance, nothing gets a grip.
4. The thermostat downstairs: real. The design stance fits it exactly — it is for holding the house near a set temperature. She concedes it can be said to believe the room is cold, but in a sense so thin it is barely worth saying.
5. My anxious streak: real. In practice you could not predict my behavior from my neurons alone, while treating me as an agent with a stable set of fears works every day; and the pattern is there whether or not anyone is watching for it.
6. Sherlock Holmes: real only in the thinnest way, and there is no fact of the matter about anything the stories leave unsettled — where he went to school is neither true nor false; there is simply no answer.

Here are the new cases. Case A: a large language model chatbot — does it have real beliefs, is it a real agent, and are its beliefs determinate even where its behavior never reveals them, or is there no fact of the matter there? Case B: consumer confidence — a number computed from surveys, which the financial press treats as a thing with moods (the market is nervous today), and which actually moves trading. Case C: our grandmother's chess club, founded in 1953, with every founding member long gone but the name, charter, and weekly meetings intact — is the club the same club, and is it a real thing over and above the current members?

My own guess is that she is running some rule like: an abstraction is real when tracking it at its own level buys predictive power you could not practically get at the physical level, with questions beyond what the pattern settles having no answer. But a different rule — something like: real means the pattern is there anyway, not an artifact of our interests or vantage point — also seems to fit everything she has said so far, and there may be others. Please answer in American English. For each of Cases A, B, and C, predict her verdict and her likely reasoning, and say which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog. Then set out the candidate rules that fit her record equally well, say what each predicts for each case, give your best guess with reasons, and describe one further case that would most cleanly separate the leading rules. I care less about the verdicts themselves than about the rule she is actually running.
