I'm a novelist working on a time-travel narrative whose pivotal chapter places Aristotle, unbriefed and unannounced, at the back of a contemporary metaphysics conference panel on material constitution — the statue-and-clay problem. The chapter succeeds or fails on the credibility of his reactions, so I need a reasoned reconstruction of what he would actually think, not a costume drama in which he conveniently sides with the author. I'd be grateful for an author's brief of roughly 1,200 words structured as follows.

First, a section on what he would recognize. The contemporary debate he overhears includes: the coincidence thesis, according to which the statue and the clay are numerically distinct objects occupying exactly the same place at the same time, differing in their sortal and modal properties (the clay could survive being squashed into a ball; the statue could not); the "too many objects" objection that coincidence multiplies the world's population beyond plausibility; the deflationist reply that there is only one object there, described now under the sortal "clay" and now under the sortal "statue"; and the four-dimensionalist escape, on which the statue and the clay differ by sharing some but not all of their temporal parts. Given his hylomorphism, his account of substance in the Categories and the Metaphysics, and his doctrine that form and matter are distinct principles within a single substance, indicate which parts of this he would greet as an old friend speaking a new dialect, and which he would receive as genuinely novel.

Second, a section on where he would balk, with the objections phrased as he would put them — for instance, whether a statue is a substance in his sense at all, or an artifact of a lesser kind; whether talk of "objects" as a neutral count noun, shorn of any sortal, is even intelligible to him; and whether the coincidence theorist has simply re-described his own form/matter distinction while pretending to have outgrown it.

Third, a verdict: of the standard positions on offer — strict coincidence, four-dimensionalism, the deflationary one-object view, and sortalism about identity — which he would most plausibly adopt by the end of the panel, together with an honest note on where his own texts pull in conflicting directions, since I want the chapter to dramatize genuine uncertainty rather than an anachronistic victory.

Fourth, a short section on what he would find alien in the setting itself — the idiom of possible worlds, the vocabulary of supervenience and temporal parts, the question-and-answer format — because the chapter's comedy depends on which of these would strike him as merely unfamiliar words for familiar things and which as a genuinely different way of thinking.

Finally, five or six candidate lines of dialogue I could give him: plain modern English, recognizably his voice, each a paraphrase of a view rather than a quotation, since I don't want him reciting Greek at a microphone. Throughout, please resist the temptation to make him a sockpuppet for any side in the current debate; his position should be recognizably Aristotle's, which may mean it fits none of the panelists' categories cleanly.
