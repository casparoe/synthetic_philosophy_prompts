I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on democratic theory, and we've spent two weeks on populism and the concept of "the will of the people." For my term paper I want to work through something concrete, and I'd like your help developing the analysis before I write it up. Here's the puzzle. Referendums are often defended as the most direct way of consulting the people's will, and populist movements in particular invoke referendum results as an unmediated expression of what "the people" want. But when you look at how referendums are actually designed and administered, the practices are strange and, on the face of it, inconsistent with each other. Constitutional referendums typically require supermajorities or high turnout thresholds, while others — including ones with sweeping consequences — pass by bare majority. Some referendum outcomes are treated as final and sacred, while others get effectively re-run or renegotiated until the "right" answer arrives (as happened when EU member states whose voters initially rejected treaty ratification were asked to vote again, sometimes after the treaty was cosmetically adjusted — whereas the Brexit result was treated as irreversible and its re-running widely condemned as anti-democratic). Governments sometimes hold referendums intending to ignore a losing result they don't like, and courts sometimes annul results on procedural or substantive grounds while insisting this doesn't touch popular sovereignty. Even the sequencing is curious: the same question framed differently, or asked in a different order after other votes, seems to yield different "wills."

So my question: what theory of "the will of the people" does the actual practice of holding referendums presuppose? Does the machinery only make sense if there is a pre-political, unified, discoverable popular will — roughly the populist or Rousseauian picture — or is it equally (or better) explained on a constructivist or pluralist view, where the counting procedure partly creates the will it claims to record, and where "the people" is really many partially overlapping publics whose preferences don't aggregate into a single coherent thing? Does a supermajority requirement, for instance, presuppose that simple majorities can fail to express the real will — and if so, what expresses it better, given that the supermajority threshold is itself set by ordinary institutions? And is the practice of re-running "wrong" answers consistent with the practice of sacralizing other answers, or do both follow from one coherent (perhaps paternalistic or trustee-style) view of what referendums are for?

I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory or theories embedded in referendum practice, say clearly which parts of the practice strictly presuppose a populism-friendly metaphysics of the will and which parts are compatible with several rival theories, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — that is, where no settled view of the popular will is actually required, just as a matter of what makes the machinery work. Then close by telling me what a referendum regime would have to look like if it were deliberately designed around a pluralist, constructivist view of the popular will instead — what would change about thresholds, finality, re-runs, and who interprets the result — and whether such a regime could still be honestly marketed as "letting the people decide." Please note where you think my framing is off, if it is.
