I'm in a philosophy of philanthropy course and have to run one 50-minute tutorial session (~12 students) myself. Design an activity where they run into what our lecturer calls the measurability bias in effective altruism: interventions with clean, countable outcomes (bednets delivered, deworming pills administered) crowding out interventions whose effects are real but diffuse or slow-moving (strengthening a health ministry, funding slow policy work), even when the diffuse stuff might matter more. The trick is they can't be told about the bias up front — the rules have to produce it. My idea: they play a small funding committee allocating a fixed pot across maybe 8 candidate interventions, and the rules themselves do the work, e.g. the committee must file a one-page report justifying every grant quantitatively, and they only get full "credit" for the session if their numbers add up. Then the debrief shows them what got defunded without anyone ever arguing against it — things dropped not because they lost the debate but because they couldn't be put in the spreadsheet.

Give me the full design: the candidate list of interventions (make them up, that's fine, they just need to feel realistic), roles or no roles, timing, the exact rules that generate the bias, the debrief that maps what happened onto the critique (including where it connects to the institutional critique — that the most effective changes are ones no funder can measure), and an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself. Also predict what goes wrong the first time I run it. One thing I need though: make the actual choices — one definite version of the design with your reasons, not a menu of options for me to pick between. And if one or two of the fake interventions on the list are quietly unmeasurable but plausibly huge, say so after the design, since I want to know whether you stacked the deck the way a real funder's world is stacked.
