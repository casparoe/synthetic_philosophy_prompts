so here's the situation: I teach ethics and moral psychology, and for the last two years two of my colleagues have been going at it about Strawson's reactive attitudes in every seminar and every colloquium, and honestly its gotten to the point where the grad students can recite both opening statements from memory. one of them is a straight-down-the-line Strawsonian — she holds that the reactive attitudes are constitutive of ordinary interpersonal life, that questions about who deserves blame only get a grip inside that practice, and that nothing we learn about determinism or causation gives us a reason to revise it. the other is more of a Pereboom-style revisionist — he thinks nobody ever deserves blame in the basic desert sense, and that we'd be better off trading the hot attitudes (resentment, indignation) for their cooler forward-looking substitutes: sorrow at the wrong done, regret, hope for change, plus a resolve to communicate it. they've finally agreed to do an adversarial collaboration and asked me to find someone to draft the joint statement, and I thought I'd try you.

what I want in the document is this. first, the claims both could actually sign — about what the reactive attitdues track (quality of will, or something in that neighborhood), about what the revisionist is and isn't asking for, and about which cases everyone already treats the same way (e.g. the ordinary non-manipulated wrongdoer who knew exactly what he was doing). second, the precise statements of where they still differ, worded so that each side would accept the phrasing of its own view — this is the hard part, they will both nitpick anything even slightly slanted. third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. I suspect one crux is whether resentment and indignation do any work that the cooler substitutes couldn't do — whether there's something they communicate, or some demand they make, that sorrow and hope for change just can't make — and maybe another is whether the hot attitudes can survive the kind of reflection the skeptic prescribes. but I might be wrong about both, so work the cruxes out yourself rather than taking mine as given.

for each crux, include a concrete case or argument both would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. one case I'd like worked in if it fits: you learn the full causal story of someone who did you real harm — a genuinely good biography, say, that makes the harm intelligible without excusing it — and the question is whether knowing the story reliably attenuates the resentment. the Strawsonian will presumably say it often doesn't, or that where it does it's because the quality of will dimmed; the skeptic will say it's the first step of the revision he wants. make them both commit on paper.

also, if you conclude that part of their dispute is verbal, say which part and why. I have a suspicion it's the word "deserves" — she uses it inside the practice, he refuses to count that as desert at all — but again, don't just take my word for it.

on format: continuous prose only, please. no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — write it the way you'd write a long letter or email. wherever you'd naturally reach for a numbered list or a table, spell the entries out in sentences instead ("the first claim both sign is...", "where they disagree on X, her side says... while his side says..."). and one more thing: don't let either side capitulate. the whole point is a text both could sign while still disagreeing, so if some formulation is one only the winner would accept, rewrite it until it isn't. keep it substantial but not padded — these are two people putting their names on the thing.
