I spent thirty years as a biochemist, most of them on gene regulation, and now in retirement I have been working through the philosophy of biology, which brings me to a controversy I watched unfold near the end of my career. In September 2012, the ENCODE consortium published some thirty coordinated papers in Nature reporting that roughly 80% of the human genome shows some form of biochemical activity — transcription factor binding, DNase hypersensitivity, histone marks, transcription of non-coding regions — and the accompanying press release was widely reported as having demolished the old idea of "junk DNA." Evolutionary geneticists responded with an unusually hostile counterattack: they charged that the consortium had defined a functional element so loosely, as any locus with detectable biochemical activity, that the definition amounted to doing biology without evolution by natural selection; that activity of this kind is largely the noise of polymerases and binding proteins sampling the landscape, the residue of dead transposons, and selfish elements marking their territory; and that on comparative evidence, only something like a tenth of the genome, at most, bears the signature of purifying selection, the rest being free to drift. The rhetoric on both sides was intemperate, and I confess I found the whole affair embarrassing at the time; it has only recently struck me that it might be a live instance of something the philosophers had already mapped.

Here is the analysis I want. Treat the episode as a collision between the adaptationist programme that Gould and Lewontin attacked in their 1979 spandrels paper and the alternative picture they urged — constraint, drift, exaptation, and the rest of their kit of non-adaptationist explanations. Be disciplined about the mapping. Say explicitly who, in this episode, is playing the Panglossian they derided: is it ENCODE, reading function into every observed activity, or their critics, who arrived with a selection-based notion of function as though it were an operational definition rather than a theoretical commitment? Then say where the spandrels categories actually fit: is the ~90% of the genome apparently free from constraint the genomic analogue of a spandrel, in their sense of a non-adapted by-product later available for co-opted use, or does that analogy misfire, and if so, where — for instance, in that a spandrel in their sense is a by-product of an adaptive structure, whereas a dead transposon is a by-product of a parasitic element with its own evolutionary agenda? And then run the analysis in the other direction, as I gather a good case study must: the critics plainly won the exchange on points, but did they win by making a falsifiable prediction the consortium's framework could not accommodate, or mainly by definition-fighting over the word "function"? What does that outcome show about the spandrels critique itself — whether a programme whose explanatory resources are non-adaptations (drift, constraint, exaptation) can ever be caught out by evidence in the way adaptationism can, or whether it is structurally unfalsifiable in exactly the way Gould and Lewontin accused the adaptationists of being?

I want the analysis to end by saying honestly where the spandrels framework is silent about this episode, rather than forcing it to speak: if, for example, the framework says nothing about how to apportion credence between "biochemically active but non-functional" and "functional but weakly selected," say so plainly. Please lead with the bottom line in your very first sentence, and give the reasoning after it, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked.
