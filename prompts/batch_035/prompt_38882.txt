I'm a philosophy PhD student who just finished TA-ing the upper-level metaphysics course this semester, and the professor has asked me to draft the final-exam essay question on essentialism — specifically origin essentialism, the question of whether I could have come from a different sperm and egg. I want to be honest that my motives are partly personal. Two months ago, while filling out my own fertility-clinic paperwork, my mother finally told me the full story of my conception: I was IVF, the kind where several embryos are created from a single cycle. One was transferred and became me; another from the same batch is now my younger sister. And shortly after transfer, mine split, which is why I also have a monozygotic twin brother. Since then I haven't been able to stop thinking about the fact that my twin and I seem to share a single origin — one zygote, one sperm, one egg — and yet he is not me, while my sister shares my parents but not my zygote. The question "would the child from the other embryo have been me?" has stopped feeling like a seminar curiosity, and Kripke's passing suggestion, in the footnotes to Naming and Necessity, that someone who developed from a different sperm and egg would simply not have been that person, has started to feel like it's making claims about my family.

What I want from you is the following. Please take the role of a metaphysician who has taught this material for a decade and writes exam questions that cannot be answered by reciting the lecture slides — think through where the real crux lies before you draft anything. My own sense is that the crux is threefold: first, the ambiguity between reading "I could have had different origins" as the claim that this very person could have had them, versus the much weaker claim that someone very similar to me could have existed with different origins; second, whether origin essentialism is a substantive metaphysical discovery about persons or an artifact of how proper names function; and third, whether the standard argument proves too much — if this exact zygote is essential to me, what about the day of conception, the petri dish, the particular winning sperm? Your exam question must explicitly build in my two cases — same zygote yielding two people, and same parents yielding a different zygote — rather than the generic sperm-and-egg version, so that a student cannot coast on memorized labels.

Produce exactly three deliverables: (1) a single essay question answerable in sixty minutes, closed-book; (2) a model answer of no more than 600 words that a genuinely strong student could plausibly have written under exam conditions — not a literature survey, and no citations, since I want reasoning rather than name-dropping; and (3) a marking scheme of five must-hit points worth 4 marks each, 20 total, with a one-line note on how students typically botch each point, and a closing sentence stating what specifically separates an A answer from a B answer. The entire package — question, model answer, and marking scheme together — must come in under 1,000 words; please be ruthless about cutting anything that doesn't bear directly on the crux.
