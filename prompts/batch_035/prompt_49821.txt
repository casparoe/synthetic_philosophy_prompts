I'm working on a long-ish essay (5,000 words maybe, for the kind of general audience that reads pop history but would tolerate some actual archaeology) arguing that "collapse" is a bad concept and that what we label collapses are mostly reorganizations that only look like failures from the top down. My core claim is that there's a selection effect baked into the sources: the evidence we use to detect and date collapse — palace archives, monumental inscriptions, administrative records, prestige goods — is precisely the evidence that only central institutions produce. So when a society decentralizes, its record-keeping dies, and archaeologists inherit a world where the elites have gone quiet and infer that everything went with them. The texts stop, so we say the civilization stopped. My working title is something like "Collapse is an artifact of literacy," which I already suspect is too cute.

The case studies I'm planning to hang this on are the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean (the palaces burn, Linear B disappears, but village life and craft production carry on, and people keep trading obsidian and copper like nothing happened at the top), the Classic Maya "collapse" (the southern lowlands cities go quiet in the 9th-10th centuries while Chichen Itza and the northern lowlands are doing fine, which makes "the Maya collapsed" look more like "half the Maya world reorganized"), and Easter Island, where the self-inflicted-ecocide story everyone knows rests heavily on late-stage moai carving, oral traditions collected centuries after the fact, and now researchers arguing that rats ate the palm forest more than people did. In all three I want to make the same move: the label "collapse" tracks the loss of centralized, literate, monumental culture, and only sometimes tracks anything like a fall in population or welfare — and even when it does, probably a much smaller fall than the word implies.

What I'd love your comments on. First, is the selection-effect point actually right, or am I overreaching — has someone in archaeology obviously made this argument already, and is there a standard rebuttal I'd be embarrassed not to address? Second, does the Maya case really support me, or is there decent evidence for genuinely large-scale depopulation in the southern lowlands that I'd be conveniently downplaying? Third, a friend who's a demographer keeps pushing back: if a region loses most of its population, that's a collapse under any reasonable definition, and "welfare didn't fall much" is doing alot of unearned work — is she right that I'm smuggling in a pretty elite-friendly standard of what counts as a loss? And finally, more big-picture: is the essay as sketched actually interesting, or is it just contrarian erasure? I do NOT want to end up writing "actually everything was fine," since some of these episodes really were catastrophes for alot of ordinary people — but I worry the framing I've chosen quietly pushes me in that direction, and I'd rather hear about that now than after I've drafted the whole thing.
