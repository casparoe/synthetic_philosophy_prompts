I write literary thrillers, and a recurring craft problem has led me into a corner of epistemology I can't find my way out of. The problem: when may my narrator say "she knew"? A detective in chapter one can "know" the husband did it on far thinner evidence than a character in a chapter-ten courtroom would need, and readers accept the shift without complaint — what a fictional person can be said to know seems to depend on what's at stake for her and on how much the surrounding prose has raised the temperature. Researching this, I wandered into the debate between epistemic contextualists, who say that the content of "knows" itself shifts with conversational context — this is where the famous bank cases come in — and the proponents of pragmatic encroachment or interest-relative invariantism, who say the meaning of "knows" stays fixed but whether a subject counts as knowing varies with her practical stakes; and then there are the relativists, following MacFarlane, who relocate the shift to the context of assessment rather than the speaker's or the subject's. What strikes me as an outsider is that all three camps appeal to essentially the same evidence — bank deposits, lottery tickets, the airport case, and the shifting intuitions of ordinary speakers — and appear to have reached a stable equilibrium in which everyone knows everyone else's moves and no one has budged in years. My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the dispute is not really about knowledge at all but about what kind of data ordinary knowledge attributions constitute — whether the intuitions elicited by these cases are evidence about semantics, about knowledge, or merely about conversational propriety — so that underneath a metaphysical-sounding disagreement lies a methodological one about how much weight philosophy of language should carry in epistemology. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a genuine substantive dispute here that the standard cases simply lack the power to resolve?

Please answer as a professional epistemologist with a working command of the philosophy of language literature. Think carefully before committing to a diagnosis, and weigh whether the disagreement might instead be verbal, or a case of the sides answering different questions under one heading, or simply hard. 2. **Diagnosis** (~400 words) — the kind of disagreement this really is, supported by what each side says rather than by labels.
3. **What would count as progress** (~200 words) — a distinction, an experimental result, a formal semantic proposal, or an argument that no progress should be expected.
4. **A note for the novelist** (~100 words) — whether any of this should actually change how I use "she knew."
