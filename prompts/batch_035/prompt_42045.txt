I'm co-teaching a seminar on theories of well-being, and our unit on hedonism ended somewhere genuinely unresolved. The taste of cold water on a hot day, the hour of hard argument, the second wind on a long walk, the quiet after a dreaded phone call finally happens — the students accepted that these are all pleasures, but every candidate we tried for what they share either turned out to be a feature of one subclass masquerading as the essence (a bodily "feel" that fits thirst but not a proof), or was so thin ("they're all wanted while present") that it couldn't distinguish pleasure from money or fame. I want to close the unit with a poem rather than a summary, because the honest summary is that we don't know.

Write a sestina: six sestets with the standard rotation of end-words (6-1-5-2-4-3), then a three-line envoi containing all six end-words in the usual two-in-the-middle, one-at-the-end arrangement. The six end-words must name, or clearly evoke, six pleasures of importantly different kinds — at least one bodily, one intellectual or contemplative, one social, and one that is mostly relief, the lifting of something rather than the arrival of anything positive; the remaining two are yours, but choose them to widen the spread rather than double up on a category already present.

Here is the philosophical work I want the form to do. The word "pleasant" — and its obvious synonyms — must not appear anywhere in the poem, including the envoi, since the existence of that property is exactly what's in dispute and the poem may not quietly help itself to it. Instead, let the sestina's machinery carry the thought: the same six end-words recur through every stanza, in each one rearranged by the permutation, so that the reader is continuously given the experience of a recurring pattern while the content never supplies a recurring property — recurrence without essence, which is the whole problem, embodied. Each stanza should read as though some candidate unifier is about to surface, and the rotation into the next stanza should quietly take it apart again. The envoi is the one place the poem may look up and acknowledge the question directly, and it should end holding both live options open — that the six share a single felt quality we happen to be unable to describe, and that they share nothing but overlapping resemblance, with the shape of a category and no core — without choosing between them.

The audience is a group that has spent three weeks on whether pleasures differ only in quantity, so they should recognize the problem without a footnote; but someone who knows none of it should simply read a good sestina. No philosophical vocabulary beyond what the images can carry, and no lecturing: the form is the argument.
