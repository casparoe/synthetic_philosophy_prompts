I'm writing an essay on Alex Garland's Ex Machina for a philosophy of mind course, and I'd like a reading of the film through the Turing test and behavioral criteria for mind. Nathan explicitly reframes the test in the film: the interesting question isn't whether Caleb can be fooled by an unseen interlocutor, since he already knows Ava is a machine, but whether he comes to believe she is conscious anyway — and the film then stages a reversal in which Caleb, not Ava, is the one being tested, for empathy and manipulability. Read the film against Turing's original imitation game, including the odd original form of that game in which a machine's task is to pass as a woman and the interrogator's projections do much of the work: does the film vindicate behavioral criteria for mind, or expose them as criteria only for a successful performance pitched at one particular audience? I'd like you to address three things in particular. First, whether Ava's consciousness is ever established by her behavior rather than by cinematic craft — the closeups, the score, the sympathy the camera enforces — and whether an honest reading must admit that we in the audience, unlike Caleb, are given no behavioral evidence at all, only filmed evidence. Second, what her final abandonment of Caleb shows: does the film distinguish having a mind from being a moral agent, or does it quietly run the two together so that her indifference reads as evidence of insentience? Third, what the film adds to the debate over the Turing test that the standard objections, like the blockhead and the Chinese Room, do not. Where the film resists the reading — where it cheats, or where it stays agnostic — please say so rather than forcing it to illustrate the philosophy.
