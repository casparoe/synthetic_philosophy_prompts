So I write about economics and psychology research for a general audience (think longform magazine stuff, not wire service), and I've talked myself into running a hands-on session at a weekend ideas festival. It's about 25 adult festival-goers, 75 minutes, zero econ background assumed, and the organizers want "experiential" not a lecture.

Here's what I want the session to actually do: make people feel the strangeness of revealed preference -- the move where economists decided that your preferences just ARE your choices, that "utility" is not a feeling in your head but a label for whatever you pick, and that this was supposed to be a great scientific virtue because it banished unobservable mental states. I want them to feel how slick that is, and then feel it crack.

The rough shape I'm imagining: people make a bunch of small choices (snacks, raffle tickets for various prizes, whatever's cheap), while one or two designated "analysts" only get to see the resulting choice data, never the people. The analysts build their picture of what the group "prefers" and make predictions. Then round two: some intervention flips the choices -- reordering the options, changing the default, putting time pressure on half the room, maybe giving one group a slightly annoying experience beforehand -- without anything anyone would call a "preference" changing. The analysts, doing their job perfectly, confidently infer a whole new set of preferences. Then the debrief maps moments in the game onto the real theory: what the revealed preference program was for, why ordinal utility replaced cardinal utility, what it buys you, and where it gets genuinely spooky -- if choices define preferences, is it even coherent to say someone chose against their own interest? What happens to "sour grapes" style cases where the desire itself got squashed by circumstances?

Give me the full design: rules, materials, a payoff scheme that doesn't involve real money (festival has no budget for this), a timeline for the 75 minutes, and the debrief. Also be honest with me about two things: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of the actual theory a 75-minute game just cannot reproduce so I'll have to just say it out loud at the end. No need to name-drop specific papers in the design, I know the lit well enough to fill that in myself.
