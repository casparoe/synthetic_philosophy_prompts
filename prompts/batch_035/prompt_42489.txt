I teach an upper-level seminar on ethics of mind that mixes philosophy students with CS students who have actually built neural networks — about 14 people, one 90-minute session. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel the specific shape of the digital minds problem: not the science-fiction version, but the boring institutional version, where you have to commit to a policy about entities whose experience is permanently unobservable from the outside.

My idea — feel free to restructure it if you have something better — is that they constitute a welfare review board for simulated systems. They receive dossiers of maybe six or seven hypothetical systems I'll prepare in advance, each described in mixed physical/behavioral/architectural terms. The dossiers should be constructed so that the plausible grounds of moral status deliberately cross-cut: a conversational system that reports distress fluently but whose training objective was pure next-token prediction with no reward component for self-representation; a reinforcement-learning agent with a persistent valuation state and physiological-analogue stress signals that barely communicates at all; a whole-brain-emulation-style system with verified architectural fidelity to a rat but no expressive output; a model whose training deliberately optimized for appearing to suffer during evaluation because suffering-looking outputs were upweighted by raters; and so on. I want the class to have to write, as a board, a licensing rubric — which systems get welfare protections, at what cost ceiling, under what burden of proof — and I want them to hit the point where they realize they cannot agree on whether they're disagreeing about the evidence in the dossiers or about what kind of fact would even count as evidence.

The activity needs a payoff scheme that doesn't use real money but creates real stakes: I'm imagining an asymmetric scoring rule the class discovers only after they've committed — false negatives cost orders of magnitude more than false positives — so they run into the precautionary structure of the problem by watching their own rubric get evaluated, not by being told about it beforehand. They should feel the reductio on the other side too: if the asymmetry is large enough, a rational board licenses everything, including systems nobody in the room thinks has any inner life, and the rubric collapses into something a colleague would call obviously insane. Ideally the rubric-writing and the collapse happen in the first hour, and there's a second round where they can revise — because I want them to experience the difference between the first, confident draft and the post-collapse draft where every clause is a hedged compromise nobody fully endorses.

Please specify: the timeline minute by minute, how to split the 14 into roles or subcommittees, what the dossiers need to contain so the crossings actually work, exactly how the scoring rule is revealed, and the debrief structure that maps the activity's moments onto the parts of the philosophical problem — the asymmetry of errors, moral uncertainty versus ordinary factual uncertainty, and the way the "what would even count as evidence" question reappears inside every apparently factual dispute about the dossiers.

Two further requirements. First, predict what will go wrong the first time I run this: my CS students will try to settle it with architecture talk, the philosophers with definitional talk, and I suspect the whole thing can deadlock without a forcing mechanism — tell me how to prevent that without smuggling in my own view of which systems count. Second, be honest about the feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce. My worry is that the activity stipulates a ground truth (I know which dossiers "really" have experience because I wrote them), which is exactly what real moral life never provides, so the exercise may quietly train the wrong instinct — that there is a fact of the matter being scored correctly or incorrectly, rather than a fact of the matter we can never check. If that's unavoidable, tell me what I must supply in the debrief to undo it.
