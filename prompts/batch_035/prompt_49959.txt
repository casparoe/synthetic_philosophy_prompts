I direct the introductory philosophy sequence at a large state university: I write the lecture notes, four graduate teaching assistants run the discussion sections, and roughly six hundred students a year treat those notes as authoritative, including for the exams on which my TAs and I grade them. Over a semester the notes accumulate several hundred discrete factual and interpretive claims — dates, definitions, attributions of arguments, renderings of technical terms, sentences of the form "X held that p." I check each claim as I write it, but I also know, in the way every author knows, that some small number of them are wrong, and every year at least one student finds one. What I want from you is a decision procedure, grounded in whatever epistemology actually has to say about the lottery and preface paradoxes, that my TAs and I can run on any individual claim before it goes into the notes: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; where the procedure ends with "assert it flatly"; where it ends with "hedge or attribute"; and where it explicitly stops and hands the decision back to a person because the question is no longer epistemic — how much hedging students will tolerate before they stop trusting any sentence at all is a pedagogical judgment, not a probability. The procedure should say what to do when we cannot attach anything like a number to a claim, and it should be honest about which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

The procedure also has to respect the central lesson of the paradoxes, which is that each claim in the notes can be individually well-supported while their conjunction is something I should positively disbelieve. So I need two things, not one: a per-claim test, and a policy for the aggregate — whether to run a standing disclaimer in the syllabus, whether such a disclaimer coheres with continuing to assert each item flatly, and what, if anything, I owe the students who lose exam points in the meantime because they faithfully repeated an error we never caught. For the constraints: claims arise during lecture prep at a rate of roughly twenty to forty per week; I have perhaps two minutes per claim; the TAs have one year of graduate training and no background in formal epistemology; and I alone sign off on the exam key, which must stay consistent with the notes.

Once you have laid out the procedure, run it on three cases. (a) A date of composition for a historical text that the standard textbook gives one way but that a minority of specialists dispute. (b) An interpretive claim in an area outside my specialty — say, a sentence about what some medieval philosopher meant by a technical term — that I have carried from a single secondary source I read years ago and never checked against the primary text. (c) A definitional claim I put on the exam key three years ago and have reused every year since, which a student has now challenged with a passage from the primary text that appears to point the other way. I want you to flag honestly where the procedure will misfire and what it cannot settle; and I should warn you that in case (c) I fully expect to dislike its output, since the claim is already printed on three years of past exams. Tell me what it says anyway. Please write your answer in American English.
