I'm a novelist deep into a literary novel where a seventeen-year-old, Mia, has been declared brain dead after a car crash, and late one night an organ procurement coordinator — an older nurse, very practiced, a bit tired — has to sit the parents down in the family room and get them to genuinely understand what brain dead means before the donation ask is even on the table. I've done some shadowing in an ICU for research so I have the texture of the room, the coffee, the horrible carpet, but what I don't have is the conceptual spine of the scene, and I don't want to fake it. What I need from you isn't dialogue, it's a plan of the explanation itself: the order the pieces have to come in for these particular parents (does she establish death first and the machine second, or explain the machine and then take it away?), the one carrying example the coordinator would reach for — I've come across the light-switch analogy in my research but also clinicians warning that some of these analogies backfire, so tell me what the example has to have and what it has to lack — and, most useful for me, the misconceptions the parents will arive with or form in real time, in the order they'll actually surface them.

I can guess at some: brain dead sounds to laypeople like a very bad coma, so they'll be half-listening for when she wakes up; the chest is still rising because of the ventilator, her skin is warm and pink, so every sense the parents have is telling them she's alive; and at some point somebody probably asks about the news story of a brain dead person who supposedly recovered, or whether she can feel pain, or whether she'll feel anything during the retrieval. Where in the sequence should each of those get absorbed — is it better if the coordinator preempts the warm-and-pink problem before the parents can even say it, or only answers when asked, and what does that choice do to who has power in the room? Also, what should she flat-out decline to explain at that moment: the whole-brain versus brainstem definitional stuff, the fact that the very concept was partly built around transplant machinery, the countries that define it differently — I'd love all of that to be subtext the reader senses and the character never says out loud, but tell me if any piece of it has to be said for the parents' decision to be honest. And how would she check at the end whether it actually landed — is there a question she can ask whose answer tells me, as the novelist, whether the parents understood the fact itself or just submitted to the authority of the person saying it?

One more thing, since this is really what the chapter turns on: at the very end I want the mother to ask, quietly, so the machine is the only thing keeping her heart beating — if it's keeping the heart, then what is it that's dead. I need to know whether that's a question the scene can answer honestly in the coordinator's own terms, or whether I should let it hang there, because I suspect the honest version is that it hangs.
