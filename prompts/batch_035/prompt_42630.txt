I'm a Bayesian statistician. My daily vocabulary is built around generative models: sampling procedures, reparameterization, non-identifiability, invariance and equivariance under group actions, maximum-entropy distributions, and default priors such as the uniform and Jeffreys priors. I have no formal training in philosophy, and I know Bertrand's paradox only as a folk theorem — "if you ask for a random chord in a circle, the answer depends on how you draw it" — and the principle of indifference only as a vague rumor that it was once supposed to justify flat priors.

Please translate the classical debate over Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report.

1. Restate the principle of indifference in generative-modeling terms. It sounds to me like a claim that symmetry constraints on a family of distributions determine a unique distribution — something like "if the likelihood is invariant under a group action, the prior should be the invariant measure on the group's orbits." Is that what the principle actually says, or is it stronger or weaker? Where it's stronger, say exactly what the extra claim is.

2. Restate each of the classical answers to Bertrand's question — the chord problem — as a fully specified sampling procedure: what is being drawn, from what measure, and under what parameterization. My working hypothesis is that the "paradox" is nothing but an underspecified generative model: three answers to one question because one question masks three different experiments. Is that hypothesis correct as a translation, or does it quietly assume something the classical debate disputed?

3. Address the standard statistical rejoinders within my vocabulary and then say whether they capture the philosophical positions. For instance: the Jeffreys prior as a reparameterization-invariant "objective" choice; the maximum-entropy distribution subject to stated constraints; the view that any prior is fine because it's just an assumption to be stated explicitly and updated. Which philosophical positions do these map onto, and do they dissolve the original debate or merely relocate it?

4. Finally, the residue report. Tell me: which parts of the classical debate translate cleanly into my vocabulary; which parts translate only with distortion, and what the distortion costs; and which parts have no expression at all in terms of generative models, measures, and invariance. In particular, I want your view on whether the inexpressible residue points to something my vocabulary is missing — e.g., a notion of rational credence that makes sense before any sampling procedure is specified — or instead to something in the original debate that was less clear than it looked, so that my vocabulary's refusal to express it is a feature rather than a gap.

Please be exact about the direction of translation: do not import assumptions of my framework (e.g., that every prior is ultimately an explicit modeling choice, or that rational credence just is a distribution in some model) and present them as findings about the philosophical positions. If a classical position would reject one of those assumptions, flag the rejection explicitly rather than laundering it through the translation.
