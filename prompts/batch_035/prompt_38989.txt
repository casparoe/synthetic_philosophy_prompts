---

The metaphor goes back to Plato: a skilled carver cuts where the structure already divides the animal, rather than hacking through the middle of a bone. Saying the sciences carve nature at its joints commits us to the following: some scientific groupings are not just convenient for us but answer to real structure in the world, so that what falls under a category is not up to our purposes or interests. Locke already believed this: he held that our word gold refers by tracking the real essence of gold, and this real essence is what chemists later identified as atomic number 79. Putnam and Kripke worked out the semantics on this pattern: a kind term's reference is fixed by samples and a causal chain rather than by a description, so discoveries about underlying structure can change what the term applies to without changing what we meant by it. This is why induction over natural kinds is safe: every sample of gold conducts electricity, and that is no coincidence, since the conductivity flows from the same microstructure.

The strongest challenge comes from biology. Darwin showed that species shade gradually into one another, and modern genetics confirms that there is no genetic property possessed by every member of a species and by no member of any other species: every candidate property is either shared with other species or absent in some members of the species itself. So species, the most central categories of biology, do not have essences.

Hull and Ghiselin answered this by arguing that species are natural kinds with historical essences: what makes something a member of a particular fruit fly species is not its genes but its place in a specific lineage. Boyd would add that kinds need not have strict essences at all: a kind can be a homeostatic property cluster, held together by causal mechanisms that keep its members similar, which seems right to me.

Still, on reflection these replies fail. Calling a lineage a kind with a historical essence is just renaming the fact that species are genealogical individuals, and individuals are not kinds. And a cluster without an essence is just a resemblance class, and resemblance comes in degrees. Since species lack essences, they are not natural kinds after all. And if the most central categories of a paradigm science are conventional rather than joint-carving, we can conclude that the sciences generally do not carve nature at its joints; the only genuine natural kinds are the chemical elements.
