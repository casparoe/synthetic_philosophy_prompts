I'm a retired geologist, spent 35 years dating rocks and arguing about isotope ratios, and now that I've got time on my hands I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole. At a dinner party last month the conversation turned to a friend's run of terrible luck, and somebody offered the old line 'what goes around comes around' -- the idea being that since he'd been a bit of a jerk in his divorce years earlier, the universe was now settling the score. When I pushed back a little (occupational habit, I interrogate claims for a living), another guest said the karma was just obvious, that even a scientist should accept that actions have consequences, and that was that.

Here's what bugged me, and I'd like your help untangling it. That one sentence -- 'what goes around comes around' -- seems to be doing a lot of different jobs at once, and I can't tell which claim my dinner companions were actually committed to. My hunch is that at least one version of it is trivially true, at least one is substantive but probably false, and at least one is unfalsifiable in a way that would make Karl Popper spin in his grave, but I'm not confident I've carved it up right.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that could be hiding in this slogan? Off the top of my head there seems to be a spectrum, something like: (a) a platitude about ordinary social causation -- treat people badly and they'll reciprocate, gossip spreads, nobody wants to help you move; (b) a claim about internal states -- guilt and resentment do their own accounting, the cheater's peace of mind suffers even with no external sanction; (c) a genuine metaphysical thesis -- a law-like regularity in the universe, on par with gravity, that rewards and punishes conduct across time and maybe even across lives; and (d) something in between, a theodicy-shaped claim that the world is morally ordered even though we can't see the mechanism. But I might be missing readings, or some of these might collapse into each other, and I honestly don't know which ones are actually part of karma as the tradition understands it versus pop-culture versions that drifted in later.

For each reading you lay out, I'd want three verdicts: is it trivially true, substantive-but-contested, or plain false; would a natural scientist have any principled reason (as opposed to a prejudiced one) to accept or reject it; and, crucially, which reading was my dinner companion actually invoking when she used the slogan to explain my friend's bad luck? Because the ordinary-social-causation version does nothing to explain his situation -- everyone involved in the original mess has moved away, his current troubles have nothing to do with those people -- so if that's the version she meant, the explanation was a non sequitur. But if she meant the metaphysical law, then I want to know what, if anything, could count as evidence for it, and whether the fact that we never observe anyone's ledger being settled in this life forces the reading to go trans-life (reincarnation) to stay alive.

Also, if you could say a word about where the burden of proof should sit here -- is it on me to show the world isn't morally ordered, or on her to show it is -- I'd appreciate it. That got heated at the table too, and I'd like to be better armed next time it comes up. Keep it in plain language please, I'm new to this; I've only just learned what a 'conditional' is.
