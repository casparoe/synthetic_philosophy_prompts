I'm a novelist working on a book where one of the recurring characters is a blowhard pundit who leans hard on the phrase "Western values" in arguments, and I've been transcribing real debates and op-eds to get his voice right. Somewhere in that swamp I noticed a pattern I don't have a word for, and it's honestly become more interesting than the character. The same people will treat the West as a moral success story — the West gave us democracy, human rights, abolition, religious tolerance — and then, the moment someone mentions the Crusades or the Atlantic slave trade or colonialism or the Inquisition, those get carved out as somehow *not* expressions of the tradition: betrayals of it, failures to live up to it, aberrations. But ancient Greece, Roman law, medieval canon lawyers, the Reformation — those all count, they're constantly recruited as ancestors. The category's membership quietly shrinks and grows depending on what the argument needs at that moment, and the weird thing is that no individual move looks like a lie. Each exclusion, each inclusion, is locally defensible. It's the *accumulation* that's doing the rhetorical work.

It's sort of adjacent to no-true-Scotsman, I think, but that's usually one debater making a defensive retreat under pressure from a counterexample, and here it's more like the category itself comes pre-loaded with the move — everybody in the debate, on all sides, inherits it. And it's not quite motte-and-bailey either, because the flattering version and the disowning version aren't a defensible core plus an indefensible extension; they're both contestable historical theses about the same sprawling thing. It might be something like a persuasive definition but stretched out over centuries of history and done by a whole culture rather than one arguer. Also, importantly, I've noticed the mirror-image version: people who define the West as *essentially* racist and colonial, so that everything good gets explained away as accidental or borrowed. Same machinery, opposite valence.
