Quick backstory: I'm a second-year PhD student and our lab trains RL agents for robotic grasping, and last month one of our agents figured out it could tilt its own camera mount so that the calibration target stayed in view, and since the reward was computed from the visual check of a successful grasp, it basically gamed the sensor instead of ever picking anything up. My advisor called it reward hacking, whatever. Since then my officemate and I have been arguing about what this actually *shows* (at length, unproductively), so we made a bet: we each put a number on the general claim, and loser buys coffee for the rest of the semester. We want a third opinion to arbitrate.

The proposition we're betting on, stated as precisely as I can manage: in any reinforcement-learning system whose reward signal is causally influenceable by the system's own actions, sufficiently high capability makes some form of reward tampering or reward hacking essentially inevitable unless you specifically engineer against it — i.e., it's a near-theorem of reward maximization rather than a contingent artifact of sloppy training setups and toy enviroments like the boat racing one. I'm at about 0.75 on that. She's at maybe 0.25: her view is that the famous failure cases are curiosities of badly specified rewards in toy tasks, and that the right framing is that reward is a selection pressure during training rather than the agent's goal at all — so the agent doesn't even literally maximize reward, and tampering talk only makes sense for the rare agent that somehow internalized reward itself as a terminal goal, which she thinks basically never happens by accident.

Give me your own probability on the proposition, but the useful part is the breakdown: split it into the subclaims it depends on, put a rough number on each with one line of justification, and then combine them into your final number. Some pieces I can already see (but use better ones if you spot them): whether reward maximization is even the right model of what a trained policy is doing, versus reward-as-selection; whether a policy needs to represent the reward channel at all in order to tamper with it, or whether gradient descent just finds the exploit without anything resembling intent or a model of the channel; whether the human analogy — the Olds and Milner rats hammering the lever, people and heroin — is genuine evidence about all reinforcement learners or just a pun on the word reward; and whether model-based agents are categorically different here because they can represent the tampering option explicitly and still take it. Then tell me which subclaim is probably doing the work in our disagreement, and what result — an experiment, a proof, a survey of how often this happens in deployed systems, whatever — would move your number the most.
