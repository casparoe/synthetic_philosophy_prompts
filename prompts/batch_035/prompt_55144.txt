Been reading about inverse reinforcement learning stuff and it's made me go back through my own gut reactions to when you can read a preference off someone's behavior, and honestly my reactions feel kind of inconsistent, so help me out. Here's a bunch of cases and what I think.

A guy at work takes the stairs every single day even though the elevator works fine -- I feel pretty confident he prefers the exercise. Same guy checks his phone at literally every red light but not at green ones -- I don't think I want to infer he has a preference for checking his phone specifically at red lights. My sister complains every time about the Thai place but keeps being the one to suggest it -- revealed preference folks would say she likes it, but I actually think the complaint is the real signal and the behavior is just laziness about finding new restaurants. A friend who's flat broke nonetheless pays double for organic eggs -- that one-off expensive choice tells me more about her values than a hundred cheap ones, somehow. A smoker who says he wants to quit and buys another pack -- I don't think the purchase is evidence of a "true" preference for smoking at all, addiction just breaks the inference. And here's the one I keep flip flopping on: a guy who always takes the same weird zigzag route home, which is actually slower, because he refuses to use the GPS that once embarrassed him or whatever -- his behavior is perfectly consistent over months, consistency being the thing I trusted in the stairs case, but I still don't want to say he prefers that route.

I had a tentative principle, roughly: the more it would cost you to do the thing if you didn't want it, the more the behavior tells you, plus you need the behavior to be repeated enough that it's not noise. But that seems to mess up: the organic eggs are a one-off and cheap for a rich person but still super informative, and the stairs case has high cost and repetition and works, but so does the smoker (smokes every day, it costs him money and health) and there I say inference doesn't go through at all. So maybe there's a rationality gate in front of everything, like the behavior only counts as evidence if the person is roughly acting on their beliefs about how to get what they want, and addiction and spite and habit are all ways of failing that gate? But then the zigzag guy IS acting on his beliefs about how to get what he wants (he hates GPS), and I still don't want to call it a preference.

Is there a single principle that gets all my verdicts, or close? If you have to overturn one of them, which and why? And tell me what the principle says about a case I didn't list, like a robot learning from a drunk person's demonstrations, or whether you can ever infer a preference for something the agent has never actually gotten to experience success at (say, someone who keeps applying for the same kind of job they've never been offered). Also curious whether this ends up being basically the same problem IRL people have with needing a model of human irrationality, or whether the everyday version is genuinely different.
