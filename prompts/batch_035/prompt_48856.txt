I've got a draft essay for our department's philosophy society blog thing (mixed audience — a few people who know Scanlon properly, mostly people who did one ethics course) and I want comments before I send it round. The essay is on contractualism and aggregation, arguing for a move I haven't seen anywhere but which feels like it must exist somewhere, so one thing I want from you is whether I've accidentally reinvented someone else's view.

Here's the draft in compressed form:

**Para 1 (setup).** Contractualism as I present it: an act is wrong if it would be permitted by no principle that no one could reasonably reject. The individualist restriction: the strength of anyone's objection to a principle is fixed by how that principle bears on them taken individually — you can't add up lots of people's small burdens into one big complaint. Hence the aggregation problem: contractualism seems to say you may impose one death rather than any number of minor headaches, which strikes most people as badly wrong.

**Para 2 (the two-sided evidence).** On the one hand, transmitter-room style cases (one person bears something terrible so millions can watch a broadcast) — we side with contractualism's answer. On the other hand, in rescue cases numbers obviously matter, and contractualism handles that by saying each of the fifty has an individual complaint of being allowed to die, as strong as the one person's. So numbers matter within a kind of burden but not between kinds. Fine so far, and fairly standard.

**Para 3 (my move).** The standard reading treats the restriction as a ban on adding across persons. I argue the deeper reading is a ban on adding across *kinds* of burden. Complaints of the same kind can be summed (that's how rescue works); but a complaint below a certain severity can never be converted, by sheer numbers, into a complaint of a higher kind. Call it the kind-matching rule. Non-aggregation isn't an extra axiom bolted on, it falls out of what kinds of complaint there are.

**Para 4 (test case).** The itch dome: a council can run a midge-spraying programme giving 400,000 residents mildly more comfortable summers, at the predictable cost of one random resident recieving a permanent severe painful condition. Kind-matching says don't spray — the mild complaints aggregate to 400,000 but stay within their kind, and the severe condition is another kind entirely. But then I notice: rescue cases where you can save one from death or fifty from permanent paralysis look like cross-kind comparisons too, and there we do want to compare. My draft tries to say paralysis and death are near enough the same kind while itchiness is not, which felt slippery as I typed it.

**Para 5 (the answer I attempt).** I appeal to what a principle is like to live under: the person with the permanent condition can confront the spraying principle and say it makes their life go a certain way that no number of slightly nicer summers could count against in a way they must recognise. I think this is supposed to do the kind-matching work for me, but reading it back I worry its just hand-waving dressed up.

What I want from you:

1. Have I got Scanlon right in para 1-2, or am I flattening him somewhere it matters?
2. Does the kind-matching rule actually explain non-aggregation, or is it a restatement of it in fancier language? Be honest — I can tell they're suspiciously close.
3. Does the itch dome case help me or sink me, given the paralysis wrinkle in para 4?
4. Is there an obvious published view this is a worse version of? (Tell me the shape of it in general terms — I can chase references myself, I just want to know if the territory is occupied.)
5. Is para 5 doing any work at all, and if not, is there a way to make it do some?

Be blunt, I'd rather rewrite it now than get taken apart at the reading group next week. Also, its due Friday, so sooner is better.
