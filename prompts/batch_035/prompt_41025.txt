I'm finishing a course on axiomatic set theory, and a running argument with a coursemate has left me with a pile of verdicts about the axiom of choice that I suspect don't add up to any coherent position. For finitely many nonempty sets, picking one element from each is obviously fine — I gather that's even provable without choice, so it's no test. For a countable family of nonempty sets of reals, I'm also fine just choosing one from each; the "pick the least element" trick fails without a well-order, but somehow I still feel like countable choice is harmless. Every vector space has a basis: fine, and I'd genuinely mourn losing that. Trichotomy of cardinals — for any two sets, either they're equinumerous or one is strictly smaller — feels like plain arithmetic to me, not a substantive assumption. Tychonoff's theorem, that a product of compact spaces is compact: fine. But here is where it turns. The well-ordering theorem, that every set — the reals included — can be well-ordered: I can't make myself believe this; any well-ordering of the reals is a completely arbitrary object I could never specify, and I'm inclined to say there's no fact of the matter about it. And Banach-Tarski, a solid ball decomposing into finitely many pieces that reassemble into two balls the size of the original: unacceptable; whatever the pieces are, they aren't anything I'd call parts of a ball.

The embarrassing part, which my coursemate keeps pointing out, is that the fine cases and the unacceptable cases all flow from the same principle. Every vector space has a basis and trichotomy of cardinals each require the full axiom of choice — exactly the strength needed for the well-ordering theorem I want to reject, and Banach-Tarski is proved by well-ordering. So I can't just say "choice, but not too much of it": either the basis theorem and trichotomy are hostage to the pathologies, or my discomfort is confused. My tentative repair is something like: choice is fine when the sets in the family are themselves describable or tame — reals, vectors, spaces with structure — and only goes wrong for arbitrary collections of arbitrary sets. But this can't be right either, because the reals are as describable as it gets, and well-ordering precisely them is what I can't stomach, while Zorn's lemma, applied to a horribly arbitrary poset of linearly independent subsets, is what gets me the vector space result I like.

So here is what I want from you. Tell me what, if anything, my verdicts add up to: state one or a few candidate principles precisely — for instance, "accept choice restricted to definable families," or "accept consequences about algebraic objects but not about sets of reals," or simply "full choice, and learn to love Banach-Tarski" — and show, for each clause, which of my cases motivates it. Then tell me what each candidate says about cases I haven't listed and haven't formed a verdict on: does every connected graph have a spanning tree; does every field have an algebraic closure; is there a nonprincipal ultrafilter on the naturals; does every partial order in which every chain has an upper bound have a maximal element, i.e., Zorn's lemma itself; and can Lebesgue measure be extended to all subsets of the reals with the properties countable additivity and translation invariance. Finally, be blunt about which of my verdicts my best option forces me to surrender, and what it costs to keep it — for instance, whether refusing trichotomy or the basis theorem leaves ordinary mathematics usable, or whether I should just bite the ball.
