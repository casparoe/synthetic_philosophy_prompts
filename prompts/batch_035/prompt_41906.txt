I'm a PhD student in archaeology, working on Terminal Classic settlement patterns in the southern Maya lowlands, and I've been keeping a private list of my verdicts on which historical episodes count as genuine societal collapse and which are merely transformations that look dramatic from a distance. I'd like to know whether there is a principle behind these verdicts, or whether I'm just pattern-matching to whichever narrative I was taught first.

The verdicts: the Terminal Classic collapse of the southern Maya lowlands is a genuine collapse — dynastic kingship ends, long-count dedications cease, and population drains from the civic centers — even though Maya peoples, languages, and much cultural practice persist to the present. The Late Bronze Age collapse in the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE is also a genuine collapse, even though Egypt weathered it and the devastated Levantine cities were largely rebuilt within a few centuries. The end of the western Roman Empire, by contrast, I do not count as a collapse: the landholding aristocracy, the church, Roman law, and a functioning tax-and-administration apparatus continue under the successor kingdoms, and the eastern half of the empire persists for another millennium. The decline of the Harappan cities I also decline to count as a collapse: Mohenjo-daro and Harappa are abandoned, but the population seems to disperse into the countryside and eastward, with little evidence of warfare or famine. The Greenland Norse I do count as a collapse, small as that society was. And on Rapa Nui I waver: my gut says collapse — deforestation, the end of statue production — but I suspect the catastrophic population loss really arrives with European contact and its aftermath, so perhaps there was no pre-contact collapse at all.

I have a tentative principle I'd like tested and repaired rather than accepted: a collapse is the involuntary loss of social complexity that goes unabsorbed — organizational capacity that simply disappears rather than migrating to a successor or neighboring system. On its face this sorts the Maya and the Bronze Age from Rome, but I can see it forcing my Indus verdict the wrong way and possibly contradicting my gut on Rapa Nui.

What I want from you: state, precisely, the principle or small set of candidate principles that best captures these verdicts, with each clause motivated by a specific case above; say what the resulting account predicts for cases I haven't mentioned — Angkor, Chaco Canyon, Cahokia, the Western Zhou, the third-century crisis in the Roman world — and which of my verdicts the best principle simply cannot save, with your recommendation as to which should give. Please also address whether collapse is a threshold concept or a matter of degree, since my gut insists the Maya episode is somehow more of a collapse than the Bronze Age one and I cannot articulate why. And please do not simply re-run the familiar debate between those who see collapse everywhere and those who insist everything is continuity and change: treat my verdicts as the fixed data and extract the structure.
