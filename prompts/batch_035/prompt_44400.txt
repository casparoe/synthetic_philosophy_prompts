My political science reading group has spent months on what we assumed would be a preliminary question—whether the left/right distinction tracks anything real—and we cannot get past it, so I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another tour of the positions.

One side holds that the distinction is a durable feature of mass politics: wherever democracies exist, parties and voters sort themselves into recognizable lefts and rights, and survey items on economic redistribution predict responses to items on tradition, authority, and national identity well enough to suggest a single underlying dimension. The other side holds that the bundle is contingent and unstable: the original seating arrangement of the French Revolution has nothing to do with why anyone lines up as they do today, the correlation between the economic and cultural clusters is far from perfect and varies across countries, and the emergence of welfare chauvinists (left on economics, culturally restrictive) and market liberals (right on economics, culturally permissive) shows that what looks like one axis is really two or three that happen to correlate in some eras and not others. We have also gotten tangled in a normative layer: some of us think that even if the empirical sorting is real, using the labels actively distorts politics by forcing multidimensional disagreement onto a single line, so the debate is partly about measurement and partly about what we should do with the words.

Here is my working hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis: the disagreement is not empirical at all but about what a political classification is *for*. The realists implicitly treat left/right as a descriptive sorting tool whose success is measured by predictive power, while the skeptics treat it as an explanatory or even normative device whose success is measured by whether it carves political reality at its joints—and predictive adequacy and joint-carving come apart in a domain where the categories may partly constitute, rather than merely describe, the coalitions they name. If that is right, then arguing about survey correlations concedes too much to the realists, and arguing about conceptual gerrymandering concedes too much to the skeptics, which would explain why neither side's favorite evidence ever lands.

Please tell me: Is my diagnosis right, or is the disagreement a hidden empirical one (say, about whether the inter-item correlations are strong and stable enough to support a single dimension), or a verbal one about what we mean by "the left/right distinction" (a statistically recovered factor, a set of historically continuous parties, a family of substantive ideologies)? Which of the things each side actually says supports your diagnosis? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress—a specific kind of evidence, a conceptual distinction between the sorting function and the explanatory function of the labels, or simply agreeing to use different vocabulary for different purposes—or should we expect no resolution, and why? Please respond in American English.
