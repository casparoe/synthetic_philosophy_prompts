I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and my podcast's next episode is with a philosopher who has spent her career defending the impossible-worlds approach to knowledge and belief — the idea that we should model what a real, cognitively limited agent believes by allowing "worlds" into our semantics where contradictions hold, conjunctions lack their conjuncts, and valid inferences fail. The interview is meant for a general-but-attentive audience, and my guest is far too quick to wave off objections with the remark that impossible worlds are just a modeling device, like frictionless planes in physics, and that demanding more from them confuses a formalism with a metaphysics. I want a question sequence that makes that deflection progressively harder to deploy.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher specializing in formal epistemology, and begin with a brief brainstorming pass on where the impossible-worlds view is genuinely weakest before you write anything, so that the questions press on load-bearing points rather than on tired objections. Then draft eight to ten interview questions. I want at least one question each on the following, with each main question accompanied by the follow-up she is most likely to face from her standard repertoire:

1. The granularity problem: how the framework distinguishes an agent who fails to believe a long tautology from one who believes its negation, given that impossible worlds seem available in any level of logical misbehavior we might care to stipulate.
2. The worry that the view is unrestricted — that for any observed pattern of non-omniscience there is some gerrymandered set of worlds that "explains" it, so nothing could ever count as evidence against the framework.
3. A concrete case I'd like to put to her: a working mathematician who accepts the axioms of arithmetic, accepts each individual step of a four-hundred-page proof, and yet refuses to accept the theorem the proof establishes because she hasn't had time to check the composition of the steps in sequence. Does her framework say anything non-trivial about such an agent, or does it merely relabel her non-omniscience rather than illuminate it?
4. Whether the frictionless-plane analogy survives the fact that, unlike in physics, the ideal agent in epistemic logic is one no finite system — no computer, no collected institution of mathematicians — could ever approximate, and whether this makes the idealization different in kind from the physicist's.
5. What, if anything, the view predicts about where real agents' errors will cluster — for instance, why people routinely miss step-heavy inferences but rarely believe a conjunction while denying both of its conjuncts.

The problem she works on is usually traced back to Jaakko Hintikka's early-1960s work on knowledge and belief, so you may gesture at that history, but please do not ask for literature reviews or expect her to cite specific papers — she prefers to talk in terms of examples. Keep the register accessible: minimal formalism, no unexplained jargon, and questions phrased so that a curious listener could follow every one of them.
