I first ran into the phrase one thought too many in an online argument about what a person should do if they could rescue their drowning wife or a stranger, but not both: someone claimed that if you paused to check whether an impartial calculus licensed choosing her, you would have had one thought too many, and that this refuted the calculus rather than the hesitation. Since then I've heard the same phrase do very different jobs. A friend used it against a Kantian-sounding acquaintance who said he visits his elderly grandmother out of duty, on the ground that duty was the wrong sort of thought to have where love should have been. And I've seen it used against someone who, defending special obligations, tried to give a principled justification for why his wife's life should count extra to him — as though articulating the justification at all were already the betrayal. I'm told the phrase goes back to Bernard Williams, and that the canonical case is the drowning one, where the husband's only permissible reason is said to be simply that she is his wife; but I'm less interested in the history than in what the phrase actually asserts.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses the one-liner could be expressing, as a menu from weakest to strongest. For example: is it a trivial claim about what should pass through one's head in the seconds before acting, with no anti-theoretical implications at all? Is it an evidential claim — that needing the thought is diagnostic of defective character, while merely having it available beforehand is fine? Is it a constitutive claim about which thoughts, had at which times, constitute defective agency or a defective relationship, so that the act or the marriage is somehow made worse by the deliberation? Is it a normative claim about the justification of partiality — that it is in principle unjustifiable without remainder, or that the justification exists but must remain tacit? And is the temporal element essential — does the slogan distinguish between thinking through one's partiality in calm reflection beforehand, which even critics of the thought agree can be admirable, and thinking it at the moment of action? Finally, tell me which of these readings would actually support each of the three uses I described: the refutation of impartial moral theory, the objection to acting from duty, and the objection to justifying partiality on principle. I suspect those three uses cannot all be backed by the same thesis, and I'd like to see, for each, what the slogan would have to mean for the use to work — and whether, so interpreted, it is still plausible.
