In the philosophy of mind reading group I help run, we've been working through Dennett's Consciousness Explained (1991), and one member keeps leaning on the analogy Dennett builds his account of the self around: the self as a center of narrative gravity. The idea, as she deploys it, is that a center of gravity is a perfectly respectable posit — it's not a physical thing you could find by opening up the object, no experiment will ever detect it directly, and yet it's not a mere illusion either; it's a rigorously defined abstraction that physics cannot do without. She says the self is exactly like that: a theoretical posit over a person's behavioral, physiological, and narrative processes, which explains why neuroscientists will never locate "the self" in the brain, why that failure is no embarrassment at all, and why the whole debate about whether the self is real dissolves once you see it — real in the way a center of gravity is real, rather than real in the way a kidney is real. Last week she went further: since an abstraction can't be a subject of experience any more than a center of gravity can be, we should stop looking for the "one who is conscious" — there's a narrative going on, and a center of narrative gravity posited within it, but nobody home.

I have a feeling the analogy is doing more work than a careful look would license, but I'm not the one to conduct the audit. Could you take it on? I'd like the correspondence table laid out explicitly — what plays the role of the physical object, the mass distribution, the physicist doing the positing, the calculations that fix the center — and then the points where the mapping visibly strains. In particular I want to know which features of real centers of gravity the analogy quietly imports that selves arguably lack: uniqueness (for a rigid body there is exactly one center of gravity), definitional precision, being fixed entirely by the underlying "mass" rather than partly by the positing itself, and the inability of an abstraction to act back on the processes that define it — whereas a person's self-narrating arguably reshapes the very processes doing the narrating. For each disanalogy, please say whether it actually matters for the conclusions my colleague wants to draw, or whether it's incidental, and then tell me straight whether her three conclusions — that the neuroscientific failure to find the self is a category error, that the realism question about selves dissolves, and that an abstractum can't be the experiencer, so there is no subject — survive once the disanalogies are on the table. If the analogy is salvageable in a weakened form, say what the weakened form still supports; if there's a better comparison for the work she wants done (a fictional character who co-authors her own story, a legal person, a Schelling point, a center of gravity that could keep a diary), tell me what it is and why it tracks the self better.

Please lead with the audit and keep the correspondence table compact, but end your reply with a single line of the form "Verdict: ..." stating, in one sentence, which of her three conclusions survive the audit and which do not.
