I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere, the EA Forum, and the econ blogs since roughly 2010, and like most of that demographic I came in with strong pro-market priors and trained myself to treat squeamishness about particular transactions as a cached "sacredness" response to be debugged rather than honored. It mostly worked. But when I lay out my actual verdicts on repugnant markets, they don't sort along the pro/anti-market axis at all — they're a mess, and I want to know what principle, if any, generates them, or which of them I should abandon. I've read the Sandel/Satz literature, and my verdicts only half match either side.

My verdicts, concretely:

1. Regulated kidney-donor compensation (roughly $50k, waiting period, independent screening): clearly fine, arguably urgent.
2. Price-gouging bottled water at $20 after a hurricane: defensible — I'll defend it at a party. The same markup on the last vial of insulin at the same pharmacy: not fine, and I don't think that's just about elasticity.
3. Selling my vote in a national election: absolute no, even though one vote is astronomically unlikely to matter. Selling my vote in my homeowners' association election about what color to paint the fence: fine, honestly kind of funny.
4. Paid surrogacy with counsel and safeguards: fine. Paid plasma donation: fine — someone has to supply the world.
5. The Frey and Oberholzer-Gee finding where offering Swiss communities financial compensation for hosting a nuclear waste repository cratered support that had previously been given for free: I take the crowding-out result seriously as evidence about motivation, and yet I refuse to extend it to kidneys, where I want more money changing hands, not less.
6. A prediction market on whether a named public figure gets indicted this year: fine, socially useful. A liquid market on whether a specific private couple's marriage survives to next year, open to people who know them: not fine — though I can't state the difference in a sentence, and by my usual lights "markets shouldn't touch the personal" is exactly the kind of aesthetics objection I'd mock.
7. A university quietly auctioning the last 5% of each admitted class to full-freight bidders above a floor score: I flip-flop every time it comes up, which is itself data, I suppose.

Questions: Is there one principle — or a ranked family of clauses (coercion, unequal bargaining power, corruption of the transaction's meaning, signaling effects on bystanders, and my least favorite candidate, purity) — that fits all seven? Or is my pattern just status-quo bias with a libertarian paint job? Where you can't save a verdict, tell me which one to give up and why that one rather than the others. Then apply your best principle to two cases I haven't listed: paying professional line-standers to hold seats at congressional hearings, and a public hospital selling a fast-track ticket to its emergency-department waiting room.
