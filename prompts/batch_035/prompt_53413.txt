I'm taking a philosophy seminar on collective responsibility, and for my final paper I want to use what's usually called the Fischer controversy in West German historiography as my case study. My advisor says the historical side and the philosophical side of my draft aren't talking to each other yet, so what I'd like from you is a proper two-way analysis. First, the facts, which I'll give you so we're on the same page (no need to cite anything back to me, just take them as given): in 1961 the German historian Fritz Fischer published Griff nach der Weltmacht, which came out in English in 1967 as Germany's Aims in the First World War. Fischer argued that Imperial Germany deliberately risked a general European war in July 1914 and pursued vast annexationist war aims once it started -- the September Program of 1914, the Mitteleuropa scheme, the whole grab for world power -- and, most explosively, that these ambitions grew out of long-standing goals of the German power elite, creating a line of continuity from pre-1914 German policy toward Hitler's expansionism. This landed like a bombshell in West Germany, because the respectable story had been that Hitler was a unique aberration, not the product of anything deeper in German history. Senior conservative historians like Gerhard Ritter went after Fischer hard; he was accused of smearing the nation, the fight went way beyond seminar rooms, and the whole thing reopened the question that the Versailles war guilt clause had already politicized a generation earlier: Germany's responsibility for 1914.

Now here's the philosophical lens I want you to apply, and I want the analysis to run in both directions. The seminar covered the standard menu of positions on holding a nation (rather than specific individuals) responsible: the collectivist view on which the nation itself is an agent that can bear responsibility, possibly across generations; the strict individualist view on which only persons are responsible agents and 'Germany is to blame' is at best a shorthand for claims about particular dead decision-makers; and the middle positions where responsibility gets shared or diffused across a population without positing any group mind, or where later generations inherit duties of repair, remembrance, and truth-telling without inheriting guilt itself. So, direction one: use each of these positions to say what the Fischer controversy was actually about. When the West German establishment fought Fischer so fiercely, who or what did they think was being blamed? The Kaiser's government? A class of elites? The nation as a persisting agent that in 1961 was the Federal Republic? And what work was the blame-assignment doing, since in 1961 nobody was seriously proposing punishment -- was it about historical truth, about the self-understanding and identity of a new democracy, about West Germany's standing in the world? Then direction two: what does the episode show about each theory? My hunch is that the ferocity of the reaction is itself evidence that people on all sides treated the nation as a real bearer of something, which looks bad for strict individualism, but I want you to be honest about where each theory is simply silent about a historiographical dispute like this, and about which bullets the collectivist has to bite (e.g., in what sense was 'Germany' in 1914 the same entity as West Germany in 1961, given the regime changes in between?).
