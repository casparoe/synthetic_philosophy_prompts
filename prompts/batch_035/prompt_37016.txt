After a career in condensed-matter experiment — decades of fitting models to noisy data, a good deal of Bayesian inference, and the occasional seminar on statistical methodology — I have retired and, somewhat to my own surprise, taken up the philosophy of probability. There I have encountered the principle of indifference, the rule that when one has no reason to favor one possibility over another, one should assign them equal probabilities, and Bertrand's paradox: the question of a "randomly drawn" chord of a circle, where depending on how the draw is parametrized one obtains 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4 as the probability that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle.

Restate this entire debate — the principle as classically stated, the chord paradox, and the principal attempts at repair — in the vocabulary I actually use: sample spaces and their parametrization, choice of dominating measure, invariance under transformation groups, Haar measure, maximum entropy, noninformative and reference priors, Jeffreys priors, and the simple fact that "uniform" is a property of a measure relative to a parametrization, not of a set. Translate, as faithfully as you can, Jaynes's transformation-group resolution of the paradox, and the objections to it — for instance the observation that the same symmetry requirements seem to reproduce all three classical answers rather than selecting one, and the point that when a problem statement, like van Fraassen's cube factory, does not determine a transformation group at all, the machinery simply has nothing to say.

Then give me the residue report. I want to know, part by part, which elements of the classical debate map cleanly onto things I already understand as a working probabilist; which map only with distortion; and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. Two specific questions. First: is the philosophers' principle of indifference genuinely about a normative feature of knowledge states — something about what an agent is entitled to conclude in the absence of information — or is it just a half-formalized ancestor of invariance-based prior selection, in which case I have already understood it? Second: where the residue remains — the cases, like the wine-and-water division problem or the cube factory, where symmetry considerations underdetermine an answer — does that point to something my vocabulary cannot say, or to the original principle having been less clear than it looked?

One caution. Do not let the mathematics settle by fiat what is in dispute. If translating the principle as "invariance under the symmetries of the problem" already builds in part of what the philosophers were arguing about — namely, whether lack of reason to discriminate genuinely entails symmetry of belief — flag that as a substantive assumption rather than presenting it as a neutral rendering. I would rather see an honest report of what the translation carries and what it drops than a demonstration that my own habits of thought were right all along.
