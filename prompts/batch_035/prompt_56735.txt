Analyze the International Astronomical Union's 2006 vote in Prague — the one that demoted Pluto to "dwarf planet" — as an episode in the philosophy of natural kinds. I know the astronomy: the discovery of Eris threatened to swell the count of planets into the dozens, the new definition requires that a body has cleared its orbital neighborhood, and Pluto fails that test. What I want is the philosophical mapping. On an essentialist picture of natural kinds, what was the IAU doing — discovering that the word "planet" had been failing to track a joint in nature, or legislating a convention? Then run the episode through a homeostatic-property-cluster view: the big eight form a cluster (self-gravitated roundness, dynamical dominance, formation history) that Pluto, Eris, and Ceres only partially instantiate, and on that view the vote looks less arbitrary. But note that the winning criterion is relational — a fact about a body's gravitational neighborhood rather than its intrinsic constitution — and say what a kind whose membership turns on orbital dynamics does to the thesis that the sciences carve nature at its joints. Consider also the losing proposal, the one that would have kept Pluto a planet by counting every round trans-Neptunian object: was that a rival carving of the same joints, or a refusal to carve at all? Finally, run the analysis the other way: does the episode show that natural-kind talk fits astronomy badly because "planet" began as an appearance-based folk term for wanderers and has been drifting ever since, or does it show the framework working as intended, with the vote functioning as ordinary scientific error correction? Be disciplined about which parts of the episode are theory-laden facts and which are just professional politics, and say where the natural-kinds framework is silent and shouldn't be forced to speak.
