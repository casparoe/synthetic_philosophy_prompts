I run hiring at a mid-size company (~200 people), and I've gotten pretty skeptical of our credential screens. We filter on things like CS degrees, take-home projects, referrals from current employees, stints at certain big-name companies -- but I suspect half of these are things candidates can cheaply acquire to *look* good rather than honest signals of what we actually want. I've been reading a bit about signaling theory, including the handicap principle from biology, and the core idea I want to operationalize is roughly: a signal is only trustworthy when it's costly in a way that's *more* costly for the kind of person we don't want than for the kind we do. That, and maybe the point that if the signal is cheap for everyone it just pools everyone together and tells us nothing.

Please turn that into a procedure my recruiters and I can actually run on any screen we use or are considering hiring for -- a set of questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a clear "drop this screen," where it says "keep it but reweight it," and where it admits it can't decide and a human has to make a values call about what we're even trying to hire for. It should also flag the failure modes where the cost structure looks right but the signal still misleads -- like when the signal is costly for everyone but costly in the wrong currency (spare time instead of skill), or when lots of other companies copy the same screen and it stops discriminating between anyone.

Then run the procedure on three of our current screens: requiring a CS degree, an unpaid take-home project that takes candidates maybe six hours, and referrals from current employees. Run it honestly, including on whichever one is my favorite -- I suspect the referrals one, since everyone here loves referrals. And tell me what the procedure can't do, like whether it can tell us at all whether the trait being signaled is worth signaling in the first place.
