I serve on my university's animal care and use committee, which reviews research protocols involving live animals. Our current framework treats insects as essentially beneath notice: individual ants are not covered by anything beyond basic humane-treatment language, and protocols involving them have historically been approved administratively without full committee review. A principal investigator in our ecology department has now submitted a protocol that is raising an unusual objection from within the committee itself. The study involves collective learning in Argentine ant colonies: colonies must reorganize their foraging networks in response to experimentally altered conditions, and some arms of the study involve deliberately stressing colonies -- chilling them, disrupting their pheromone trails, removing queens, and ultimately destroying the colonies at the study's end. One of our committee members, who studied philosophy of mind before switching to neuroscience, argues that our welfare review is aimed at the wrong level of organization. On her view, the individual ant is roughly analogous to a neuron: it lacks anything worth protecting in itself, but the colony exhibits learning, flexible problem-solving, and integrated information-processing at the collective level -- the sort of thing Hofstadter dramatized in the Anteater dialogue in *Gödel, Escher, Bach*, where the conscious entity "Aunt Hillary" is an ant colony whose component ants are oblivious participants. If the colony, not the ant, is the locus of whatever cognition and possibly valenced experience occurs, then protocols that stress or destroy colonies deserve scrutiny that protocols harming individual ants would not, and our invertebrate exemption rests on a category mistake.

Our committee must issue a written determination before the next funding deadline, and the options on the table are: (a) approve administratively under the existing invertebrate exemption, since the science of colony-level experience is too speculative to ground policy; (b) approve with conditions, e.g., requiring the PI to minimize colony-level disruption where it does not compromise the design and to justify colony destruction; or (c) treat colony-level interventions as triggering full committee review going forward, on the precautionary ground that uncertainty about where experience resides should cut in favor of protection. The PI's department is already grumbling that option (c) would burden a whole field of research on social insects for the sake of a thought experiment, and our veterinary staff notes that if we extend review to ant colonies, colleagues will reasonably ask about slime molds, bacterial biofilms, and the software agents in our engineering school's swarm robotics program.

Please draft the memo I will circulate ahead of the meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action, argue for it in terms the whole committee can work with, and give the losing position -- whichever it is -- an honest and serious treatment rather than a caricature. In particular, it should say something substantive about what follows and what does *not* follow from the premise that cognition in social insects is distributed across the colony rather than localized in individual organisms: does distributed cognition make welfare obligations *more* demanding, less, or simply different in kind? The committee would also benefit from a workable principle for where our review boundary sits, if not at the individual organism -- something we could actually articulate in revised guidelines without opening the door to reviewing every simulation in the engineering school.
