I'm a senior analyst at a nonpartisan foreign-affairs research institute, and my team produces the recurring strategic-environment assessments that get read by legislative staff and mid-level policymakers. There's a fight we have every cycle and never resolve: whenever someone claims a major power is in decline — or insists it isn't — one faction demands the outside view (what happened to comparable powers in comparable positions), another objects that no two empires are comparable enough for base rates to mean anything, and we fall back on whichever analyst wrote the draft. I want a written procedure my team can actually run that turns the outside view into a disciplined argument rather than a rhetorical gesture. Specifically, it should cover: how to select the reference class for a claim about a great power's trajectory (all past hegemons, all great powers whether dominant or not, only powers with a certain economic or technological profile, only post-industrial ones — and what to do when the choice of class looks like it was reverse-engineered from the desired conclusion); how to define "decline" so that class membership and outcome are coded the same way across historical cases, including what to do about still-standing powers whose futures are censored; how to combine the resulting base rate with the case-specific evidence that everyone insists makes this power unprecedented; what to do when two defensible reference classes yield sharply different base rates (whether they should be averaged, reported as a range, or treated as a red flag that the question is malformed); how to update when the event horizon is fifty years but the assessment cycle is quarterly; and where the procedure should stop and state plainly that the outside view cannot settle the question. It should also say which steps are genuinely mechanical and which are judgment calls dressed up as steps. Then run it on three claims: that the currently dominant military power will not lose primacy within fifty years; that a long-standing regional alliance will outlast its present membership; and that a rising power's construction of overseas port and infrastructure interests presages territorial expansion within three decades. For at least one of the three, the procedure should be allowed to reach a conclusion that would embarrass our institutional priors — we have been telling readers that the alliance is durable for twenty years — and the procedure should be built so it cannot be quietly tuned to flatter us. Constraints: two weeks per assessment, open-source material only, and readers who will not accept a conclusion justified purely in formal epistemology jargon, so each step needs a one-sentence plain-language rationale a staff member can repeat in a briefing without us.
