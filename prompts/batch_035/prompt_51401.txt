I teach an introductory political philosophy course, and every year, right after we finish the unit on Rawls, some student brings up Vonnegut's "Harrison Bergeron" — the October 1961 story that opens "The year was 2081, and everybody was finally equal. They weren't only equal before God and the law. They were equal every which way" — and announces that the Handicapper General's America is exactly where Rawlsian egalitarianism lands: a state that weights the strong with forty-seven pounds of birdshot, masks the beautiful, and blasts the intelligent with government sirens every twenty seconds, all under constitutional amendments guaranteeing that nobody is smarter, better looking, or quicker than anybody else. Setting aside the awkward fact that the story appeared a full decade before A Theory of Justice, I'd like a serious reading rather than a quick dismissal, because I suspect the story is doing something more interesting than refuting a position Rawls never held. Read it through the veil of ignorance and the difference principle: would the parties in the original position, not knowing whether they'd be born a Harrison or a Hazel, ever choose the handicapping regime over a scheme that permits inequalities so long as they work to the least advantaged? Does the narrator's sympathy lie with the gifted son torn apart for his excellence, or does the ending — George and Hazel left unable even to remember their son's death, the tragedy dissolving into average contentment — indict a world without excellence and a world that has simply stopped thinking, both at once? And, most importantly for how I run the seminar: does the story, read honestly, actually support the anti-egalitarian conclusion my student draws from it, or has it merely been drafted into a fight Rawls would sit out?

I want a committed answer, not a survey of defensible interpretations: take a definite position on whether the story genuinely bears on Rawls or has been conscripted against a leveling-down doctrine that the difference principle was designed to reject. Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion — for instance, one on what the Handicapper General's regime and Rawls's well-ordered society actually share, one on the strongest version of my student's use of the story, one on where the story resists that use, one on what the ending adds that the theory alone does not, and one delivering your verdict.
