I'm part of a long-running online reading group that just worked through Benedict Anderson's Imagined Communities and the Hobsbawm and Ranger volume The Invention of Tradition, and I've been asked to post a statement of my own position on nations and nationhood so the other members can attack it. They are exactly the sort of people who will find every crack if I don't find it first, so before posting I want an outside consistency audit. Here is the position as I plan to state it:

(1) The modernist, constructivist picture is basically right: nations are recent artifacts, assembled in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries out of print capitalism, mass schooling, censuses, conscription, and the deliberate invention of traditions; there are no genuinely ancient nations in the sense nationalists claim, only older raw materials that were reworked into something new. (2) The principle of national self-determination is nonetheless sound: a people, once it exists as a self-conscious nation, has a strong prima facie right to its own state. (3) I also hold that a democratic demos cannot simply vote itself into existence out of an arbitrary collection of people — the demos is prior to and bounds democratic decision, which is why a bare regional majority can't by itself settle a secession question. (4) I'm fairly sympathetic to states actively sustaining national identity through schooling, language policy, and national service, and I don't regard this as brainwashing when democracies do it — I think it's a precondition of the solidarity that welfare states and democratic legitimacy require. (5) At the same time, I would deny that a purely interest-based grouping — a union, a religious congregation, the shareholders of a large company — has anything like a nation's claim to self-determination, even when its members are far more intensely and deliberately unified in their goals than the members of most nations are.

What I want from you: go through these commitments pairwise and in whatever combinations are actually load-bearing, tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be, and rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give, what giving it would cost, and finally identify the single revision that would clean up the most. Three constraints on how you do it. First, mark what is contested scholarship rather than presenting my premises or your corrections as settled: I gather the modernist consensus itself has serious critics who think nations, or proto-nations, are much older than Anderson and Hobsbawm allow, and I don't know how much weight that criticism deserves — don't paper over it and don't concede it wholesale either. Second, don't do the reflexive thing where every constructivist premise is answered with a shrug about how everything social is constructed anyway; if (1) is going to do real work against (2) or (5), say exactly what work, and if it can't, say that plainly. Third, if you think one of the five is not mine to keep at all — incoherent on its own, not merely in tension with the others — say so and defend the claim, because I'd rather hear it now than in the comments.
