i'm teaching a small seminar on decision theory and want to close the last session with a poem, because of course i do. the theme is updatelessness — the idea that you decide once, in advance, as the person you were before you got the information, and then carry that decision through every later observation, because that earlier self is the one whose choice is correlated with how things went for all your copies across the possible worlds.

write it in the voice of an agent working through a run of transparent-newcomb-like situations. exactly five tercets, please — no more, no less. a single refrain closes each of the first four stanzas, word-for-word identical each time, while the stanzas themselves describe different things the agent has just seen: the box visibly full, the box visibly empty, a twin who peeked and took both boxes and did fine, whatever you like. the identical refrain set against changing evidence is meant to be the theory itself — the decision was made once, before, and no observation reopens it. then in the fifth stanza the refrain comes back with exactly one word changed, and that one word is where updatelessness either pays off or costs the agent something real. your call which way it goes, but make it land — dont let the last stanza feel like a shrug.

the test im aiming for: someone who knows FDT or UDT should be able to say what the refrain is doing and why it mutates only at the end, and someone who has never heard of any of this should still get a good poem about keeping a promise to the person you were before you knew things.
