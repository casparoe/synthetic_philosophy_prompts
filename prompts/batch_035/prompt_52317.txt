Before I submit my position statement for a bioethics workshop on embryo selection, I want an outside check on whether my views actually form a coherent package. Here is what I have written down so far.

(1) I endorse a moderate form of procreative beneficence, roughly the principle Savulescu is associated with: when prospective parents can choose among the children they might have — as with preimplantation genetic testing — they have a genuine moral reason to select the child expected to have the best life prospects, and I am inclined to call this reason an obligation, though a defeasible one.

(2) I take the non-identity problem seriously. I do not see how bringing into existence a person whose life is worth living can wrong that person, since the alternative for them was never existing at all. A couple who implant an embryo with a genetic condition rather than a healthy one therefore do not seem to wrong the child who results — that child could not have had a better life, because a different embryo would have been a different person.

(3) I hold that genuine moral obligations are always owed to someone. I do not believe in free-floating impersonal duties — for instance, I reject the claim that we are obligated to create additional happy people simply because that would make the world better overall.

(4) On disability, my sympathies lie with the social model: impairment as such is not a harm, and most of what is bad about being deaf in our society is a contingent and unjust arrangement that could in principle be otherwise. I also think the expressivist objection to screening — that selecting against disability sends a demeaning message about existing disabled people — has at least some force.

(5) Despite all of the above, I hold two verdicts with real confidence: a deaf couple who use embryo selection deliberately to have a deaf child act wrongly, and a couple who select against a condition like Down syndrome act permissibly, even admirably. These feel like my fixed points.

(6) Finally, I am a thoroughgoing liberal about the state's role: it should remain neutral among contested conceptions of the good life and stay out of reproductive decision-making entirely — no mandates, no incentives, no nudges toward having "better" children.

What I would like from you is an audit rather than a survey of the literature. Go through the package and tell me which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and how severe each genuine tension is. For each genuine one, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether there is some way of reframing one of the commitments that restores coherence without merely relabeling a contradiction. Do not assume you know which commitment I am least attached to; I have tried not to signal that, and I would rather you reason it out.
