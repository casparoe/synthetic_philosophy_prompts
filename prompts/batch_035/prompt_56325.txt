I write about science and ideas for a mid-size outlet — mostly climate coverage, food systems, the psychology of why people do or don't change habits — and I've gotten genuinely stuck on my own gut reactions to a bunch of everyday cases. So I figured I'd lay out my verdicts and ask whether there's actually a principle underneath them or whether I'm just running on vibes I absorbed from whichever PSA was playing when I was twelve.

My verdicts on when one person's contribution to a big collective outcome matters:

1. Not voting in a national election when I live in a state that goes the same way every cycle by a million votes: I feel like a bit of a jerk, and I think I actually did something wrong. My one vote had essentially zero chance of mattering and I still think skipping it is wrong.

2. Eating a chicken burrito: makes no moral difference. The bird's already dead, one burrito doesn't move the number of chickens raised, and I genuinely don't feel bad.

3. Taking a weekend flight to see a friend: I keep not doing it, and feeling vaguely guilty when I do, even though one flight changes the climate exactly as much as my burrito changed the chicken industry — i.e., nothing you could point to.

4. Boycotting a company over how it treats warehouse workers: I do it, on principle I guess, but I can't for the life of me expalin how this differs from the burrito case. Same structure — my few dollars vanish into a revenue stream of billions.

5. Donating blood: I go every few months, and I'd tell you it matters, even though I have no idea whether my type is ever actually short at the bank.

6. Picking up a piece of trash on a trail that isn't mine: obviously yes, doing your bit, and I'd quietly judge someone who walked past it.

And the one I keep going back and forth on: at work events there's always leftover catered food, some of it meat, and it gets tossed if nobody eats it. A leftover pulled pork sandwich from an order that was placed and paid for weeks ago moves demand not at all — even the "your burrito might cross some invisible reorder threshold and trigger a shipment of 100 more birds" story doesn't apply, which is the only story I've ever heard for why a single purchase could matter at all. So if my "no difference" verdict on the burrito is right, the leftover sandwich should be covered even more strongly. And yet it feels different, and I lose the thread trying to say why.

Is there a principle that gets all six verdicts, or close? My guess is there are at least two live candidates: something like "be a participant in the collective practice you'd want everyone to be part of," which makes skipping the vote, the boycott, the blood, and the trash pickup all wrong — but then makes the burrito wrong too, which I don't accept; versus something like "only discrete threshold-crossing effects count," which saves the burrito but sinks the flight, the vote, and honestly the boycott. So tell me: which of my verdicts have to go, what the surviving principle says about the leftover sandwich, and what it predicts on some case I haven't listed here.

Two requests. First, if you think my list is just incoherent and there's nothing under it — no principle, just a pile of vibes — say that plainly instead of constructing a clever theory to dignify it. Second, if you think the reorder-threshold idea I half-remembered is wrong, or doesn't do the work I'm assigning it, push on that too. I'd rather be told I'm confused than be told something smooth.
