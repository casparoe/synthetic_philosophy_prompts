I'm on the historical commission of Kettleboro, a small New England town, and we've been tasked with recommending what to do about next summer's tercentennial pageant. Since 1958 the pageant has staged "the Stand at Miller's Bridge": local legend holds that in 1780 the town militia held off a raiding party at the bridge and saved the village. A doctoral student at the state university has just gone through the muster rolls, correspondence, and colonial records, and her conclusion is that there was no stand at all — the militia broke and ran within minutes, the raiders burned some stores unopposed, and the only people hurt were civilians. Her documents also show something the legend leaves out entirely: the town's few Black families and the remnants of the local Indigenous community sheltered many of the fleeing residents, and one of them, a man the records call Pompey Freeman, is said to have pulled two children out of a burning barn. So our cherished memory appears to be false in what it claims and incomplete in what it omits — though it may preserve something real about the town's self-understanding that the muster rolls don't capture.

The commission is badly split, and the argument has gotten more principled than I expected. One faction holds that the pageant is not a history lesson but a ritual — that a community's memory of itself belongs to the community, that its function is to bind generations together, and that letting the archives rewrite it is a category error, like correcting a hymn. The other faction says that whatever the pageant is, we would be knowingly staging a falsehood in front of schoolchildren and calling it our heritage. Meanwhile, the descendants of the omitted families have petitioned the selectboard — and notably, they are not asking for cancellation, only for a rewrite that includes their ancestors, which suggests they think the memory can survive correction rather than being destroyed by it.

It's personal for me too: my late grandmother helped write the 1958 script and played the Widow Miller for over a decade, and I grew up on the story. What I need help thinking through is what a shared memory is actually *for*, and what each side of our dispute owes the other. When a community's memory and the documentary record conflict, does one owe the other deference, and in which direction — and does the answer change when the memory is not merely inaccurate but flattering in a way the record is not? Is there a real distinction between a memory that is wrong about facts but faithful about identity — about who the town takes itself to be — and a memory that is just a flattering fiction? And practically: is there a version of the pageant that could honor my grandmother's work and the tradition it anchors while telling the newer story honestly, or does trying to serve both masters doom us to a muddle that betrays each?
