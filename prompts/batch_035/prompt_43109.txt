Construct a thought experiment in the bioethics of advance directives, built to order, so that three familiar rationales for honouring or overriding them deliver a specified and unusual pattern of verdicts.

The three views the case must engage, defined here so nothing else need be presupposed. First, precedent autonomy: the view, associated with Allen Buchanan and Dan Brock, that an advance directive binds because, and only insofar as, the earlier self's choice was an autonomous one — competent, adequately informed, and made independently of manipulation, including subtler self-directed pressures such as grief or an insistent promise. Second, experiential welfare: the view, pressed by Rebecca Dresser, that once competence is lost, what counts is the current patient's experiential interests — pleasure, comfort, engagement — so that where the demented patient's present and future experience is good, life-prolonging treatment serves her interests. Third, critical interests: Ronald Dworkin's view that a directive's authority flows not from the earlier choice's procedural pedigree but from its expression of the patient's critical interests — her judgements, however they were formed, about what makes her life as a whole a better life.

The pattern to be engineered is the reverse of the usual alignment, in which Dworkin's critical interests march alongside precedent autonomy against the welfare view. Your case must be one in which precedent autonomy and experiential welfare agree that the directive should be overridden and treatment given, while the critical-interests view says the refusal must be honoured.

Constraints on the construction: the setting must be entirely realistic — a geriatric ward or care home, ordinary interventions such as antibiotics or clinically assisted nutrition and hydration, no philosophical or futuristic furniture. The scenario itself is to be a single paragraph. The directive's defective provenance must fall short of anything that would invalidate it under actual legal and clinical standards — it was capacitous, medically informed, and properly witnessed — so that the case remains genuinely hard rather than resolved by doctrine. And the verdicts must be generated, not stipulated: do not secure the critical-interests verdict by narrative framing (no authorial hints that the refusal was "deeply her"), and do not secure the other two verdicts by making the provenance egregious or the demented life miserable. The defect in provenance should be exactly as serious as the autonomy conditions require and no worse; the patient's contentment should be ordinary.

The case must also be built with two independent knobs: removing the compromised provenance — supposing the same refusal written in calm, unpressured circumstances — should reconcile precedent autonomy with the critical-interests view, and removing the contentedness of the current patient — supposing the dementia is the vacant, unrecognising state the earlier self dreaded — should reconcile experiential welfare with it. Each dissenter must be tied to exactly one knob.

After the scenario, walk through each view's verdict and reasoning. Then identify which feature of the case is doing the work — I take it to be the gap between the procedural conditions that make an advance choice a valid exercise of autonomy and the substantive relation between that choice and the patient's lifelong organising commitments — and say what happens to the pattern if that gap is closed. Finally, give the strongest attack on the case from the side that comes out worst: either the precedent-autonomy theorist arguing that Dworkin's appeal to the patient's sovereignty over her own life quietly reintroduces the very decisional conditions his view was supposed to do without, or the welfare theorist arguing that critical interests do not lapse with competence — that a patient still capable of valuing music and familiar faces has critical interests of her own, so that the critical-interests view has misidentified whose integrity is at stake. Take a stance on which attack is more damaging.

The purpose of the case is to test whether the critical-interests rationale is genuinely a third view or merely precedent autonomy in different dress; any construction that settles the matter by authorial fiat rather than by the theories' own lights therefore fails.
