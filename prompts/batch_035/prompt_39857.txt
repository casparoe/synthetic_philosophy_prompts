In September 2007, the British mortgage lender Northern Rock suffered the first visible retail bank run in Britain since the nineteenth century, and I would like it analyzed as a case study in common knowledge of rationality and epistemic game theory. Here are the salient facts, so the analysis does not turn on contested detail. Northern Rock funded its lending chiefly by borrowing in wholesale credit markets rather than from depositors. When those markets froze in the summer of 2007, the bank could not roll over its funding, and on 13/14 September the Bank of England announced emergency liquidity support. The announcement itself — not a revelation of insolvency — triggered the run: depositors queued outside branches and drained roughly 8-12% of retail deposits over the following days, with the queues and the news coverage of the queues broadcast continuously. UK deposit insurance at the time covered 100% of only the first £2,000 of a deposit and 90% of the next £33,000, so a typical saver had money at genuine risk, while the Bank of England and the FSA publicly and repeatedly stated that the bank was solvent. On 17 September the Chancellor of the Exchequer announced a full government guarantee of all Northern Rock deposits, and the run stopped almost immediately.

I know the textbook machinery, so please go past it rather than through it. Start with Diamond-Dybvig as the underlying coordination game — who the players actually were (retail depositors, wholesale lenders, the bank, the Bank of England, the Treasury), what the real option set and payoffs were (queue now and lose at most time; wait and risk partial loss under the insurance cap; the bank's option to fund at the emergency facility), and where the classical analysis assumes literally common knowledge of the payoffs and of everyone's rationality. Then do the epistemic work carefully, in stages: what exactly the liquidity-support announcement changed in the information structure, and why a public announcement intended as reassurance functioned as a coordinating signal for withdrawal; how the queues and their television coverage created common knowledge among depositors who would otherwise have had only private worries — common knowledge of what, precisely: that the bank was in trouble, that others knew, that others knew that others were withdrawing; how Rubinstein's email game bears on the claim that near-common knowledge will not sustain the cooperative equilibrium, and whether depositors' situation approximated the conditions under which that result bites; and how the global-games treatment (noisy private signals about fundamentals selecting a unique equilibrium rather than multiple equilibria coordinated on a sunspot) describes the episode better or worse than the pure common-knowledge story. Then treat the 17 September guarantee as the crucial experiment: the government changed the payoffs so that withdrawal was no longer dominant, but it also made solvency support common knowledge — disentangle which change ended the run, and say what evidence in the episode (the speed of the stop, the roughly 88-92% of deposits that never moved, the branches with no queues at all, the fact that withdrawal was arguably rational for capped depositors even given a solvent bank) discriminates between the accounts.

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does this episode show about the theory? Is it a confirmation of the common-knowledge mechanism, an anomaly (a run that stopped because payoffs changed, without any change in beliefs about fundamentals), or a case the equilibria-focused literature never anticipated — the authorities intervening in the epistemic structure itself, broadcasting queues to the very depositors whose coordination they feared? Be disciplined about the mapping — say where epistemic game theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak — and be honest about what no model in this family explains, including whether the trigger was epistemic at all or simply a rational response to the insurance cap.
