I'm a science journalist and last year I covered the whole "did the Anthropocene just get killed off" saga -- the geologists voted down the proposal to make it an official epoch, along with the mid-20th-century start date and the Crawford Lake sediment core that was supposed to be its "golden spike." After my piece ran I got drinks with two of my sources and they re-litigated the entire thing in front of me, and honestly I couldnt follow it well enough to say who was landing punches. So I'm handing you their exchange, as best as I can reconstruct it, and I want you to referee.

Source 1 ("Greta") is a Quaternary stratigrapher who thought the vote was correct. Source 2 ("Marco") is an earth system scientist who was furious about it.

(1) Greta: geology works on long timescales. The Holocene has run about 11,700 years; an "epoch" that is 75 years old with no end in sight is a category error. If the last 75 years gets to be an epoch, then every century gets to be one. Just call it an "event," like the Great Oxidation Event, and everyone gets what they want.

(2) Marco: the stratigraphic evidence isn't the problem. Crawford Lake has annual sediment layers, and they show the mid-century step change better than a lot of official boundaries -- plutonium isotopes from bomb tests, fly ash, heavy metals, the whole package. So if the objection was "you can't see it in the rocks," that objection is dead on arrival.

(3) Greta: visible isn't the same as permanent. Some of those signals could partly reverse -- decarbonization, remediation, a plastics treaty. And the signals show up at different times in ocean sediment vs. lake sediment vs. ice anyway. A time unit defined by markers that might fade, and that don't even appear everywhere at once, isn't a unit, it's a mood.

(4) Marco: notice the bait and switch. First the complaint was "not visible in strata," now it's "might not be permanent." The goalposts keep moving to wherever the epoch dies. And every boundary geologists ever ratified was drawn with partial hindsight and a dollop of arbitrariness -- the golden spike is about coordinating, not discovering a fact.

(5) Greta: here's my real worry. Crutzen's word escaped the lab. Half the people using "Anthropocene" mean 1950, half mean agriculture, half just mean "humans are wrecking things." If we formalize one date, it becomes a scientific fact. If we don't, it stays an argument -- and I'd rather have an honest argument than a fake fact.

(6) Marco: and I'd rather have a fact that says we broke the planet than an argument. Refusing to formalize isn't neutral: it hands the term to whoever shouts loudest, and it quietly signals that the change isn't real enough to name. Scientists name things all the time knowing the names get revised. What you call a "fake fact" is just a fact made by people you don't like.
