I'm a novelist, three books deep into a project where every viewpoint character comes from a culture whose moral code the others find monstrous, and somewhere in the last year of drafting I realized I can't tell anymore whether I believe moral relativism or whether I just find it the most convenient thing to write from. I've got one friend who's a committed relativist (crude version: whether an action is wrong is relative to some framework — a culture's, a person's, there's no framework-free fact of the matter) and another who's a committed moral objectivist (there are moral truths that hold regardless of anyone's framework), and they've been arguing in my group chat for so long that I've started writing their arguments into minor characters. What I want from you is the document they should have produced instead: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between those two camps.

Specifically: the claims both sides can actually sign — about the extent of real moral disagreement between cultures, about what anthropological evidence does and doesn't show, about the difference between disagreement and people just wanting different things, whatever else is genuinely common ground. Then the precise disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording (the relativist has to nod at how "wrong" is defined in their line, the objectivist at theirs). Then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them — and for each crux a concrete case or thought experiment both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

Here's the kind of thing I mean by a concrete case, and you can do better: one of my cultures has a custom where forgiving someone without being asked is treated as a serious wrong — a theft of the offender's chance to make amends on their own terms — and I've noticed that readers from very different backgrounds still converge on treating a character who violates this custom as being in the wrong in roughly the same proportion. Is that evidence of shared underlying values under different surface norms? Or does it just show I wrote the scene to be legible to my readers? Another one that keeps coming up between my two friends: take two members of very different moral communities, both fully informed about each other's practices and history, and put them in ideal conditions for as long as you like — do their judgments converge? Each side needs to say what they'd predict, and also what would count against their own view here, since that's the part adversarial collaborations always dodge.

Also, and this is the part I most want to see: my suspicion, after listening to them for a year, is that some nontrivial chunk of their fight is verbal — the objectivist hears "relativism" as denying that anyone is really wrong about anything, the relativist hears "objectivism" as claiming some particular culture already has the answers. If you think part of the dispute dissolves once the wording is fixed, say which part and exactly how. And if some part doesn't dissolve — the part that would survive even after all the terminology is agreed on — isolate that part, because that's the part I need to understand to write the ending of this book.

Format: a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table, then the cruxes each with its case and the two predictions side by side. Keep it something both of my friends could actually put their names on — nothing slanted, nothing vacuous.
