I've been reading about Adam Smith's take on empire and colonies and I want an episode analysis. Quick recap so we're on the same page: Smith published the Wealth of Nations in 1776, right as Britain was losing the thirteen colonies, and his argument there was basically that the whole mercantile empire was a con run for merchants and manufacturers — the monopoly on colonial trade cost consumers and the nation more than the colonies were worth in revenue, and if Britain actually wanted to keep the colonies it should offer them representation in parliament proportioned to what they contributed in taxes, which he knew the public at home would never swallow. He also predicted the colonies would end up outweighing Britain and breaking off one way or another.

So: analyze the American Revolution through Smith's framework. Map who the players are on his account — the merchant interests who captured the state, the domestic public whose national pride was doing a lot of the work, the colonists, the ministry — and what his framework says each of them was actually optimizing for. Then say what it predicts about how the thing ends, and where it breaks. Does it explain why the colonists revolted over taxes that were fairly small in absolute terms, or does that need republican ideology, the slave economy, western land speculation — things Smith's framework has no slot for? Be honest about that rather than stretching the theory to cover it.

Then run it the other direction: what does the episode do to Smith? He predicted seperation sooner or later and it happened, which sounds like a win, but he also thought a peaceful federal union was genuinely on the table if Britain offered representation early — was that a real possibility or the one place he let his own hopes do the arguing? And finally, does the framework travel? Would it have told Britain anything useful about holding onto Ireland, or later India, or is it strictly a theory of settler colonies under a trade monopoly and nothing else?

Don't be charitable to Smith just because he's the patron saint of free markets — the point is to find where the theory has to be supplemented or abandoned.
