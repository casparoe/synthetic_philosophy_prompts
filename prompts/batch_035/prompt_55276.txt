A podcast on language and thought covered the sorites paradox recently, and the host's summary has stayed with me as both genuinely clarifying and somehow suspect. As I recall it, he said roughly this: the ancient Greek Eubulides noticed that if you have a heap of sand and remove a single grain, what's left is still a heap; by mathematical induction, you can keep removing grains one at a time, and it remains a heap at every stage, so eventually a single grain counts as a heap. Since that's absurd, the moral is that vague words like 'heap' have no sharp boundaries — and a word without sharp boundaries doesn't have determinate meaning at all. The only way to block the argument is to insist there's some precise number of grains at which the heap ends, which is too absurd to take seriously, so most philosophers have concluded that classical logic simply fails for ordinary language. Wittgenstein essentially solved all this by showing that everyday words are family-resemblance concepts, held together by overlapping similarities rather than a common essence, which is why the paradox never really arises for them. The host closed with a practical lesson: this is why the law has to set arbitrary cutoffs like an age of majority, because the sorites shows there is no principled fact of the matter about when childhood ends.

I'm inclined to agree that there's no sharp line between heap and non-heap, but I don't believe that makes the word meaningless, and I can't see how Wittgenstein could have 'solved' the paradox when philosophers of language still argue about it. Please go through this summary claim by claim: what a philosopher working on vagueness would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong — and then tell me whether the closing point about law and arbitrary cutoffs follows from whichever parts of the summary survive correction.
