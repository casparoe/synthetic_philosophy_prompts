For our exam we are supposed to be able to reconstruct the position of the philosopher who famously argued that the numbers do not count, along with the main objections to it. The problem is that I missed the class where we went through the actual paper, and my study partner, who gets most of his philosophy from podcasts and explainer videos, offered our group what he called the definitive summary. Here is his account, more or less as I wrote it down:

The author, a philosopher named Taurek, argued in the 1970s that when you can save one person or five others, morality does not require you to save the five. His key move is that comparisons of better and worse can only be made from the standpoint of particular individuals: there is no legitimate standpoint from which you can add up everyone's losses and declare one outcome worse overall than the other. Since each person on both sides faces the very same loss, namely death, each of the five is no worse off than the one, and the one is no worse off than any of the five. Because the numbers give neither side a stronger claim on you, the fairest thing you can do is flip a coin between the two groups, which amounts to giving each of the six individuals an equal one-in-six chance of being saved. On this view you are actually required to flip, and if you save the five simply because there are more of them, you are effectively treating the one person's life as worth less than each of the others' lives. Critics replied that this cannot be right, because each of the five can protest that you could have saved them and chose not to, and the author never really had an answer to that objection.
