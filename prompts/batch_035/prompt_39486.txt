A friend and I spent the whole of last night on a single question — whether a hypocrite is in a position to blame the very thing she does — and we finished exactly where we started, which has begun to seem diagnostic in itself. What I want from you is not a verdict on who won but a diagnosis of the stalemate: what kind of disagreement is this, and what, if anything, would count as progress?

The terrain, as we staked it out. Take a lecturer who plagiarizes when it suits her and then delivers a cold dressing-down to a student caught cheating, or a driver who texts at the wheel but curses freely at anyone who drifts lanes. We found we agree on almost everything about these cases: the student and the lane-drifter are culpable; the complaint made against them is true; nothing in it is insincere at the moment it is made. We agree, too, that something is wrong with the hypocrite's blaming — the same words rankle from her in a way they would not from a person with clean hands. What we could not agree on is what that something is.

My friend defends what she calls the standing view: the wrong is that the hypocrite lacks the standing to blame. The student has, she says, a claim against being blamed by this particular person — not a claim against being blamed as such, and not because the blame is false, but a claim generated by the blamer's own violation of the norm she invokes. Her most interesting move borrows from Raz's notion of an exclusionary reason: standing is not a further reason against blaming that gets weighed into the balance, but a second-order consideration that switches the first-order reasons off; the hypocrite's blame is not exactly wrong, it is inadmissible — the way a judge who took a bribe is not exactly mistaken but no longer entitled to rule. She generalizes the point: standing is also lost by meddlers, who have no business in the matter, and by those who benefit from the very practice they condemn — three routes, one inadmissibility. And she notes, I think correctly, that the retort "who are you to say so?" is not, in this context, the fallacy it is usually filed under.

I resist all of this, on two prongs. First, the ordinary-defects view: the only things wrong with the lecturer are ordinary vices, available to a complete theory without any new norm — selective enforcement, arrogance, an unfairness in exempting oneself from a standard one imposes on others. Nothing here needs a claim held by the target; it needs only a bad character in the blamer. Second, an accusation: "standing" is a term of art that relabels our shared datum — that the blame rankles — and then offers the relabeling back as an explanation. When I press her to distinguish inadmissible blame from merely vicious blame, she says the victim is wronged rather than merely witnessing a bad performance; when I ask what the victim is owed, the answer circles back to not being blamed by a hypocrite. Neither of us moved an inch, and the same impasse, I gather, has been running in the literature for decades with the same arguments on both sides.

Here is my hypothesis, offered as no more than that. The stall persists because we are answering different questions under one heading: she is describing the propriety conditions of the practice of blaming — what one may do within the practice, as one may not sue without standing — while I am evaluating the blame as a piece of behavior, asking whether any wrong occurs in the world. And behind that, I suspect, sits a genuinely substantive disagreement about what blame is: if blame is essentially a sanction or imposition, her claims and inadmissibility fall out naturally; if it is essentially a communicative protest, then the hypocrite's defect is a credibility and fairness defect in a piece of speech, and talk of claims is a category mistake. If that is right, no argument framed at the level of standing will ever move either of us, because each side's theory of blame has settled the standing question in advance.

So: is my structural hypothesis right, or is this a hidden verbal dispute, or a genuine first-order disagreement about standing that is simply hard, or bedrock intuitions about fairness that neither side can argue for? Diagnose it from what each of us actually says, as I have reported it. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a case that forces one side to bite a bullet, a distinction that splits the three routes to lost standing, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected, and why. One request about the form of your answer: separate what is settled among people who know this literature from what is genuinely contested, and do not present either theory of blame, or your own diagnosis, as though it were the consensus. If your diagnosis depends on a contested view of what blame is, flag that dependence rather than building it silently into your premises.
