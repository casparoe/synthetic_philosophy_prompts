My mate and I saw Macbeth last week (theatre tickets, then several pints after, which in hindsight was a mistake) and got into a proper three-hour row about whether the play is deterministic. She reckons the witches prove it: they tell Macbeth the future, everything they say comes to pass, so the future was fixed all along and Macbeth's choices are basically set dressing. I'm not convinced but I couldn't quite say why, so I want you to have a go at reading the play through the philosophy of future contingents — the Aristotle sea-battle stuff about whether statements about tomorrow are already true or false, and what that means for whether the future is open.

Here's the crux I keep snagging on. The prophecies all come true, but they come true through equivocation: "none of woman born / Shall harm Macbeth" is vindicated only because "Macduff was from his mother's womb / Untimely ripp'd" — a caesarean, so not 'born' — and Birnam Wood only 'comes' to Dunsinane because the soldiers cut branches to carry in front of them. So the statements about the future end up true, but true under descriptions nobody intended. Is that a fixed future, or the future being open while the wording does all the work? And does the play itself notice? Macbeth's dying complaint about the fiends that "palter with us in a double sense" and "keep the word of promise to our ear, / And break it to our hope" reads almost like he's doing philosophy of language on the battlefield.

A few things I'd want you to take seriously rather than hand-wave: (1) the first prophecy comes true *before* Duncan's murder, which is what convinces Macbeth to act at all — so the prophecy is inside the causal chain that makes the later ones true (he butchers Macduff's family to make "assurance double sure", which is precisely what makes Macduff the man who kills him). Is the play dramatising something the sea battle debate misses — prophecies that partly cause their own truth? (2) Banquo's descendants are shown as a line of kings stretching to "the crack of doom", yet within the play that prophecy conspicously does NOT come true — Fleance just vanishes after Act 3. Does the play quietly admit its own equivocations, or does that reading only work from outside the fiction? And (3) my mate's actual question, which I stil can't answer cleanly: if you follow Aristotle and say statements about future contingents lack a truth value now, what should you say about the witches? Are they uttering truths, falsehoods, or neither — and is 'true but only under a description nobody had in mind' even a category the open-future literature has room for? (If the Ockhamists help here, by all means bring them in, but don't bend anyone out of shape to fit the plot.)

So: give me a reading that's fair in both directions — don't reduce the play to a philosophy lecture, but don't distort Aristotle to fit it either — and end by saying whether you think Shakespeare has hold of something the formal debate about bivalence never quite captures. Ideally in terms I can deploy next time she brings it up, which will definitely happen.
