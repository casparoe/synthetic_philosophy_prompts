You're a philosopher of history, so please give this a proper workout. I've come round to a fairly strong presentism: I think historians not only can't escape the standards of their own time, they shouldn't try -- a historian today who brackets her condemnation of the Atlantic slave trade in order to understand slaveholders on their own terms is doing something closer to moral evasion than to rigour. But I've added a twist I don't see discussed much, and it's the part I'm least sure about: I hold an asymmetric version, on which moral presentism is fine but epistemic presentism isn't. I can blame a plantation owner for his values, but I can't blame a medieval physician for not believing in germs -- his epistemic position made that belief reasonable even tho the slaveholder's cruelty wasn't excusable in anything like the same way. So my question is: what would count against this package? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. I'm especially interested in (a) cases where the moral/epistemic asymmetry seems to break down -- e.g. whether the slaveholder's racism was a belief or a value, and whether that distinction even holds up; (b) the self-undermining worry that my own present standards are just as parochial from further down the line, so presentism licenses a kind of rotating scapegoating rather than anything stable; and (c) whether the asymmetry just quietly reimports the contextualism I claim to reject. Tell me which of these a committed presentist would concede as real damage and which they'd wave off, and be honest if the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability -- and whether that's even a problem for a view of this kind. Start with a short brainstorm of candidate defeaters before sorting them, and think carefully; don't just give me the stock lectern summary of the presentism debate.
