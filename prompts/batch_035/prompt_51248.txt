I hold that my belief in other minds is justified as an inference to the best explanation: the total pattern of another person's behavior, physiology, evolutionary history, and development is better explained on the hypothesis that they have experiences roughly like mine than on any rival hypothesis, and that is the whole of my justification for believing in them. I came to this view in a graduate epistemology seminar, after concluding that the classical argument from analogy — the version associated with Mill — is embarrassingly weak: it is an enumerative induction from a sample of exactly one, me, and there is no non-circular way to establish that I am representative of the class of organisms with my kind of body. The abductive reformulation seemed to fix everything at a stroke, because it no longer matters that I have only one case to reason from; what matters is comparative explanatory power. I've since noticed that the view carries commitments I did not fully appreciate when I adopted it, and I want to know how exposed it really is.

Specifically, I can see at least three directions of trouble. First, if the justification is abductive, my confidence that a given system is minded should move with the evidence the way confidence moves everywhere else — which means cases where behavior and experience dissociate (blindsight, split-brain confabulation, patients who report no experience while discriminating above chance) should lower my estimate of how good behavioral evidence is, and systems whose behavior resembles mine while their substrate and causal history do not (current language models are the obvious case) should raise my credence in their sentience. I am genuinely unsure whether I accept these consequences, and I want to know whether rejecting them means quietly abandoning the view. Second, there is the Wittgensteinian line that mind-attribution is not inferential at all — that behavior and avowals function as criteria for mental states rather than as evidence for them — which does not compete with my view but denies its presupposition, and I don't know what, if anything, would decide between treating that as a defeater and treating it as a change of subject. Third, there is the developmental and comparative literature suggesting that mind-attribution appears early, automatically, and with a systematic pattern of errors, which might bear on whether it is the kind of belief that is held on abductive grounds in the first place.

So: what would count against the view? Give me the observations, arguments, possible cases, and results from psychology or cognitive science that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward; say which of these a committed defender would concede as counting against the view and which they would, reasonably or otherwise, dismiss; and be honest about whether the view has been insulated by qualifications — for instance, by the move that any apparent counterexample just shows we misjudged what the best explanation was, which risks making the view unfalsifiable in a way that matters, since it is supposed to be an epistemological thesis about how a belief is justified rather than an empirical thesis with a domain of its own. Finally, tell me what you yourself would find most persuasive: a demonstration that the same abductive pattern yields the wrong verdict on some edge case, a principled reason to think explanation never gets you to experience, or something else entirely.
