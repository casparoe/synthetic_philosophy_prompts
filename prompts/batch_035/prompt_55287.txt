I sit on the ethics committee of a mid-sized hospital, and since our state's medical aid-in-dying statute took effect this spring, I have been asked to run the orientation session for our palliative care volunteers — the people who sit with patients and their families, not the clinicians. The statute follows the familiar model: available only to mentally capable adults with a terminal prognosis measured in months, self-administered after a waiting period, with conscience protections for clinicians who opt out. The volunteers will be drilled on the legal mechanics elsewhere; my charge is to convey, in under an hour, the conceptual scaffolding beneath those mechanics, so that when a patient asks a volunteer whether taking away the feeding tube is "the same thing" as what the statute permits, the volunteer understands what is and is not being asked.

Please plan the session rather than explain the doctrines to me — I know them; what I do not yet know is their teaching order. Specifically, I want from you: (1) the sequence in which the core pieces should enter — killing versus letting die, the intending/foreseeing distinction as it underwrites escalating palliative sedation, the self-administration requirement as the statute's hinge, and the prognosis gate — together with the dependency structure that fixes the order; my instinct is that self-administration cannot be motivated until the first two distinctions are in place, but I may well have the dependencies backwards, and I would like to know if any piece can be deferred entirely. (2) The single patient case that should carry the session. I am drawn to a composite patient whose pain is well controlled but who finds her progressive deterioration unbearable, because that case forces apart pain from suffering and autonomy-based justification from mercy-based justification — tell me whether it can bear the weight, and what it must include and deliberately lack. (3) The misconceptions the volunteers will bring or form, in the order they will actually surface, and the precise point in the sequence at which to head each off. My own guesses: that double effect will be heard as a loophole implying terminal sedation just is slow euthanasia; that the statute will be taken to have settled the underlying ethics rather than navigated between them; and that the six-month prognosis will read as arbitrary discrimination against the chronically ill rather than as a principled boundary whose logic connects back to the letting-die distinction. (4) One question I can put to the group at the end, the answer to which would show the distinctions have been internalized rather than memorized — ideally something a volunteer could plausibly be asked by a family member in the hallway. (5) What I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud — I currently assume the metaphysics of intention and the comparative evidence from other jurisdictions, but I am open to persuasion, including that something I have listed as essential belongs in this refuse pile instead.
