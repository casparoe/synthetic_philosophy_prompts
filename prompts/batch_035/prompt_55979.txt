I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology finishing my dissertation on the metaphysics of biological individuality, and next term I'm running discussion sections for a large undergraduate evolution course. The students come in having memorized a two-line definition of the biological species concept from their textbook and treat every other species concept as a weird alternative that only philosophers care about. I want to disabuse them of that by making the disagreement concrete, and my plan is a handout with a decision tree: students answer questions about cases and end up at the species concept they actually hold, which for most of them will not be the one they memorized.

Please write that decision tree for me. To be clear about the brief:

You are an expert in philosophy of biology with deep familiarity with the species problem literature, both the classical positions (Mayr's biological species concept, the phylogenetic species concept in its diagnosability-based and monophyly-based variants, the evolutionary species concept, the ecological species concept, the cohesion concept, phenetic clustering approaches) and the underlying metaphysical questions (are species individuals, classes, or lineages; monism versus pluralism about species concepts). Think carefully before you begin: start by brainstorming which questions genuinely split the positions, and only then assemble the tree — I want the tree built from the logical space, not from a list of names with questions reverse-engineered to reach them.

The tree should start from a shared commitment all the students have (that some species are real, that there's some mind-independent fact of the matter about how many species of, say, Galápagos finch there are) and end at leaves corresponding to the major concepts, plus, where relevant, monist versus pluralist readings of each. Each internal node should be a question a student with no philosophy background can answer from their intuitions about a concrete case, phrased so that they can't tell which answer leads where. The cases I'd like you to draw on — you may add others if you find better splitting cases — include: ring species, where gene flow never breaks down along the ring yet the ends behave as distinct species; obligately asexual lineages like bdelloid rotifers, where the interbreeding criterion has no purchase at all; frequent hybridization without merger, as in Helianthus sunflowers or wolves and coyotes; diagnosable but shallow morphological or genetic differences within an interbreeding population; and the ancestor-descendant chain, where a strict reading of any contemporary-delineated concept seems to imply that no species can ever survive a splitting event, which would make it false that Homo erectus and Homo sapiens are distinct.

Two further requirements. First, wherever a combination of answers corresponds to no defended position, say so explicitly and give a sentence on why nobody holds it — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. I want students to see the empty cells in the logical space, not just the occupied ones. Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate given your node questions, and resist the temptation to paper over the gap with a question students would only answer one way if they already knew which concept it was supposed to produce.

Format the output as the tree itself (questions, branch labels, leaves with the position name and a one-sentence gloss), followed by a short section of notes for me on the empty cells and the inseparable pairs. Keep the whole thing usable as a two-page handout. Take your time and be rigorous: the measure of success is that a student who honestly answers the diagnostic questions ends up somewhere that surprises them, and can then see exactly which commitment of theirs drove them there.
