Quick background so you know where I'm coming from: I'm a retired physical oceanographer. Forty-odd years of measuring currents from ships and moorings, and now that I've got time on my hands I've fallen down the philosophy of physics rabbit hole and can't climb back out. Something bugged me my whole career and I only recently got the words for it, so let me lay it out and then ask my question.

Practically everything we did assumed you can tell, from inside a sealed box, whether the box is rotating. A gyrocompass finds true north without ever seeing the sky. An inertial unit on a submarine tracks every turn and drift with no external reference at all. After storms, the surface currents don't just relax back — they go round in circles, clockwise in our hemisphere, with a period of about seventeen hours at our latitude, which the textbooks call half a pendulum day, and that period is set by the earth's spin even though the water is only reacting to wind. Geodesists are even more upfront about it: they distinguish gravitation from gravity, where gravity is gravitation plus a centrifugal term coming from the rotation. Somewhere in the pipeline an engineer is always told which frames count as inertial and which are rotating, and Coriolis and centrifugal terms get added or subtracted, and nobody stops to ask: rotating with respect to WHAT? The nearest large mass is the earth and we're riding with it, not turning relative to it. The stars are never consulted. Newton had his bucket — water climbing the sides when the bucket spins — and said the water turns with respect to absolute space itself. Mach came along later and said, roughly, that you should count the distant masses, and that a bucket spinning alone in an otherwise empty universe wouldn't feel a thing. And general relativity, as I understand it (correct me if I'm off), hands the job to the metric field, which seems to be neither Newton's space nor just a bookkeeping of relations among material bodies — it has its own degrees of freedom. I may be garbling some of that, tell me if so.

So here's the actual question. Take the working practice — gyrocompasses, inertial navigation, the geodesists' gravity/gravitation split, our half-pendulum-day oscillations — as a going concern, the way you might take a courtroom's rules of evidence. What does the practice presuppose about space or spacetime? Does it need Newton's absolute space, is it compatible with Mach's answer, or does it quietly require something like the metric field being a real physical thing? Where is it underdetermined — that is, would several of these views license exactly the same corrections, so the practice can't choose between them? And if we swapped the presupposed view for another, would anything in the practice actually have to change, or only the footnotes in the textbooks?

Please structure your reply like this, in this order, roughly these lengths:

4. "Where it's underdetermined" — about 150 words, be honest about which parts of the practice could NOT decide between the candidates, and keep those seperate from the parts that might.
5. "What would have to change" — about 100 words, whether any instrument, correction, or convention would come out different, or whether it's all just interpretation after the fact.
