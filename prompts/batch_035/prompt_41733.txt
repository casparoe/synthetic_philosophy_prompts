Bit of background before the actual request, because it shapes what I want out of it: I'm a retired neuroscientist — about three decades doing single-unit electrophysiology in visual cortex, first in cats, later macaques, with a late-career detour into human EEG when the animal work got politically harder. Since retiring I've gotten into philosophy of mind, which has been a humbling kind of fun: I spent my whole career measuring things I was never once asked to define. Now I can't stop tripping over the fight between the global workspace camp and the overflow camp, and it strikes me as a textbook case of two groups talking past each other while both pointing at real data. The workspace people (Dehaene-style) say a content is conscious exactly when it wins the competition and gets broadcast to a fronto-parietal workspace — consciousness IS access, more or less. The overflow people (Block-style, with Lamme's recurrent-processing version in the background) say local recurrent processing in sensory cortex is already enough for phenomenal experience, so report-based experiments systematically underestimate what's in consciousness, since reports require access and access is the bottleneck, not experience itself. Each side reads the same experiments — Sperling's partial report, change blindness, inattentional blindness, no-report pupillometry — and comes away with opposite conclusions. It reminds me of nothing so much as arguments I witnessed over neural coding in the 90s, where half the dispute was empirical and half was two labs meaning different things by "representation."

So here's the ask: write the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, in the spirit of the kind of thing Kahneman used to push for exactly because arguing in journals wasn't converging. I want the claims they genuinely agree on — including careful statements about what the experimental paradigms actually demonstrate versus what each side has merely inferred from them, since a former experimentalist is allergic to that distinction being blurred. Then the precise formulations of their disagreement, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording as fair (this is the hard part, and where slanted phrasings always creep in — "the workspace is just a report mechanism" or "overflow is just a post-hoc interpretation of iconic memory" are exactly the kind of sentences that should NOT appear). Then the cruxes: I suspect one is whether no-report paradigms can measure phenomenal content without covertly building access in through the back door, and another is whether the vivid feeling of rich peripheral detail should itself count as evidence of rich phenomenology or just as a representation of richness — but you may find better ones, and I'd trust your judgment over my hints. For each crux, name a concrete experiment, lesion case, or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance, so it's the kind of thing that could actually be adjudicated rather than re-litigated forever. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table (disagreement | workspace-side wording accepted by that side | overflow-side wording accepted by that side | verbal or substantive), then the cruxes with predictions. And if part of the dispute turns out to be verbal — say, equivocation on "conscious of" versus "consciousness as such" — say which part and why, because my hunch after a life in science is that a good chunk of it is, but I'd want it argued and not just asserted.
