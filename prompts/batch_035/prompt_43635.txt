I'm grading take-home exams for a third-year epistemology module and want a second opinion on one answer before I committ to a mark. The question I set was:

"Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge says that knowing requires a belief that tracks the truth of what's believed. Explain the two tracking conditions and how they handle ordinary perceptual knowledge, then explain why the theory seems to threaten the closure principle — the principle that if you know p, and know that p entails q, you're in a position to know q. Use the case of knowing that you have hands while not knowing that you're not a handless brain in a vat."

And here is the student's answer as submitted:

"On the tracking theory, S knows that p iff p is true, S believes p, and the belief tracks the truth of p. Tracking has two conditions. Sensitivity: if p were false, S would not believe p. Safety: if p were true, S would believe p. For example, I know I have hands: in the nearest world where I don't have hands, I've lost them in an accident, and there I believe I don't have them, so sensitivity holds. But I don't know I'm not a handless brain in a vat: the nearest world where I am a BIV is one where I'm being fed hand-experiences, so there I still believe I'm not one, so sensitivity fails. Since having hands entails not being a handless BIV, and I know that entailment, closure says I should be in a position to know I'm not a BIV — but by tracking I'm not. So Nozick has to deny closure, which he does: he bites the bullet and says knowledge just isn't closed under known entailment, full stop. So you can know p, know p entails q, and still fail to know q. This is the widely ridiculed result that you know you have hands but don't know you're not a handless BIV, and Nozick's response is essentially so much the worse for closure."
