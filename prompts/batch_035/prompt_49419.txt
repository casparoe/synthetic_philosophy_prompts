At thanksgiving my uncle went off about immigration again. His picture: a country is like a club. A golf club gets to have membership criteria and noone calls it wicked for turning applicants down; members pay dues, elect a commitee, and vote on the rules; being picky about newcomers isn't hostility to outsiders, it's just what makes the club a club. So strict immigration controls are basically admissions policy — prudence, not injustice.

A friend told me the philosopher Michael Dummett (the philosophy of language guy, apparently) spent decades actually campaigning on this and wrote a short book arguing the state's right to turn people away is much weaker than everyone assumes, and that the whole debate is infected by racist thinking no one notices. Don't worry about quoting him exactly, I'm more interested in his general line.

So audit my uncle's analogy. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the dues, the commitee, the clubhouse, getting expelled — then where the mapping breaks, and which of the breaks actually matter for the conclusion he wants (that being selective about admission isn't a matter of justice at all) versus which are just nitpicks. Say whether Dummett's stuff, or a better analogy if there is one, can explain where the club picture goes wrong rather than just asserting that it does. End with a last line starting "Verdict:" saying whether the club analogy can carry the conclusion it's being used to support.
