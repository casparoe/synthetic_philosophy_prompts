I'm helping teach a social philosophy survey and next week we pair Orwell's 1984 with Foucault's Discipline and Punish, and I want a reading of the novel through Foucault's account of disciplinary power, but I keep getting stuck on something that seems like a real problem for the pairing. Foucault's whole story is that the spectacle of sovereign power — the public torture and execution — gets displaced by quieter disciplinary techniques: surveillance, normalization, the examination, the training of docile bodies. But Orwell gives us both, and the spectacular stuff seems to be running the show. The Ministry of Love literally tortures Winston, Room 101 is engineered around his deepest fear, and O'Brien lays out the future as a boot grinding on a human face forever. So is 1984 actually a portrait of disciplinary society, or does it quietly show that Foucault's genealogy is off — that sovereign violence was never displaced, just adminstered in the background? Or is the novel smarter than that, and the torture isn't mere punishment at all: O'Brien doesn't want Winston dead, he wants him to sincerely believe 2+2=5 and to love Big Brother, which looks less like the scaffold and more like normalization pushed to its endpoint — confession and examination fused with pain.

A few things I'd want the reading to actually deal with rather than hand-wave. The telescreens: is that a panopticon (everyone watchable, no one sure when), and does Winston's thoughtcrime undercut the panoptic reading since the internalization hasn't fully taken, or actually support it, given that he has to hide in a little alcove and eventually betrays everything anyway? The proles: the Party barely disciplines them, just spectacle and cheap entertainment, which seems to cut against Foucault's claim that discipline spreads across the whole social body — or does it fit, since discipline gets applied selectively to dangerous populations in institutions? And Winston's job rewriting old newspapers at the Ministry of Truth seems like power/knowledge in its most literal form — the regime's grip depends on controlling the archive. Please also be honest about where the reading strains: it's anachronistic to run a 1949 novel through a 1975 book, Orwell's psychology of fear and the body might do work Foucault's framework has no room for, and the Big Brother personality cult is weirdly sovereign and personal for a disciplinary state. What does the novel give us that Foucault's history doesn't?
