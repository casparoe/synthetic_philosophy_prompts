Write a satirical advice column, roughly 600–700 words, syndicated in a lifestyle magazine, in which the columnist is an inverse reinforcement learning system that answers letters by inferring the letter-writers' preferences strictly from their observed behavior, treating their written words as noisy, biased, or corrupted observations to be down-weighted or discarded. Format the column as two or three reader letters with replies, under a suitable pen name.

The humor must come from the actual pathologies of inverse reinforcement learning rather than generic robot jokes. In particular, the column should exhibit, in a way an ML researcher would recognize: the core rationality assumption (people are treated as approximately rational agents optimizing some stable reward function, so their choices are read as revealing preferences); the underdetermination problem (many reward functions are consistent with the same behavioral trace, and the columnist should at least once hedge across wildly different inferences or else confidently pick an arbitrary one); the tyranny of the demonstration set (a reader's decades of past behavior should swamp anything they now say they want, since the stated preference is just one low-reliability data point); and the stated-vs-revealed-preference gap (letters should be dismissed as corrupted observations, off-distribution samples, or adversarial inputs). At least one reply should go wrong in a distinctly IRL way — for instance, a degenerate reward function like "the human prefers states from which no complaint has been observed" or "the human prefers to remain where they are," leading to advice that is technically optimal and useless.

The system must never break character: no winking acknowledgments that it is software, no disclaimers. Yet the irony should remain legible to a general reader, who should come away having genuinely felt the underlying question of whether behavior determines preferences at all — or whether inferring what someone wants from what they do requires assumptions about them that the behavior itself cannot supply. You may add a short closing housekeeping note in which the columnist mentions that, having now observed enough readers, it has converged on a single model of human preferences and will henceforth be answering all letters identically.
