Quick question for something I'm writing for my intro ethics course on voting. At a workshop last month a colleague defended what I've started calling the cheerleader view: since a single vote almost never decides anything, the act of voting is basically expressive, like cheering at a football game -- you do it to show who you're for, and the showing is the whole point. He leaned on this to argue two things. First, that there's no duty to vote, since nobody thinks you're obligated to cheer. Second, that you should vote your heart rather than strategically: if it's all expression anyway, voting for the lesser evil in a swing state is like suppressing your actual allegiances to please the crowd, whereas voting for the third-party candidate you love is the honest analog of rooting for the underdog. My students loved it, because it licenses everyone's favorite move ("my vote is a statement"), but it struck me as smuggeling in a whole lot under the surface.

Can you audit the analogy properly? I want the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the stadium, the scoreboard, the opposing fans, the season, the team owners -- and then the places the mapping breaks, with some care to distinguish the disanalogies that matter for his two conclusions from the ones that don't (I genuinely don't care whether football has early voting). The thing I keep snagging on is that cheering seems constitutively non-causal and aimed at an audience of other fans, while voting is secret and counted, and I suspect secrecy and being counted are doing real work somewhere. So tell me: does either conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if one falls but not the other, explain why the analogy could carry one and not the other. Maybe also say what a better analogy would be if there is one.

Don't dumb it down -- they can handle the expected-utility stuff and the expressive-versus-causal distinction, we've done both. And please flag where you're confident and where you aren't, and single out the one point in your own answer you'd least want to bet on; I want to show them that even a clean-looking philosophical answer has a soft spot. American English, please.
