I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, and I've gotten obsessed with how I change my mind at work when the *evidence itself* is uncertain. I was arguing with a friend who did stats in college and she said my gut calls are probably inconsistent, so I want someone to tell me if there's an actual principle behind them or not. Here are my verdicts on cases from the last few months:

A flaky integration test — fails maybe 30% of the time even when the code is fine — fails once. I update toward "my change broke something," but only a little; if the test were reliable, the same single failure would've moved me a lot. Two independent flaky tests failing on the same run: each failure should move me, and the moves should roughly combine, not just stack linearly. Me being 70% sure I glimpsed "ERROR at payment-service" while scrolling past a terminal too fast: I should update almost like I really saw it, just scaled down to 70%. When I'm genuinely 50/50 on whether a log line said WARN or ERROR, I shouldn't update at all — the uncertainty cancels out. But here's the one that bugs me: when our most overconfident teammate says "I'm 90% sure it's the cache layer," I barely move, while when the calm person with the good track record says the exact same words with the same number, I move a lot. If the rule is "update as if the evidence happened, weighted by how confident you are it happened," then the *source* of the confidence shouldn't matter, only the number — and that seems wrong to me, but I can't say why without giving up the "scaled-down glimpse" verdict, which also feels right. And one more complication: last sprint I updated hard on a test failure and only later learned that test had been failing all week for an unrelated infra reason. It felt like I should be able to take the update back, but I can't see how the weighted-average idea handles that, since the new information is about the reliability of the evidence, not about whether the evidence happened.

So, is there one rule that captures all or most of these verdicts? (Someone on a forum told me to look up "Jeffrey conditioning," but every explanation I found assumes I already know what an "evidence partition" is.) If no rule can get them all, I want you to actually pick which of my verdicts to throw out and defend that choice — don't give me three candidate frameworks and leave the choosing to me, commit to one. And tell me what your rule says about a case I haven't decided yet: the well-calibrated teammate says 60% sure, the overconfident one says 95% sure — do those cancel out, does one win, and on what grounds?
