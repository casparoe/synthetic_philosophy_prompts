For the past two years I have been running a monthly philosophy discussion group at our local community center, attended mostly by retirees who have no background in philosophy but a great deal of patience and life experience. Next month's session is on dream skepticism, and the topic has recently become uncomfortable in a way that I think makes it more important, not less: since starting a dopamine agonist for my Parkinson's diagnosis, my dreams have become extraordinarily vivid, and I regularly dream of people and places decades gone — my late wife most of all — in enough detail that for a few seconds after waking I genuinely do not know which state, the dream or the grief, is the one I am returning to. My doctor tells me this is a known side effect; Zhuangzi seems to have anticipated it by some twenty-three centuries, and I would like to do him justice in front of an audience that will include friends who have noticed I seem tired.

I do not want a lecture on what Zhuangzi meant; I want a plan for leading the session. The text I am anchoring on is the end of the second chapter of the Zhuangzi, the one conventionally translated as something like the Discussion on Making All Things Equal. Almost everyone will have heard of the butterfly anecdote — Zhuangzi dreams he is a butterfly, flies about contentedly, then wakes and, the text says, does not know whether he is a man who dreamed he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is a man. But I am more drawn to the shorter, stranger passage that appears just before it, which I will paraphrase rather than quote: while dreaming, one does not know one is dreaming; one may even try to interpret a dream within a dream; only after waking does one know it was a dream — and there will be a great awakening after which one realizes that all this was itself a great dream, even as the fool takes himself to be awake and confident that he knows who is a lord and who is a herdsman. Then the text closes with the remark that between Zhuangzi and the butterfly there must be some dividing of things — a transformation, perhaps — and this is called, roughly, the transformation of things.

Please structure the plan around the following questions. First, the order of ideas: should I open with the famous butterfly story to give the group a familiar hook, and only then reveal the great-awakening passage as the sharper blade, or does that sequencing spoil the point — and what does each opening order make it harder for the group to see afterward? Second, the carrying example: I am considering using my own experience of dreaming of my late wife, and I want your judgment on whether a raw personal example carries the idea better than a neutral one (an airport, a phone that will not dial) or whether it risks collapsing the discussion into condolences. Tell me what the right example must have and what it must lack. Third, the misconceptions, in the order the group will actually raise them: that the passage is a slogan for nihilism or relativism, so nothing matters; that it is simply the Cartesian evil-demon worry wearing silk robes, and thus already answered by whatever they remember of Descartes; that the anecdote asserts we literally are butterflies; and that vivid dreams, or reliable waking-up tests, dissolve the problem — with a note on where in the sequence each misconception should be headed off rather than merely corrected. Fourth, a closing question I can put to the room that would show they have grasped the difference between saying I cannot currently tell which state I am in and saying there is no fact of the matter to tell — since my sense is that the transformation-of-things remark gestures at the second while most readers hear the first. Finally, tell me what I should openly decline to cover in a seventy-five-minute session with this audience — I am tempted to say the whole equalizing-of-things program and any Chinese philology — and how to say so without sounding dismissive.
