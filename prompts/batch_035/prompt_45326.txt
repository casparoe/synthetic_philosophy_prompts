I've been arguing with my brother about when it's rational to give in to someone threatening you, and I've collected my gut verdicts on cases but can't tell if they add up to a principle. The cases: (1) a mugger with a gun says wallet or your life — hand it over, obviously. (2) Ransomware encrypts my laptop and wants $300 to unlock files I could rebuild in a weekend — pay, it's just cheaper, though the IT guy at my old job insisted that paying marks you as a payer and gets you hit again, and I half-believe him. (3) I think states are right to quietly pay ransom to pirates while refusing to pay hostage-taking terrorists, even though my brother says that's not a principle, just picking which threateners you happen to like. (4) A blackmailer who will demand monthly payments forever, starting small — refuse, don't even pay the first time, even though paying the mugger once was fine. (5) But when the same kind of blackmailer threatens to leak intimate photos unless I pay once, I'd probably pay — I flip on this one and can't justify it. (6) An email claims to come from a very powerful actor (my brother's version: an AI from the far future) that will simulate and torture millions of copies of me unless I send $5000 now — don't pay, and this holds even if I thought it was 1% likely, which definately makes no sense on expected-value math, yet everyone I've asked agrees with me without being able to say why.

The nearest I can get to a principle is something like "comply only in one-shot encounters where the threatener can observe your choice and where compliance mechanically ends the threat" — but that seems to condemn paying the mugger if there's any chance he follows me, and I can't see it delivering the pirate/terrorist split or explaining why (4) and (5) come out differently. A friend mentioned an idea called surrogate goals, where you pre-commit to treating a threat against anything of yours as a threat against something you'd never negotiate over, so every threat becomes a little less worth making. Does my pattern of verdicts support that move, or does it show the opposite — that I think compliance is fine exactly when compliance works? Either way, please state the principle precisely, flag which of my verdicts it has to overturn, and tell me what it predicts for a case I'm stuck on (7): a kidnapper says he'll release the hostage, but only if I leave the cash in a park first, so he can verify I paid but I can't verify he'll deliver. Keep the whole answer under 400 words.
