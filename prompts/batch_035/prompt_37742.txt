After a long and, toward the end, rather heated conversation with my friend Daniel last weekend, I have been unable to stop thinking about a proposal he defended for distinguishing manipulation from legitimate persuasion, and I would like to see the position worked out and stress-tested properly in an essay.

Daniel's proposal was roughly this: an influence attempt is legitimate persuasion only if its efficacy would survive the full disclosure of its mechanism to its target. A rational argument, he claimed, works by putting considerations in front of you that you can endorse while seeing exactly what is being done; a manipulative tactic, by contrast, works only if its workings remain hidden — if the salesperson announced that the artificial countdown on the website is there to exploit your loss aversion, the countdown would lose its power, whereas a sincere argument does not lose its power when the arguer says "here is my argument." I pushed him to make the test more precise, and he settled on what he called the delta formulation: what matters is not the total effect of an influence attempt, but its marginal effect — the difference between what the attempt achieves and what it would achieve on a fully informed, attentive, well-rested listener. Manipulation, on this view, is influence whose delta depends on the mechanism staying dark.

I left the argument with the sense that the test is both promising and somehow wrong, but I could not pin down where it goes wrong, and I would like an essay that does so. Please reconstruct the transparency test as charitably as you can, including the delta refinement, and then evaluate it against the following cases, which are the ones we kept circling back to: (1) a merchant who truthfully announces a scarcity tactic ("there are only three left, and I am telling you this partly because scarcity makes people want things more") and, remarkably, still moves product; (2) a friend's sincere emotional appeal — say, a plea to visit more often that draws its force from visible hurt — which seems to become less effective, or at least less moving, the more one coolly analyzes it, and which the test therefore seems to condemn; (3) a physician prescribing a treatment partly for its placebo effect, where the efficacy question is entangled with the disclosure question in complicated ways; and (4) default rules, such as automatic enrollment in pension schemes or opt-out organ-donation registries, whose architects are typically quite open about the behavioral science behind them.

In working through these cases, please consider two asymmetries that struck me after the fact: tactics that survive naming even though they seem manipulative (for instance, reverse psychology that works better once announced, or flattery that flatters precisely by being transparently insincere), and appeals that dissolve under analysis even though they seem paradigmatically legitimate. Assess whether these asymmetries refute the test outright, or whether the test can be repaired — for example, by distinguishing the disclosure of the mechanism from the disclosure of its target, by locating legitimacy in whether the influence routes through the target's evaluative capacities rather than around them, or by treating transparency as necessary but not sufficient. Feel free to develop any repair of your own if the ones I have sketched seem inadequate.

The essay should reach a definite conclusion: either the transparency test, in some specified repaired form, captures the manipulation–persuasion distinction, or it does not, and something else does the real work in the cases above — in which case say what that something is. I am not interested in a verdict of "it is complicated, and more research is needed"; take a stand and defend it.
