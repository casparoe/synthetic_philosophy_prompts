At a recent symposium on environmental history, I sat in on a disagreement between two researchers whose joint project I had been covering — a study of the seventeenth century that connects the Little Ice Age, successive harvest failures, and the wave of rebellions and wars from the 1640s onward. They had drafted the study together and then fell out over a single word in the conclusion: whether climate and disease should be described as "actors" in the story.

The historian, who drafted the passage, defended it. She said she is not claiming weather has intentions: an actor, in her usage, is anything whose presence or absence reorganizes the situation other parties face — a volcano closing a shipping season or a plague emptying a labor market belongs in the explanation on the same footing as a monarch or a minister, and refusing to call it an actor simply reproduces the old habit of writing history as if only humans were ever in the room.

The geographer objected that this concedes too much. Actors act, he said, and readers will hear the connotation no matter how the term is defined in a footnote; the discipline spent half a century expelling environmental determinism, and the moment a frost becomes a protagonist, every famine reads as destiny again. He added a sharper point: even granting the difference-making definition, it proves too much, since a broken axle or a silted harbor also reorganizes what everyone faces, and we do not call axles actors. What separates an actor from a cause, he said, is that an actor can respond — it registers what others do and adjusts — and while pathogens may pass that test through evolution, a cooling trend does not; it just happens to people.

She answered that this splits the difference oddly: if responsiveness is his criterion, then germs and pests qualify and she is happy to take them, but the deeper problem is that his alternative is not neutrality. Writing only human agents into the causal story is itself a substantive and, she thinks, false thesis — it explains a bacterium reshaping a continent's demography by leaving the bacterium out of the cast, and readers will notice the missing character even if the word "actor" never appears.

He replied that she was confusing what belongs in an explanation with who belongs in a dramatis personae, and that the whole trouble with determinism was never the omission of non-humans but the quiet conversion of pressures into purposes — a conversion her framing, however hedged, invites readers to perform. At that point they agreed to let it rest, and, since my article needs to describe their disagreement rather than pretend it is settled, I would be grateful for a careful adjudication: who is right at each step of the exchange, whether the historian's definition of "actor" can survive the geographer's broken-axle objection, and whether anything in the dispute is a false opposition that both of them are missing.
