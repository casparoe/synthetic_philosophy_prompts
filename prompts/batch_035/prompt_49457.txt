My roommate and I have been arguing for like a year about evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics and we finally decided to try writing down what a real adversarial collaboration between us would look like. She's basically in the Sharon Street camp: our moral faculties got built by fitness pressures that were totally indifferent to moral truth, so if objective moral facts exist, the fact that our beliefs line up with them would be a coincidence that needs a story, and the evolutionary story is a rival to the tracking story. I'm a stubborn moral realist and I keep pushing the third-factor reply in the spirit of David Enoch: the very features of creatures that made cooperation fitness-enhancing (vulnerability, suffering, mutual dependence) are themselves the moral facts, so the "coincidence" is explained without saying our faculties track anything spooky.

Write the joint statement we should be able to both sign. I want:

1. A numbered section of claims we both accept -- about what evolutionary biology and moral psychology actually show, what the debunking argument needs to succeed, and what it does NOT show by itself.

2. A disagreements table where each residual disagreement is phrased so that whoever holds the view would accept the wording as fair to their side. If some of our fight turned out to be verbal (we each use "coincidence" and "reliable access" differently, I think), flag which parts are just about words and why.

3. The cruxes. I'll suggest a few but you refine them: (a) the counterfactual history test we came up with -- if the evolutionary record had gone differently, say a lineage where ruthlessness toward outgroup members was fitness-optimal while the moral facts stayed fixed, would our evolved beliefs have come out different? She says yes, and that this proves no tracking; I say it only shows our access is mediated by contingent history, not that it's unreliable. Work out whether this test actually separates us or whether we each interpret it to fit our priors. (b) The generalization problem -- she admits the argument threatens perceptual and mathematical beliefs too unless she draws a line, I say if it doesn't draw a line it self-undermines. (c) Whether beliefs about distant strangers and future generations, which don't obviously serve fitness, give the realist an asymmetry the debunker owes us an account of.

4. For each crux, a concrete argument or thought experiment we'd both agree bears on it, plus what each of us would predict about it, written down in advance so we can't weasel later.

Start with the joint statement itself, no preface or commentary -- lead with the claims we agree on, then everything else. Keep the language plain, neither of us is a professional philosopher and we don't want it sounding like a journal article. Don't declare a winner; the whole point is a document both of us could put our names on.
