I serve on my city council — I drive a delivery route for a living and my schooling ended at high school, but I've spent years thinking about nuclear strategy on my own, partly because I grew up downwind of a missile field. I need a written opinion to circulate before next month's vote, and I'm asking you to draft it for me.

The situation: the state emergency-management office has offered a matching grant to retrofit the basement of our new community recreation center as a nuclear fallout shelter — reinforced doors, filtered air, and supplies to keep six hundred people alive for two weeks — on the condition that the city run an annual public drill and post permanent signage. The county emergency manager is enthusiastic. Two of my colleagues say refusing free protection for our residents would be indefensible. A third says shelters are theater, and worse: that running drills and posting signs teaches people that a nuclear war is the kind of thing a city can get through.

My own worry, which I've worked out by myself and can't find addressed anywhere: deterrence works because the weapon is too horrible to use, and every step a city takes to make the war survivable seems to me to shave a little off that horror. Multiply us by every city on both sides, and I worry that the shelter under our rec center is a small vote for a world where somebody, someday, finds the math workable. My colleague argues the opposite: a country visibly able to take a hit is a country whose promise to strike back is more believable, and that believability is what keeps the missiles in their silos. I honestly cannot tell which way the argument cuts, or whether it's the sort of thing that can be settled at all.

Draft the council's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasoning my colleagues and the public could actually engage with, and a recommendation concrete enough to act on at the meeting. Treat both the horror argument and the credibility argument seriously enough that a supporter of whichever one loses would recognize their view in the memo rather than a caricature of it.

And here is the part I care about most: I don't know the literature, so I can't tell where the real disagreements lie. Mark clearly which claims the memo rests on are essentially settled among people who study this — including, where it matters, what is reasonably known about how much good such a shelter would actually do, and how that depends on the scenario — and which claims are genuinely contested. Where something is contested, present the strongest version of each side and say plainly that it is contested; don't quietly pick a winner and dress it up as prudence or common sense.
