I'm about to start comparing theories of decision under ambiguity, and I want the standard of evaluation fixed before I look at any particular theory. Write the desiderata: the requirements any adequate theory of choice in situations where probabilities are not given must meet, with the case behind each requirement, and with your own view of which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps.

Set up the test cases the theory must face, since the desiderata should be motivated by them. In the two-color Ellsberg problem, urn A is known to contain 50 red and 50 black balls, while urn B contains 100 red and black balls in unknown proportion; most subjects prefer betting on red from A to betting on red from B, and also prefer betting on black from A to betting on black from B. In the three-color problem, a single urn holds 30 red balls and 60 balls that are black or yellow in unknown proportion; most subjects prefer betting on red to betting on black, and prefer betting on black-or-yellow to betting on red-or-yellow. It is settled that each pattern is inconsistent with expected utility maximization given any single probability assignment over the unknowns, and I want that treated as fixed; the desiderata should settle what a theory owes us in light of it.

The requirements I want you to develop and motivate should include at least the following, though you may add others and should argue for your ordering: (i) a declared position on whether the theory vindicates or corrects the modal choices, with the price paid openly — a theory that calls them rational owes a story about what goes wrong in money-pump and Dutch-book arguments aimed at ambiguity attitudes, while one that calls them errors owes an account of why sophisticated subjects keep making them; (ii) no collapsing of ambiguity into compound risk — the theory should predict, or at least permit, the experimental finding that subjects who readily reduce two-stage lotteries still pay a premium to avoid ambiguity, which is what separates genuine second-order-probability models from ambiguity models; (iii) accommodation of the comparative and framing effects — ambiguity aversion that shows up only when ambiguous and unambiguous options are evaluated side by side, and the sign reversals between the gain and loss domains; (iv) an operational, non-circular measure of ambiguity — not whatever the ambiguity-averse agent happens to shun; and (v) a position on dynamic choice — the known tension in which an ambiguity-averse agent refuses a bet now that they would accept after updating on a partition of states, so that some combination of dynamic consistency, consequentialism, and non-neutral ambiguity attitudes appears to be jointly unsatisfiable. For requirement (v) especially, say which leg of the trilemma you would give up and why, and flag any other pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — if the requirements list is itself partly a no-go result, that is the part I most want to see. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude actual theories.

Close with a brief scoreboard — one short paragraph each — for the three main families: subjective expected utility with an error story for the Ellsberg choices, maxmin expected utility, and smooth second-order models. Keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scoring.

An epistemic note on how I want the piece written: throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. The inconsistency results above are settled; whether the sure-thing principle is a requirement of rationality, whether ambiguity attitudes can survive the dynamic arguments, and what exactly distinguishes ambiguity from compound risk are all contested — present them as such, and do not upgrade any camp's favored position to the consensus. Where you state a requirement that really amounts to taking sides in one of these disputes, say so rather than presenting it as neutral.
