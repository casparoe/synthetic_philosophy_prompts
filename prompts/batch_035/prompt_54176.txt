I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a long-form magazine, and I'm working on a piece about the global wave of statue removals and renamings — Confederate monuments, colonial figures, contentious street names, and the quieter disputes over named fellowships and lecture series. Somewhere in the piece I'd like to give readers a way to locate themselves in the argument, but the standard op-ed framing (keep it up or tear it down) is too crude to do that honestly. The interesting disagreements sit at a level below the verdict: about what a monument actually *does* when it stands in a public square for a century.

So rather than write the usual pro/con sidebar, I want a decision tree that starts a reader at the concrete case of a statue of a morally mixed historical figure in a city square — slaveholder, colonial administrator, eugenics advocate, take your pick of examples — and walks them, question by question, to a position. The tree should be built from the logical space, not from a list of schools, and each branch node must be answerable without knowing any position labels. I'd like the following axes to appear somewhere among the nodes, though you're free to add others:

1. Whether continued display asserts anything *now* — whether a monument is an ongoing act of the community that keeps it, or just an inert artifact that stopped saying anything when it was erected.
2. If it does assert something, whether the assertion is endorsement of the person, mere gratitude for one achievement, or something in between (a "we owe this man" reading that doesn't require admiring him).
3. Whether the honoree's wrongdoing counted as wrongdoing by the moral standards of his own time and community — I've noticed that "everyone owned slaves then" and "he was unusually cruel even for his era" get readers to different places, and I want that distinction to do work.
4. Whether the medium matters: whether a statue in a square, a street name invoked daily by people who never chose it, and a named fellowship used on a CV every year should come apart in the analysis, or whether they're all one case.
5. What remedies the reader accepts — plaque, counter-monument, relocation to a museum, removal, destruction — and whether remedy choices are downstream of principle or independent gut reactions.

The leaves should include, at minimum: principled retentionism; contextualized retention (the plaque defense); museum-relocationism; counter-monumentalism; straightforward removalism; and whatever maximalist view follows from applying the same logic to every name in sight, including the awkward ones (founders, figures everyone lionizes). For any combination of answers that nobody seems to occupy, include it anyway and say in one sentence whether you think the slot is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And if your tree genuinely cannot separate two leaves — say, the plaque defenders from the relocationists, if both agree on everything the tree asks about and differ only on remedy — flag that pair explicitly rather than letting the tree pretend to a separation it can't perform.

Since this will run alongside an interactive graphic, please deliver the tree as structured data rather than prose: a JSON object with three top-level fields. First, "nodes": an array of objects with fields "id", "question" (phrased so it can be answered cold), and "answers" (an array of objects with "label" and "leads_to", where "leads_to" is either another node's id or a leaf's id). Second, "leaves": an array of objects with fields "id", "position_name" (or "unnamed"), "one_line_statement", "awkward_commitment" (the consequence of holding it that the reader will least enjoy), and "occupancy_note" (defended today, historically occupied but abandoned, or apparently empty). Third, "unseparated_pairs": an array of objects with fields "leaves" and "why_the_tree_cannot_split_them". Aim for seven to nine branch nodes and six to nine leaves. Keep the question language plain enough for a general magazine readership — no jargon that requires a philosophy degree — and American English throughout.
