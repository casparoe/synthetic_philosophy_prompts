My grandmother moved in with us in the spring after her second stroke, and it's put me in a bunch of conversations I wasn't prepared for. The one I can't stop chewing on happened at thanksgiving with my cousin's boyfriend, a neuroscience PhD student, who laid out an argument that I think proves way to much, but I genuinely can't find the bad step. It goes like this: (1) moral status comes in degrees — a human matters more, morally, than a mouse, which matters more than a beetle, and pretending otherwise is just squeamishness; (2) whatever grounds status has to be something about the organism itself, not species labels, since species membership is biological bookkeeping and can't do moral work; (3) what plausibly does the work is the machinery for experience — suffering, wanting, pleasure — and that machinery is neural; (4) more of the right machinery means more of the capacity, the way a bigger engine means more horsepower; (5) so moral status scales with how much of that machinery you've got, which in practice he says means cortical neuron counts. He's not naive about the obvious counterexample — elephants and whales beat us on raw brain size — so he's already patched the argument once: it's not absolute size, it's neurons in the relevant structures, and by that measure humans are back on top (something like three times the cortical neurons of an elephant, I think he said, maybe more).

Here's my problem. Run the argument his way and it doesn't stop at ranking species. My grandmother lost a chunk of cortex to the strokes and the dementia keeps taking more — her scans literally show the brain shrinking. If status scales with the machinery, then her moral status is objectively lower now than five years ago, lower than her neighbor's, and there was some afternoon in the past when it crossed below mine. I can't accept that. But each premise still looks okay to me one at a time: status really does seem graded, species membership really does seem like a bad ground, experience really is what matters morally, and experience really is carried by neural machinery. He says he's happy to bite the bullet — shrugs and says it doesn't mean anyone gets to treat her worse, only that the accounting has changed — but a view on which her status has a downward trajectory feels like a view that proves too much. And I can't shake the feeling that the fact he had to patch the theory once already, specifically to keep humans on top, is itself the tell. So, help me out: which step should I reject, what does rejecting it cost, and is there a version of his view that keeps graded status across species without making moral status something a person can slowly bleed out through their ears? For what it's worth, if I have to give something up I'd rather keep (1) than keep (4) — I'm fine with status being graded, I just can't accept that grandma's is in decline.
