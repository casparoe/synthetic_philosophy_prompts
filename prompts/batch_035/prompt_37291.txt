so my friend and i had a huge argument last night about Dennett's color phi thing — you know, two dots flash, one red one green, and subjects swear they saw the dot change color partway across the screen, even though the color info wasn't even available until the second dot. I walked away annoyed but also thinking my friend basically won, and that got me thinking about my philosophy of mind seminar, because my students are doing with this topic exactly what my friend accused ME of doing: memorizing the two labels and thinking the job is done.

Here's the argument, so you know what I want the exam to capture. Dennett says there are two ways to explain what's going on. On the "Stalinesque" version, the brain holds the feed back a bit and edits it before it ever reaches consciousness, so the subject never experienced anything but the doctored version. On the "Orwellian" version, the subject really did experience the unedited version and then their memory of it got rewritten almost immediately. My friend's point, which I now grudgingly accept, was that arguing about which one is TRUE is a trap — both stories presuppose there's some determinate fact about when the content "became conscious," which is exactly the Cartesian-theater clock Dennett is trying to get rid of. That's why he says something like: the question "but when did I REALLY experience it?" has no answer, not a hidden one. My students, though, will happily write "Dennett says it's Orwellian, memory is just rewritten, consciousness is delayed" and move on. That's a C answer dressed up as an A answer.

So: write me one essay question for the final. Upper-level seminar, 90 minutes to answer. The key constraint: don't use phi itself as the case, they've seen it too many times. Instead give them a fresh but structurally parallel case you invent — some situation where a subject's retrospective report seems to attribute content to an experience that, on the timeline, they couldn't have had. Maybe something auditory, or something with a delayed display, your call. The question should be worded so that the tempting move is to correctly explain both revisions and then pick one — and only the students who see WHY the picking is the trap get the top marks.

Then give me a model answer, roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain. I want the scheme to say explicitly: a B answer correctly explains the Stalinesque and Orwellian options and commits to one with some argument; an A answer has to show that the forced choice presupposes the determinate timing the theory rejects, and say what follows from denying that there's a fact of the matter (e.g., for how we should treat the subject's first-person report). If the model answer can also gesture at how Libet-style backwards referral relates, without assuming students ever read Libet, work a few bonus marks for that in.

One more thing — no asking students for citations or reading lists, they get nothing but the question and their own understanding. Keep the whole thing answerable from what a good student carries in their head.
