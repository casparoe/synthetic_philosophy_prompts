I keep running into the approval-directed vs goal-directed agents distinction (from Paul Christiano, I think) and I can't get it to sit right in my head. The pitch as I understand it: instead of giving an AI a goal and letting it plan over long horizons, you have it evaluate each candidate action by predicting whether its overseer would approve, then pick the action with the best predicted approval. Supposedly this avoids locking in a shaky goal early on and keeps a human in the loop on each decision.

But here's my confusion: doesn't the agent just end up goal-directed anyway, with "maximize predicted approval" as its goal? All the usual instrumental convergence worries — acquiring resources, preventing shutdown, deceiving the overseer so the approvals keep flowing — seem to apply one level up. Is the reply supposed to be that predicting approval is a local, myopic task, so the agent never has to model long-term consequences? But then how does it take any nontrivial action, since almost anything worthwhile has long-run effects the overseer can't forsee when judging a single step? And isn't wireheading just waiting to happen once the approval signal becomes the thing being optimized rather than the thing the signal is about?

So basically: does the approval-directed vs goal-directed distinction do real work, or is it just relocating the goal and hoping the relocation is load-bearing? Please walk me thru the actual argument, including whatever the standard response to the "it's just a goal about approval" objection is.
