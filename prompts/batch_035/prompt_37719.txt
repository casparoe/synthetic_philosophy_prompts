I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about the knowability principle — the thought, familiar from verificationist and anti-realist traditions, that every truth is in principle knowable. He pressed the Church–Fitch result, and we both know how the basic proof runs: if every truth is knowable, and knowledge distributes over conjunction, then from the admission that some truth goes unknown it follows that some truth is unknowable — the Moorean conjunction of a truth with its never being known. So the unrestricted principle conflicts with even modest non-omniscience. I conceded the unrestricted version. What I want to hold is a restricted one: every non-epistemic, contingent truth about the concrete world is knowable in principle. My thought was that the conjunction Fitch's proof manufactures is itself partly epistemic, so my restriction lies outside the proof's reach — though near the end my friend claimed, none too clearly, that the proof can be re-run with a disjunction or conditional embedding, so that the offending conclusion gets smuggled back inside a larger non-epistemic truth, and I confess I did not follow him.

Tell me what would actually count against my restricted position. Does the restriction genuinely block the Fitch argument or merely postpone it — can the proof be repaired so that the unknowability of some plain, contingent truth about the world follows from principles I am still committed to, and if so, is that repair fatal or should a defender dig in on the status of the premises it uses? Beyond that: what should I make of the fact that the restriction's own boundary is murky (is the claim that no epistemic truths are in play itself a non-epistemic truth?), of truths that physics suggests are unknowable in principle rather than merely in practice — goings-on beyond the cosmological horizon, information destroyed in black holes — and of the worry that my appeal to knowability "in principle," by idealized knowers, has insulated the view so that nothing could ever count against it? I would also like to know whether the restricted principle retains any of the semantic significance the unrestricted one was supposed to have, or whether I have traded a refuted view for a harmless platitude. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what a committed defender of restricted knowability should concede from what they can resist, and tell me what you would find most decisive — I suspect it is the question of whether my friend's repair of the proof works, so be blunt about it.
