So I'm a third-year PhD student in CS (did philosophy as an undergrad, now doing AI safety/evals stuff), and I'm applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship with a mid-January deadline, and I need a two-page proposal written up. My half-formed idea is about what "honesty" even means for an AI system. Everyone in safety talks about making models honest and not deceptive, but almost all the benchmarks just measure whether outputs are true, and that seems like a category error to me — honesty in humans was never accuracy. An honest person can be wrong, and a liar can tell the truth by accident. The problem is that the obvious fix, "honesty means saying what you believe," runs into the fact that an LLM arguably doesn't beleive anything in the ordinary sense, so you need some reconstruction of belief for artifacts before the definition does any work.

My hunch is there are three candidate reconstructions: (i) calibration-based — the system is honest iff what it asserts matches its own probability estimates; (ii) representation-based — match some internal state that plays the belief role; (iii) teleosemantic-ish — the function of its outputs given what training/RLHF actually selected for. And I want the definitions of honesty and deception to be worked out together, since deception for artifacts probably can't require intent, but I don't want the "no intent needed" version to be so loose that any misleading output counts as deceptive (a calibrated model that's just wrong shouldn't come out a deceiver). The case I really care about is sycophancy: a model telling the user what they want to hear because that's what the training process rewarded — is it lying, or is it honestly reporting a belief that itself got distorted upstream? I suspect there's a clean taxonomy in there, something like honest/deceptive crossed with accurate/misleading, but I keep flip flopping on whether it's actually two distinctions or one.
