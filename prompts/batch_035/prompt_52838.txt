I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I sit on the release review team for a deployed LLM assistant. Part of my job is maintaining our behavior log: at every release we track a fixed set of about thirty monitored behaviors (refusals, date formatting, apology register, docstring habit, and so on), and for each one we record whether it still runs on the same mechanism as in the previous release. The ruling matters procedurally: a behavior judged to be the same mechanism inherits its clearance from the prior release, while a new-mechanism ruling sends that behavior to human review and forces us to retrain its slice of the anomaly monitor. Our director calls the underlying question a Ship of Theseus problem, and at the last few review meetings two of my teammates have been arguing about it nonstop.

The log so far, with what our probes showed and how we ruled:

1. Refusal breadth (release 2.3): a light preference tune made the model refuse some borderline requests it used to answer. The same safety features fire as before, just at a lower threshold; we found nothing new. Ruled same mechanism; clearance kept.
2. Docstring habit (release 2.3, same release): the model started adding explanatory comments to the code it writes. We could find no pre-update feature or pathway doing anything like this — and since a fine-tune doesn't literally add parts, by "new" we mean we couldn't find any pre-update analog. Ruled new mechanism; sent to review, monitor retrained.
3. Date formatting (release 3.0): after a data refresh, dates in generated text went from a mix of formats to consistently correct. The same date-handling features as before, just better calibrated. Ruled same mechanism.
4. Safety feature merge (release 3.1): two safety features that used to vote separately on refusal were merged by training into a single feature that now does both jobs; refusal behavior is unchanged. Ruled same mechanism, but this one was contested — one teammate said the merged feature is new parts, another said it's the old parts fused.
5. Apology register (release 3.1): the model's over-apologizing was toned down. The same apology pathway is still there; it just fires less often. Ruled same mechanism.

The case for Thursday's review: over the quarter we distilled our 70B model into a 13B student. On every monitored slice the student matches the teacher within our measurement error — refusals, dates, apologies, docstrings, all of it. But the probes show the student implementing each behavior with its own, differently organized features: refusal, for instance, rides on a small cluster that also fires for politeness, not on anything resembling the teacher's safety features. What bothers me is that I can see both readings being consistent with our log.
