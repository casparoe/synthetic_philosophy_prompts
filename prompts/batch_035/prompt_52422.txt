I teach philosophy of science to second-years, ~18 students, one 90-minute session. We've just done Popper, and next up is Lakatos, but I don't want to lecture research programmes at them — I want them to actually run one, and run it badly, before they get any of the vocabulary. Design an in-class game where teams get data revealed from some hidden regularity I control (number sequences, card draws, whatever works best mechanically), and they have to explain and predict it. The one rule I want baked in: at the start each team writes a core claim on a card, and they're never allowed to revise that card — everything else they do has to be auxiliary assumptions wrapped around it. Points for correctly predicting data before I reveal it, nothing for explaining it after the fact. If a team wants out, they can't just start over — they can only defect to another existing team's programme, taking a penalty. My hope is that by the end one team has a core claim surrounded by a pile of increasingly baroque epicycles that only ever fire backwards, while another team made a risky early prediction that paid off, so the debrief can just name what they've already been doing for an hour: hard core, protective belt, novel vs post hoc accommodation, progressive vs degenerating, why nobody abandons a programme until a rival shows up. Give me the concrete mechanics and timeline, a scoring scheme that reliably produces atleast one degenerating programme without me rigging it, the debrief mapping, and an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time (my worry is teams quietly cheating on their core card, or one team guessing my rule right away and steamrolling — how do I handle those?). Also flag where the game misleads: in real science there's no facilitator with a fixed true rule, and nobody's competing for grant money.
