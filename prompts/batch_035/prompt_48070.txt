In my course on the ethics of war we've just reached Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and my professor keeps leaning on an analogy that historians of the bombing campaign also use: the atomic bombings were the firebombing of Tokyo continued by other means — Hamburg, Dresden, Tokyo, and then Hiroshima — so that by August 1945 the moral line had already been crossed, and the bomb was a difference of degree, not of kind. In discussions the analogy is made to do two jobs at once: it deflates the claim that Hiroshima was a uniquely monstrous act that deserves its own moral category, and it relocates the real question to whether the whole campaign of area bombing against cities was justified. I'd like an audit of the analogy for my term paper. Lay out what maps onto what — the target city, the civilian deaths, the deliberate destruction of the urban core, the acceptance of mass civilian casualties as a means of pressure — and then work through where the mapping breaks: the radiation casualties, including delayed and heritable harm with no counterpart in incendiary attack; the fact that a single bomb and a single decision replaced thousands of sorties, lowering the cost and visibility of repeating the act; and the precedent the weapon set for wars that fortunately never came. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusions from the ones that don't, and tell me whether either conclusion survives once they're on the table — or whether the analogy was carrying the deflation all along. Please write in continuous prose only: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
