For the last three years my wife and I have been renovating an old farmhouse we bought cheap. We are perhaps two-thirds finished, badly over budget, and a structural engineer recently told us the remaining work will cost roughly what we have already spent. My brother, who did our books for the first year, says we should stop and sell. When I told him we had put too much into the house to walk away now, he answered that I was throwing good money after bad. I have been turning that proverb over ever since, and I have come to suspect it is several claims wearing one coat, some of them obviously right and some of them not, and I cannot tell which claim my brother actually needs for his conclusion about the house.

On its face the proverb seems to say that money already spent should count for nothing in deciding what to do next, that the future looks the same whether I spent eighty thousand dollars on the house or nothing at all. I can see why that would be true of the dollars themselves. But several things I have noticed in my own case keep making me doubt that the proverb is only saying that, and I would like to know whether they are complications the proverb has quietly absorbed or just my own rationalizations. When I imagine abandoning the project, the years and money spent seem to curdle into waste, whereas finishing somehow justifies them retroactively, and I cannot fully convince myself that this difference in how my past looks to me is nothing, since my future life contains that looking-back either way. I also suspect that some of what passes for respecting sunk costs is really just bookkeeping: the wiring and the new roof are sunk costs of the kitchen, but they are also improvements to the whole house that a buyer will pay for, so the question of whether continuing is honoring the past or investing in the future seems to depend on where you draw the project's boundaries. And there is the matter of my wife's uncle, who has abandoned one project after another for fifty years and now cannot start anything; each of his decisions to quit may have been defensible in isolation, yet something about him makes me think the decisions were not made in isolation at all.

What I would like is a careful menu, not an essay defending one line. Please lay out the distinct precise claims the proverb could be expressing, from the plainly true to the substantive to the false, and for each one say whether it is a platitude, a defensible principle, or a mistake, and what would have to be true for it to apply to a case like mine. Then tell me which reading, if any, actually supports my brother's advice to sell, and whether anything on the list could support finishing, or whether my instinct to see it through is just the fallacy he says it is. I am not asking you to decide the renovation for me, though I confess I would not mind knowing whether the honest answer is that the proverb settles nothing until the bookkeeping question is settled first.
