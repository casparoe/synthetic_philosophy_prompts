I spent the better part of my career in physical acoustics -- thirty-two years at a national lab, mostly ultrasonics and signal processing -- so for most of my life if you asked me what music is I'd have said pressure waves, and what's *interesting* about music is the harmonic series and a good deal of encultured conditioning on top. Since retiring I've gotten deep into philosophy, and this past year a reading group here in town worked through Schopenhauer. His view of music stopped me cold: every other art, he says, copies appearances -- the Ideas, via particular things -- but music is supposed to be different in kind, a direct copy of the will itself, the thing behind the appearances, not just another picture of the visible world. I went in expecting mystical hand-waving and came out half-convinced, which was not the plan.

Here's the version of the view I've actually settled into, which I want stress-tested. Call it an isomorphism thesis: what tonal music does is track the *form* of willing -- striving, suspension, partial satisfaction, restless renewal -- in its own medium. The leading tone straining toward its resolution, an appogiatura leaning and needing to fall, a modulation away from home and the long structural return, even the way a cadence gives relief and then leaves you oddly empty a few bars later: these mirror the dynamics of desire-and-satisfaction not as *content* (music isn't about my desire for anything in particular) but as structure. That, plus the fact that music moves us without depicting anything at all and without concepts, is why it feels like it reaches something the other arts only represent. My old instincts push back -- nothing wrong with pressure waves! -- but the isomorphism story lets me keep the physics and still explain the peculiar grip. That's my position.

What would count against it? I can guess at some candidates and I want a fair sorting of them, from fatal to merely awkward: congenital amusics who can't track pitch relations but report being deeply moved (does that show the isomorphism does no work?); musical traditions built on scales and syntaxes that don't run on tension-and-resolution anything like the European common practice stuff -- Javanese gamelan tuning, for instance, or the stronger cross-cultural results on how much consonance preference is learned rather than acoustic; and the prosody explanation, that the expressive contour of melody is just recycled speech intonation, which would make the whole thing far less mysterious and far less Schopenhauerian. There's also what my old labmate used to call the flexible-analogy problem: given enough ingenuity you can map any formal structure onto any dynamical story, so maybe my "isomorphism" is unfalsifiable by construction. So -- which of these would an honest defender of the view concede as real defeaters, which would they wave off as missing the point (since the will sits outside space, time, and causality, maybe no experiment could touch the thesis at all), and is that insulation a fatal flaw or just what kind of claim this is? Rank the defeaters for me. For what it's worth, the cross-cultural evidence is what I'd personally find most persuasive either way, and I'd rather hear that I'm wrong than that my view can't lose.
