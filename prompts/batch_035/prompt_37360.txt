So last night my friend Maya and I got into a huge argument that went til like 2am about the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism. She was doing the classic Rawls/Nozick-style move — utilitarianism wrongly treats people as mere containers of utility, it allows one person's huge gain to compensate another person's crushing loss, and that ignores that each of us lives exactly one life from the inside. Fine as far as it goes. But I kept pushing her: what exactly is the objection enforcing? Is it a claim about compensation (that losses to A can never be made up for by gains to B), or a claim about aggregation itself (that there's something suspect about summing wellbeing across persons in the first place)? She'd flit between the two depending on the example, and eventually we realized we might be onto something: the objection as usually stated bundles at least two distinct complaints, and I suspect the famous putative instances of it — the surgeon/transplant case, the utility monster, the scapegoat — mostly trade on the compensation version, which a rights-based or prioritarian view can capture without any special doctrine about separateness at all. If that's right, then "separateness of persons" as its own objection might be doing less work than everyone assumes, and the interesting question is whether any residual, distinctly aggregative version survives once you peel the compensation reading off.
