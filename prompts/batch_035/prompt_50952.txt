I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about whether one person's welfare can be compared with another's, and I've been going by my gut on cases. Comparing the badness of one person's migraine with another person's stubbed toe: confident, the migraine is worse. Ten years of mild chronic pain for one person against one year of severe pain for another: still confident, I'll rank them. Comparing how much a devoted foodie enjoys a great meal with how much a contemplative monk enjoys his morning prayer: here I start to stammer. A parent saying the benefit to their child outweighs the parent's own sacrifice: I accept it readily. A health ministry ranking treatments in a cost-effectiveness table, aggregating gains and losses across millions of very different lives: I accept that too. But when someone suggests that a person with unusually intense pleasures and pains — a utility monster — should count for more in the social calculus, I want to refuse, even though that seems to follow if the comparisons are generally fine. And when someone born with a condition that limits many activities reports that their life is going well, I resist the conclusion that their life is worse than average — yet I'll cheerfully say a person in a low mood this week is worse off than their cheerful colleague. The best principle I can come up with is that comparisons work when there is a shared, familiar dimension of experience — pain, hunger, mood — and break down or become offensive when the lives or values being compared are too different. Test that principle against my cases: where does it hold, where does it leak (I suspect the parent and the ministry are already trouble for it), and what does it predict for a case I haven't given — say, whether we can claim a net improvement when a policy takes a small loss from each of a million comfortable people and gives a large gain to a hundred thousand poor ones? Finally, tell me which of my verdicts you'd advise me to give up, since I doubt all of them can survive together.
