Population ethics has been going in circles for roughly forty years, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another tour of the positions. Ever since the 1970s exchange between Sikora and Narveson over whether creating happy people, as opposed to making people happy, benefits anyone, the pattern has been fixed: Parfit's mere addition paradox shows that the obvious principles generate the Repugnant Conclusion; someone proposes a repair — critical-level views, person-affecting restrictions, variable-value or lexical theories; and the repair turns out to violate an intuition that the original theory had made us care about, culminating in Arrhenius's impossibility theorems, which show that no population axiology can jointly satisfy a short list of conditions each of which seems undeniable on its own. Almost nobody changes their mind, and the debaters are rarely disagreeing about any empirical fact.

My working hypothesis is that the standoff isn't a substantive disagreement about population at all, but a collision between two roles that the word "better" plays: ranking outcomes for a fixed set of people, which we understand well, and ranking outcomes across additions and subtractions of people, where there is no one for the extra goodness to be good for. On this view the two camps are answering different questions under a single heading, and the impossibility theorems are simply what that collision looks like from the inside. Is that right? Or is there a genuine substantive disagreement hiding underneath — about whether lives barely worth living are positive at all, about aggregation, or about whether existence can be a benefit — such that the debate is hard rather than ill-posed?

Please diagnose the disagreement using what the sides actually say, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a new theory, a different kind of formal result, a stipulative splitting of the question into two, or an argument that we should give up on a single coherent answer and accept pluralism about population size. I'm asking because I keep running into the same split in the public pronatalist debates about falling birth rates, where both sides recycle the philosophical positions without noticing they're at an impasse; but the diagnosis is the priority, not a survey.
