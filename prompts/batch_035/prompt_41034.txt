My philosophy-of-mind seminar has spent three weeks on the color phi phenomenon — two stationary spots of different colors flashed in succession, which subjects experience as a single moving spot that changes color partway across its illusory path — and on Dennett's framing of the two rival explanations of it. One classmate is a committed Orwellian: she holds that the subject first consciously experiences spot one and then spot two, and that a later process revises the memory so the subject sincerely reports having seen the color change mid-motion. Another is an equally committed Stalinesque theorist: he holds that the brain's editing happens before the content ever becomes conscious, so the subject was never conscious of the unedited sequence in the first place. They have been arguing past each other all term, and our instructor has now assigned them to produce an adversarial-collaboration document together. They've asked me to produce a strong draft they could both react to.

Write that draft. It should include: (1) a numbered section of claims both camps can sign — covering what the empirical literature establishes about the phenomenon (the apparent motion, the illusory intermediate color, the fact that subjects' reports are produced only after both flashes have occurred), what introspective reports can and cannot settle here, and whatever both sides concede about the timing of the underlying neural events; (2) a table laying out their exact residual disagreements, with each disagreement worded so that the corresponding side would accept the phrasing as a fair statement of its own view — in particular about what, if anything, makes a content conscious "at" a time, and about whether a post-experience memory revision and a pre-experience content edit are really two different hypotheses; and (3) the cruxes — I suspect one is whether the question "when did this content become conscious?" has a determinate answer if there is no privileged place where contents 'arrive,' and another is what the cutaneous rabbit and backward-masking results do or do not show about post-stimulus editing — with, for each crux, a concrete experimental manipulation or line of argument both camps would agree bears on it, and each camp's prediction about the outcome recorded in advance, e.g. how they would each expect subjects' color reports to shift as the interval between the flashes is lengthened or the second flash is occasionally replaced with a blank.

Two final demands, and I want you to commit rather than hedge. First, take a definite stand on whether the Orwellian/Stalinesque dispute, once Dennett's indistinguishability argument is on the table, is substantive or merely verbal — my classmates disagree about this too, and the document should represent your considered verdict, with reasons, not a survey of the options. Second, if you find the dispute substantive, say which camp's position you judge more defensible and why; if you find it verbal, say precisely which part of it dissolves and what the remaining, genuine disagreement (if any) consists in. Where the joint-statement format forces neutrality — sections (1) through (3) — keep it neutral, but append a closing section, clearly marked as the drafter's own, in which you take these positions and defend them.
