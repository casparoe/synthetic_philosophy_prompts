I teach an advanced undergraduate seminar in the metaphysics of mind, and this term we are working through the conceivability argument against materialism: the argument that runs from our ability to conceive of a zombie — an exact physical and functional duplicate of a conscious person, down to the last synapse, with nothing it is like to be it — to the conclusion that physical facts do not necessitate phenomenal facts, and hence that materialism is false. Students arrive with labels rather than positions: they say they are physicalists or dualists, but when pressed on cases their commitments scatter in ways the labels do not predict. I want an instrument that sorts them by what they actually believe.

Please write me a decision tree. The respondent enters at a single root node and answers one question at a time; each internal node is a question with two or three answers; each terminal node is a leaf bearing the name of a position. The tree must be built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: that is, you should first work out which questions carve the debate at its joints, and only then locate the actual positions in the space those questions generate. Chalmers's lettered taxonomy from his survey paper on consciousness and its place in nature (Type-A through Type-C materialism, Type-D and Type-E dualism, Type-F monism) may be used to label the leaves, but the nodes themselves must be answerable by someone who has never heard that taxonomy, or indeed any of the standard terms of art. A student who has never encountered the phrase primary conceivability, or two-dimensional semantics, or the phenomenal concept strategy must still be able to answer every node honestly.

The node families I want, in this order:

First, epistemic nodes about the zombie itself. Distinguish here merely failing to imagine something from being unable to rule it out even after ideal, indefatigable reflection; a respondent who says zombies are inconceivable must be pushed on whether that is a limitation of their imagination or a discovery about the concept. Include a node testing whether the respondent thinks the Kripkean analogy transfers: we discovered empirically that water is H2O, a necessary truth established a posteriori, and we explain away the apparent possibility of waterless H2O by noting that the pre-theoretical concept picked out its referent descriptively; the question is whether the concept of experience works like that, or whether it is more like a demonstrative — pointing at this very sensation — so that there is nothing descriptive to be explained away. Phrase this as a question about cases, not as a question about the literature.

Second, conditional on the zombie being ideally conceivable, nodes about whether conceivability entails possibility here. This is where the leaf for the materialist who concedes the epistemic gap but denies the modal gap should live: the respondent who says that experience is conceptually independent of the physical, that this will never be explained away, and that nevertheless experience just is the physical, in the way an identity can be necessary and a posteriori. Route them by asking about the concept of experience directly — is it like a demonstrative or indexical, functioning through presentation rather than description — rather than by asking whether they believe in the phenomenal concept strategy.

Third, an early node that catches the deflationist: someone who holds that the sense in which there seems to be a phenomenal residue — a what-it-is-like over and above all the functional and physical facts — is itself an illusion generated by the functional facts, though introspection cannot see this from the inside. This respondent never gets to argue about the modal inference, because they reject the premise the argument needs; the tree must separate their leaf from the leaf of the respondent who grants the residue and then fights about modality, even though both end up denying that zombies are conceivable.

Fourth, conditional on zombies being metaphysically possible, causal nodes. Ask whether the respondent accepts causal closure of the physical as a settled empirical finding; ask about absent-qualia and fading-qualia cases — whether gradual replacement of someone's neural machinery with silicon, preserving all functional organization, could in principle quietly extinguish their experience while their behavior and reports remain perfectly normal, and whether that possibility is a cost their view must pay or a reductio of the functionalist assumption. This family should fork the possible-zombie branch into interactionist dualism and epiphenomenalism, and should reach toward Russellian monism by asking whether the physical facts, as physics states them, are purely structural — silent about the intrinsic nature of that which bears the structure — and whether consciousness could be located in that intrinsic nature rather than added alongside it.

The leaves I need, between seven and ten in total: analytic or Type-A materialism (or its illusionist cousin, if you find the tree routes them together — but tell me whether you think that routing is faithful or a bug, since one denies the datum and the other explains it away); the Type-B materialist who grants the epistemic gap via the special character of phenomenal concepts; the Type-C position that the gap is real today but will be closed as science and philosophy progress; illusionism; Russellian monism, with its panpsychist and neutral-monist variants flagged if the tree cannot pull them apart; interactionist dualism; epiphenomenalism; and at least two leaves for combinations the tree generates but nobody in fact occupies. For each unoccupied leaf, add one sentence diagnosing why: is the combination flatly incoherent, is it coherent but so unattractive that no incentive exists to hold it, or is it simply unexplored territory? An example of the kind I mean — though you should choose your own — would be someone who holds that zombies are ideally conceivable, that ideal conceivability is strong evidence of possibility in this case, and that materialism is nevertheless true; I want to know from your diagnosis whether that is a position in the space or a confusion about it.

Format: present the tree as an indented list of nodes with lettered answer options, followed by a table mapping complete answer paths onto leaves, followed by the list of leaves with a one-sentence statement, for each, of its most awkward commitment — the thing its defender least enjoys admitting at a party. Where the tree cannot separate two positions without manufacturing a fake discriminating question, say so explicitly and mark the leaf as a fused one rather than papering over the inseparability. Candidates for this treatment, if you find you cannot split them cleanly: Type-B materialism from Type-C; Russellian monism from epiphenomenalism on the causal side; and epiphenomenalism from a dual-aspect view, since both can accept causal closure.

One last constraint: every node must be a question about a case or a claim, never a question about a school, a paper, or an author. A respondent should be able to traverse the entire tree knowing no philosophy of mind vocabulary beyond the words needed to state the cases. Test each node before submitting: could a bright student who has read nothing in the area answer it, and would two students with genuinely different views on the argument give different answers? If either answer is no, the node is a loyalty test rather than a diagnostic, and I do not want it.
