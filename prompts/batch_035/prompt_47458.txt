A while back a friend described to me, at a long dinner, the puzzle known as Parfit's hitchhiker: you're stranded in the desert, a passing driver will take you to town only if she believes you'll pay her when you arrive, and once you're safe, paying is against your interest — so the person who gets rescued is someone constitutionally incapable of walking away, someone whose "irrationality" at the payment moment is exactly what got them the ride. I have no philosophy background; I just kept chewing on it afterward, and something about it has been bothering me. In every version I've ever heard, the person whose inability to act in their own interest is the winner is the one *receiving* the help — the hitchhiker, the borrower, the one who needs to be believed. I keep wondering whether the trick runs the other way: can you build a case where the *helper* is the one who has to be incapable of doing the self-interested thing at the crucial later moment?

So here is what I'd like you to construct. Build a thought experiment in which the party whose "irrationality" pays is the one extending the help, not the one receiving it. Constraints: it should be an everyday scenario — a workplace, a family, a small business — no perfect lie detectors and no superhuman predictors; the other people in the story should be judging character roughly the way a shrewd neighbor or a seasoned manager would. One paragraph for the scenario itself. The disposition involved must not be an obviously bad one that no sensible person would have; it should be a policy that is genuinely good to hold in general, even though it fires at a loss in the specific case you build. The closest I've managed on my own is something like a manager informally known for never going back on a promise of flexibility — but every draft I attempt collapses into "well, she's just protecting her reputation," which feels like it dissolves the puzzle rather than solving it.

After the scenario, please walk through the mechanics carefully. First, state what a fully forward-looking, self-interested helper does at the moment the disposition is supposed to fire, and why the other participants' anticipation of exactly that makes the whole arrangement fall apart before it starts. Then tell me plainly which feature of the case is doing the work — what it is that lets the helper's disposition be relied upon at the earlier decision point even though it costs the helper at the later one. Finally, address the objection I keep running into: that the helper isn't irrational at all, just playing a long game with her reputation, so there's no real inversion of the hitchhiker here. If something in the design of the case blocks that reply, say what; if nothing does and the case is just disguised reputation-management, say so honestly, and tell me what would have to be true — even if it can't quite be arranged in an ordinary workplace — for the inversion to be genuine.
