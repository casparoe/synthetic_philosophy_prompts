I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on Kant's ethics, and last week we discussed the murderer at the door — the case Kant addressed in his short reply to Benjamin Constant, where the question is whether you may lie to a murderer who asks where your friend is hiding. I raised the ordinary objection that refusing to lie in that situation seems monstrous, and a classmate shut the discussion down by intoning 'let justice be done though the heavens fall', as though that one line settled the matter in Kant's favour. Our tutor nodded along, and I didn't have the vocabulary to push back on the spot. What I would like is a proper disambiguation of that proverb: what distinct precise claims could someone be making when they say it? Please lay the readings out as a menu, running from ones that are near-platitudes (say, that justice matters more than convenience or expediency) through substantive and defensible ones (say, that certain constraints must not be traded off against aggregate outcomes, however bad) to radical or arguably false ones (say, that one is obliged to bring the heavens down rather than permit the smallest injustice oneself). For each reading, say whether it is plausible, and — this is the crucial part — whether it would actually support refusing to lie to the murderer, or whether it is simply orthogonal to that case.

There are three things I would especially like covered. First, Kant's own reasoning in the lying essay is, from what I can gather, stranger and more specific than the slogan suggests: something about a lie wronging humanity in general, and about the liar becoming accountable for whatever consequences follow, whereas the truthful person is not. Does any reading of the proverb actually correspond to that reasoning, or has my classmate quietly substituted a more generic deontological rigour for Kant's position? Second, is the proverb perhaps best read not as a first-order claim about what should happen in the world at all, but as an agent-relative claim about what I may do — a distinction that would leave room for deeply regretting the outcome while still holding that the lie is not mine to tell — and if so, does that reading make the position more or less defensible in the murderer case? Third, my classmate used the proverb to end an argument rather than to advance one: flag which readings, if any, are so contentious that deploying them as a conversation-stopper amounts to begging the question, and which are so weak that they couldn't carry the conclusion anyway.

I would like something structured enough that I can bring it to next week's seminar and respond point by point, ideally with a short verdict at the end on which reading my classmate most plausibly had in mind, given the work the proverb was doing for him in that room.
