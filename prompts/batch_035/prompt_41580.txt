I've been trying to work out, from my own reactions to particular cases, when I think comparing one person's welfare against another's is philosophically legitimate, and my verdicts pull in different directions. I'd like help extracting a principle, if there is one.

The cases and my verdicts:

1. Comparisons within a single life are unproblematic: I can rank my own headaches against each other and against my backaches, and I'd trust others' similar reports about themselves.
2. Two patients arrive with the same injury, but one is in visibly worse pain, and there is one dose of the stronger analgesic. Give it to the worse-off patient. The interpersonal comparison seems not merely permitted but required.
3. I accept the standard argument for progressive taxation: a marginal dollar does more for someone poor than for someone rich. This is a cross-person comparison of utility gains, and it seems entirely fine to me.
4. Nozick's utility monster — a being that derives vastly more utility from any resource than any human does. If I take my verdicts in cases 2 and 3 seriously, I seem committed, step by step, to feeding everything to the monster. I refuse to accept that, and what bothers me is not any single transfer but the practice of summing across persons in the first place.
5. I would impose a mild headache on one person to relieve another's agony without hesitation, but imposing that headache to give a third person the pleasure of an excellent meal feels wrong to me even if the meal's pleasure outweighs the headache on the ledger. Relatedly, comparisons of suffering strike me as on firmer ground than comparisons of enjoyment — case 2 seems solid in a way that case 3's fine-grained hedonics does not.

My tentative principle was that interpersonal comparisons are legitimate when rough and ordinal — enough to rank outcomes in a local decision — but illegitimate when treated as precise cardinal quantities to be summed. I suspect this doesn't survive contact with my verdicts: the monster can be fed one ordinal step at a time, so my discomfort in case 4 seems to concern aggregation itself rather than precision.

So, is there one account of when and how interpersonal welfare comparisons are meaningful that captures all of this? If not, which of my verdicts would you overturn, and why? Please state the account (or a small set of candidates) precisely, note which case motivates each clause, say what it predicts about cases I haven't mentioned — for instance, weighing the self-reported life satisfaction of someone with a longstanding disability against that of a healthy but miserable person, or comparing a human's suffering to an animal's — and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot save.
