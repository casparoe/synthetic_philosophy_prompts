I'm preparing a philosophy essay on Hugo's Les Misérables and want to read it through the paradox of deontology — the puzzle that deontological constraints can require one to act in ways that produce worse outcomes than violating those constraints would, and that such constraints seem coherent only from an agent-relative standpoint that consequentialists regard as irrational. Two episodes look central to me. First, Valjean's night of anguish over whether to reveal his identity and save Champmathieu, the innocent vagrant about to be convicted as Jean Valjean in his place: the novel stages this as a struggle between not letting another suffer in his stead and the utilitarian case for silence, since the workers of Montreuil-sur-Mer depend on the factory he built as Mayor Madeleine. Second, Javert: at the barricade Valjean is given the task of executing him and instead quietly releases him, and Javert, unable to reconcile his absolute allegiance to the law with his debt to the man who spared him, lets Valjean go free and drowns himself in the Seine. Please work out this reading: where does Hugo dramatize the doing/allowing distinction and other agent-relative constraints, and should Javert's suicide be understood as the collapse of a purely deontological code when its deliverances conflict, or as something else — a code so total that it leaves no room for mercy? I'd also like you to say where the novel resists the reading: Hugo writes in the vocabulary of conscience, the soul, and progress rather than of constraints, and his stance on social questions is broadly reformist and outcome-oriented. Does the novel, read this way, contribute anything to the philosophical debate over deontological constraints that the stock examples — torture cases and the like — do not?
