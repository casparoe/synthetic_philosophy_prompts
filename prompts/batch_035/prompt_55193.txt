Two philosophers have been arguing past each other about moral responsibility for over a decade. The first is a Strawsonian: she takes the lesson of P. F. Strawson's Freedom and Resentment to be that the reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, hurt feelings, guilt — are constitutive of genuinely interpersonal relationships, that the participant stance is not in the market for a general rationale, and that neither determinism nor the philosophical arguments against desert could undermine it. The second is a free-will skeptic in the tradition of Derk Pereboom: he holds that desert-entailing blame requires a kind of sourcehood no human agent possesses, that manipulation arguments make this vivid, and that we should progressively retire blame in favor of forward-looking moral address of the sort we already extend to children and to people in the grip of severe mental illness.

They have finally agreed to write the joint statement that should have been produced long ago, and you are to draft it. The statement must contain the following sections:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. These should be substantive rather than diplomatic filler: for instance, about what resentment's phenomenology presents its object as being; about the extent to which reactive attitudes are, in practice, responsive to argument; about what Strawson's objective attitude can and cannot accomplish; about which familiar practices — excuses, exemptions, mitigation on grounds of character-shaping history — both sides read the same way.

2. A disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated twice: once in wording the Strawsonian would accept as a faithful statement of her view, once in wording the skeptic would accept as a faithful statement of his. Neither formulation may smuggle in the other side's presuppositions.

3. A cruxes section. Include at least the following three, plus any others you judge genuine: (a) whether the Strawsonian case for the reactive attitudes is a psychological impossibility claim, a normative vindication, or a descriptive plea about our concept of responsibility — and which of these the skeptic's demand for a justification of resentment actually engages; (b) whether reactive attitudes intrinsically involve a desert claim, or whether they can be reconceived as moral address severable from desert (the Strawsonian will want the severance for some attitudes but perhaps not for the felt character of resentment; the skeptic will deny the severance holds anywhere that matters); (c) whether the skeptic's revisionary program is symmetrical — whether it applies to guilt and self-directed attitudes as strongly as to resentment, given that first-personal attitudes may partly constitute one's agency in ways third-personal blame does not.

4. For each crux, a case, argument, or stipulated scenario that both parties agree bears on it, with each party's prediction recorded in advance. You may invent scenarios — for example, a community that raises its children with blame-minimized but expectation-rich moral address and does not collapse into either moral indifference or doctrinaire forgiveness, or a manipulated agent stipulated to be behaviorally and phenomenally indistinguishable from an ordinary wrongdoer — but the two sides must disagree about the prediction or about its import, never about whether the case is well-formed.

5. A closing section: if any part of the residual disagreement is verbal — for instance, if one side uses holding responsible to mean desert-entailing blame and the other to mean moral address — identify that part, explain why it is verbal, and state what substantive disagreement would remain once the terminology is tidied away.

Keep each side's position recognizably Strawsonian and recognizably Pereboomian throughout: not a generic compatibilism against a generic skepticism. Neither party may be granted the last word.
