I write features on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a long piece about the science of pleasure: neuroscientists tracing so-called hedonic hotspots in the brain, national well-being indices that treat happiness as a measurable quantity, and a small but vocal group convinced we should be engineering bliss. What strikes me, after months of interviews, is how often all this empirical confidence rests on an old and unresolved philosophical question that nobody in the lab ever states outright: what do all pleasures have in common? The warm glow of a hot bath, the click of finally understanding a proof, the taste of a ripe peach, an orgasm, the absorption of a long conversation — if a hedonist says these are all the same thing in different amounts, what is that thing? And if they are not the same thing, what becomes of the idea that pleasure is a single currency in which a life's quality can be reckoned?

I want the logical geography straight before I write a word, so please build me a classification instrument — either a decision tree or a short diagnostic quiz with a scoring key, whichever you judge does the job better. Start from the hedonist's basic commitment, that everything finally good about a life is pleasure or the absence of pain, and make every internal node a question someone could answer without knowing any of the position labels: Does the enjoyment of a gripping chess game have to feel like anything at all, or is absorbed engagement without any felt glow still pleasure? Is the masochist's or chili-eater's painful sensation, when it is welcomed, itself a pleasure, or only the occasion for a different one? If two experiences are equal in felt intensity and duration, and nearly everyone who has tried both prefers one, is the preferred one strictly better — and does that question even make sense on your view? Could there be a pleasure with no felt character whatsoever, detectable only by its downstream effects on mood and behavior? The leaves should be the live positions in this debate: a single common felt quality shared by all pleasures; pleasure as a kind of attitude rather than a kind of sensation; a family-resemblance or cluster answer on which there is no one thing in common; the Millian move of denying a common element and distinguishing pleasures by quality rather than quantity; and the outright denial that "pleasure" names anything unified enough for the hedonist's purposes.

After the instrument, please do two more things. First, note which combinations of answers your questions allow but no known position occupies, and say briefly whether each empty cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive to anyone who answers that way, or because it is simply unexplored. Second, flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot genuinely pull apart, rather than papering over the gaps. I'm aiming for roughly eight to ten questions or branch points, accessible to educated readers with no philosophy background, and I would welcome a short paragraph of framing at the top that I might adapt as the opening of my piece.
