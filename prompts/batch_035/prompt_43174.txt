I am a program officer at a small philanthropic fund that makes roughly forty grants per year, typically in the $50,000–$500,000 range, across global health, education policy, and a small long-termist portfolio. Our grantmaking decisions are consequentialist in spirit: we try to fund what will do the most good. Over the past year our committee has deadlocked repeatedly on a problem I've come to learn has a name in the philosophy literature — cluelessness. Two examples: we funded a deworming program partly on the strength of long-run income effects from a few studies, and one member argued the far-future effects of a different deworming campaign (resistance, labor-market shifts, whatever) swamp any measured medium-term benefit, in an unknowable direction. Separately, we passed on a grant to reform pharmaceutical procurement in a mid-sized country on the grounds that we couldn't model the political economy, and another member argued that this standard, applied consistently, would have us fund nothing at all, since *every* intervention has untraceable ripple effects — the absence of an argument for harm is not an argument for absence of harm.

I understand the literature distinguishes *simple* cluelessness (we can't be certain what the effects are, but we can assign probabilities and expected values, as in ordinary risk) from *complex* cluelessness (we can't even assign the probabilities, because our evidence systematically underdetermines which causal stories are live — a distinction drawn, if I have this right, in work by Hilary Greaves). I also gather there are candidate responses: probabilistic weighting of scenarios, falling back on tie-breaking heuristics like "don't make things actively worse," diversifying a portfolio so unknown downside risks cancel, prioritizing option value and reversibility, and simply denying that opaque long-run effects matter for decision purposes. I do not want an essay on the philosophy. I want a decision procedure my committee can actually run on a proposal in a single two-hour meeting.

Please write that procedure. It should specify: (1) the questions the committee asks about a proposal, in order, each phrased in plain language rather than jargon, with a stated criterion for what counts as sufficient to move to the next question; (2) the points at which the procedure terminates in a "decline," a "fund pending diligence," or a "escalate to values judgment," and what those designations mean operationally; (3) how the procedure distinguishes simple from complex cluelessness in practice — what kind of evidence, if produced by an applicant, upgrades a case from the second category to the first — and what the committee should do when it cannot tell which category it is in; (4) explicit markers of the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, so we don't mistake them for findings; and (5) an honest statement of what the procedure cannot do, including any systematic bias it introduces (for instance, whether it structurally privileges interventions whose harms are visible and near-term over ones whose harms are invisible and distant).

Then run the procedure on two proposals from our actual pipeline. Proposal A: $400,000 to support a randomized evaluation of a low-cost water-quality intervention in a region with high childhood diarrhea mortality, with the intervention itself to be scaled only if the trial succeeds. Proposal B: $150,000 in unrestricted funding to a small research group working on making pandemic preparedness decision-making more robust, whose theory of change runs through influence on national policy a decade from now. For each, show the procedure's output at every node.

One requirement I want you to keep even though I dislike it: at least one of the two runs must end in a recommendation I would find uncomfortable, and you should say plainly why the discomfort is the procedure working as intended rather than a defect in it. If you think the honest answer is that no procedure of this kind should exist and the committee should instead change how it talks about grants, say that at the end, but still deliver the procedure first.
