My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about whether time really passes, and the argument started because she handed me a popular physics book that presents the Rietdijk–Putnam argument roughly like this: 'In special relativity there is no absolute simultaneity: two observers moving relative to each other slice spacetime along different surfaces and disagree about which distant events are happening now. But relativity also tells us that no observer's frame is privileged, since all inertial frames are equally valid. So if an event is real for one observer, it simply is real. And by considering a third observer moving suitably, any event in your future can be made part of someone else's present. Chaining observers together, every event of your life, including what you will decide tomorrow, already exists and is real for someone right now. In effect, Einstein proved in 1905 that the universe is a four-dimensional block with no flowing present, and that determinism, long treated as an open scientific question, was settled the moment special relativity was accepted. The feeling that the future is open, and any freedom we think we have in shaping it, is an illusion.'

My friend takes this to be airtight and now talks about her own future decisions as though they were already written. I am actually sympathetic to the block universe on other grounds, so it is not the conclusion I am resisting — but two steps bothered me. The slide from an event being real for an observer to its being real, period, seemed to be doing a great deal of the work while being presented as a direct lesson of relativity; and the leap from everything being real to free will being an illusion felt far too quick. She tells me I am simply refusing to accept where the physics leads.

Could you audit this summary for me? Please go through it step by step and tell me which parts a philosopher of physics would accept as standard, which are simplifications they would wince at but tolerate in a popular treatment, and which are genuine misrepresentations. I would especially like to know whether the principle about no privileged observers is itself a theorem or consequence of special relativity, or a further philosophical assumption riding on top of it; whether the chaining-together step is as innocuous as it sounds, or whether there is a principled way for a critic to block it; and whether the closing claims about determinism and free will follow from whatever survives your corrections, or only from the parts that do not. Please answer in American English.
