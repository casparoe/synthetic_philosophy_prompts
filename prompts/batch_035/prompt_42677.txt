QUESTION (20 marks total)

n children are in a room. k of them have mud on their foreheads. Each child can see every other child's forehead but not their own. No mirrors, no talking. All the children are perfect logicians, and this is common knowledge — likewise it's common knowledge that everyone hears everything the father says and that everyone answers truthfully.

The father enters and announces publicly: at least one of you has mud on your forehead. Then, once per round, the father asks all children simultaneously: "Do you know whether you have mud on your own forehead?" Each child answers yes or no — about whether they know, not about whether they have mud.

(a) [12 marks] Show that if exactly k children are muddy, all the muddy children answer yes in round k. When do the clean children first answer yes, and why?

(b) [8 marks] Now suppose k ≥ 2. Before the father says a word, every child can already see a muddy face: the muddy children each see k − 1 ≥ 1 of them, and the clean children see all k. So the content of the father's announcement was already known to every single child. Given that, what does the public announcement accomplish that the children's own eyes did not? Would the father get the same outcome by instead going up to each child individually, out of the others' earshot, and whispering "at least one of you has mud"?

STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) If k = 1, the muddy child sees no mud anywhere, so the father's statement tells it that it's the one, and it answers yes in round 1. If k = 2, each muddy child sees exactly one muddy face and reasons: "if my forehead is clean, then only that other kid is muddy, and that kid sees no mud, so it'll know in round 1." Round 1 passes with two no's, so each muddy child concludes it must be muddy as well, and both answer yes in round 2. Inductively, if there are k muddy children, none of them can possibly know before round k, and the round k − 1 no's are precisely what tells each of them it's muddy, so all k of them answer yes in round k. As for the clean children: a clean child sees k muddy faces, so before round k it cannot distinguish between the true count being k (it's clean) or k + 1 (it's muddy). If it were muddy, the yeses would arrive a round later. So once the yeses arrive in round k, the clean child knows it's clean — but since answers within a round are simultaneous, the earliest it can say so is round k + 1. So clean children answer yes in round k + 1.

(b) For k ≥ 2 the announcement is a null move content-wise. Every child can already see at least one muddy face — the muddy children see k − 1 and the clean ones see all k — so the father is telling them all something they already know. Nothing is learned from the sentence itself; what does all the work in part (a) is the children's own logic plus the public round-by-round questioning. So yes, the father would get exactly the same outcome by whispering to each child individually: the sentence is already known to everyone, so whether it's delivered publicly or privately makes no difference when k ≥ 2. (The k = 1 case is different, since there the lone muddy child genuinely learns something.) This is also a nice illustration of Aumann's agreement theorem: once the father's statement makes the situation common knowledge, the children's posteriors have to agree, and that's why they all answer at the same round.
