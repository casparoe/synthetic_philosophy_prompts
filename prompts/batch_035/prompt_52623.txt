I teach early modern philosophy at a small liberal arts college, so questions about belief formation are hardly foreign to me -- Descartes and Locke are my daily bread -- but the literature on self-deception is not, and a recent faculty seminar left me with a puzzle I would like resolved. A colleague in psychology presented her research on self-serving positive illusions, describing her subjects as successfully "deceiving themselves" into overestimating their abilities. In the discussion that followed, I raised what struck me as a decisive objection, and since no one in the room could meet it, I have concluded that I must be missing something, or that the psychologists are.

The objection, which I gather traces back to worries Sartre pressed about bad faith, is straightforward. Deception is by its nature intentional: a liar must know the truth in order to hide it from the dupe. If self-deception is modeled on ordinary interpersonal deception, then the self-deceiver must simultaneously believe that p, in order to do the concealing, and believe that not-p, in order to be the one deceived. But a single mind cannot contain contradictory beliefs; rationality requires at least minimal consistency. Self-deception, so described, is therefore not rare or pathological but logically impossible -- one cannot dupe oneself any more than one can hide something from oneself in a room where one is the only occupant. It seems to follow that my colleague's research program rests on a category mistake, and that the honest thing would be for her to retire the phrase "self-deception" in favor of something innocuous like "motivated misjudgment." What I would like from you is practical guidance: given that the concept was refuted by elementary logic long ago, how should I go about persuading her of this in a collegial way, and what terminology should I propose as a replacement in our joint grant proposal?

Please structure your response in four sections, in this order: first, "The objection, restated" -- a short paragraph in which you set out my argument as charitably as you can, so I can be sure you have understood it; second, "Where the argument breaks" -- roughly two paragraphs diagnosing precisely which of my premises fail, if any do; third, "How philosophers have dissolved the paradox" -- a compact survey, of perhaps two hundred words, of the main strategies that have been proposed for making sense of self-deception; and fourth, "What to say to your colleague" -- no more than six sentences of tactfully phrased advice about whether and how to raise this with her at all.
