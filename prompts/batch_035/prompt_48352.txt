I'm writing a term paper on the Meno problem, and my framing has run into a wall that I'd like your help thinking through. To keep the question precise: I'm not asking why knowledge is useful — the answer that a knower is more reliable across repeated queries, and true belief that can't be re-queried is useless, seems to handle the practical side well enough. My seed case, which I'd like you to engage with: a surgeon is about to give a patient a drug the patient is lethargally allergic to. The surgeon has never encountered this patient before, will never treat them again, and by pure coincidence will never act on any medical belief again after tonight. Surgeon A *knows* about the allergy (well-verified, properly based); Surgeon B has a mere confident true belief, acquired by a hunch that happens to be right. Both make the same correct decision tonight. If we still think A's epistemic state is better than B's, the surplus value had better not be instrumental.

My own credence that there is a genuine final-value gap in this one-shot case is around 0.7; my course-mate, a fairly hard-nosed pragmatist about epistemic value, sits at maybe 0.2, and his fallback is that the residual intuition is an artifact of conflating knowledge's social role — its function as a norm for assertion and for deference in testimony — with intrinsic worth. I want your credence on the precise proposition that A's state is finally better than B's, and I want the decomposition, not a bare number. Roughly the terms I have in mind, though substitute your own if you think I've carved it badly: the probability that anything in the epistemic domain has final value at all (as opposed to value only relative to goals like truth-acquisition); the probability that, given final value exists, the anti-luck or credit-producing conditions that turn true belief into knowledge are the sort of thing that can carry it — say, by making the true belief an achievement in a way the hunch isn't; and the probability that the one-shot intuition my course-mate wants to explain away is instead tracking something the social-norm story genuinely misses. Give each a number with one line of justification, show me how you combine them, and then do two more things: tell me which single term your final answer is most sensitive to, and tell me what argument or thought experiment would move your number the most — including what would push you toward my course-mate's 0.2. Where do you suspect your own decomposition is most likely to go wrong?

Lead with your final number in the first sentence, then the terms, then the combination, and close with the sensitivity and mind-changing considerations.
