Take the phenomenon of Hamilton — Lin-Manuel Miranda's 2015 musical about Alexander Hamilton — and analyze it through Rousseau's arguments in his Letter to d'Alembert on Spectacles. By way of seeding the facts I care about: the show opened off-Broadway in early 2015, transferred to Broadway that summer, and became a commercial and political sensation almost immediately — a hip-hop retelling of the founding era with actors of color playing the white founders, celebrity audience members, ticket prices that ran into the hundreds and then thousands of dollars, a filmed version released on streaming in 2020, and a widely publicized education program that brought schoolchildren in to study primary documents, write their own performance pieces, and see the show. There was also the November 2016 incident in which the cast, after a performance attended by the Vice-President-elect, delivered a direct message to him from the stage about their fears for the new administration, prompting days of furious political argument about whether a theater audience booing and lecturing an elected official was admirable civic courage or exactly the smugness its critics suspected.

The Rousseau I have in mind is the 1758 letter he wrote against d'Alembert's suggestion, in the Encyclopédie's article on Geneva, that the city should establish a theater. His central claims, as I understand them: that theater is not a school of virtue and never has been, contrary to the inherited classical view; that watching suffering onstage purges us of compassion rather than exercising it, because weeping for imaginary misfortunes discharges the duty we owe to real ones; that an audience in a darkened room is a collection of isolated spectators, not a citizen body, and that this solitude is the point — theater substitutes for the public festivals where a people is reminded it exists as a people; and that whatever Parisians get from their plays, a small republican city with intact civic bonds has something to lose and nothing to gain. His counterproposal, famously, was not theater but open-air public fêtes in which no one is a spectator because everyone is a participant.

What I want is a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions. Map Rousseau's mechanism onto Hamilton: if his claim is that theatrical emotion is counterfeit civic feeling, what exactly is the counterfeit in this case — the audience's identification with the founders, the credit the audience collects for consuming a progressive allegory, or something else? The case seems at first glance to embarrass Rousseau on several fronts: a show did plausibly teach a generation of teenagers more about the 1790s than their textbooks did, and the education program looks less like his denunciation of passive spectatorship and more like his own festival model — students performing for each other, with professional actors in a supporting role. But it also seems to confirm him in updated form: the outrage economy around the Pence incident was theater-shaped civic engagement, bold words spoken to a captive audience of the already-convinced, and the show's own political cachet was inseparable from its function as a luxury good. So tell me honestly which of Rousseau's specific premises survive contact with this case and which don't, where his framework is simply silent (he was writing about tragedy and comedy, not jukebox celebrity culture or hip-hop's sampling aesthetics, and I'd like you to say whether that matters or is an excuse), and what the case does to the critique as a whole: does Hamilton force a qualification of the Letter, refute it, or actually vindicate it in a form Rousseau couldn't have anticipated? I'm not looking for a hatchet job on either the show or the letter — I want the strongest version of Rousseau's analysis applied unsentimentally, and an account of where it genuinely breaks down.
