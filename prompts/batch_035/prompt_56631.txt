For the past year, my reading group in philosophy of physics has been working through Kurt Gödel's cosmological papers and his short philosophical essay on relativity and idealism, which he published in 1949 in the Library of Living Philosophers volume honoring Einstein. As you may know, that same year Gödel exhibited an exact solution of Einstein's field equations: a universe of homogeneous, uniformly rotating dust with a negative cosmological constant, in which closed timelike curves pass through every point, so that in principle an observer could travel to their own past, and in which there is no invariant way to slice spacetime into a global succession of universal "nows." Gödel drew a striking philosophical conclusion: a universe in which time cannot objectively lapse is a physically possible universe, and since time can only be objective if it is objective in every physically possible universe, time in our world — the felt passage from an open future to a fixed past — must be an appearance, something contributed by the observer rather than by the world. Two members of the group have now argued about this for a full year without budging, and they have agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration. I would like you to draft the joint statement they should be able to sign.

The first member accepts the broadly Gödelian conclusion: relativity theory continues what Kant began, and the rotating universe shows that objective temporal passage is not a feature of physical reality at all. The second member is a spacetime realist: relativistic time — proper time along worldlines, causal ordering, the cosmic time of our own apparently well-behaved expanding universe — is fully objective, and Gödel's argument overreaches by drawing conclusions about actual time from a merely possible exotic solution. Their recurring points of friction include: the modal bridge principle that whatever is not objective in every physically possible universe is objective in none; the standard objection that this principle proves too much (a physically possible universe without, say, magnetic fields would hardly show magnetism to be ideal); the Gödelian rejoinder that if physics itself cannot settle whether time is real, that already undermines the reality of time; and the question of whether the second member is even addressing Gödel's target, since no one denies that local clock readings and before–after orderings are real — the dispute is supposedly about passage and the privileged status of the present, not about temporal metrics.

Please write the document such a collaboration should produce: a numbered section of claims both members can accept (about the mathematics of the rotating solution, about what does and does not follow from the existence of closed timelike curves, about the distinction between global and local temporal structure, and about what each takes Gödel's argument to actually claim); a table of their remaining disagreements, with each disagreement phrased so that the member holding that view would accept the wording as a fair statement of their own position; and a list of the genuine cruxes separating them. For each crux, identify at least one argument, theorem, or empirical finding that both members would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each member would predict or conclude if it were established — for example, what follows for each of them from a proof that solutions matching our actual cosmological observations can nonetheless admit closed timelike curves, or, in the other direction, from a chronology-protection-style theorem showing that our spacetime forbids them. Finally, if part of their dispute strikes you as verbal rather than substantive — for instance, a merely terminological disagreement over what deserves the name "real time" — say which part and why, and separate it from what remains a genuine disagreement.
