My flatmate is doing a masters in cognitive science and he walked me into this argument about predictive processing and I can't get out. (1) On the predictive processing story the brain is basically a machine for minimizing prediction error: it constantly predicts its own sensory input, and when prediction and input mismatch it either updates the predictions (that's perception) or acts on the world so the input comes to match (that's action). (2) The easiest way to get prediction error near zero is to put yourself somewhere with almost no sensory variation — a dark, quiet room — and stay there. (3) Novelty generates prediction error by definition: an unfamiliar city, music you'v never heard, a jump scare in a film. (4) So a prediction-error minimizer should seek the dark room and avoid anything new. (5) And if someone says exploring pays off by reducing error later, note that staying put also keeps your future error low — about the room you're already in — and at way lower cost along the way. (6) But animals and people are the opposite: we explore, we get bored of familiar things, we pay money to watch horror films. (7) So minimizing prediction error can't be the basic drive — there must be a separate curiosity or novelty drive stacked on top of it, and PP is at best a fragment of the story. He thinks this is fatal to the whole free energy picture. I want to keep (1), PP explains too much stuff that I don't want to give up, and (6) is just obvious. My hunch is that something slides between (2) and (4), like the difference between minimizing error right now versus over a whole life, but I can't make it precise. And when I tried that on him he asked whether long-run minimization is even well defined — wouldn't a creature that sits in the dark until it dies of thirst rack up a pretty low lifetime average error too? So tell me where exactly this goes wrong, which step I should reject, and what rejecting it costs me.
