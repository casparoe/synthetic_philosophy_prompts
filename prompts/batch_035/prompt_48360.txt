Here's the thing that puzzles me. Most large organizations run some version of "stage-gate" project governance: at each gate, a committee is supposed to re-justify the project from scratch, and the official doctrine is that money already spent is irrelevant — you evaluate as if you were deciding today whether to launch this project, counting only remaining costs against remaining benefits. That's just the sunk-cost rule from decision theory, institutionalized.

But the same organizations do two other things that don't obviously fit. First, in the actual gate meetings, people argue in completion percentages: "we're 80% done, walking away now wastes everything we put in." Everyone officially rejects that argument, yet it keeps working, and projects that are far along almost never get killed — everyone kind of knows the 80%-done framing is doing the real work even tho it's officially banned. Second, the accountability apparatus — post-mortems, blame, how a write-off gets announced — treats the money already spent as mattering enormously, just not for the decision: it matters to persons and careers. So sunk costs are supposed to be irrelevant to the decision but decisive for the blame.

So here are my questions. What theory of practical rationality does the official doctrine presuppose — something like: a decision is a forward-looking intervention, and the decider at the gate is a fresh time-slice with no history? And does the completion-percentage practice presuppose a rival view — perhaps that the decider is a temporally extended agent whose past choices partly constitute what the project is now worth to them (narrative views of agency, if you like) — or can a single theory license both practices, so that the apparent inconsistency is really just underdetermination? Also try to say whether the blame practice really is in tension with the forward-looking doctrine, or whether sunk costs could matter to blame for control-based reasons that have nothing to do with the decision itself.

Then there's the thing I most want your view on. Consider the dollar auction: the highest bidder gets the dollar, but the top two bidders both have to pay whatever they bid, whether they win or not. Each incremental bid — "just 5 more cents and I get the dollar" — is locally reasonable, but the policy of bidding at all is terrible, so the rational evaluation of a single move comes apart from the rational evaluation of a policy. My suspicion is that the sunk-cost doctrine is definately not a node-level truth about rationality; it's a policy-level patch, and its real function is to break dollar-auction dynamics inside organizations, where each individual gate decision ("just one more tranche and we're done") is locally defensible in exactly the bidder's way. If that's right, the doctrine presupposes a theory not about what rational agents believe but about how institutions systematically corrupt local reasoning — and "money already spent is irrelevant" is a useful fiction rather than a truth. Is that right? Where does the reading break down? And if we took the dollar-auction reading seriously, what would actually have to change in how the gates are designed — would we, for instance, stop pretending the evaluation is "as if new" and instead directly evaluate the going-forward policy, factoring in the organization's known bias toward continuing?

One more thing: don't treat my framing as fixed. If you think the useful-fiction reading is wrong, or that the three practices hang together better than I'm suggesting, push back — but tell me which specific argument would flip you.
