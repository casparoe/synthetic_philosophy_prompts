I teach political philosophy to second-years — a 90-minute seminar, usually about 14 students. Next term I've got a week on Sen's liberal paradox, and honestly the result lands flat when they just read it on the page: they file it under cute technicality and move on with their lives. I want them to feel cornered by it instead. Design me an in-class activity where they build the trap themselves.

Rough shape I'm imagining: open with a founding convention where the class drafts and ratifies a mini constitution for their little society — something like a sovereignty clause (each member is the final authority over one assigned personal matter of their own) and an unanimity clause (if literally everyone prefers outcome A to outcome B, the society cannot end up at B). They should pass both without much fuss, feeling extremely sensible about it. Then hand out sealed preference cards giving each of them a trivial personal choice plus opinions about their neighbour's choice — and engineer the cards so that the outcome the sovereignty clause forces is one everyone, in their assigned roles, would reject under the unanimity clause. Payoffs in tokens or chocolate, no real money. Then let them thrash out what to actually do, with two or three of them cast as a constitutional court that has to rule when the clauses collide.

What I need from you, concretely:

- Full rules and a materials list I could print and run with no prep beyond photocopying.
- The actual preference-card setup, spelled out. I'm thinking 7 pairs of students, each pair getting a different flavour of the same underlying structure (loud music, what someone reads, what they wear to the seminar, whatever), plus one or two pairs whose preferences are harmless so the class doesn't smell the setup immediately.
- A timeline that fits 90 minutes including the debrief.
- How to run token payoffs so the assigned preferences actually bite.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the formal pieces — minimal liberalism, the Pareto condition, and the impossibility result itself — so they see that the two clauses they thought were just common sense can't jointly hold for all profiles. And I want the debrief to take the obvious objection seriously ("these meddlesome preferences are weird, real people don't care what their neighbour reads") rather than waving it off, because that's where the genuinely interesting philosophy is.

Two more things. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my guess is someone tries to lie about their card, someone else argues the cards are unrealistic, and one pair just quietly renegotiates instead of following the rules — and how I should handle each without killing the exercise. Second, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce. I'm aware Sen's theorem is about every collective decision rule on every preference profile, and one nasty engineered profile isn't that, so tell me exactly which parts of the theorem I have to supply myself at the end, and where the game risks teaching them the wrong lesson if I'm not careful.
