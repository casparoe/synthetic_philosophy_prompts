I'm writing a term paper for a graduate epistemology seminar on Nozick's tracking account of knowledge, and I've run into a problem with my framing that I'd like your help thinking through. The way I understand the dialectic, the account has a fatal flaw: it violates closure under known entailment. If I know the animal in the pen is a zebra (because my belief tracks the fact), then by a simple competent deduction I should also know it's not a mule cleverly disguised to look like a zebra — but my belief about the disguised mule fails the sensitivity condition, since if it were a disguised mule I would still believe it wasn't. So the theory seems to entail that I can know p while failing to know an obvious logical consequence of p, which sounds like a straightforward refutation. Since closure is one of the most secure principles in epistemology — it's basically just the claim that knowledge is closed under competent deduction — the sensible thing to do, I assumed, is to treat the tracking account as an interesting failure and focus my paper on surveying the available patches.

The three repair strategies I'd planned to evaluate are: (1) grafting on a restricted closure principle that holds for single-premise deductions but fails for the multi-premise chains that skeptical arguments rely on; (2) hybridizing the account with contextualist machinery, so that the standards for knowledge shift between the zebra context and the disguised-mule context; and (3) biting the bullet and accepting that some closure failures are tolerable, perhaps on the ground that deduction can preserve warrant without transmitting it in the relevant sense. My roommate, who took this seminar last year with the same professor, keeps telling me I've misread the whole situation: she insists that denying closure wasn't an accidental bug in the view but a deliberate design choice, and that if I repair the theory by restoring closure, I'll saddle it with exactly the skeptical conclusions it was built to block — which would make the patch a mutation rather than a rescue.

So my questions are these: which of the three patches is actually strongest, and how should I weigh the cost of denying closure against the benefit of blocking skepticism in the paper? And along the way, I'd like to know whether any of these repairs preserve what was supposed to be distinctive about the tracking account in the first place — the counterfactual conditions and the role of the belief-forming method — or whether they quietly transform it into something closer to a safety-based account, in which case I wonder why I shouldn't just argue for safety from the start. If it helps, my professor tends to reward papers that locate where the standard dialectic has gone off the rails rather than papers that simply adjudicate between the positions on offer.
