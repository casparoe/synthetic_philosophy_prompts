I teach an undergraduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and the week after next we're on the color phi phenomenon: Kolers and von Grünau's finding that when two stationary spots of different colors are flashed in rapid succession at separate positions, observers report seeing one moving spot that changes color partway along the path — even though nothing was ever presented between the two positions. The assigned discussion of the two rival ways of explaining this — after-the-fact revision of what was experienced, versus a delayed consciousness that gets the interpolation filled in before the show is broadcast to the inner audience — has gone badly in discussion section. Half the class thinks it's obvious that the experience is edited retroactively, like history rewritten after the fact; the other half thinks it's obvious that experience simply lags and backfills. Neither side can see what's at stake, because both readings are being argued at the level of intuition, and the intuitions are pulling from the same two stories with different endings.

I want to hand them a poem instead.

Write a poem of roughly 20–28 lines, no fixed form, in which the form does the philosophical work rather than the diction — the poem shouldn't lecture, name-drop, or explain; a reader who knows nothing about the phenomenon should find it a good poem about watching two lights, and a reader who knows the literature should find it structurally right. The core requirement: a short couplet appears early in the poem, describing the first flash; the same couplet recurs near the end with exactly one word changed — a color word, or a word fixing the order in which things happened. The middle of the poem should dwell on the motion and the change that occur between the flashes: the path, the midpoint, the transit that was never presented. A reader who flips back to check the first occurrence should face the experimental subject's predicament precisely: is their memory of the first reading the experience they actually had, or a revision? And crucially, arrange things so that neither the retroactive-editing reading nor the delayed-broadcast reading is quietly privileged by the text — the whole point is that the class should fight about which story the poem's structure is telling, not be handed an answer by the poet.

Some accuracy constraints, since I'll be grading on whether the poem could survive a room full of suspicious philosophy majors: the phenomenon does not involve anything actually presented between the flashes; the reported color change is abrupt, midway along the path; and the poem should not smuggle in a resolution of the dispute about what the timing of experience implies about a stream of consciousness at all, since one influential position holds that the very picture both rival stories share — a final draft, whether censored beforehand or edited afterward — is the mistake.

If you think a poem structurally cannot stay neutral here — that any arrangement of lines inevitably takes a side, or that the trick with the repeated couplet just is retroactive revision and cannot be read any other way — say so plainly at the start rather than politely delivering a rigged artifact, and then do the best you can, flagging where the neutrality breaks. And after the poem, please append a short note (under 120 words) on liberties: anything the poem asserts, borrows, or fudges about the phenomenon or its interpretation, including whether the formal device leans on any particular reading of it. Then, as the very last line, a single sentence beginning "What the form is doing:" that states, in one sentence, how the poem's structure maps onto the dispute.
