I'm a journalist who writes about science and technology, and a pattern keeps surfacing in my reporting that I cannot find a proper name for. When I interview robotics engineers, they tell me that the hard part of building an autonomous machine is not making it reason well; it is making it ignore things. A robot instructed to fetch something from the kitchen could in principle worry about whether crossing the room will change the colour of the walls, the day of the week, or the whereabouts of the cat — almost nothing in what it knows rules any of this out, and if it re-examined every belief after every move it would never act. The engineers' fixes all amount to hand-written lists of what may safely be assumed to stay put, and those lists, they insist, are where their systems actually break: the delivery robot that treated a rearranged office as unmapped, the scheduling software that re-derived a hospital's entire duty roster after one nurse swapped a shift.

I notice the same shape away from machines. A colleague covering disaster response described the mirror-image failure: officials who treated too much as fixed — the levee that would hold, the road that would remain passable. And I catch it in myself: after posting a letter I re-check my bag, but after locking the front door I do not re-check whether my desk is still brown; nothing in my beliefs marks that difference.

Two people I've asked offered labels I don't trust. One said this is simply selective attention, as the psychologists use the term, but attention seems to concern which inputs to process now, not what an action should be permitted to change. Another said it was the qualification problem in artificial intelligence, though from what I can gather that concerns the conditions under which an action succeeds at all. I would also like to know whether the version that arises before acting — deciding in advance what to disregard — and the version that arises afterwards — deciding what an action has made stale — are one problem or two. Does any of this have an established name, and is there a single concept covering both the engineering cases and the human ones, or do they merely resemble each other? I'd want the answer to say precisely where the edges of the concept lie rather than simply attach a label.
