I am a novelist, currently at work on a book whose central figure is a hospice physician and convinced antinatalist. In the chapter I am drafting, she lays the following argument on her adult daughter, who has just announced a pregnancy, and I need to know whether the daughter's eventual rebuttal should expose a flaw or concede the point. The argument runs: (1) The presence of pain is bad, and the presence of pleasure is good. (2) The absence of pain is good even when there is no one to enjoy that absence, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is somebody who is deprived of it. (3) Hence every birth is a harm, since it introduces pains whose absence would have been good without introducing pleasures whose absence would have been bad. That much is the position she has held for years. But in the scene she goes further, and this is where I, as her author, begin to lose my footing. (4) Consider one of her own dying patients, a man whose remaining life is thoroughly happy. (5) A painless death would prevent all of his future pains, and by (2) those absences are good even though no one will exist to enjoy them. (6) A painless death would also prevent his future pleasures; but once he is dead, there is no longer anybody to be deprived of them. (7) So a painless death is, by the very asymmetry she wields against her daughter's pregnancy, a net benefit even for a happy man. (8) And what holds of one patient holds of anyone: the same reasoning licenses painlessly ending any contented life. She is not a fool, and my novel should not make her one; I do not want to hand the daughter a rebuttal that collapses on inspection.

The trouble is that the argument does not feel simply fallacious to me. The daughter's most natural objection — that the dead man *is* deprived, that he was somebody — seems to threaten premise (2) itself. If the man's past existence suffices for there being somebody who is deprived, then the never-born are disanalogous, and the antinatalist conclusion about birth quietly loses its support; but if his past existence does not suffice, then the deprivation clause in (2) never did any work against the never-born either, since the possible people who never come into existence were never anybody at all. That, at least, is how my character would reply, and I cannot tell whether she is right to. Before I write the daughter's answer, I would like a considered diagnosis. Which step of the argument is weakest — and is the trouble a hidden equivocation between people who never exist and people who cease to exist, or is the argument valid, with the real cost falling on one of the premises? What exactly does each repair cost the character's antinatalism: can she keep her view about birth while giving up the conclusion about death, or does the repair gut the original asymmetry? I would rather she lose the exchange on a point of principle than on a trick, but I need to know which it is before I write either of their closing lines.
