I work in a small government foresight unit. Every few years we inherit a shelf of long-range predictions made decades back — population, energy, resources, technology take-up — and part of my job is telling ministers which of the underlying theories to keep taking seriously and which to quietly bin. Occasionally one of these reviews ends up quoted in a ministerial speech, so the stakes are a bit higher than they look. The easy cases are the ones that came true and the ones that were always crankish. The hard ones are where the date has passed, the predicted thing didn't happen, and the theory's defenders say the mechanism is sound, only the timetable was off — "right but early". At the moment that phrase gets thrown around as if it settles the argument, and I've got no way of telling when it's legitimate and when its just a polite way of never giving up.

Please write me a decision procedure my team can run on a single missed long-range prediction. Ground it in what philosophy of science actually has to say here — the obvious point that you can't just Popper a conditional claim with a date stapled to it, the Duhem point that a prediction bundles the theory in with auxiliary assumptions about technology, human behaviour and institutions, and Lakatos's distinction between a rescue that generates new testable content and a rescue that merely absorbs failure. It should keep the theory seperate from its auxiliaries at every step, since that's supposedly where the whole adjudication lives. I want a set of ordered questions, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "stop taking this seriously" verdict, and where it hands off to the desk head because the question is one of values rather than facts.

Then run it on three cases: the original Malthusian prediction about population outrunning the food supply, where the failed auxiliaries were things like fertility behaviour and crop yields; one of the recurring global peak-oil forecasts where the predicted date kept sliding to the right; and a third case of your choosing where you suspect the procedure gives an answer I won't like — I'd rather discover that now than in front of a minister.

Constraints: it has to be runnable by a policy analyst in one afternoon, using nothing more than arithmetic, no software, and no ability to go back to the original authors. And be honest about which steps are really judgement dressed up as procedure — I can live with that. What I can't live with is pretending we've built a machine when we've built a checklist.

Finish by telling me what the procedure can't do: where "right but early" is genuinely undecidable from the inside and somebody just has to own the call, and how I should frame that limitation when I present the results upwards.
