I write historical fiction — eleven novels now, most of them set in the seventeenth century — and I have come to suspect that my genre's unwritten constitution rests on a philosophical position nobody in it has ever articulated. The rules are these: I may invent my protagonist's every thought and put wholly invented words into the mouths of real people, but I may not relocate a documented event — I can give Cromwell opinions he never held, yet if I shift a battle by a month to suit my plot, reviewers will say I have betrayed the reader's trust. The modern convention for managing all this is the author's note: a page at the back itemising what was invented, treated as a matter of professional honour, almost of oath-taking. Yet no author's note has ever prevented readers from absorbing the invented material as fact; my Cromwell is now, for some thousands of people, simply the historical one. And my publisher uses the same phrase — based on true events — on my novels and on works of invented history that present themselves as revisionist non-fiction. My question is what view of history, and of the line between history and invention, this whole practice presupposes. Does the distinction between what a person thought (fair game) and when a battle happened (sacred) track anything real, or is it an accident of what documents survive? Does the author's note presuppose a clean border that the genre's own technique renders impossible to draw? And if the underlying commitment is really that invented thought is somehow more revisable than invented fact, is that a coherent position or a confusion the practice quietly depends on? I would also like to know what the practice would look like if it presupposed the opposite — if it took the novelist's real power, the overwriting of readers' sense of the past, as the thing to be disclosed rather than the mere list of inventions.
