I finally watched Children of Men (Cuarón's film, not so much the P.D. James novel, though tell me if the novel changes your answer) after reading some philosophy about why human extinction might be bad beyond the deaths it involves. The idea I have in mind, from a recent-ish book called Death and the Afterlife, is roughly this: a lot of what we care about — medical research, political reform, art, even just planting trees — only makes sense against a background assumption that humanity will continue after we die. Take that assumption away and even the people still alive might find their projects drained of point, not because anyone dies sooner, but because the value of what we do now partly depends on a future we're implicitly counting on. The book's version of the thought experiment keeps everyone alive living out their full lives; humanity just stops after the youngest generation.

The film seems made for this. No child born in 18 years, everyone knows it's the end, and the world is coming apart — despair, nihilism, refugee cages. But here's what bugs me: the film also shows actual social collapse, violence, authoritarianism, infrastructure rotting. So when people in the film have lost their sense of purpose, is it because there's no future, or just because their lives are objectively terrible? That seems like it matters for whether the film dramatizes the philosophical point or quietly cheats to get its atmosphere of despair.

Read the film through this idea. Some scenes I'd want you to take seriously: the cousin's warehouse of rescued art (David, Picasso, etc.) and his line about how people not making it doesn't matter if the art survives — is that a rival theory of the afterlife, or a coping strategy the film undercuts? The moment in the battle where gunfire stops because people hear a baby cry. And Theo going from apathy to risking everything for Kee's pregnancy — meaning seems to come back before the world gets any better, which if anything supports the conjecture harder than the doominess does. Does the film know the difference between "society collapsed" and "humanity is ending," and where does it trade on the confusion?

I don't need you to have the book memorized or quote the film — just work from the plot and what's on screen. Do give me a verdict: does the film vindicate the idea, complicate it, or does it need a reading that ignores half the film to support it? And throughout, keep some daylight between what's uncontroversially there in the film and what's your interpretation — if you're leaning on a contested reading of a scene or of the philosophy, flag that rather than presenting it as the obvious take.
