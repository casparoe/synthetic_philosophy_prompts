Two members of my decision theory reading group spent an entire evening arguing about whether the money pump argument actually shows that intransitive preferences are irrational, and they asked me to referee, though I honestly couldn't. The exchange went like this.

Marco opened with the standard pump: suppose you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A. If you're holding C, someone can offer to swap you C for B for a small fee, then B for A for another fee, then A for C for a third — leaving you holding exactly what you started with and poorer by three fees. A preference structure that licenses surrendering money for nothing, he said, is about as clear a failure of rationality as one could ask for, so intransitivity is irrational, full stop.

Priya answered that the pump only works on an agent who stumbles through the trades one at a time. An agent who knows her own preferences can foresee the entire cycle and refuse the very first trade, and then she never loses a cent. So if the argument shows anything, it shows that a certain naive, trade-by-trade decision procedure is irrational — not that the intransitive preferences themselves are. Exploitability, she said, is a property of the procedure, not of the ordering.

Marco replied that this "sophisticated" agent buys her immunity by making a choice she disprefers: at the first node she turns down B-plus-a-fee for C, even though she strictly prefers B to C. So her choices no longer reveal her preferences at all — she's protecting herself by refusing, at every point, to take what she prefers. And notice, he added, that the preferences are only safe as long as she has perfect foresight and steady nerves; a preference set that has to be quarantined behind foresight to avoid disaster is defective in roughly the way a substance that's stable as long as it never touches air is unstable.

Priya said that just shows the one-choice-at-a-time framework is the wrong one. Real agents adopt plans and carry them out. A resolute agent settles on a course at the outset and sticks to it, and there is nothing obviously irrational about that — it's how anyone finishes a dissertation they would quit on any given Tuesday. Demanding that your choice at each node be defensible from that node's perspective alone is a requirement built for creatures with no grip on their own future choices.

Marco pushed back: if resolving against your own future preferences is legitimate, what constrains it? You could rationally bind yourself to anything, including terrible commitments. Commitment has to earn its keep by what it gets you, and here the only thing it gets you is escape from a trap your own preferences built — which is like praising a man for locking the door against himself.

Priya's last word was that the whole argument smuggles in an unrealistic picture of preference: a fixed, context-independent ranking over a fixed option set. Actual preferences respond to what you already hold and to how options are presented; the "cycle" may be an artifact of treating C-as-held and C-as-chosen as one and the same option.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six claims as actually made, say who is right about it and why, note where either of them concedes too much or demands too much, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing. I'd also like your overall verdict on whether Marco's opening conclusion survives Priya's sequence of objections.
