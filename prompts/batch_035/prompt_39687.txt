My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about vote-selling: he kept insisting the wrongness is just about bad outcomes (policies get skewed, the poor lose voice), while I found myself objecting that something about voting itself is degraded once it carries a price tag, regardless of how the policies come out. We never got past talking past each other, and it struck me that most people — my students included — have never even noticed there are two distinct complaints here, let alone felt the difference between them.

I teach an intro ethics course: 25 students, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class game that lets them discover the distinction themselves, before I name it. The game should run in two phases: in the first, students carry out some cooperative or civic task under a norm of unpaid contribution (peer grading, a queuing duty, allocating a scarce benefit among the group, or something else you devise); in the second, a price or explicit market is introduced into the very same setting, and the rules and payoffs — no real money, points or tokens instead — should be arranged so that participants predictably begin voicing both kinds of complaints on their own: complaints about efficiency and unfairness, and complaints about the task becoming something meaner. The debrief should then map the students' actual remarks onto consequentialist objections versus corruption/semiotic ones, and show them how the two come apart. Also include what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, plus a frank note on which part of the real debate — say, the long-run cultural effect of habituating people to markets — no 75-minute game can reproduce and I will have to supply by lecture.
