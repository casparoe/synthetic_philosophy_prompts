Context: I'm a PhD student working on Kant's ethics, and at a reading group last week the whole discussion got derailed by a folk-logic moment. A consequentialist friend in the group said lying is fine when it's harmless — lying about liking a gift, telling a friend their bad essay draft is promising — and someone else shut him down with "what if everyone did that?". Everyone nodded like that was a knockdown argument, and the meeting basically ended there. The thing is, I teach intro ethics and I've heard that line a hundred times, from students, from my mom, from strangers online, and I genuinely could not tell you what precise claim it's making. It gestures at Kant's universalizability test, sure, we've all read the Formula of Universal Law stuff about contradictions in conception and in the will. But the folk one-liner doesn't say "could you will it as universal law", it says "what if everyone did that" — and I suspect the precisifications of the folk version come apart from the precisifications of the actual Kantian test in interesting ways, and that some of them don't support the knockdown use at all.

So: help me lay out the distinct precise claims "what if everyone did that?" could be expressing. Off the top of my head there's at least (a) a causal-collapse reading — if everyone did it the practice or institution would destroy itself, the lying example can't be universalized because promising/trust would fall apart; (b) a mere aggregate-consequences reading — everyone doing it would just be bad, lots of little harms adding up — which seems to collapse the test into plain consequentialism and so can't be what's distinctively Kantian about it, and also can't touch the harmless-lies case as my friend set it up; (c) something like a practical-contradiction reading in the spirit of Korsgaard, where the maxim is self-defeating not because the world gets worse but because in a world where everyone acts on it the agent's own purpose can't be achieved; (d) a fairness or free-rider reading — you're exploiting the general non-doing of it; and (e) maybe a character or disposition reading, where the question is really about what kind of person you become. But I bet there are more, and I don't trust myself to sort out the sub-questions either: who counts as "everyone" (literally every agent, or agents in relevantly similar situations?), does the counterfactual world where everyone does it hold the rest of the world fixed or let it respond, and what modality is hiding in the "could" when the Kantian version asks what you could will?

For each reading, tell me whether it's plausible, whether it's substantive or basically a platitude, whether it's distinctively Kantian or just consequentialism in a costume, and — most important — whether it would actually refute my consequentialist friend's harmless-lies claim the way the person in reading group thought it did. Give me this as a numbered menu I can bring to our next meeting, since I want to reopen the argument properly this time. Also flag for each verdict how confident you are, and call out the single reading or sub-question you're least sure about and why — I'd rather know where the ground is soft than get a smooth answer. No citations needed, I know that literature well enough to fill them in myself. (Sorry for the typos if any slipped thru, writing this fast before the reading group minutes go out.)
