I've come to hold the view that Ellsberg-type preferences are rational rather than biased: when reliable probabilities are unavailable, it is defensible to prefer bets with known odds, and more generally to evaluate ambiguous acts by their worst case over the set of probability assignments one takes seriously — roughly the maxmin expected utility treatment of Gilboa and Schmeidler. What would count against this view? I'd like a map of its exposure, with the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. In particular: empirical patterns the view should not accommodate (for instance, if the aversion tracked who or what produced the ambiguity, or the agent's sense of expertise in the domain, rather than the availability of information as such — the direction Heath and Tversky's competence hypothesis points in; or a reversal to ambiguity seeking for low-probability gains, which a uniform worst-case story struggles with); formal results (e.g., that maxmin over a suitably chosen set of priors is flexible enough to rationalize nearly any pattern of choices); and cases where the choices violate principles that even a defender of the view would have to insist on, such as stochastic dominance. For each defeater, please say whether the view's defenders would themselves concede it or instead explain it away. Also state what the view predicts that its rivals do not, so the two camps are genuinely discriminable, and give an honest assessment of whether the view can be qualified into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that is a defect in a normative view of this kind. Personally, I suspect I would be most moved by evidence that the preference is insensitive to how much information is missing and sensitive instead to its source.
