I'm a software engineer who builds ML systems; I have no formal philosophy training, but I've read the core Mary's-room literature recreationally — Jackson's original paper and his later reversal, the Lewis–Nemirow ability hypothesis, and the phenomenal-concepts replies — well enough to follow a seminar discussion, not well enough to survive one.

Next month I'm presenting to a philosophy of mind reading group at the university. The audience is about ten people: two faculty, one a physicalist who treats the ability hypothesis as the settled right answer to Jackson and is impatient with replies that dilute it, and one who thinks Jackson's original conclusion was essentially correct and that the physicalist responses are clever evasions of a simple point; a doctoral student working on perception who is skeptical of thought experiments as a genre and will want to know why mine is worth running; and a mix of graduate students and a couple of outsiders like me who sit in.

My thesis is that pretraining a language model is the nearest thing we have to actually conducting the Mary experiment, and that this is interesting even though it settles nothing about consciousness. Mary is stipulated to have all the physical facts while deprived of certain encounters; a model is given a very large but plainly incomplete set of third-person descriptions of the world in text form, and then at deployment it is, in a loose sense, released into the room — it starts interacting, receiving images and feedback, being updated or fine-tuned. So the question the knowledge argument poses — is anything genuinely new acquired at release, or only abilities over facts already possessed? — becomes in this setting at least partly empirical, and we can try to measure it, which we can never do with Mary, who is a fiction defined by stipulation. My sketch, which I expect to be attacked: (1) the model before deployment stands to its later self roughly as Mary in the room stands to Mary after release, with the crucial disanalogy that "all physical facts" is violated — the training corpus is nowhere near complete, and I do not want to paper over that gap, since if it does all the work my analogy is decorative; (2) what the model plausibly acquires at deployment looks, on inspection, less like a new proposition and more like whatever the ability hypothesis says Mary acquires — new discriminatory and imaginative capacities exercised over facts already encoded — except that here we can, in principle, test before and after; (3) the analogy cuts both ways and I want to resist any reading on which it adjudicates the original question about qualia, because I don't believe it does, and I don't want to be maneuvered into defending a claim about machine experience I can't support.

Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet. I need the objections this particular audience will actually raise — not the generic internet objections about parroting or hype — ordered by how much damage each would do to my case, each phrased the way the specific person raising it would phrase it: the ability-hypothesis physicalist will phrase his one way, the pro-Jackson faculty member another, the perception doctoral student a third. For each objection give me a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft and what I should concede up front rather than contest, and flag the one point in my own position I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices. Finally, tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and what to do if it's asked anyway.
