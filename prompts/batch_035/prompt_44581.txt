At a methods seminar on the replication crisis, a senior postdoc dismissed a large-N study whose p-value was well below 0.001 with the remark that with a large enough sample, everything is statistically significant — so, she said, the result told us nothing. That one-liner circulates constantly in discussions of the crisis, and I want to understand what work it can actually do. Please lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan could be expressing, and for each say whether it is a mathematical triviality, a substantive empirical assumption, or an outright confusion. In particular, disentangle: the claim that rejection regions shrink as N grows, so any nonzero true effect will eventually be detected; the claim that null hypotheses of exactly zero effect are almost never true in the social and biomedical sciences, so significance is nearly guaranteed in advance; the claim that any two variables one might measure are correlated at some nonzero level in the population; and the claim that statistical significance carries no information about practical or theoretical importance. For each reading, state whether it is a platitude, defensible but tendentious, or false, and say explicitly which readings — if any — would license dismissing a particular significant finding as uninformative, as opposed to merely reinterpreting it as evidence of a possibly tiny effect. If the true readings are irrelevant to the dismissal while the readings that would do the work are false, I would like that said plainly. A bulleted menu of readings with verdicts, ordered from most to least charitable, would be ideal, as I intend to raise it at the next seminar.
