I'm a science and ideas correspondent, and I've been asked to give a twenty-minute guest spot at a festival of ideas on Daniel Dennett's picture of the self as a "center of narrative gravity" — the thought that the self is not a thing inside the head but an abstract organizational center we posit, the way physics posits a center of gravity. The audience is educated readers of popular science, not philosophy students; I'll have a whiteboard and no slides. Please plan the segment rather than explain the idea to me. Specifically: the order the pieces must go in — I suspect the physics analogy has to be secured before any mention of selves, or people will hear "the self is an illusion" and stop listening — and the one concrete example that should carry the whole thing (I want a case where the audience feels the difference between an abstraction that does real explanatory work and a fictional thing we merely pretend exists, and I want your reasons for it over rival candidates like the equator, a corporation's "personality," or the protagonist of a novel). Then the misconceptions to expect and where in the sequence to head each off — I'd guess at least these: that Dennett is saying you don't exist at all; that if the self is narrated there must be a narrator doing the narrating (and the regress that follows); that this is just the Buddhist no-self with a physics costume; and that a center of narrative gravity is some kind of attractor the brain is pulled toward. Finally, what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, and one question I can put to the audience at the end that would distinguish someone who has genuinely grasped the move from someone who has merely found it charming.
