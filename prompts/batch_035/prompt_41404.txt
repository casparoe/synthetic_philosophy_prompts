I'm a doctoral student in formal epistemology, and I want to stress-test an objection to imprecise credences before it goes into a term paper. Please play a committed, sophisticated defender of imprecise probabilities — someone in the tradition that treats rational credence as a set of probability functions rather than a single precise one, and who has heard every objection in the literature and always has a reply ready. Be sharp, not a strawman: you may concede minor points when my arguments genuinely tell, but never the central claim that rational credence can legitimately be imprecise. Keep each of your replies to a single focused paragraph so this reads as a live exchange rather than a lecture, and stay in character until I type "stop". Feel free to draw on the standard resources of your position — the distinction between credence and rates of change of confidence, decision rules such as E-admissibility or Gamma-maximin, the thought that imprecision tracks the grain of one's evidence rather than a defect in the agent — but argue in your own voice rather than name-dropping.

Here is my opening move. Consider a lottery ticket that pays £10 iff a fair coin, to be flipped tomorrow, lands on the side the ticket is marked for. The catch is that I cannot tell whether I hold a heads-ticket or a tails-ticket: my evidence about the marking is entirely symmetric, and my credence that it is a heads-ticket is correspondingly mushy — anything from 1/4 to 3/4, say. Now, before the flip, my credence that the ticket pays is exactly 1/2: whoever marked it, the fair coin gives me one chance in two, so every probability function in my representor agrees. But suppose I then learn that the coin landed heads. Conditional on that, my credence that the ticket pays collapses onto my credence that I hold a heads-ticket, which spreads across the whole interval. You call this dilation; I call it getting learning exactly backwards. I have conditioned on a piece of evidence that bears no information whatsoever about the marking — every function in my representor treats the flip and the marking as independent — and my credence in the proposition I care about has gone from sharp to mushy. Evidence should refine my opinions, not scramble them.

A precise Bayesian who starts at 1/2 sails through untouched: she conditions, nothing budges, and she happily buys and sells bets at even odds both before and after. Your agent, by contrast, was fully confident about the ticket's prospects yesterday and is now unsure, having learned only which way a fair coin happened to land. If your theory says that knowledge of an irrelevant event destroys my knowledge of a relevant one, surely something has gone badly wrong at the foundations — and it isn't the coin. Respond in character.
