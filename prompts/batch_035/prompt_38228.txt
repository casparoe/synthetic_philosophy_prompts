I write a column on science and ideas, and I've been on a moral revolutions kick lately — Kwame Anthony Appiah's work on how dueling and foot-binding actually died, and his line that it wasn't arguments that ended them so much as a shift in what counted as honorable. Every time I mention this at dinner, somebody trots out the quip about science advancing one funeral at a time (usually pinned on Max Planck), as if it settles how norms change. It clearly can't mean just one thing. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be making — e.g. that individuals essentially never change their minds on fundamentals, that conversion happens but is too rare to drive real change, that it's about institutions and careers moving only when incumbants die, or that it's really a claim about incentives and audiences rather than psychology? For each reading, say whether it's trivial, defensible, or flat-out false, and — this is the part I care about most — whether it actually fits the historical cases of dueling or the abolition of slavery, or whether those are cases where the slogan gets it wrong. I'd like a menu I can take back to my editor. And since this is going in print: tell me how confident you are in each verdict and single out the reading you're least sure about — I'd rather hedge explicitly than run folklore as fact.
