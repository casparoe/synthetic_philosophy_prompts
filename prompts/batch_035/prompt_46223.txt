I'm a retired physical chemist (35 years running an NMR lab, so my idea of a good time is a noisy spectrum) who joined a philosophy reading group after retirement, and last week's meeting turned into a proper slanging match between two of our members about global workspace theory and whether it has anything to say about phenomenal consciousness. I took notes because I genuinely could not referee it and I want to know who was right about what. Here's the exchange as it went, more or less:

(1) Derek, who's an engineer by training, opened by saying the whole "hard problem" is a chemist's error, by which he meant we're treating consciousness like a substance that must be hiding somewhere, when really it's just information being broadcast across the brain instead of staying local. Once a representation wins the competition for the global workspace and gets broadcast to all the specialist modules, it's conscious. Access and phenomenal consciousness are the same thing described in two vocabularies, and the mystery dissolves.

(2) Priya said that was far too quick, and brought up the old partial-report experiments — the ones where you flash a grid of letters, people say they saw all of them, but can only name the few they're cued to report. So the phenomenal capacity of the moment seems to outrun what's globally available for report. If experience is just what's in the workspace, and the workspace can only hold and broadcast four or five items, where does the rich impression of the whole grid come from? She said experience overflows access, so the two come apart and Derek's identification collapses.

(3) Derek said no — the unreported letters are sitting in a fast-decaying sensory buffer, which is itself a kind of access, just fragile access. "Potentially accessible" is still accessible, like a file on disk rather than in RAM. The theory never promised that consciousness equals what you can report right now, only what's available to the system for report, planning, etc. within a short window.

(4) Priya shot back that this waters the theory down until it's unfalsifiable — if everything from a 300-millisecond buffer to a full broadcast counts as "access," then saying consciousness is access places no constraint on anything, and you could equally say consciousness is whatever happens in the brain that's a bit available. A theory that can't lose isn't explaining anything.

(5) Derek's last move was the one I found most interesting, and I want you to take it seriously even if you end up rejecting it. He said look, in the lab this "ignition" business — the finding that around 300 milliseconds after a stimulus there's a sudden, all-or-nothing late brain response that tracks whether people will report seeing the thing, versus the earlier graded activity that doesn't — suggests consciousness is categorical, a threshold phenomenon, not a dimmer. He claimed that a workspace with graded, analog broadcast wouldn't be a workspace at all, because broadcasting at half strength to all modules is functionally the same as no broadcast, the way a public announcement mumbled below hearing threshold is no announcement. Thresholds are things I can measure, he said; dimmer-switch consciousness is something nobody can even define operationally. Priya just said "so now you're defining consciousness by report again, which is exactly what the overflow case shows is too narrow," and there it ended, with both of them looking at me, and me refilling my glass.

So please adjudicate move by move — say for each of the five moves whether it's basically right, basically wrong, or right in part, and why. Also tell me if there's some point both of them are missing that would reframe the whole thing. I'm an old spectroscopist so don't be shy about mechanism, but I'm new enough to philosophy that I'd like the distinctions spelled out rather than assumed. Then give me an overall judgment on the main question — does global workspace theory, as each of them was using it, help with phenomenal consciousness or not?

Please put your final judgment in a fixed form at the very end, as a single line starting "Verdict:" followed by one sentence saying who was more right overall (Derek, Priya, or neither) and the single most important reason.
