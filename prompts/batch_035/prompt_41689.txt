I'm a PhD student in sociology writing a chapter on memory politics in the Federal Republic, and I've run into a pairing problem I'd like your help thinking through: the Historikerstreit of 1986 and Zygmunt Bauman's Modernity and the Holocaust (1989) seem to me to belong to the same argument, yet they rarely get argued against each other. I want an analysis of the Streit through Bauman's lens — and, just as much, of Bauman's lens through the Streit.

Since you can't look things up, let me fix the salient facts as I understand them and correct me if I've got them wrong. In June 1986 Ernst Nolte published an essay in the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung arguing, in essence, that the Holocaust was a defensive and ultimately derivative response to an earlier Bolshevik precedent — the Gulag came first, and what happened at Auschwitz was a copied and escalated form of it. Habermas replied in Die Zeit that July, accusing Nolte and sympathetic historians of working to normalize the German past and detach it from national identity, and defending instead a political identity built on the memory of the crime itself. The ensuing dispute was fought almost entirely on the terrain of comparability: whether the Holocaust was singular or one atrocity among others, and what sort of comparison relativizes rather than illuminates.

Now the mapping I want, done carefully. Bauman's claim is that the Holocaust was not a lapse into barbarism but a functioning of modernity itself: rational bureaucracy, the division of labor that separates moral conscience from technical task, the gardening conception of the state that treats populations as things to be weeded and ordered, and the moral distance that mediates between office and outcome. So here is the first thing I want you to work out: what does this thesis do to each camp in the Streit? Nolte wanted a causal explanation anchored in Bolshevism; Bauman offers a rival causal explanation anchored in modernity — one that is compatible with the Gulag's priority while drawing the opposite moral conclusion, since modernity is a condition we all live in rather than a provocation someone else committed. And yet notice the awkward symmetry: Bauman makes the Holocaust comparable too — comparable to other modern bureaucratic enterprises, from the enclosure of land to industrial planning — which suggests that Habermas should have found him as threatening as Nolte, or more so. Or should he not? Maybe uniqueness can be preserved along one axis (the moral meaning of the crime) while conceded along another (the causal and technical means), and part of your job is to tell me whether the Streit's participants ever separated these axes, or whether their whole disagreement collapsed them.

Second, run it the other way: what does the Streit show about Bauman? Something I find striking is that nobody in 1986 argued sociologically — the exchange was moral and political, about identity, memory, and the obligations of the descendants of perpetrators. Does that reveal that Bauman's functionalist explanation and the memory-political frame simply talk past one another — he explains how it became possible, they argue about why it must be remembered — or does it show something is missing in Bauman, e.g., that an account centered on enabling conditions cannot say why the machinery was aimed at the Jews in particular, which was precisely the question over which German identity was felt to hang? And a point I'd especially like your judgment on: both camps in the Streit seem to have shared the assumption that explaining the Holocaust, rather than simply commemorating it, is itself a move toward relativizing it. Bauman's project rests on the opposite assumption — that seeing the crime as an outcome of modern civilization makes it more, not less, urgent and germane. Suppose his book had been dropped into West Germany in 1986 instead of 1989: would the participants have heard it as an ally of Nolte's normalization, since it decenters German particulars, or as the strongest refutation available, since it dissolves the Bolshevik-provocation story? Or is that counterfactual unfair for a reason I should articulate — perhaps that a diagnosis of modernity as such cannot enter a dispute that was, at bottom, about what a specific nation's descendants owe?

Please be disciplined about where the lens is silent rather than stretching it to speak, and tell me plainly if you think the two literatures don't talk to each other for a good reason.
