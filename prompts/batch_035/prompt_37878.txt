I am taking a second-year course in political philosophy, and we have just moved from domestic distributive justice to global justice. In this week's seminar, discussion turned on whether compatriots have claims on one another that distant strangers lack, and in particular whether a wealthy state's obligations run first to its own poor. When one student argued that substantial foreign aid is effectively optional — that our duties go to the domestic poor first, to the homeless, to underfunded local services, to struggling regions — she repeatedly closed each point with the proverb 'charity begins at home', as though it settled the ordering. My tutor seemed to accept it as a serious principle, but to me it felt like the proverb was doing more work than a proverb should be able to do, and I could not say exactly where it breaks.

Could you lay out the different precise theses 'charity begins at home' could be expressing, arranged from the near-trivial to the substantive? As I understand it, the candidate readings differ a lot in strength. It might be a claim about the mechanics of effective giving: local institutions know local needs and rarely misdirect resources. It might be a claim about ordering under scarcity: some priority must be fixed, and proximity is as good a tiebreaker as any. It might be a claim about associative duties: shared citizenship generates obligations of the kind generated by family or friendship. It might be a claim about permission rather than duty: partiality toward compatriots is excusable even if not required. Or it might be something much stronger: that the moral claims of distant strangers are systematically weaker, or even that their suffering counts for less at the level of what ought to be done. For each reading, I would like you to say whether it is plausible, whether it actually supports putting compatriots first in cases where money spent abroad would relieve much greater need at lower cost, and whether it yields my classmate's conclusion that foreign aid is at best a discretionary leftover after domestic obligations are met.

Please set this out as a list I can bring to the next seminar, and end with a short note on which of the readings people who deploy the proverb casually are most likely to have in the back of their minds — and whether the proverb itself ever does any argumentative work, or only reassures the person who recites it.
