Been keeping a list of these infinity-machine cases and writing down my gut verdict on each one, and I want to know whether they add up to a principle or whether some of the verdicts just have to go. Walking to the door by crossing infinitely many nested half-distances: obviously completable, we do it every day. A runner who alternates tiny runs with shorter and shorter pauses, squeezing the whole trip into two minutes: finishes, fine. A vase where at each step you drop in ten balls (1-10, then 11-20, etc.) and fish out the lowest-numbered one: at the two-minute mark it's empty — that still feels right to me, even though at every single step the count went UP by nine. The flip-switch lamp (Thomson's, I think): no fact of the matter whether it's on or off at the end, the story just doesn't say. But swap the flip-switch for a dimmer whose brightness swings in ever-smaller oscillations that settle toward half: now at the end it's definitely at half. And the same lamp story but with a real hand and a real switch: I'd say it just can't be done, the story physically breaks, no answer — except I'm giving a definite verdict on the vase, which is exactly as impossible, so physical impossibility can't be what's doing the work. Here's the tension I can't shake: nothing happens AT the two-minute mark in any of these, the clock just arrives — and for the lamp that's exactly why I say there's no fact of the matter. But then what made the vase empty? No ball was ever the last one fished out, so what removed the remaining ones? My current guess at a principle is something like: the end-state is fixed whenever each persisting thing gets its own last event (every ball has a finite step where it leaves and never comes back), while the lamp's on/off never gets a last event of its own. Test that against my cases, patch it or replace it, and then tell me what it predicts for the two cases I genuinely can't decide: whether the runner is moving or standing still at the exact instant he's at the finish line, and an infinite row of billiard balls where a nudge gets handed down the chain in ever shorter collisions until, at the end, everything is at rest — is "the whole thing ground to a halt, energy gone, full stop" a verdict the principle even allows? And be honest about which of my verdicts your principle has to overturn — if saving all of them requires something baroque, I'd rather drop a verdict than buy the baroque version.
