I teach an intro course on contemporary moral problems, and we're coming to the end of a unit on the doctrine of doing and allowing — killing vs letting die, active vs passive euthanasia, that whole cluster. For the final session I want to do something different and close with Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men instead of another journal article. Everyone in the class will have read it in high school, so nobody needs to catch up on the plot, and the ending is exactly on point.

Take the role of a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of killing and has a soft spot for novels that do moral philosophy better than the papers do. Think through this carefully before writing — maybe start with a short brainstorm mapping which scenes carry the doing/allowing distinction and which cut against it — then give me the reading I can actually teach.

Here's the angle I want you to take seriously. The standard framing says George kills Lennie, which is a doing, but the realistic alternative was letting Curley's mob have him, which is an allowing, or at least a non-doing. So the novel stages the exact question the doctrine is about: was George's act made worse by being a killing, or better, or neither — does the doing/allowing distinction do any work at all in our verdict on George? Then push on the part the papers handle less well. Earlier in the book, Candy lets Carlson shoot his old dog, and Candy's regret afterwards is not that the dog died but that he let a stranger do it — he thinks he should have done it himself. If you take that regret seriously, the morally loaded thing isn't doing vs allowing at all, it's something about who does it and what it costs them — agent-relative residue, or something like that (I don't fully know what to call it, thats part of what I want from you). Does the dog scene restructure how we read the ending? Is the point of George shooting Lennie himself, quietly, while telling him about the farm, that the manner of the doing absorbs what would otherwise be wrong with it? And where does the novel resist that reading — is there any honest way to say the ending is just a mercy killing the doctrine should permit, or is Steinbeck leaning on our anti-consequentialist intuitions to make the utilitarian verdict feel tragic anyway?

Two ground rules. First, don't have the students quote the text and don't you produce quotes either — work from the plot and scenes as they're commonly remembered, and flag any place where the reading turns on how exactly a scene goes, so I can double-check the novel beforehand. Second, don't smuggle in the trolley problem. If trolley stuff genuinely wants to show up, tell me why it's needed here and not just reflex.

At the end, give me one paragraph on what the novel shows about the doing/allowing distinction that the argumentative literature doesn't — something I can put up on the board as they walk out.
