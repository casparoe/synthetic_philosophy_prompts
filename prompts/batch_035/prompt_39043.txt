I'm taking a second-year course on the philosophy of history, and our seminar on cyclical versus linear conceptions of history has just covered Ibn Khaldun's account of the dynastic cycle: that group cohesion — his 'asabiyyah' — is strongest among hardened peoples on the margins, carries them to conquest and statehood, and then decays as ruling elites grow sedentary, luxurious, and dependent on paid servants rather than kinsmen, so that a dynasty rarely survives much beyond a few generations before being displaced by new outsiders with fresh cohesion. For my essay I want to test this machinery on the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, and I'd like your help working out how far it stretches. Please treat the Bolsheviks as the founding group — with ideology perhaps functioning as a surrogate for the kinship Ibn Khaldun actually had in mind — and then map the stages as carefully as you can: where in the sequence the Stalin-era consolidation belongs, whether the Brezhnev years play the role of luxury and sedentary decline, and, crucially, who is supposed to play the role of the cohesive outsiders who displace the old dynasty, since the USSR was not conquered but dissolved largely from within. Then be honest about what the theory cannot explain here — the role of economic exhaustion, elite defection, and the fact that the Party's own official history was pointedly linear — and say whether the episode works as evidence for cyclical against linear views of history, or is just an ironic coincidence dressed up as one. No reading list, please; just the analysis. And since I'll be defending this in front of a sceptical seminar, tell me plainly where the mapping is most likely to fail and what consideration would most plausibly push you to the opposite verdict about whether Ibn Khaldun's model genuinely fits the case.
