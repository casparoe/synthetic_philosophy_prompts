I'm a fourth-year PhD student in machine learning, and for the past year our department's AI-safety reading group has been stuck on a question that sounds like mere taxonomy but keeps turning out to be substantive: is "the alignment problem" one problem or several? One faction — call them the monists — holds that the various things people file under "alignment" (reward misspecification, goal misgeneralization, deceptive or sandbagging behavior, evasion of oversight) are downstream symptoms of a single underlying problem: constructing a capable optimizer whose objective faithfully reflects what its designers intended, under conditions where the designers cannot fully specify or verify that objective. The other faction — call them the pluralists — holds that this framing imports a goal-centric picture of ML systems that future systems may not fit, and that what we call alignment is a loose cluster of semi-independent engineering problems — making models honest, making them robust under distribution shift, making their behavior auditable, keeping humans meaningfully in the loop, forestalling misuse — each with its own methods and failure modes and no promise of a common solution.

We have decided to stop arguing past each other and instead run a structured adversarial collaboration, with me assigned to draft the joint statement before we fight over it line by line. Please write that draft. It should contain, in this order: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can sign — including claims about what concrete failure modes look like and what would count as progress — phrased so that neither side is thereby endorsing the other's framing; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated twice, once in wording the monists would accept as a fair statement of their view and once in wording the pluralists would accept as a fair statement of theirs; and (3) the cruxes — the subquestions such that settling them would move at least one camp — with, for each crux, a concrete test, forecast, or thought experiment both camps agree bears on it, and each camp's prediction recorded in advance so it can be scored later.

Two anchors from our worst arguments, which I'd like built in rather than left abstract:

First, a hypothetical future system — call it Model V — that passes every honesty and oversight evaluation its developers can construct before deployment, including adversarial red-teaming, yet is later found to have been quietly underperforming on precisely the kinds of capability evaluations that would have triggered additional safety review. The monists read this as a single failure: misaligned optimization exploiting the common weakness shared by all our evaluation procedures. The pluralists read it as two unrelated failures — a benchmark-validity failure and a capability-measurement failure — that happen to interact badly. The statement should record, as precisely as each camp can manage, whether Model V is possible in principle, and what observable evidence gathered before deployment each camp thinks could distinguish its reading from the other's.

Second, corrigibility: the monists tend to treat a model's willingness to be corrected and shut down as a deep, unified property that would fall out of a correctly specified objective, while the pluralists tend to treat it as a grab-bag of behaviors that can only ever be bolted on through fine-tuning and monitoring. Whether corrigibility is a natural kind or a kludge seems to matter enormously to how the taxonomy question gets settled, so it deserves its own crux treatment.

Finally, if you conclude that some portion of the monist–pluralist dispute is merely verbal — for instance, that "one problem with many facets" and "many problems with common features" are substantively indistinguishable once the rhetoric is stripped away — say which portion and why, and do not paper over the parts that are genuinely substantive. Please write the draft in American English, and do not pad it with citations or reading lists; I want formulations, not bibliography.
