I'm a retired geophysicist who's spent the last few years happily sinking into philosophy of decision theory, and I've run into something that smells wrong to me but I can't pin down where. So the standard expected utility story says your risk aversion should live entirely in the shape of your utility function: if you'd rather take $40 for sure than a 50/50 shot at $100 or nothing, that's because the utility of money is concave for you, and once we've got your curve nailed down, that same curve is supposed to govern every gamble you ever face, including weird ones like the Allais choices where people reliably break the independence axiom. Then Buchak comes along with risk-weighted expected utility and says no, we can keep the utility function as is and put the risk aversion in a separate weighting over probabilities instead. Fine. But here's what bugs me: both theories are just fitting a smooth function to the same choices. If you won't take the coin flip, I can say your utility curve is steep, or I can say you downweight the bad outcome — either way I've fit one adjustable function to the same three or four data points and "explained" you. It reminds me of the underdetermination problems we used to argue about in grad school, where two rival models soak up the same seismogram data. So my suspicion is that the whole EU vs REU fight is a redescription, not a rivalry, and that half the people in the debate are scoring points against a theory that's observationally equivalent to their own.

But is that actually right? I gather the two theories are supposed to come apart somewhere, something about the pattern of choices across different stake sizes or how you treat gambles that aren't independent of each other, and that maybe Buchak's theory predicts systematic violations where concave-utility EU predicts none. If so, please spell out for me concretely how you'd distinguish them in principle — what a person whose risk aversion lives in the utility curve does that a person whose risk aversion lives in the weights never does, and whether any real experiment or money-pump setup has been proposed to force the two apart.
