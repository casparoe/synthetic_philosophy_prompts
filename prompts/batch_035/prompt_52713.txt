i run a small linguistics study group and want to build a "how much Whorf do you actually hold?" quiz for our website. the problem with every version of this i've seen is that people self-report — everyone says "sure, language influences thought a bit", which sorts nobody. so no question can ask what you believe about the hypothesis. each question has to be a concrete experimental setup where the reader commits to a prediction about how it comes out, before hearing the result.

i want 8-10 questions, each with 2-3 answer options, drawn from cases like these (use at least six, and i'm open to others of the same grain):

- the russian blues setup: speakers of a langauge with separate obligatory words for light vs dark blue do a speeded same/different judgment on color patches — do they show an advantage for cross-category pairs, and crucially, does that change when they're doing something at the same time that ties up inner speech?
- kay and kempton's setup: english speakers vs speakers of a language whose one word covers blue and green, judging which of two chips is more similar to a third — does the lexical boundary warp the similarity judgment, or does the eye win?
- levinson's rotation design: watch chips on a table, turn 180 degrees, reconstruct the array — speakers of an absolute-direction language (guugu yimithirr style) vs a left/right language, who remembers the layout in which frame?
- lucy's yucatec sort: do speakers of a language without plural marking and with a more substance-flavored noun grammar group objects by material where english speakers group by shape?
- mandarin time primes: does thinking about earlier/later shake loose under vertical primes for mandarin but not english speakers?
- categorical perception in 4-month-olds: if prelinguistic infants already discriminate color better across categories than within them, where do the categories live?
- pirahã number tasks: with exact number words only up to three, can people hold an exact match of, say, seven batteries in mind, or does everything above three go approximate?
- the chinese-counterfactuals study that later got picked apart — maybe include this one as a trap question, and tell me how you'd use it.

then the scoring key. leaves should include at minimum: the strong determinist (flag whether any serious person actually occupies that leaf or whether it's a strawman); the attention-and-memory neo-whorfian (language shifts habits and recall, not raw perception); a thinking-for-speaking leaf (effects only while you're formulating an utterance); a leaf where language intrudes only when the task lets it and inner-speech interference is what switches it off; and the mentalese/universalist leaf (core cognition is fixed, language effects are lab noise). flag honestly which pairs of leaves no question in the quiz can actually pull apart — i suspect the last two interference-sensitive leaves give the same answers, and if so, either add the question that would seperate them or admit the quiz can't. also flag any answer pattern that comes out incoherent (e.g. big verbal-interference effects AND language rewrites perception itself) and tell me what the results page should say to a reader who lands there.
