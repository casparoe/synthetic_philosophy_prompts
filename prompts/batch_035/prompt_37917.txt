I'm editing a student philosophy journal, and next week I'll be interviewing a philosopher who works on a Lewisian line on penal substitution: she takes Lewis's observation that we already accept substitutionary punishment in mundane cases (paying a friend's fine, serving a shift for a colleague) and extends Lewis's counterpart-theoretic machinery to the doctrine of the atonement, arguing that Christ's suffering can literally count as our punishment because the crucified Christ is, in the relevant respect, a counterpart of each of us. Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the view. I want at least one question each on: (a) why the counterpart relation she invokes would transfer penal liability without also transferring things like property or marriage, given that counterpart relations are typically context-relative; (b) whether the account overgeneralizes so that any innocent could be punished in place of any guilty person, and whether, if divine permission is supposed to block this, the counterpart machinery is doing any real work; (c) the proportionality worry that a single death, however severe, cannot equal the aggregate punishment owed by billions of offenders; and (d) her recent extension of the account from vicarious guilt to imputed merit — the claim that Christ's obedience becomes ours through the same counterpart relation. For each of these, add a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give.
