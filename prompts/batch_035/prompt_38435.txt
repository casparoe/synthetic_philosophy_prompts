You are a professional philosopher specializing in the ethics of consent, and I would like you to adjudicate a family disagreement about a real-feeling case rather than deliver a lecture on the theory of consent generally. Please reason carefully about each move in the exchange below before giving verdicts.

The case: my sister was admitted through the ER with severe abdominal pain. The surgeon told her she needed her gallbladder removed that night, said there was a small chance of complications, and mentioned only in passing that antibiotics and waiting were an alternative he wouldn't recommend. She signed the form about ten minutes after first meeting him, while in considerable pain. The surgery went fine.

At dinner, my sister argued her consent was hollow. Her husband disagreed, and the exchange went several rounds:

1. She said the consent was invalid because it was extracted under duress. Coercion doesn't require threats, she said: the surgeon controlled both the information and the clock, and exploited her pain and fear to get a signature. Exploiting someone's circumstances to extract agreement is enough to vitiate consent.
2. He replied that on her definition every emergency treatment becomes coerced. A coercer has to make you worse off than you would otherwise be if you refuse; but if she'd refused, she'd simply be left where her illness already put her. The surgeon didn't create the harm that backed the pressure.
3. She conceded the baseline point but said he was still making her deliberatively worse off than she needed to be: the compressed timing, the one-sentence mention of the alternative, the phrase "wouldn't recommend" doing the work of a warning while sounding like pure expertise. That may not be lying, she said, but it's deception by design.
4. He answered that deception requires falsehood, and every sentence the surgeon uttered was true. Doctors are entitled to recommend, and if informed consent required the full menu presented with equal billing, nothing urgent could ever be consented to.
5. She then shifted ground: the work he was assigning to truthfulness belongs to competence, and competence isn't a toggle but a threshold that pain and time pressure degrade. The surgeon chose a moment when she was at low capacity, so even if the information was accurate and no one threatened her, the whole setup was aimed at her signature rather than her decision. That, she said, is the difference between consent and mere compliance.
6. He closed with a challenge: reverse the case. If she had wanted the surgery and the surgeon had leaned on her to wait, the structure would be identical, and she'd be praising his caution. A standard for valid consent can't depend on whether we happen to like the outcome it produced.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or right in part, and why. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything both of them are missing.
