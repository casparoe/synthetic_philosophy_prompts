My colleague Dana sits with me on a small community grant panel, and over the years she has accumulated a track record of verdicts on the distributional hypotheticals we pose to test each other's views. Her rulings to date:

1. A fixed budget can go either as $5,000 to a destitute member of the community or as $6,000 divided among ten comfortably-off members. She awards it to the destitute member without hesitation.
2. A freak accident destroys a billionaire's fortune, leaving everyone else exactly as they were. She denies the outcome is an improvement in any respect: worse overall, and not even better from the standpoint of equality.
3. An invention improves one well-off member's life, with no effects on anyone else. She calls this a small but genuine improvement.
4. A benefit of fixed size can go to one member just below the community's comfortable middle or to one just above it, the two being otherwise similarly situated. She directs it to the member below, while conceding this was her closest call.

Next month we decide a real question. We have $50,000 for a community health fund. It could all go to Ruth, the worst-off member by a wide margin — chronic pain and isolation — with the complication that her condition is expensive to treat, so the full $50,000 would buy her only a real but modest improvement in how her life goes. Alternatively it could be spread across five neighbors with moderate, cheaply treatable anxiety, each of them near the middle of the community's range, producing a much larger total improvement in how their lives go.

Predict Dana's verdict on this case. But what I actually need is the surrounding analysis: the candidate principles her track record is consistent with (there are at least four worth taking seriously, and they disagree about Ruth); which of her earlier rulings fit each principle cleanly, which merely strain it, and which rule it out; what each surviving principle predicts about Ruth versus the five neighbors and why; your best guess, with reasons; and one further hypothetical — specified as concretely as the four above — that would best discriminate among whichever principles are still standing after this case.

Please give me the analysis as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- candidate_principles: an array of objects, each with the fields name (a short label), statement (one sentence stating the principle precisely enough to generate verdicts), fit_with_track_record (one of clean, strained, or ruled out, followed by a clause identifying the ruling that strains or kills it), new_case_prediction (one of Ruth, the five neighbors, or undetermined), and prediction_reason (one or two sentences).
- best_guess: an object with the fields choice (one of Ruth or the five neighbors), principle (the one Dana is most plausibly running), rationale (two or three sentences), and confidence (one of high, moderate, low).
- discriminating_case: an object with the fields description (the hypothetical, fully specified) and separates (which of the candidate principles it distinguishes, and what each would say about it).
