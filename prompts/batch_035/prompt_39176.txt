Compare Douglas Hofstadter's and Melanie Mitchell's Copycat project with Dedre Gentner's structure-mapping theory of analogy, as accounts of what analogy-making fundamentally is. Copycat operates in a deliberately tiny microdomain of letter strings — if abc changes to abd, what is the analogous change to ijk? — and models concepts as fluid structures built from interacting pressures that can slip into one another; for example, the concept of successor may slip to predecessor when the target string runs backwards, so that xyz is answered with wyz. Structure-mapping, by contrast, treats analogy as the alignment of relational structure between two represented domains: object attributes are discarded in favour of relations, and connected systems of relations are preferred in accordance with a systematicity principle. Which of the two, in your view, better captures what is distinctive about human analogy-making, and which would scale more plausibly to analogies drawn outside artificial microdomains, such as comparing a national economy to a household budget? In answering, consider whether slippage — one concept being pulled toward another under pressure — can be accommodated within structure-mapping's more austere, quasi-syntactic representations, or whether it constitutes a fundamental objection to them. Justify your verdict, and identify at least one respect in which each framework seems to misdescribe the phenomenon.
