I'm giving a talk at a peace research institute in Oslo next month -- the audience is mostly security-studies and peace-research types, very Scandinavian, professionally allergic to anything that smells like militarism but also quietly proud of Finland's territorial defense doctrine. My thesis: the usual liberal defense of militia-style conscription -- that it's just an extension of armed neutrality, a purely defensive posture that keeps the state out of other people's wars -- doesn't actually do the justificatory work. The argument that survives is a republican one: conscription is justified because a population that is itself the army has a kind of collective standing that a professional army undermines, regardless of whether the country is neutral. My sketch: deterrence-by-population (no decapitation strike works when everyone is trained and the rifles are in their closets) is a public good that only gets produced if ordinary citizens produce it, and once you accept that reasoning you're commited to conscription for non-neutral states too, which I want to bite the bullet on. Prep me for the Q&A: the objections these people will actually raise, in their vocabulary (they'll talk about the militarization of society, gender and conscription, whether the Nordic social contract can carry it), ordered by how much damage each would do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- whether a credible-by-population army even makes sense once cheap drones and precision munitions let a small professional force do what mass used to do. And at the end, flag which of the replies you're least confident actually holds up, and how sure you are about that ranking.
