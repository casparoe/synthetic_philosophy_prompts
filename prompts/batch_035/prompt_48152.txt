I am a philosophy professor preparing a seminar session on the extended mind, and I would value the judgment of a professional philosopher of mind with deep familiarity with this literature. Please think carefully before answering, and take a definite position rather than surveying options neutrally.

I have constructed an argument that I suspect proves too much, and I cannot locate the flaw with any confidence:

(1) A state counts as part of a subject's cognitive system when it plays the right functional role: it is reliably available when needed, its contents are endorsed rather than re-checked, and it guides reasoning and action the way memory normally does. Clark and Chalmers's Otto and his notebook illustrate the role, and I find that case convincing: the notebook entries are, functionally, Otto's beliefs.

(2) Parity of treatment: once (1) is granted, it is arbitrary to deny the status to an external state that plays precisely the role an internal state would have to play to count as cognitive; location cannot matter if role is what matters.

(3) Nothing in that role is hostage to the kind of vehicle, so any state playing the role counts as cognitive, wherever it is realized — in a notebook, a phone, or another person's head.

(4) I have trained myself to rely on a certain colleague the way Otto relies on his notebook: asking her is effortless, I act on her answers without verification, and she is reliably there. By (2) and (3), her occurrent judgments, at the moments I consult her, are literally parts of my mind.

(5) By the same reasoning, every expert or device I reliably and uncritically consult — the reference librarian, the taxi driver, the search engine that finishes my thoughts — contributes states to my mind, so my mind is distributed across most of the people and instruments I habitually lean on.

(6) The upshot appears absurd: a single token state can belong to many minds at once; when my colleague is asleep, part of my mind is temporarily unavailable; when she retires, I suffer a partial mental loss. Worse, the criterion threatens to spread to any environmental regularity I exploit, dissolving the difference between having a mind and inhabiting a helpful world.

My students will want to know which step to reject. My own suspicion is that (3) is where parity overreaches — that Otto's notebook differs from my colleague in some respect that matters (constancy of access? prior endorsement of the specific contents? the fact that the notebook's contents were once mine?) — but every candidate difference I try either excludes the notebook as well or looks like an ad hoc boundary drawn solely to stop the spread. Rejecting (2) instead seems to collapse the entire extended-mind program, which I would rather not do, and simply accepting (5) and (6) strikes me as principled but perilously close to a reductio of my own premises.

Please identify the weakest step or inference, explain why it seemed plausible, say precisely what rejecting it costs elsewhere in the argument, and assess whether biting the bullet on (6) is really as absurd as it sounds. I am most interested in where a defender of the original Clark-Chalmers thesis should draw the line, and in whether that line can be drawn on principle rather than by sheer conservatism about what we are used to calling a mind.
