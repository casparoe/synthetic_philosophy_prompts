Next month I present to my department's philosophy of mind reading group, which has just finished a term on realization, levels, and mental causation. My topic is Dennett's careenium, the closing thought experiment of his 1991 paper "Real Patterns," and my thesis is a deliberately strong reading of it. For the members who missed that session, here is the setup as I will present it: imagine an idealized pool hall whose table is so close to frictionless that a skillfully launched system of balls will careen around indefinitely, colliding and re-colliding without settling. The regulars of this hall, in the telling, learn to set up self-sustaining and self-reproducing configurations in the ball traffic -- little persistent structures whose "lives" consist in ongoing patterns of collisions, and which, once running, do whatever their organization allows to keep themselves going. Call these the simms. Now someone fires a single heavy ball -- a simmball -- across the table on a path that would plow straight through a simm colony, and the colony, purely in virtue of how its pattern is organized, arranges the incoming collisions so that the simmball is deflected. Every collision along the way is just ball striking ball; no extra force appears anywhere in the hall. Dennett asks whether the simms can deflect the simmball -- whether the pattern-level facts can push the pool-hall-level physics around -- and my answer is yes: the deflection is caused by the simms, by the pattern and not merely by the balls, and that is exactly what makes the pattern real rather than a bookkeeping convenience. I then generalize: the intentional level in creatures like us is that kind of level, so the symbols do push the substrate around, in the only sense required for genuine causal efficacy -- no new forces, no closure violations, and no overdetermination once causation is allowed to live at more than one grain.

The room will not be gentle. Of roughly a dozen people, two are senior faculty who regard Kim's causal exclusion argument as decisive and nonreductive physicalism as a failure of nerve; one is a card-carrying interventionist about causation who thinks talk of levels is empty until it is cashed out in difference-making terms; one is a visiting complexity scientist who suspects both camps are confused about what "levels" are in the first place; two grad students will defend anything that keeps the intentional stance afloat; and the organizer has a habit of asking the most damaging question last, quietly.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will actually arrive, phrased the way each of those people would phrase them, marked by how much damage each could do; with each, a two-minute reply, what I should concede up front, and where my reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. And tell me which single concession, if extracted and pressed, would collapse my strong reading into the weaker deflationary one I am trying to avoid -- so I know exactly where I cannot retreat.
