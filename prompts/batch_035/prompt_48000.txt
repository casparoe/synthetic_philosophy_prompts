I write literary fiction — three novels in, the last one a fairly unrelenting story about a marriage dissolving under the strain of a child's long illness — and I have become genuinely puzzled by the norms of my own trade. Consider what the craft of tragedy actually asks of me. My jacket copy is expected to promise devastation: publishers put phrases like "a novel that will break your heart" on the cover as a selling point. Yet the same editorial apparatus treats "bleak" as a rebuke and "devastating" as a compliment — the identical property, named differently depending on whether the reader is presumed to have enjoyed it. We have an informal reread test: a sad novel is judged to work if readers voluntarily return to it, which is odd if the experience were really aversive. And at the very moment I am trying to make a loss hurt most, the craft tradition instructs me to aestheticise it — the beautiful sentence at the deathbed, the controlled chapter break, the foreshadowing that lets the reader brace. There is also the norm that suffering must be "earned": an unearned tragedy draws the misery-porn accusation, and genre romance can even contractually require a happy ending while literary fiction cannot. It rather looks as though the whole apparatus is engineered to make readers grieve and to keep them comfortable enough to keep turning pages — and possibly to come back.

My question is this: taken together, what theory of why we enjoy sad art do these craft conventions presuppose? I can see that they might fit Aristotle's thought that tragedy works through a purgation or working-through of pity and fear, or Hume's suggestion that the eloquence of the treatment converts an otherwise unpleasant passion into a pleasant one, or some view about safe distance and make-believe, or the idea that readers want to rehearse loss in a meaningful order rather than a chaotic one. Please reconstruct the implicit theory — or theories — as best you can, say which rival accounts the practice quietly rules out and which it is merely compatible with, and tell me whether the conventions are mutually consistent or whether, say, the reread test and the aestheticisation norm presuppose different things. Where the norms underdetermine the theory, say so rather than forcing a single reading. Then tell me what would actually have to change in my practice if the best-supported theory turned out to be false and one of the rivals were true instead.

Please also be candid about the fragility of your own reconstruction: at the end, tell me which argument or consideration would most likely move you to a different verdict about what the craft presupposes, and where your reasoning is most vulnerable. And use British English throughout.
