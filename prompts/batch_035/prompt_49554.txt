I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a large daily, and I'm drafting a long essay for the ideas section about what our own fact-checking apparatus quietly assumes about truth. Here is the practice I want to understand. Every checkable claim in a story — a date, a statistic, an attribution of a finding to a study — is verified by a researcher before publication, and it runs only if the desk's confidence in it clears an informal but real threshold; claims that fall short are cut or hedged. Yet no one at the paper believes the conjunction. Our annual transparency report cheerfully concedes that the previous year's journalism, taken as a whole, certainly contained errors, and any honest editor would sign a preface saying as much about any 6,000-word feature in particular: each sentence individually earned its place, and at least one of them is very likely false. Philosophers have names for the two shapes here — the lottery paradox, where it is rational to believe of each ticket that it will lose while irrational to believe they all will, and the preface paradox, where an author rationally believes each claim of her book and rationally believes the book contains at least one falsehood. There are further wrinkles. We grade public figures' claims on a six-point scale when we fact-check them, yet treat our own copy as all-or-nothing: publish or don't. Our corrections policy is retrospective only — we assert now, repair later — never prospective. And our headline standard demands that the headline be defensible standing alone, even though everyone knows it compresses qualified claims into an unqualified one.

What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit epistemology of this practice. What view of assertion, or of justification, does the practice only make sense under — a threshold view on which justified belief is not closed under conjunction, a distinction between what we may assert and what we may believe, or pure pragmatism about reader trust with no epistemology underneath at all? Say which rival views the practice quietly rules out, whether its parts presuppose consistent things (the graded scale for politicians' claims against the binary gate for our own; the certifying act of publication against the annual admission of error), and where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, so that several theories of knowledge would license it equally well. Finally, tell me what would actually have to change in the newsroom if the desk adopted a straightforwardly probabilistic regime — credences attached to claims, published confidence bands — since my readers will want to know whether the current practice is a coherent alternative to that regime or merely its unexamined ancestor. Pitch the analysis so an intelligent lay reader can follow it, and name the philosophical positions where the names help rather than hinder.

End with a final line of the form "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase of no more than fifteen words naming the view the practice presupposes, or stating plainly that no single view does.
