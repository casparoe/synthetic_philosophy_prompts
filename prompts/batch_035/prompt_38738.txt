I'm a science journalist putting together a feature on whether fields outside the empirical sciences "make progress" the way physics or medicine obviously do. For the philosophy section I asked a physicist friend of mine, and he gave me what I gather is the standard scientist's take — I've seen versions of it in a couple of pop-science books too. Here's roughly what he said: philosophy has made no progress in 2500 years because philosophers are still arguing about the same questions Plato argued about. It asks the big questions but can never answer them, because it has no experiments. The only real progress philosophy ever made happened when its questions got settled by turning into science — that's where physics came from (natural philosophy) and psychology too (out of philosophy of mind). So the only way philosophy progresses is by handing its problems to people with actual methods and, effectively, committing suicide. What's left — free will, consciousness, morality — is precisely the stuff that has never gone anywhere. Ask a philosopher what the field has definitively established in the last century and you'll get silence.

I suspect this is too glib — the philosophers I've interviewed for other pieces clearly think their field moves forward somehow — but I don't know enough to push back intelligently. Can you audit the summary for me? Which parts would a working philosopher actually concede, which parts conflate things philosophers keep distinct (like "we still debate question X" vs. "we've learned nothing about X"), and where is a defensible modest claim being blown up into something bigger? A few specific things I want to nail down:

1. Is the "philosophy only progresses by becoming science" line an actual defended position with a name, or just a cocktail-party line? Is the history even right — did psychology actually split off from philosophy of mind, or from something else?
2. The "no experiments" bit — is that true as stated, or does it miss something (thought experiments? the fact that math has no experiments either and nobody says math never progresses — though maybe that analogy is a cheat, you tell me).
3. Is the "ask a philosopher what they've established and you'll get silence" challenge fair, or is it setting the bar somewhere no field outside math could clear — including, say, medicine?

My editor will definately want me to give the philosophers a fair hearing, so I'd love a sense of what the strongest version of their "yes, philosophy progresses" response looks like — including whether they'd point to specific cases where a view got abandoned for cause rather than just going out of fashion.
