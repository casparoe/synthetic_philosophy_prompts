Two colleagues of mine have been arguing about the tragedy of the commons for the better part of a decade, and they have finally agreed to run a proper adversarial collaboration and publish a joint statement. One is an economist of a broadly Coasean temperament: he holds that the root of commons problems is missing property rights and that the default remedy is to define exclusive rights and let exchange and prices coordinate use, with state intervention as a fallback when transaction costs get ugly. The other is an institutionalist squarely in the Ostrom tradition: she holds that communities of users routinely solve commons problems themselves with rules, monitoring, and graduated sanctions, and that imposed privatization or top-down regulation often destroys functioning local arrangements. Each thinks the other's diagnosis rests on cherry-picked cases. They've asked me to help them structure the document, and I'd like to hand them a strong draft.

Please write the joint statement as I imagine they'd want it. First, a numbered section of claims both can sign — I want this to include the shared logic of the problem itself (non-excludability and subtractability as the two features that generate overuse risk, the distinction between a genuinely open-access resource and a common-property regime with rules, and the ways Hardin's 1968 framing conflated these), as well as shared claims about the empirical record that neither a doctrinaire privatizer nor a romantic communitarian could accept. Second, a table of their precise disagreements, each phrased so that the side holding the view would accept the wording — for example, on the proper default diagnosis, on whether "privatize or regulate" is a false dilemma, and on how far Ostrom's design principles travel to hard cases: large user populations, cheap exit, mobile resources like fisheries, aquifers with fuzzy boundaries, and the global atmosphere. Third, the cruxes. I suspect one is whether peer monitoring and graduated sanctions can survive cheap exit and user heterogeneity, and another whether state-enforced property rights function as a complement to or a substitute for local institutions — but I'd like you to identify whatever cruxes you actually think would move one of them, and to separate the genuine disagreements from ones that turn out to be verbal. One candidate verbal dispute to check: whether "privatization" means only state-issued individual title, or any exclusion regime whatsoever, including one a village builds and enforces for itself.

For each genuine crux, include a concrete case, natural experiment, or study design that both would agree in advance bears on it — for instance, comparing adjacent fishery jurisdictions where one moved to individual transferable quotas and the other to community co-management, or a public-goods experiment varying the cost of exit and the availability of communication — and record each side's prediction about what it would show, stated specifically enough that they could later be shown to have been wrong. They are both stubborn, so part of the value is in the wording: formulations that are neither vacuous (they'll refuse to sign a document of truisms) nor slanted toward either side. Please take the drafting seriously as a piece of constructive diplomacy in prose, not as a survey of the literature and not as an opportunity to declare a winner.
