I teach an upper-level ethics seminar on moral worth — the question of when an act is not merely right but done for the right reasons, and whether worth attaches to what the agent does or to how she stands toward what she is doing. There are 15 students and one 75-minute session. Design an in-class game in which roughly half the students are "actors," each secretly assigned one of four motivation profiles: a calculator who re-optimizes expected outcomes case by case, a rule-follower who applies a fixed principle regardless of consequences, a habit-agent who acts from settled affection or routine without deliberating, and a conformist who does whatever the likely audience will reward. Over several rounds, each actor makes a series of choices in which the right thing to do stays constant but the cost of doing it, the visibility of the choice, and the temptation to defect all vary. The other students are "diagnosticians" who watch only the recorded choice patterns and must wager their points on which profile each actor was given — the payoff scheme should use points only, no real money. Crucially, the profiles and the debrief should be phrased behaviorally at first, so that no one hears a theory name until the game is over. The debrief should then map what happened onto the philosophical distinction between the rightness of an act and its worth, centering the idea that a single outwardly right action underdetermines motive and that worth shows up, if at all, only in a pattern across cases where temptations and audiences shift. Include the rules and materials, a timeline for the session, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on which feature of acting for the right reasons — say, the agent's own take on what she is doing — a game based on observable behavior cannot reproduce and I must supply in discussion. Keep the whole design under 800 words.
