My friend Dan, who's read way more philosophy than me, tried to explain Twin Earth to me after a few beers and I want to check whether he got it right. Here's roughly what he said: there's a planet exactly like Earth except the wet stuff in the oceans and rivers isn't H2O, it's some other molecule, call it XYZ, that fills glasses, puts out fires, and tastes just like water. There's a guy on Earth and his exact duplicate on Twin Earth — same brain, same upbringing, same everything — so their heads are in literally identical states. But the Earth guy's word "water" refers to H2O and the Twin Earth guy's refers to XYZ, so the two of them mean different things by the same word while being in identical mental states. Which, Dan said, proves meanings just aren't in the head, and what your words mean is fixed by what the stuff out there actually is, not by anything you know or believe. He added that this was already true in 1750 before chemistry existed — water was H2O and the word meant H2O even though nobody on the planet knew it — so what individual speakers believe is basically irrelevant to meaning. He said Putnam also had a nice example about elm and beech trees showing that when ordinary people use those words we're really just deferring to botanists, and another one where a patient complains about having arthritis in his thigh and it still counts as him meaning arthritis. Then the punchline: since what's in your head doesn't determine what you mean, externalism ends up implying you can't even be sure what you're thinking, so it's basically a form of skepticism about your own mind, and that's why most philosophers nowadays consider the whole argument refuted.

Something about the flow of that felt off to me, like the last part was doing way more work than the example supports. The bit about two identical guys meaning different things I'm inclined to accept — that's kind of the point of the thought experiment, right? But the jump from there to "you can't know your own mind" and "it's been refuted" feels like Dan sneaking in his own conclusions under the radar. Can you go through his account piece by piece and tell me which parts a philosophy person would nod along to, which parts are subtly off, and whether the skeptical-sounding conclusion actually follows from the stuff before it?
