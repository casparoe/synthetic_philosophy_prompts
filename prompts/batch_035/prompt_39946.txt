I'm a third-year PhD student in formal epistemology, and our department runs a small forecasting group that meets fortnightly to place low-stakes calibration bets on questions in and around philosophy of science. Once the dust settles on a dispute, most of our disagreements turn out not to concern the target proposition at all but rather which reference class a frequency should be drawn from, and over the two years I've been a member, the de facto adjudicator of these disputes has been a retired actuary in the group, whose rulings have been strikingly consistent. Her rulings so far: (1) when we bet on whether a friend's startup would survive five years, she ruled for the class of same-sector ventures founded by people with prior founding experience, against the class of all ventures — on the ground that the drivers of survival, sector economics and founder learning, are common within the narrower class and heterogeneous across the broader one; (2) when we estimated the probability of a fatality on the first crewed flight of a new spacecraft, she ruled for the class of first flights of new crewed vehicles across all programs, against the class of all crewed flights, despite the latter having an order of magnitude more data — on the ground that the operative uncertainty is the maturity of the vehicle, not spaceflight as such; (3) when we bet on whether a colleague up for tenure would get it, she refused both the department-wide tenure rate and the field-wide rate and insisted on the class of candidates whose records, as judged by the information available at the time of the bet, resemble our colleague's — on the ground that for a bet about a person we know, the class must be matched on what we know, not on institutional labels; (4) when we estimated whether our department's newly hired postdoc would still be in academia in five years, she ruled for the broad class of quantitatively trained postdocs across all fields, against postdocs within our own discipline — on the ground that what drives academic retention for such people is the outside-option market, which is common across fields.

At the next meeting we have to set a credence for a bet on whether the present paradigm for training frontier models — large-scale pretraining on web text, with compute, data, and parameters scaled in tandem — will still be the dominant route to more capable systems in 2035, or whether it will have stalled. The classes on the table: (i) earlier AI paradigms that enjoyed a period of striking progress followed by a long stall, such as symbolic AI in the 1970s, expert systems in the late 1980s, and connectionism's first wave; (ii) technologies whose progress traced an empirical curve that held across several orders of magnitude, such as transistor density or the cost per sequenced genome; and (iii) the undifferentiated class of technologies that were ever hyped as transformative.

What I want from you is not a ruling of your own on the 2035 question but a prediction of hers. First, articulate the rule or rules that best rationalize her four rulings, including at least one alternative rule that fits them about equally well. Second, say where those rules come apart on this case and what each of them would predict her to rule — I am less interested in the final answer than in whether the track record genuinely determines it. Third, give your best guess as to her ruling, with your reasons for preferring it. And fourth, since her rulings so far may genuinely underdetermine the answer, propose the next reference-class dispute we should stage — the further case that would most cleanly separate the candidate rules you've identified.
