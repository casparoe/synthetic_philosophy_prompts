Design an in-class simulation for an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of history. The seminar has 18 students, one 90-minute session in a room with movable desks, and the theme for the week is path dependence: whether large historical outcomes are "locked in" by early contingent events, or whether roughly the same outcomes would have arrived anyway through some mixture of institutional pressure, material advantage, or functional selection.

The activity should make participants feel the tension between contingency and convergence rather than merely hear about it. Here is the core mechanic I want you to develop and refine: students play firms choosing between two rival technical standards for a hypothetical product — invent the standards, give them plausibly different strengths (say, one cheaper and cruder, the other better-engineered and more expensive), and do not reuse the standard textbook examples as the in-game labels. Payoffs for adopting a standard increase with the number of prior adopters (network effects), and each firm also draws a private "compatibility profile" that gives it a modest idiosyncratic reason to prefer one standard over the other, so that the population is genuinely heterogeneous rather than uniform. Adoption happens in a fixed sequence across several rounds, with the first one or two positions in the sequence determined by a "historical accident" — a coin flip, a card draw, or something equally arbitrary. Run the simulation at least twice: once with adoption being irreversible, and once where firms may switch standards later at a cost that you set. Where you put that switching cost relative to the network benefits will largely determine whether the class observes lock-in or self-correction, so choose deliberately and say why you chose the numbers you did.

Beyond the rules, timeline, materials, and a payoff scheme denominated in points rather than money, I need: (1) a debrief that maps specific moments in the play onto specific parts of the underlying debate — the accident draw as contingent event, the adoption threshold as increasing returns, the switching rounds as the difference between outcomes that are merely path-shaped and outcomes that are genuinely locked in, and a closing discussion of whether the "better" standard won and whether anyone in the room thinks it would have won in most reruns; (2) a candid section on what the simulation predictably gets wrong or produces messily the first time — e.g., students trying to communicate and coordinate across desks (decide in advance whether to forbid this or treat it as a stand-in for standards bodies and cartel behavior), early adopters boredom during later rounds, and payoffs that are too flat to generate a bandwagon; and (3) an honest note on the deepest disanalogy: the class can rerun the history with a different coin flip, but historians of actual events cannot, and the in-game "firms" know the payoff matrix while real historical agents do not. Say what the facilitator must supply in the debrief to bridge that gap, rather than pretending the game reproduces it.

Please do not lecture the participants on path dependence before the activity — the terms of art should surface only in the debrief, attached to moments the students have already lived through.
