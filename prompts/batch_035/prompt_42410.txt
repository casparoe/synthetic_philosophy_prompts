I'm writing a novel where a small-town epidemiologist, Maren, spends Thanksgiving fighting with her brother Henrik, who edits the local paper and just published a hospital mortality league table. I want to stress-test Maren's position before I let her win the argument in chapter 14, so please play Henrik: sharp, a little petty, genuinely convinced the tables are a public service, willing to concede a footnote but never the front page. Stay in character until I type "stop", keep each reply to one paragraph, and open every reply with your verdict in the first sentence followed by your reasons — no throat-clearing. If you need a factual question answered, ask it as Henrik would at a dinner table.

Here's the data in the world of the novel, so you know what both siblings know. St. Anselm's, the big city hospital, had 210 deaths among 3,000 admissions last year; Lakeside, the regional one, had 54 among 1,500, which is why Henrik's table has Lakeside on top by a mile. But split by severity at admission: among severe cases St. Anselm's lost 200 of 2,000 while Lakeside lost 36 of 300, and among mild cases St. Anselm's lost 10 of 1,000 while Lakeside lost 18 of 1,200. So within each group St. Anselm's does better, and it only looks worse overall because everyone knows it takes the cases nobody else will touch. One more thing you should know, because Henrik knows it too: the severity scores in that table come from the hospitals' own intake coding, and I've decided Henrik is the kind of editor who's going to hammer on that.

Maren's opening move, right after the carving, slamming your front page on the table: your league table is going to get people killed, Henrik, because every single patient who walks through either door is more likely to walk out of St. Anselm's, and mom needs surgery friday and you're about to send her to Lakeside. Don't hold back — I need to find out if she survives you.
