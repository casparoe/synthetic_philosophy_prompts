so I'm a novelist, working on my third book, and I've gotten myself stuck on something that's more philosophy than fiction at this point. My main character is a woman who every night, in full sincerity, decides she'll quit her job and start the pottery studio thing in the morning, and every morning she talks herself out of it. My editor keeps saying "she just lacks willpower" and I keep feeling that's not just lazy writing but like, philosophically lazy too? So I've been reading up and I stumbled into this whole thing about hyperbolic discounting and now I need someone to untangle it for me before I write another word.

Here's what I need explained, as concretely as you can: people say the hyperbolic discounter has "reversing preferences" — near in time she prefers keeping her stable job (small-sooner reward, whatever), far in time she prefers the studio (larger-later). And the claim seems to be that this reversal pattern is somehow the *explanation* of akrasia, of weakness of will. But I don't get how it can be an explanation rather than just a redescription? Like if you tell me she prefers A up close and B from afar, you've told me the shape of her preferences, not why she acts against the one she "really" holds. Or have you? That's the crux I want untangled.

Some specific things I'd love you to hit:

- With exponential discounting preferences never flip as time passes, with hyperbolic they do. Why does that matter so much for whether the person is irrational? Is the akratic actually irrational in the hyperbolic picture, or does the discounting framework somehow excuse her? (One thing I read suggested something like: if you genuinely prefer A when A is near, then choosing A when it's near isn't acting against your own judgement at all, it's just... acting on your current preference. Which would mean my character isn't weak-willed, she's just a sequence of different selves in a trenchcoat. Is that a serious position people hold? Do they?)
- The Davidson-style problem where akrasia seems impossible because you can't intentionally do X while judging all-things-considered that Y is better. Does hyperbolic discounting dissolve that paradox or does it just dodge it? My gut says it dodges it but my gut has been wrong before on this stuff.
- And here's the thing I really need for the book: suppose I buy that akria is preference reversal. Does anything follow about how my character should be *written*? Like, is there still room for the felt phenomenon — the lying awake at 3am, the sense of betraying yourself — inside the discounting model? Some of what I read talks about the person as a kind of bargaining household of temporally extended selves, which is honestly a great image for fiction, but I can't tell if it's a metaphor people actually defend or just vibes.

Please explain all this in plain language, I don't have any formal economics background. And actually take a side at the end: does hyperbolic discounting *explain* weakness of will, merely *describe* it, or does it show the whole notion is confused? I need to know what I think my character's deal is before I write the middle third of this novel.
