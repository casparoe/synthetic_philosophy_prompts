In the novel I'm drafting, two characters each utter, word for word, the same sentence: a first-person, present-tense claim of the form "p, but I don't believe it." One utterance should land as flatly absurd, in the way Moore found; the other should be natural and quietly devastating, so the reader feels the difference. Construct the two cases for me. Constraints: the same proposition p and the very same words in both; ordinary, realistic settings — no hypnosis, no brain injury, no science fiction; and the second case must not get its naturalness from past tense, from the "believe in" sense (as in ghosts or astrology), or from the speaker being simply mistaken about her own mind. One paragraph per case. After that, run the standard check on each: what asserting the first conjunct expresses, what the second conjunct cancels, and why the result is or isn't absurd. Then tell me which single feature of the second case is doing the work, and give the strongest reply of a philosopher who holds that such sentences are always absurd when sincere and present-tense — in particular, whether her best move is to claim the second case is just the astonishment idiom ("I can't believe it!" said of a lottery win) wearing mourning clothes, or an equivocation between belief as endorsement and belief as a settled, felt state; and say whether that reply succeeds. I ask because I need to know whether my second character's line is philosophically respectable or merely sentimental.
