During the pandemic I ran the "Swiss cheese" infographic in a piece about layered defense: masks, ventilation, testing, vaccination — each a slice of cheese with holes, catastrophe only when the holes happen to line up. My memory is that the model comes out of safety engineering, from James Reason's work on human error and system accidents — correct me if I've misattributed it, but the details of the pedigree aren't what I'm writing about.

What I'm writing about is that, since then, I keep hearing historians reach for almost exactly the same picture, usually about the July crisis of 1914: the alliance blocs, the mobilization timetables, the German blank cheque, the domestic politics of humiliation — a stack of slices, each with a hole, and the assassination at Sarajevo merely the alignment that let the hazard through. The conclusion being drawn is that the war was overdetermined: remove Gavrilo Princip and the holes line up somewhere else in 1915 or 1916, so explanations that dwell on any single slice — and blame attached to any single actor — are missing the structural point.

Here is what bothers me, and it's the reason I'm writing to you rather than to the historians. In the version of the model I learned during COVID, the slices are defenses and the holes are failures; the disaster is a hazard penetrating a defensive system that should have stopped it. In the historians' use, the slices look like causes, not defenses, and the alignment of the holes just is the event. The picture seems to have been turned inside out without anyone acknowledging it. I want an audit of the analogy as it is actually being used, not a survey of positions.

Specifically, please give me:

1. A correspondence table with exactly these columns: (a) element of the original Swiss cheese model as used in safety engineering; (b) its counterpart in the July 1914 usage; (c) whether the mapping holds, strains, or inverts; (d) whether that strain matters for the overdetermination conclusion or is merely incidental.

2. A short inventory of the features of the original model that get quietly imported into the historical use — for instance, that the slices are deliberately designed barriers, that the holes are independent of one another, that any intact slice would have been sufficient to block the hazard, that the model is prospective advice about where to add layers — and, for each, a judgment on whether the import does real work for the historians' conclusion or is dead weight.

3. A committed verdict. Once the disanalogies are on the table, does the stacked-slices picture still support the claim that the war was overdetermined and that individual-level explanation and attribution of responsibility are therefore misguided? Or was the analogy carrying that conclusion the whole time — with the overdetermination claim needing independent argument that the cheese never supplied? If there is a better analogy for what the structural historians actually mean — fuses in series, a pile of dry tinder versus the spark, banked dominoes, or something you devise yourself — name it and say what it captures that the cheese cannot.

Please end with a JSON object summarizing your verdict, with these fields: "verdict" (one of "carries", "strained-but-survives", "fails"), "load_bearing_disanalogy" (a single sentence identifying the disanalogy doing the most damage to the historians' use), "independent_argument_still_needed" (a single sentence stating what, if anything, must be argued without the analogy to sustain overdetermination), and "better_analogy" (the name of your alternative, or "none").
