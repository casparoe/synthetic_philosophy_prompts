I'm a software engineer — about twelve years now, most recently working on evaluation pipelines for a deployed machine-learning product — and I have no formal philosophy background, so forgive me if I misuse terminology. A series of incidents at work (a recommender surfaced something genuinely harmful to a vulnerable user, and the post-mortem devolved into everyone pointing at everyone else, then eventually at the model itself) got me reading about what philosophers apparently call "responsibility gaps": situations where an autonomous system causes harm and no human seems properly blameworthy, because the harm wasn't foreseeable by any individual in the chain. I've come out of that reading with a fistful of commitments, and before I embarrass myself in the write-up, I want an audit of whether they're consistent. Here's the set:

1. I deny that responsibility gaps exist. "The algorithm did it" is never an acceptable final answer: trace back through data choices, design decisions, deployment calls, and somebody owns it — that's what accountability means, and I think letting it dissolve into a shrug would be corrosive.
2. Responsibility requires control. You're only blameworthy for harms you could have foreseen and controlled; punishing or blaming someone for the genuinely unforeseeable is unfair, full stop.
3. Modern ML generalization is genuinely open-ended. No individual engineer can foresee the specific failure modes of a system at deployment scale, no matter how good their eval suites and red-teaming are. I've watched our best people be surprised, and it wasn't negligence — it was the nature of the thing. I don't think better process closes this; I think anyone who claims otherwise hasn't shipped one.
4. Organizations can be genuinely responsible agents. Our company can be blameworthy in a way that isn't just shorthand for "some employees are blameworthy."
5. Blame is about desert, not cost allocation. It's a response to what someone deserves for what they did; a person who did nothing wrong cannot deserve blame, however convenient blaming them would be.
6. I'm entirely comfortable with our company bearing strict, no-fault liability for harms our product causes — that's just sound product liability, and compensation is not blame.
7. Case verdicts: where a reasonable engineer would have caught the defect in review, the people who shipped it are blameworthy. But where the model produces a harm no eval suite could have predicted, I still hold that someone — the executive who chose to deploy given the known residual risk — is blameworthy for the decision to deploy under that uncertainty.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, ranked by severity rather than presented as equally bad, with the cheapest fix for each real conflict and a note on what each fix costs. I honestly can't tell anymore whether my position is coherent or whether I'm quietly helping myself to whatever principle gets me the verdict I want in each individual case — and if one commitment has to go to clean the rest up, I'd like to know which, though I warn you I'm attached to more of them than I should be.
