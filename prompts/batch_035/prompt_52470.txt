As a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, I've been trying to make sense of the causal exclusion debate in philosophy of mind, and I've hit a wall that looks less like a hard problem and more like a permanent standoff. Let me lay out what I see and then ask for a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

In my line of work, layered explanation is completely routine. A production incident gets explained at the level of the application logic, the virtual machine, the operating system's scheduler, and, in principle, the hardware. Each level's story is complete in the sense that the lower level fully determines what happens at the higher level, and yet we genuinely explain, predict, and intervene at the higher levels. Nobody in software thinks the statement-level explanation is causally idle because the machine-code story is causally sufficient; nobody thinks the two explanations are competing for a single token of causation, the way two bullets racing toward one victim would be. When I then read about Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument — that if a mental event is realized by a physical event, and the physical event is already causally sufficient for the bodily movement, then either the mental cause does nothing or we accept systematic overdetermination, so mental causation threatens to drain away into the physical — my working hypothesis is that philosophy rediscovered layered description and became frightened of its own abstraction.

But I know enough to suspect this is too easy, and the debate itself looks stuck in a way that supports my suspicion. It has run for roughly half a century: Kim pressing the exclusion worry, Davidson's anomalous monism being attacked from both sides, Yablo's proportionality response, the interventionist reframing by Woodward and by List and Menzies under which higher-level causes bear their own difference-making relations. From the outside, everyone restates their position and nobody converts.

So here is what I want from you: a diagnosis of the stall, not a survey of the positions. Specifically:

1. How much of the disagreement turns on a prior, tacit disagreement about what causation is — a productive, generative relation that only the most fundamental level can bear, versus a difference-making relation that multiple levels can each genuinely hold? If that is the crux, it looks like the two camps answer different questions under one word. But then I would expect the interventionist formulation, which explicitly defines causes in terms of what you can wiggle to change what, to have dissolved the whole thing. It didn't. Why not — do its critics regard it as changing the subject from causation to something weaker, and if so, is that objection substantive or a refusal to share the word?

2. How much of the dispute is really a proxy war over physicalism? It sometimes seems that each philosopher's position on mental causation follows fairly mechanically from a prior commitment about what the completeness of physics is supposed to guarantee, so that nobody's view about exclusion proper is ever at stake. Is that right, or is there genuine give-and-take on the exclusion argument itself?

3. Where does my software analogy break? In my case, the levels exist partly because human engineers chose convenient abstractions, and the choice of which level to explain at is pragmatic. If the philosophical debate is about whether the mental level is real in a stronger, mind-independent sense, my analogy may be smuggling in exactly the point at issue. Identify where the analogy illuminates the philosophical dispute, where it quietly begs the question, and whether there is any way to settle the stronger question that my engineering practice never needs to face.

Finally, tell me what would count as progress here: a decisive case, a formal result, a distinction that splits the disagreement, a change of question — or an argument that no progress should be expected, because the dispute rests on bedrock intuitions about levels of reality that no argument can reach. If you think it is the last of these, I want an honest assessment of whether an interminable debate of this kind is defective, or whether some questions in philosophy are legitimately like this.
