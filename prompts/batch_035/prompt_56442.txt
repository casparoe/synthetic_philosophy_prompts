I teach a second-year undergraduate seminar on twentieth-century analytic philosophy, and in two weeks I run a session on the analytic–synthetic distinction built around Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" and the Grice–Strawson reply "In Defense of a Dogma." Students will have read those two pieces but nothing later — no Boghossian, no two-dimensionalism, nothing on Kripke beyond a lecture handout on the necessary a posteriori. For the session handout, please write a decision tree that starts from the question "Is there a philosophically significant notion of a sentence that is true solely in virtue of its meaning, as opposed to partly in virtue of how the world is?" and routes an honest answerer to a position on the analytic–synthetic distinction.

The requirements are as follows:

1. Build the tree from the logical space, not from a roll call of names. First fix the branch questions and the combinations of answers they permit; then locate the actual positions within that space; then note any combination that no one, to your knowledge, occupies, with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether it is unoccupied because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

2. Every branch node must be a question a student could answer without knowing which position it leads to — no node that amounts to "do you accept Quine's holism?" or "are you sympathetic to Carnap?" Where possible, phrase the nodes around concrete cases rather than doctrines. In particular, the following cases should do diagnostic work somewhere in the tree: (i) whether "nothing can be red and green all over at once" strikes you as the same kind of truth as "all bachelors are unmarried," or as something else — and if something else, what; (ii) Quine's imagined investigator who deliberately legislates "rational animal" as the meaning of "man," and whether such stipulative synonymy shows that some notion of analyticity survives even if discovery-based synonymy is murky; (iii) Putnam's observation that "cats are animals" was traditionally counted analytic yet could have turned out false — whether this shows that dictionary-style synonymy is beside the point, or instead that the analytic is a far smaller class than the tradition assumed; (iv) whether Kripke's necessary a posteriori about "water is H2O" breaks the traditional package deal linking the analytic, the a priori, and the necessary, or merely relocates which side of the line such truths fall on; and (v) whether a sentence's being undisputed among all competent speakers is evidence that it is analytic, or merely evidence that agreement is cheap when nothing rides on it.

3. The following positions must appear as leaves: (a) Quinean eliminativism about the distinction, on which the failure to define analyticity non-circularly reflects the fact that there is no fact of the matter, only degrees of entrenchment in a holistically confirmed web; (b) the Grice–Strawson status-quo defense, on which the distinction is intelligible and serviceable even though no strict definition is on offer; (c) Carnapian conventionalism, on which analyticity is relative to a chosen linguistic framework — and the tree must actually separate this leaf from (b), so include at least one node that turns on whether the question "which sentences are analytic?" has an answer that is independent of anyone's adopting a framework; (d) an epistemic reconception of the analytic, on which a sentence counts as analytic if grasp of its meaning alone suffices for justified belief, treating the metaphysical question of truth "in virtue of meaning" as separable — the kind of view later writers such as Boghossian develop — separated from (b) and (c) by a node about whether the class of analytic truths is a fact about meanings or about our entitlement; and (e) a quietist leaf for the student who thinks the whole dispute is verbal and that the two sides are equivocating on "meaning."

4. Each leaf should carry, alongside the position's name, one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the bullet its defender has to bite if pressed at the next node up.

5. Close with a short section flagging which pairs of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate — e.g., if a Carnapian and a Grice–Strawson defender would answer every node identically — and state what further question, not on the tree, would be needed to pull them apart.

Keep the tree to between six and eight branch nodes and five to seven leaves, pitched at students two years into the subject. The point of the exercise is that students arrive at their leaf sorted by what they actually believe about the cases, not by which label they find congenial, so please check at the end that no node can be answered by reciting a slogan.
