I've been following the long-running dispute between higher-order theories of consciousness and their first-order rivals — Rosenthal-style higher-order thought theory on one side, Tye-style and Dretske-style first-order representationalism on the other — and it seems to me to have stalled in a way I want diagnosed rather than adjudicated. The shared facts are all on the table: everyone grants that there is a genuine difference between a conscious perception and an unconscious one, as in blindsight or subliminal priming; everyone grants that we can normally report on our experiences; and everyone accepts that experience has some representational basis. Yet the arguments have been circling for decades. The higher-order side holds that a first-order state cannot be conscious entirely on its own, since nothing internal to its first-order content distinguishes it from the unconscious representations we are simply not aware of; the first-order side replies that the higher-order relation is just further cognitive processing, and that positing an extra representation of a state is no more capable of generating phenomenality than any other token in the system — as in the familiar charge that the view would, absurdly, confer consciousness on a non-mental item that somehow triggered the right higher-order thought. Each camp treats the other's flagship complaint as question-begging, and I have not seen anyone move in years. My working hypothesis is that the dispute is partly verbal: that "conscious state" has quietly come to mean a state one is aware of being in for the higher-order camp, but a state with phenomenal character for the first-order camp, so that the transitivity principle reads as a definition to the one side and as a substantive empirical or conceptual claim to the other. But I am not confident this explains everything — in particular the disagreement over phenomenal overflow, where both sides appear to be answering the same question about how much detail we consciously see at a glance, and the disagreement over whether the higher-order relation does any genuine phenomenal work. So I want to know: is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one, or two different questions pursued under a single heading? Where does each side's case actually exert force on the other's position, and where does it merely address its own notion of consciousness? And given your diagnosis, what would count as real progress here — a stipulative division of labor between awareness and phenomenality, an empirical result settling something about overflow or about reportability, a new conceptual distinction, or nothing at all? Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
