I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on contemporary moral philosophy, meeting for 90 minutes with fourteen students. We have just finished reading John M. Taurek's 1977 paper "Should the Numbers Count?" (Philosophy & Public Affairs), in which he argues that when a rescuer can save either one stranger or five strangers, but not both, the mere fact that five would die rather than one gives the rescuer no reason of itself to favour the five — and he proposes, in the one-versus-five case, that a fair coin flip between the two options is what equal concern for each person would really require. My students, as I expected, treat the paper as a curiosity: they find it obvious that the numbers count, and they file Taurek away as a perverse outlier to be noted and moved past. What I want is for them to feel the pull of the position before they dismiss it — to discover from the inside why someone might think that no one is made worse off by the flip than by the rescue of the five, and what it costs to insist that the numbers count.

Design a staged role-play for that session with the following elements, and tell me honestly where the design strains:

Roles and setup. Six students play the six people at risk — five who can only be saved by one use of the remedy, one who can only be saved by the other use (call him David, as Taurek does) — and one student plays the rescuer holding a single dose. The remaining students serve as a panel of judges before whom the parties may plead. Give me the scripted opening scenario (I do not want it to mention the drug shortage as a matter of "policy" or invoke triage precedents, which the students will use as a crutch).

A pleading phase. Each of the six, in turn, gets a fixed two minutes to address the rescuer directly, and the rescuer is not permitted to reply. This is meant to stage Taurek's challenge: what could the rescuer look each of the five in the eye and say, if he saves the one, or look the one in the eye and say, if he saves the five? Tell me how to brief the pleaders so that the five do not simply chorus "we are more" — I want the appeals to be personal, not arithmetical, at least in this phase.

A pairwise phase. Before the rescuer decides, the panel must record, for each of the five individually paired against David, whether saving that individual rather than David would make anyone suffer a greater loss than the loss spared. This is meant to reproduce Taurek's argumentative core: that in each pairwise comparison the losses balance, and that the aggregate is never the loss of any person. Advise me on how to run this so it is a genuine deliberation rather than a show of hands.

The decision and the ballot. The rescuer decides, but before he announces anything, the whole class (participants and panel alike) records a secret ballot on two questions: first, whether they would personally prefer the rescuer to save the five rather than flip a coin; second, whether they think the rescuer who flips the coin thereby wrongs any of the five. I want the gap between those two answers, where it opens, to do the work of showing them that they may accept the flip as permissible while still preferring the save.

The debrief. Map what actually happened onto the paper's moves: the pairwise comparisons, the "what do you say to each of the five" challenge, the coin flip as an expression of equal concern rather than indifference. The debrief should distinguish, in front of them, the thesis that the numbers count from the distinct (and more obviously true) thesis that the five have a complaint if the rescuer flips and the coin lands against them — and it should leave open whether the students' ballot answers can be reconciled by locating the harm in the procedure rather than the outcome.

Finally, two honest notes. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I anticipate at least that the pleaders for the five will be unable to resist citing their number, and that the class will try to import policy considerations from the start; tell me what else to expect and how to handle each. Second, be frank about which feature of Taurek's position the activity cannot reproduce. In the paper, the rescuer knows David personally and the five not at all, and much of the argument's force comes from that asymmetry of relation, which my staged version deliberately flattens in order to isolate the numbers question; the activity also cannot reproduce the real stakes, and the students know they are playacting. Say plainly which of Taurek's resources I must therefore supply in the debrief by talking, since the game itself cannot deliver them.
