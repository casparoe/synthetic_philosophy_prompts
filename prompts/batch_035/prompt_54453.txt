I teach political theory at a small uni, and next week I've got a 2nd-year seminar of about 16 students on Burke's Reflections. The problem is they've already decided he's just a posh man defending the status quo, and honestly the discussion so far has been them congratulating themselves for not being him. I want a game that makes them actually feel the force of the Chesterton's fence point — that the reformer whoabolishes an institution before understanding its function is not bold but careless — in their bones, before I name it or connect it back to Burke in the debrief.

Here's what I've been imagining, and I'd like you to design it properly. The students sit as a modernisation committee for some fictional institution with a pile of legacy rules — I was toying with a 1920s railway workers' co-op, or a medieval guild, or the bylaws of a fictional Cambridge college, whatever you think works best, you pick. Most of the rules look obviously silly and outdated, and the committee is under time pressure to produce a lean new rulebook, with some scoring incentive tied to how much dead weight they cut. The catch is that a few of the rules have hidden rationales — think the ones that look like pointless ritual but are actually load-bearing — and when a committee scrapes one of those, the facilitator springs a "failure event" on them later in the session, which should cost them points. You'll need to invent maybe six or eight concrete rules with the load-bearing ones worked out (the rationale should be discoverable in principle if someone had bothered to interrogate the rule, not just arbitrary), plus the failure events, plus how I adjudicate them and how the committee can, if they're clever, spend effort on interviewing "veterans" to find out why a rule exists before cutting it. No real money — points, or re-election, or something. And the failure events need to be timed so they land after the cut, not immediately, so they feel the lag between action and consequence.

A few specific worries I want you to address. First, I can see the game accidentally teaching the wrong lesson: the students conclude you should never touch anything old, which is reflex veneration, not the fence principle — the actual principle is "go find out the reason first," which licenses removal once you understand it. How do the rules/incentives make discovering the reason, rather than preserving the rule, the winning move? Second, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how do I handle it? Third, the debrief: map the moments in the game onto the argument, but also be honest about what the game cannot reproduce — in real societies the rationale is often genuinely unrecoverable, so the Burkean is deciding under permanent ignorance, not playing a game where the answer exists somewhere if you interview the right NPC; and there's a whole side of Burke about inherited sentiment and prescriptive attachment that a committee game just can't simulate, so tell me where the analogy breaks and I need to supply that myself. Two to three paragraphs of setup is fine but give me the actual rules and materials, don't just sketch the concept. 80 minutes total, and I've got a whiteboard and a budget of basically zero.
