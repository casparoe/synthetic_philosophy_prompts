I've been going back and forth on the two-envelope thing for about a week now (it started as an argument with my brother over Thanksgiving — he took one stats course in college and now just says "it depends on the prior," which I find deeply unhelpful), and I've decided the way to settle it is to write down my gut verdicts on a bunch of variants and see if they add up to anything. Here they are. (1) Plain version: two envelopes, one has twice as much as the other, I don't open mine, I know nothing about how the amounts were picked. Gut says: obviously no reason to switch, it's symetric. (2) Same setup but I open mine and see $100. Gut says: the other envelope is equally likely to hold $200 or $50, so on average $125, switch. And this feels right whatever amount I would have seen — which bugs me, because if switching is good after opening no matter what I see, it looks like it should be good before opening too, but verdict (1) said don't bother. (3) I'm told the envelopes contain exactly $10 and $20. See $10 → switch, see $20 → stay, unopened → indifferent. Easy. (4) I'm told the pair is ($10,$20) 90% of the time and ($20,$40) 10% of the time. See $10 → switch. See $40 → stay. See $20 → I did the arithmatic: the other envelope is $10 with probability .9 and $40 with probability .1, average $13, less than $20, so stay — and I'm fine with that, even though $20 sitting "in the middle" is exactly what gave me the 50/50 itch in variant (2). (5) The host, who I trust completely, promises me: whatever amount I end up seeing, the other envelope is exactly as likely to have double as half. Gut says: then always switch, and even unopened I should switch. But then if I switch and ask myself the same question about the new envelope, the same promise applies, so I'd want to switch back, and back again forever — and switching twice is just keeping the original envelope. So that verdict can't be right either, or the promise can't mean what it sounds like it means.

So that's the pile. Is there one principle that generates all five verdicts, or as many as can be saved? My tentative guess is something like "switch iff the expected value of the other envelope, conditional on everything I actually know, beats what I'm holding" — but I can't tell whether that principle saves verdict (2) or kills it, and verdict (5) seems to require the host to have a conditional distribution that maybe no actual host could ever really have, given what (4) shows happening at the middle value.

If you can give me the principle, I'd like three more things. First, tell me plainly which of my verdicts has to go and why, in terms I can check myself with a calculator. Second, what the principle predicts in two cases I haven't mentioned: the one where I'm allowed to open, switch, look inside the new envelope, and switch a second time; and the one where the smaller amount was drawn from a distribution with no finite mean (St. Petersburg style), so "expected value" talk gets shaky at the foundations. Third, if the real story is that my 50/50 feeling in (2) is smuggling in an assumption, say exactly what the assumption is, and why variant (4) exposes the problem at $20 while variant (2) hides it at $100. My brother swears the whole thing dissolves into "which prior," and if he's right I'd at least like to lose the argument for the right reason.
