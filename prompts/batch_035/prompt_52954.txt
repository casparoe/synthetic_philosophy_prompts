So I've accidentally become a landlord. Inherited my aunt's duplex when she passed last fall, moved into one unit, and I'm renting out the other. And of course my city has rent control, which I'd never thought about before but now think about constantly.

Here's the setup, roughly: I can't raise the rent on my current tenant by more than a small capped percentage each year, and I basically can't evict her without cause, even when the lease term ends. But when she eventually moves out, I can list the unit at whatever the market will bear, and the new tenant then gets the same protection against *me* raising it later. Meanwhile my buddy who works at a hardware store just got bumped up because the state raised the minimum wage -- that's a floor rather than a ceiling, obviously, but the rhetoric around both is the same stuff about fairness and not letting people get squeezed.

The thing I keep chewing on: what is the actual theory of fair pricing these rules are operating with? Because it's clearly not "whatever a voluntary deal between two adults says." But it's also not the full medieval just-price thing, where there's one correct price for a thing and deviation from it is wrong. The rules seem weirdly selective in two ways and I want to know if the selectivity is principled or just political.

First, why does the *existing* tenant get protected and the *new applicant* doesn't? My tenant of ten years can't be charged market rate, but if she left tomorrow I could charge a newcomer that exact market rate without anyone blinking. It's like the policy thinks an ongoing relationship creates an entitlement that a new one doesn't -- which is odd, because most of the squeezing I read about happens to people desperate to get in the door, not people already housed. Is the implicit view something about dependence and lock-in, that once someone builds a life somewhere they've made an investment the landlord can hold hostage? Or is it just incumbent-heavy politics?

Second, why a ceiling for rent but a floor for wages? One says a landlord can't take more than X, the other says an employer can't pay less than Y. Both are moves away from the market-clearing number in favor of whoever's presumed weaker in the bargain. Do they actually presuppose the same underlying picture -- that the weaker party to an ongoing deal shouldn't have to accept terms set by the other side's leverage -- or are they doing something different, since a tenant is locking in a price from the past while a worker is being forced forward to a new one?

So my question: what theory of fairness (or of entitlement, or of exploitation, whatever the right vocabulary is) do rent control and minimum wage laws actually presuppose, taken together as a package? Which rival theories would license one but not the other, and which would license neither? And is there a theory under which the vacancy decontrol part -- protecting incumbents but not newcomers -- is actually principled rather than arbitrary, because right now it just looks like the people who already have apartments voted themselves a benefit.

I'd genuinely like an answer that tries to reconstruct the implicit theory these laws are working with, tells me where it's coherent and where it's just underdetermined, and says what the laws would have to look like if the underlying view were swapped for a different one. Please don't just give me the standard econ 101 efficiency lecture, I've read it, I'm asking about what view of fairness makes the rules make sense.
