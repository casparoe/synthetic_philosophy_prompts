I run the practical certification exams for the county's water-treatment operators — the oral exam where candidates walk through diagnosing faults on a simulated plant control panel, and passing it is what legally clears someone to run a treatment plant solo. So the stakes are real, but we're also under pressure from the state office to clear a backlog, which works out to about fifteen minutes per candidate and two examiners (me and Ray) who have to agree on a score before the candidate leaves the room, with candidates able to appeal anything we write down. Ray and I keep having the same arguement: the candidate gives the right answer, but the reasoning underneath is broken. Last week a candidate correctly said valve V-7 had to be locked out, but only because he misread the flow gauge in a way that happened to point at V-7 — any number of misreadings would have sent him to a different valve. Before that we had someone give the correct chlorine dose after a botched calculation where two mistakes cancelled each other out, and someone who 'knew' which pump was down because of a rumor about our plant that happened to be true. Ray took a philosophy course in college and keeps calling these 'Gettier cases' — the person arrives at a true answer, maybe even with decent justification behind it, but it's luck that it's true, and knowledge isn't supposed to be luck. He wants any whiff of that to be an automatic fail; I'm not so sure, since the printed study guide we hand out is the 'justification' and it's perfectly fine — the problem is what candidates build on top of it, or under it.

What I want from you is a decision procedure we can literally run during the exam and afterwards when we're arguing: the questions to ask in order about a candidate's answer, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops at pass or fail, and where it hands off to examiner judgment because the question is a value call the epistemology doesn't settle. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of knowledge actually says that's usable here — justified true belief, why the Gettier problem mattered in the first place, the 'no false premises in the reasoning' fix and its known problems, maybe the idea that knowledge shouldn't be accidental — but be upfront about which distinctions don't survive the trip from the seminar room to a fifteen-minute oral exam, rather than pretending they do. Then run the procedure on three cases: the misread-gauge guy, the cancelled-out calculation, and a candidate who correctly diagnoses a pump fault because she pattern-matched it to an identical fault she saw once during training, even though the underlying cause is different this time. At least one of those should come out in a way I won't like, and you should flag it as such. Also tell me plainly where the procedure will misfire — the kinds of cases it sorts wrong or can't sort at all.

Two more things. If you think the whole enterprise is confused — say, because exam grading isn't actually in the business of awarding 'knowledge' and we're tormenting ourselves with machinery that doesn't apply — I want to hear that directly instead of a polite rubric that papers over it. And if the procedure can't distinguish 'got lucky in a way that won't repeat' from 'got lucky in a way that will', say so, because that distinction is exactly what the plant manager cares about when he decides who gets solo shifts, and I'd rather know we're hand-waving than believe we're not.
