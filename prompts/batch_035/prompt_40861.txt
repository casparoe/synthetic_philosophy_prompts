Write a satirical internal memo, roughly 550–700 words, from the Chief Risk Officer of a mid-sized insurance company, announcing to all staff that the firm is transitioning from precise to imprecise probabilities. The voice should be that of a slightly self-satisfied executive convinced this is a straightforward compliance upgrade; the humor should come entirely from getting the philosophy and mathematics right. Concretely: premiums and reserves now quoted as intervals rather than point values, with actuarial tables replaced by sets of priors; a new claims-settlement procedure under which a claim is paid only if it is acceptable relative to every probability in the set, and denied only if unacceptable relative to every one, with a large and growing file of claims falling in neither category; a section on "dilation" spun as a feature, in which the acquisition of new data widens the firm's uncertainty and this is presented as value for shareholders who distrust overconfidence; a customer-complaints sidebar acknowledging that clients offered a choice between a known probability and an ambiguous one keep choosing the known one, described by the memo as a customer-education problem rather than evidence of anything; and a closing paragraph in which the memo quietly confronts the objection that decision theorists actually press — that a set of probabilities does not, by itself, tell you what to do — and resolves it by delegating the choice to a committee. Keep the irony deadpan and legible: no winking at the reader, no explicit editorializing, and no named living theorists — an invented sign-off (e.g., a director of "credal governance") is fine. A formal epistemologist should recognize that the author understands what imprecise probabilities are, what problems they were meant to solve, and where the hard unresolved questions sit, and should find the satire fair rather than a caricature.
