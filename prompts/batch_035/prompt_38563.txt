Here's the draft:

"Gödel's incompleteness theorems are the most abused results in the history of mathematics. In 1931, Kurt Gödel proved that any formal system capable of expressing arithmetic is incomplete: there will always be true statements it cannot prove. Worse, he showed that no consistent system can prove its own consistency. Taken together, these results seem to demolish the Enlightenment dream of a complete, self-certifying body of knowledge. Small wonder, then, that the theorems promptly escaped mathematics and started a second career everywhere else. Lawyers have claimed that no legal system can fully regulate itself, since the constitution cannot contain its own foundation. Economists have suggested that no model of a market that includes agents reasoning about the market can be complete. Theologians gleefully announced that Gödel had proven the limits of reason itself and thereby made room for faith.

The interesting question is which of these borrowings are legitimate and which are mere incense. The Lucas–Penrose line, which argues that because we can see the truth of the Gödel sentence that a machine cannot prove, the mind outruns any machine, at least engages with the actual mathematics — though it quietly assumes that our system of beliefs is consistent, which is a bold claim about creatures who disagree with themselves before breakfast. The legal analogy, by contrast, seems to me hopeless: a constitution is not effectively axiomatized, legal truth is not arithmetical truth, and a judge is not a proof procedure. Yet there is a serious point lurking in the vicinity: self-reference does generate trouble, as the liar paradox shows, and systems that talk about themselves are not just complicated but structurally limited.

My conclusion is that the right lesson of Gödel is not 'everything is incomplete' but something more modest and stranger: that within any sufficiently powerful consistent formal system, truth and provability come apart. That gap, not any particular application, is what the popularisers should have taken home."
