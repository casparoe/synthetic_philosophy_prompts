In the invention-of-tradition framework, the paradigm cases seem to be top-down fabrications: elites or states minting ceremonies, costumes, or symbols and passing them off as ancient, with the implicit accusation that this kind of forgery is a special legitimation trick. In Anderson's framework, by contrast, the imagining is universal and not really deceptive at all — every community larger than a village where people actually know each other is imagined in some way, and there's nothing fraudulent about that. So the word "invention" seems to be doing two different jobs: sometimes it means fabrication with a false claim to antiquity, and sometimes it just means constructedness, which on Anderson's view applies to everything and so can't be a criticism of anything. What I'd love to explore is whether the critical force of the invention thesis collapses once you make that distinction explicit. Consider a spectrum: a tradition that is fabricated but presented as ancient; a tradition that is brand new and openly acknowledged as new, like a newly independent state designing its flag and holidays in full public view; and an old practice that is silently repurposed for a new nationalist function. If everyone involved knows the tradition is new, does calling it "invented" still explain anything about how it creates loyalty?
