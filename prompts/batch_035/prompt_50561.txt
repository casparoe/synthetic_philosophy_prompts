I'm in a weekend reading group — four of us, none of us academics, we just take one old book at a time and argue about it over coffee. We've just worked through the part of Book V of Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics where he asks when a trade between, say, a builder and a shoemaker counts as just, and what money has to do with it. Since then, one member, Dara, has appointed herself our judge of trades, and her rulings so far are these:

1. A builder trades part of a house for a year's worth of shoes, with the quantities fixed by comparing what each side's goods would fetch in the market: fair.
2. A doctor swaps one hour of appointments for a month of a baker's bread, straight across at the going rates, even though the baker works far longer hours: fair.
3. One neighbor trades the piano she never plays for another's record collection, which he never listens to; the money values are far apart, but each has wanted exactly what the other has for years: fair.
4. A grain dealer honors a fixed harvest price when selling to a miller in the spring, when everyone else is charging much more: fair.

Dara insists she's simply applying what we read: money makes the builder's and the shoemaker's goods commensurable, so a trade is just when the sides are somehow made equal. But I can't reconstruct a single rule from her record. Ruling 2 seems to throw out any idea that a fair trade rewards effort in proportion; ruling 3 throws out plain equality of market value; and I can't tell whether ruling 4 turns on need, on keeping one's word, or on stable prices for standing partners.

Next month she'll rule on this: after a flood, the only generator dealer left open in town sells a family a generator for ten times what he paid for it a month before — more than anyone else in the region is asking. The family's food is spoiling, their daughter needs a powered breathing machine at night, they pay without haggling, say afterward they'd gladly have paid double, and the dealer doesn't need their business, since he has a line of other desperate buyers.

Please structure your answer as follows. Under "The rules that fit her," name the two or three rules that fit all four of her rulings, a few sentences each. Under "What they predict," give one short paragraph per rule saying what it implies about the generator sale. Under "My call," commit to a prediction of her verdict with your reasons, and say whether the rule you favor is really Aristotle's or her own invention. Under "The trade that would settle it," describe one further trade where your candidate rules come apart, and say what each would rule on it.
