I've been working on an argument against epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness, and I can no longer tell whether it is a genuine refutation or a trick I've played on myself. By epiphenomenalism I mean the view that phenomenal properties — what it is like to undergo an experience — are not physical, that they are caused by physical processes in the brain, but that they exert no causal influence on anything physical in return: experiences ride along like the shadow of a moving train.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) If epiphenomenalism is true, then for any physical event, the total physical causes of that event suffice to bring it about, and phenomenal properties contribute nothing to it.
(2) My sincerely uttering "that afterimage looks orange" is a physical event, caused entirely by prior physical states of my brain.
(3) So if epiphenomenalism is true, my experiencing orange contributed nothing to that utterance.
(4) The same holds for every sincere report, avowal, or expression of judgement I ever make about my own experiences.
(5) A report that is causally insulated from the fact it reports — one that would occur just the same whether or not that fact obtained — is no better than a lucky guess, and beliefs formed on its basis are unjustified.
(6) So if epiphenomenalism is true, none of my beliefs about my own experiences are justified.
(7) But the entire case for there being phenomenal properties at all — and for the causal closure premise that got the argument started — rests on just such beliefs.
(8) So if epiphenomenalism is true, the epiphenomenalist has no justification for believing epiphenomenalism, or for believing anything else about consciousness.
(9) A view whose truth would strip its own adherents of any justification for holding it cannot be coherently accepted.
(10) Therefore epiphenomenalism is false.

I am told there is a stock reply here, turning on the observation that the word "judgement" is doing double duty — that the judgement I express and report is one thing, and the judgement that somehow registers or contains the experience is another, and that once the two are prised apart the argument loses its grip. When I try to spell this out, though, it seems to leave the epiphenomenalist relying on nothing more than a pre-established harmony between what I think I experience and what I experience, which strikes me as no improvement on the original problem. So perhaps the reply fails and the argument really does go through; perhaps the damage is already done in an earlier step; perhaps (9) is far weaker than it sounds, since plenty of views imply we know less than we thought we did.

Please write, in British English, a response that identifies exactly which step or steps to reject, explains why the offending step looked innocent when I laid the argument out, and sets out honestly what each available rejection costs. In pricing the alternatives, please distinguish debts that are simply the standing price of epiphenomenalism, which any dualist about consciousness must pay anyway, from any additional debts that this particular argument forces on the view.
