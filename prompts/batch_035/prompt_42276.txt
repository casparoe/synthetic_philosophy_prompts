my school has a philosophy club and last week a college student who's taking epistemology came to talk about "the bootstrapping problem." I wrote some of it down and I want to know if he explained it fairly, because some of it sounded too strong to me. Here's what I've got from my notes:

He said the problem is about "easy knowledge" — knowledge you get way too cheaply. The setup: suppose you have a thermometer that reads 72 degrees. You believe it's 72. If the thermometer is reliable, then a reading of 72 makes it probable that it really is 72, so now you know it's 72. But you can repeat this: each new reading gives you another bit of data about what the thermometer said, and you can check the readings against each other and eventually conclude from the whole run of readings that the thermometer is reliable. So you've used the thermometer itself to prove that the thermometer is reliable, which he called circular, and he said this shows you never really knew it was 72 in the first place. He then said this is a knockdown refutation of reliabilism (the view that a belief counts as knowledge when it comes from a reliable source), because reliabilism has to let this bootstrapping reasoning through — each step looks fine by reliabilist lights, so the reliabilist can't complain about any single one. And he said the exact same thing goes for your own senses: you can never know your vision is reliable, so you can never know anything you see. 

That last bit seems crazy to me, like don't I know there's a water bottle on my desk right now? But he's the one who's taken college classes and I'm a junior in high school, so maybe I'm just missing something. Can you go through his explanation piece by piece and tell me what an epistemologist would accept, what's a subtle exaggeration or conflation, and whether his big conclusion — that we can't know anything we perceive — actually follows from the parts of his explanation that are correct? Also if there are standard ways people defend reliabilism against this, I'd like to hear what they are so I can push back at the next meeting.
