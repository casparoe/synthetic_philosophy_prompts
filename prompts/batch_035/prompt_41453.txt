You are an experienced philosophy interviewer with a strong background in ethics, well versed in the literature on rescue cases and aggregation. I host a small podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends a Taurek-style position: in a forced choice between saving one stranger and saving a larger group of strangers, he holds that the greater number in the group gives him no reason, as such, to prefer saving the group. Before drafting anything, think carefully about the actual content of his position and the standard objections to it in the literature — brainstorm briefly for yourself first, then produce the final list.

Draft nine or ten interview questions that go well beyond having him restate the view. The questions should press where the literature presses, and each should come with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. In particular, I want at least one question on each of the following:

1. His signature device for the one-versus-many case: giving each person an equal chance by flipping a coin. Press him on whether this is a principled solution or an evasion — after all, if the numbers gave no reason either way, he could just as well save the one by whim, so why the fuss about equal chances? His likely reply is that the coin expresses equal concern for each person as an individual; the follow-up should ask whether a procedure can embody a concern that no outcome-relevant fact grounds.

2. The objection, familiar from contractualist and deontological critics, that while each person in the group has a claim against him, claims of the same strength can be combined or aggregated on one side, so that the side with more claim-bearers has the stronger combined claim. Ask him to say precisely which step in that reasoning he rejects. His likely reply distinguishes the claims of persons from sums of claims that no one holds; the follow-up should test whether that distinction holds when the harm to each individual is enormous — say, each of the many faces a grave loss — rather than modest.

3. A sequential version of the case: instead of a one-off choice, he can rescue one person now, and will face the same choice again tomorrow, with no single choice ever presenting him with five versus one. Ask whether his view permits, across the week, letting many more die than a view on which numbers count would have required saving. His likely reply is that each pairwise choice was permissibly made; the follow-up should ask whether he accepts that no fact about the sequence as a whole — only facts about each choice taken alone — ever matters, and whether that seems believable to him when he reflects on it.

4. The charge that his view, extended to disasters, licenses standing by while enormous numbers perish so long as each perishes in a case where saving them would have meant overriding a smaller but still valid claim. The follow-up should push on where, if anywhere, he thinks the extension breaks down, and what feature of the cases does the work.

Avoid generic questions about biography or motivation. Keep the tone serious and collegial — skeptical but never gotcha-ish — and phrase the questions so they can be asked aloud verbatim.
