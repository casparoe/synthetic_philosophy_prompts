I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on nineteenth-century German philosophy, and I'm writing my term paper on Nietzsche's 1874 essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life. My coursemate Lena, who did her entire bachelor's thesis on the early Nietzsche and quotes the essay the way other people quote films, has very firm opinions about the past and about what may legitimately be done with it. Before I try out on her the examples I want to build my paper around, I'd like to predict what she will say, because her rulings on earlier cases seem consistent enough that I suspect she is running a rule — possibly more than one — and identifying it would actually help my argument.

Her rulings so far, in brief. A motivational podcast narrating the lives of great scientists as an unbroken chain of triumphs in order to spur young researchers: allowed, but only as fuel for the ambitious — she says it turns poisonous the moment anyone mistakes it for a picture of how science actually advances, which she calls the good and the bad face of one and the same kind of history. A local heritage society that lovingly restores its town's old houses and archives the parish registers, with no aim beyond the preservation itself: approved, as piety toward what is small and near, though she warns that it curdles if the members come to believe that nothing outside their own patch matters. A truth commission that puts a nation's past before a tribunal and condemns it so that a fresh start becomes possible: approved, but only conditionally — the judges may condemn the past, she says, if they can suppose that a later generation will put them on trial in their turn and not spare them either. A school curriculum that fills children with dates and dynasties on the ground that knowing how the present came about is valuable in itself: rejected, as knowledge that issues in no action, an education that produces spectators. A memoirist who excavates every humiliation of his childhood in order to explain why he turned out as he did: rejected — he treats his own character as an effect of his past, she says, where a person who acted would treat it as material, and this is history in the service of self-exculpation. And a person who has simply forgotten an old injury and gets on with things: approved — the capacity to forget, she says, is as much a condition of action as memory is.

Now for the three new cases. A founder of a young company who keeps a photograph of the firm's early heroes above his desk and retells their story at every all-hands as inspiration, although everyone present knows the polished version is not how those years really went. A woman who spends her weekends tracing her great-grandparents' emigration through shipping registers and parish books and reconstructing the family recipes, who says the tracing makes her feel rooted, though it issues in no project beyond itself. And a protest movement that pulls down the statue of a former ruler as part of demanding a different future, where some of the participants, honestly, mainly enjoy the condemning. I care less about the verdicts themselves than about the rule she seems to be running: I can see at least two candidates that each fit all six of her earlier rulings, one that sorts uses of the past by the threefold distinction between monumental, antiquarian, and critical history that she is always invoking, and one that asks only whether a given use of the past enlarges someone's capacity to act in the present. My suspicion is that these rules come apart precisely on the woman with the shipping registers, since she looks at once like the heritage society and like the curriculum, but I may be missing better candidate rules or a cleaner dividing case. So please give your best guess, with reasons, for each of the three verdicts; say which candidate rule you would back and why, including how it accommodates her approval of forgetting; and propose one further case, not among these, whose verdict from Lena would settle which rule she is actually running. Please answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one writes an email to a fellow student.
