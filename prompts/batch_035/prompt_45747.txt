I'm grading a take-home exam for a graduate intro course on non-classical logic and could use a second pair of eyes on one answer. The question I asked was:

"Explain the relationship between dialetheism and paraconsistent logic. Does one imply the other? Then briefly explain how Priest's Logic of Paradox (LP) handles the Liar sentence, and describe one major objection to that treatment."

Here's the student's answer:

"Dialetheism is the view that some contradictions are true, i.e. that there are sentences that are true while their negations are also true. Paraconsistency is a property of logics rather than a philosophical thesis: a logic is paraconsistent when contradictions do not trivialize a theory, meaning you cannot infer just any conclusion whatsoever from an inconsistent set of premises. In classical logic, the principle of explosion says that from A and not-A you can derive any B, so a single contradiction makes everything provable and the theory becomes useless. Since paraconsistent logicians refuse to explode contradictions, they are in effect commited to dialetheism: declining to explode a contradiction amounts to holding that some contradictions are true. In the other direction, the dialetheist does need a paraconsistent logic, because in classical logic her single true contradiction would entail everything and she'd collapse into trivialism. So the two views really stand or fall together.

Priest's Logic of Paradox assigns sentences three values: true, false, and both true and false. When it comes to the Liar sentence, LP evaluates it as neither true nor false — the third value works as a truth-value gap, so the paradox never gets off the ground. A nice feature of this treatment is that it dispatches the semantic paradoxes once and for all: since the Liar just gets the gap value, there is no revenge problem, and even strengthened liars like 'this sentence is not true' are handled the same way."
