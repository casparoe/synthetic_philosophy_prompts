Please argue that the widely circulated inference — that the discovery of extraterrestrial life would be bad news for humanity, because it would push the Great Filter into our future — establishes far less than its proponents claim. In developing the argument, attend to three assumptions that are usually run together: (i) that the discovered life would represent an independent origin rather than the product of panspermia, shared ancestry, or contamination, and so would count as a second trial of the same probabilistic experiment; (ii) that abiogenesis is the parameter whose posterior matters most, when the filter could lie in any later evolutionary or technological step, so that what we actually need to update on is the whole distribution of step difficulties and their correlations; and (iii) that a single additional biosphere is highly diagnostic, when the evidential weight of one more data point depends on how many effectively independent trials the galaxy has already run. Argue that, once these assumptions are separated, finding microbial life on Mars would justify at most a modest shift of credence toward a future filter, not the pessimism the standard inference is taken to support. Please also state which consideration would most plausibly reverse your verdict — for instance, whether the discovery of a genuinely independent biochemistry would change the calculus — and identify where in the probabilistic reasoning your argument is most likely to fail.
