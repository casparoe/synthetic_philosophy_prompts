Next term I am teaching an undergraduate seminar titled "The End of Societies," and for the closing session I want a handout that sorts students by what they actually believe about collapse — not by which book they happened to enjoy. Please write a taxonomy of the positions on whether societies collapse or merely change, organized as a decision tree.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a canon of authors. The first splits should concern what exactly is supposed to end when a society "collapses": the number of tiers of political decision-making; the size of the population; the stock of practical knowledge, in the sense of the capacity to rebuild what was lost; or a way of life that the people themselves valued. Later splits should cover the standard of comparison (is the fall measured against the society's own peak, against its contemporaries, or against bare subsistence?), the timescale (must the fall be fast enough that a single generation lives through it?), and whether fragmentation into smaller, self-governing units counts as failure or as successful adaptation.

Every branch question must be answerable from a concrete case, without knowing any position's label. Use the Maya of the southern lowlands after about 800 CE, the eastern Mediterranean in the decades around 1200 BCE, and Easter Island after the loss of its palm forest as your recurring test cases. A question like "do you accept the standard account of Easter Island?" is therefore out of bounds; a question of the right shape would be something like "when the ruling class vanishes, the population falls, and yet the farmers keep farming and the language keeps being spoken, has a society ended or merely gotten poorer?" — but please devise better cases and phrasings than these illustrations, and do not reuse my wording.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position where one exists — the catastrophist view on which collapse is mass death and the loss of basic security; the simplification view on which it is an economically rational shedding of complexity; the transformation view on which what looks like an ending is reorganization, with the people persisting; the deflationary view on which "collapse" is a partisan word for change the speaker disapproves of — and "unnamed" where no one, to your knowledge, occupies the combination. Attach an author's name to a leaf only where that attribution is standard; a coinage is acceptable where the position is real but has never been labeled. Add one sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment.

Close with a short coda that does two things: identify the combinations of answers that no one holds, diagnosing each vacancy as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and flag any pair of positions your tree cannot separate, presenting this as a limitation of the instrument rather than something to be papered over.

Constraints: the audience is third-year students with no background in archaeology or ancient history; limit the tree to five or six branch questions and at most ten leaves, the "unnamed" ones included.
