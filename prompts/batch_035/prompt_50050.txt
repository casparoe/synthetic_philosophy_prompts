the exam question was:

"Judy Benjamin, a soldier on a training exercise, is dropped into a swampy war-games area and gets hopelessly lost. The area is divided into a Blue Army region and a Red Army region, and each region is divided into a Headquarters company area and a Second Company area; the four areas are of equal size, and Judy, having no other information, initially gives probability 1/4 to being in each of them. She gets radio contact with HQ, and is told: 'I can't say where you are, but if you are in Blue Army territory, the odds are 3 to 1 that you are in its Headquarters company area rather than its Second company area.' She receives no other information.

(a) This case (a cousin of an example due to Bas van Fraassen from the early 1980s) is often taken to show that not all rational belief changes can be modelled if one's update rules are restricted to conditionalization and Jeffrey conditioning. Explain why.
(b) What goes wrong if Judy instead treats the message as the material conditional and conditionalizes on that?
(c) What probability should Judy now assign to being in Red Army territory? Defend your answer, and say where the philosophical work is being done."

and here's the student's answer:

"The Judy Benjamin problem was introduced by Richard Jeffrey as a test case for his own updating rule. Judy learns a conditional — that if she's in Blue territory, the odds on Blue HQ over Blue Second are 3:1 — and the question is how to update on it. Plain conditionalization doesn't apply because it requires learning some proposition with certainty, and Judy hasn't learned any proposition of the form 'I am in area X'; she has only learned a conditional fact.

The obvious move is to conditionalize on the material conditional ('either I'm not in Blue territory or I'm in Blue HQ'), but that can't be right: it would drive the probability of Blue Second to zero, and the message gave odds of 3:1, not certainty, so Blue Second should retain some probability.

The correct update keeps her probability for Red territory fixed at 1/2, since the message was entirely about the Blue region, and redistributes the Blue half in the ratio 3:1: P(Blue HQ) = 3/8, P(Blue Second) = 1/8, P(Red) = 1/2. This is exactly Jeffrey conditioning on the partition {Red territory, Blue territory}: the new information leaves the cell probabilities alone and only changes the distribution within a cell, which is precisely what Jeffrey's rule is for.

Note also that Judy cannot use Adams's thesis — that the probability of a conditional equals the probability of its antecedent given its consequent — to convert what she learned into a proposition, because what she learned isn't the sort of thing that has a probability in that way.

So the answer to (c) is P(Red) = 1/2, and we can be fully confident in this. The message says nothing at all about Red territory, so no rational agent could revise their probability for Red on receiving it. This shows Jeffrey's rule handles conditionals perfectly well: once you see that the answer is 1/2, there is no residual problem for Bayesianism, and anyone who appeals to minimum-information or entropy-style principles to get a different number for P(Red) is simply misdescribing the evidence Judy received."
