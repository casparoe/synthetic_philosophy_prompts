I'm taking a grad seminar on Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and I'm writing my term paper on what my professor called (a bit dismissively, tbh) "a corner of the literature everyone thinks is handled but nobody has actually handled": hinge change. The hinge epistemology program is great at explaining why hinges can't be doubted — they're the condition of the possibility of doubt, they're exempt from epistemic evaluation, and so on — but it's weirdly thin on how hinges ever go away or get replaced. So I've cooked up a concept for the paper and I want a sanity check before I commit a month to it. The concept is what I'm calling hinge drift.

The idea: hinges don't get doubted out of existence, they just gradually stop being invoked, and one day they're gone. Like, imagine a community where the standing hinge is that disputes get settled by the testimony of the elders — nobody believes this, nobody doubts it, it's just the unquestioned frame within which asking and answering happens, the way the earth having existed for many years functions for us. Over a few generations, with written records becoming central, the old hinge just stops getting used: no one ever says the elders know best anymore, not because anyone challenged it, but because situations that would call for it stop arising. And then it's not even available — someone invoking it sounds like a pedant or a mystic, not like someone making an ordinary claim. My thought is this can't be modeled as belief revision (nothing was ever believed, so nothing was revised), and it can't be modeled as doubt (no one doubted anything), so either hinge epistemology needs a notion of hinge replacement that no one has spelled out, or the picture needs more structure than just hinges vs. hingeable — maybe the riverbed metaphor in On Certainty has to do more work than people usually give it, with the shift being slow and imperceptible in the way Wittgenstein describes the riverbed of thought as sometimes shifting.

What I want from you: comments on the idea, not on my writing style (the notes above are rough on purpose). Specifically — (1) does the drift case really resist treatment as ordinary gradual belief change, or am I just stipulating that nobody believed the hinge, when a critic will say the community plainly did believe it and then stopped? (2) Is the distinction between the hinge stopping being invoked and the hinge being *replaced* doing real work, or wether that's just a relabeling of the problem? (3) Does this ring a bell as already well-trodden — not asking for a reading list, just whether the move sounds familiar, because if so I need a different angle. And (4) honestly: is this interesting enough to carry a term paper, or is it two paragraphs of observation stretched thin? Push back as hard as you like, my professor will be harsher.
