I produce long-range geopolitical forecasts for a policy institute, and I've been asked to publish a methods statement laying out my working assumptions. Before it goes out, I want an audit of whether the package hangs together. Here is what I've converged on:

1. Outside-view priority. For questions about the durability of large political entities — whether a hegemonic order holds for another fifty years, whether a currency union survives a major shock, whether a dominant power declines gracefully or violently — the starting point and the main input should be base rates: how often entities in a suitable reference class have done the thing in question. Reasoning from the particular entity's institutions, leaders, and mechanisms is secondary and should move the estimate only modestly.
2. A causal-similarity criterion for class membership. A reference class is well chosen when its members share the causal structure that generates the outcome of interest — how the entity extracts resources, secures loyalty, and manages succession — not surface features such as sharing a label ("empire," "federation," "great power") or an era. A class of agrarian tributary empires is the wrong class for a question about a nuclear-armed, financially integrated hegemon, however many members it offers.
3. Humility about small classes. When a causally well-chosen class contains few members — as classes of hegemons and great-power systems do — I treat the resulting frequency as weak evidence, not as a probability to be held with confidence; wide uncertainty beats false precision.
4. Anti-precisionism about the far future. There are, in my view, no credible point probabilities for geopolitical events more than a century out; forecasts at that range should be qualitative, conditional, and framed as scenarios.
5. A horizon gradient. The shorter the horizon — a decade rather than a century — the more weight I give to inside-view evidence about the specific entity, since mechanisms become observable and base rates converge on current conditions.

Please go through these commitments pairwise and tell me which are genuinely in tension and how badly, ranking the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give, and what giving it up costs me methodologically. Flag any pair that only looks inconsistent but dissolves on inspection — I would rather not revise something that is not actually broken. Conclude by telling me which single revision would clean up the most. Answer in American English.
