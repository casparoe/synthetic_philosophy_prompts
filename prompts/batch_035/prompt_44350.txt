I'm writing the final for my upper-level seminar on formal epistemology, and I want a single essay question on the Sleeping Beauty problem — the version where Beauty is awakened once on heads and twice on tails, with amnesia in between. The problem is that students can more or less pass a standard question on this by reciting the halfer and thirder positions and muttering something about centered worlds, so I need a question that forces them past the labels. The question should be built around a variant that I'll describe here and that your question should introduce self-contained (our exam is open-book but the students won't have the seminar notes in front of them, so everything must be stated in the question): on heads Beauty is interviewed once on Monday; on tails she is interviewed 99 times, once per day, with her memory wiped between interviews. Students must say whether the thirder's reasoning, if accepted in the standard case, commits her to a credence of 1/100 in heads in the variant, whether the halfer can hold the line at 1/2 without an ad hoc stopping point, and what each answer implies about credence in the self-locating proposition "today is my first interview." A student who has merely memorized the two camps should not be able to fake their way through this; the crux I want tested is whether they can articulate what the disagreement is actually about — whether self-locating uncertainty is genuine uncertainty that Bayesian tools apply to, or a bookkeeping matter — and whether they can see that the temptation to say "1/100, obviously" in the variant needs an argument, not an intuition.

The question should also require them to address a trap I like to spring: an interviewer offers Beauty a bet that pays $1 on heads, to be settled once, at the end of the experiment, no matter how many interviews occur. Students often run betting arguments that silently switch between bets settled per-interview and bets settled per-experiment; the question should make them say explicitly which kind of bet supports which conclusion, and whether either kind settles the credence question or merely the pricing question. Candidates have 90 minutes, and I'd rather they argue than pad.

Please give me: the exam question itself (self-contained, no citations of the literature by name, phrased so the variant does the work), a model answer of roughly 600–700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 points total built around five must-hit moves, with a one-line note after each on how students typically fumble it. In the marking scheme, please also state in a sentence or two what separates an A answer from a B answer — that is, the specific thing a solid-but-not-stellar answer will be missing. Please write everything in American English, and keep the tone of the exam question formal but readable.
