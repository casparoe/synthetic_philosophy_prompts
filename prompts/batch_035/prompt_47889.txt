I teach philosophy and sit on my university's academic integrity committee, and I've become puzzled by where our policy draws the line on writing tools. Spell-checkers and grammar-checkers are permitted everywhere; machine translation is permitted in some departments for non-native speakers; and since last year, generative AI is banned outright for graded essays, even in draft form. The implicit distinction seems to be between tools that polish what the student has already written and tools that produce the writing itself — but our grammar software now makes sentence-level suggestions generated by the same kind of model as the banned chatbots, so the line is not even technically clean. My question: what theory of the relationship between writing and understanding does this patchwork of rules presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view — I take it to be something like: producing good prose requires comprehension, so graded writing is evidence of understanding, and outsourcing the production outsources the evidence. Then ask whether that view survives the two strange inversions of reasoning that Daniel Dennett attributed to Darwin and Turing, on which competence can arise without comprehension: if a process can generate competent output with no mind behind it, the inference from polished writing to understanding loses its guarantee, and the inference from poor writing to poor understanding was never any good either. Say whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined — perhaps several theories of authorship would license the same rules — and tell me what assessment would have to look like if the committee took the inversion seriously instead. Would we move to oral examinations, portfolio review of drafts, or something else, and what would that presuppose in turn? Please answer in American English.
