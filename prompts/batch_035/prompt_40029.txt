My roommate has an argument about competition that I can't beat but also can't believe. (1) In any competition, whoever bears a cost their rivals don't bear loses, and the losers eventually disappear. (2) Markets are competitions, so firms end up spending exactly what the rules force them to and not a cent more — a firm that volunteers extra safety or cleaner production just gets outbid. (3) But the rules are made by things that also compete: jurisdictions compete for factories, regulators compete for careers, parties compete for donors and votes. (4) In each of those competitions the same principle applies, so a rule that's genuinely costly to whoever has the most pull gets eroded, bargained away, or never written in the first place. (5) Therefore any standard that has survived for decades — weekends, lead-free paint, deposit insurance — can only have survived because it wasn't actually costly to the people with the most pull; anything that really cost them would be gone by now. (6) So there's no such thing as a durable win against a race to the bottom, and every campaign for one is just taking credit for rules that were going to happen anyway. He uses this to argue my union organizing is pointless, which is what got my back up. Where does the argument slip? I can't tell wether the problem is in (3), (4), or (5), and I'd like to know what it costs to reject whichever one it is. And if the conclusion is false, which level is it false at — the firm level, the rule level, or the level where rules get made?
