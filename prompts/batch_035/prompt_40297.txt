I'd like to practice pressing the higher-order vagueness objection against supervaluationism about vagueness. Please play a committed supervaluationist — philosophically sharp, not a caricature, willing to concede small points but never the core claim that vagueness consists in semantic indecision among admissible precisifications rather than in ignorance of a sharp boundary. Stay in character until I say 'stop', and keep each reply to about a paragraph so it feels like a live exchange. When one of your points rests on a genuinely controversial assumption, flag this in character — a serious philosopher knows where the argument is thinnest. My opening move: your view says that a sentence like 'a man of 1.78m is tall' is supertrue because it comes out true on every admissible precisification of 'tall', each of which draws a sharp line somewhere. But what makes a precisification admissible? If the admissible range has sharp boundaries, then 'admissible' is itself a precise predicate, and you have merely relocated the epistemicist's unknowable boundary from the object language into the metalanguage — the arbitrary line is still there, one level up, just unacknowledged. If instead admissibility is itself vague, then supertruth is not always determinately defined, and the notion you use to explain vagueness exhibits the very indeterminacy it was introduced to remove. Which horn do you take, and what does it cost you?
