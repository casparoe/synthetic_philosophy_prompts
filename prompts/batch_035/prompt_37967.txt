For the last two years I have co-organized a graduate seminar on distributive justice whose recurring centerpiece is the priority-versus-equality debate: Parfit's distinction between telic egalitarianism and prioritarianism, the leveling-down objection that motivated it, and the literature each has generated. My honest assessment is that the debate has stalled completely. Prioritarians keep insisting that a world in which a natural disaster blinds everyone is not better in any respect than one in which it blinds only some; egalitarians keep replying, in the spirit of Temkin, that something can be bad without being bad for anyone; and the relational-egalitarian wing associated with Anderson charges both camps with having misconstrued what egalitarianism was ever about in the first place. Meanwhile, newer complications keep arriving without anyone moving: the person-affecting worry about whose situation prioritarian gains are supposed to improve, hybrid "claims" views on which the worse off have a stronger claim to a fixed benefit rather than that same benefit simply counting for more, the apparent arbitrariness of the prioritarian weighting function (why steeper rather than flatter?), and McKerlie's complaint that both camps evaluate whole lives atemporally while the actual policies we debate distribute goods to people at times.

I no longer want another round of the arguments themselves. I want a diagnosis of the standoff. So, some questions to structure it: Is the disagreement substantive, or has it partly collapsed into a verbal dispute about what "priority" and "equality" may be used to mean — especially once relational egalitarians concede that distributive equality is not the point? Is the real crux a hidden question about the separateness of persons, namely whether anything about a distribution can be good or bad over and above its effects on individuals, with the leveling-down objection merely the most vivid way of forcing that question? Or is it instead a clash of fixed case intuitions — rescue cases pulling toward priority, leveling-down cases pulling away from equality — with no shared standard capable of adjudicating between them, so that each camp simply rations its attention to the intuitions that flatter it? A further wrinkle I would like addressed: the debate is almost always run one-shot, as a single allocation among fixed lives. Does anything change in an iterated setting, where parties can anticipate being on either side of the distribution and the views begin to converge with an insurance framing — and if so, does that reveal that the stalled disagreement was only ever about idealization, not principle? My own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: that the priority-versus-equality debate is a proxy war over whether distributive ethics must be purely aggregative, and that it will stall for exactly as long as that deeper question does. I would like this tested rather than assumed.

Please structure your response as follows:

1. "Diagnosis" (roughly 600 words): what kind of disagreement this actually is, defended against the competing diagnoses.
2. "Why the stalemate is stable" (roughly 400 words): what feature of the positions, the cases, or the standards of argument has kept the sides from moving for three decades.
3. "What progress would look like" (roughly 500 words): what would count as settling this — a distinction, a formal result, a new class of cases, a change of question — and, if nothing would, an honest argument for that conclusion.
4. "Where I would steer the seminar next" (roughly 200 words): given your diagnosis, which one or two of the complicating threads above are actually load-bearing and worth a further session, and which are decorations on the stalemate.
