My younger brother was diagnosed as autistic four months ago, at age twenty-seven, after self-identifying for years via online autistic communities and only then seeking formal assessment. My father thinks the whole thing is circular: the diagnosis describes a person shaped by the diagnosis, so what could it possibly describe? I've been reading Ian Hacking's work on "making up people" and looping effects to sort out whether my father has a point, and I've discovered that my own reactions to cases are a mess I can't see the pattern in. I'm hoping someone can extract the principle for me, because I have to write something coherent about this for a graduate seminar paper, and right now I'd embarrass myself.

Here are my verdicts. Hacking's nineteenth-century fugueurs, mad travelers who wandered compulsively and whose numbers rose as doctors classified them: here I accept the strong looping story — the classification genuinely helped create a way of being mad. The North American multiple-personality epidemic of the 1980s: also a case where looping discredited the category, since the symptoms spread and elaborated as patients, therapists, and media circulated descriptions of them. But autism, where looping is if anything even more intense — self-identification, community norms, an entire identity movement, clinicians now trained by descriptions partly shaped by autistic self-advocates — here I think the category is fine, real, valid, and my father is wrong. Tourette-like tics spreading among teenagers through social media: symptoms that are literally socially transmitted, and yet I don't conclude that Tourette syndrome isn't real. Migraine and epilepsy, by contrast, seem to me "indifferent" classifications — nobody's identity reorganizes around them — but I also feel this indifference isn't doing much work in my other verdicts. Anorexia in the era of online communities that trade tips: somewhere in the middle, and I can't say where. The nearest thing to a principle I've managed is this: looping is corrosive only when learning about the category is itself a cause of meeting its criteria — when the classification supplies the symptoms rather than merely selecting for them — and is benign when it only reshapes presentation or prevalence. But this seems to imply that a teenager who develops tics after watching tic videos has a condition that isn't real, which I refuse to accept, and it's unclear whether my brother, whose self-identification preceded and motivated his diagnosis, comes out valid or manufactured under it.

So: is there a principle, mine or another, that captures most of these verdicts? Please state it precisely, motivate each clause by the cases it exists to handle, and then tell me honestly what it predicts for cases I haven't mentioned — say, the proposed recognition of things like orthorexia or premenstrual dysphoric disorder, or the historical drift of a category's boundaries like the broadening of the autism spectrum. If no principle saves everything, I'd rather know which of my verdicts is the weak one that has to go than get a patchwork that bends for each case. And if it turns out the real distinction isn't about looping at all but about something else entirely — the direction of fit between symptoms and diagnostic criteria, perhaps, or which kinds have communities of mutual recognition — I'd want to hear that argued, not asserted.
