I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, working on a dissertation chapter in the ethics of war, and my supervisor's main comment on the draft is that it reads like it was written by two different people. Rather than restructure blindly, I want a consistency audit of the package of views I actually hold before I revise. Here is the set:

1. I accept the moral equality of combatants: soldiers on opposing sides are moral equals in bello. Each is a permissible target for the other, and — assuming they fight within the rules — neither does anything wrong in killing enemy soldiers, whatever the justice of the war they are fighting.

2. I ground combatant targetability in role rather than culpability: soldiers lose their civilian immunity when they assume the fighting function, and it is precisely this that lets blameless conscripts on an aggressor's side be killed without wrongdoing. Individual liability for posing an unjust threat is not what is doing the work; if it were, just and unjust combatants would not be equals, and I think they are.

3. Civilians retain their immunity even when they causally sustain an unjust war — munitions workers, engineers at defense firms, an electorate that votes an annexationist government into power. Mere causal contribution does not defeat immunity; something about being the one who fights does.

4. An officer in a transparently aggressive war — one where the evidence of aggression is overwhelming and publicly accessible — still acts permissibly in fighting, provided she fights within jus in bello. She does not become a murderer by doing the job her state assigned her.

5. I defend legal protection for selective conscientious objection, and I think soldiers have a genuine moral duty to withdraw from wars they know to be unjust — at minimum by refusing particular operations, and where possible by leaving the service altogether.

6. I accept a supreme emergency exemption: a community facing annihilation may intentionally attack civilians who are not liable, along the lines of the canonical defense of the early British bombing campaign against German cities — though I want the threshold set very high, at roughly the survival of the political community itself.

7. Responsibility for the injustice of a war falls on the aggressor state and its leadership, not on individual soldiers; that is why reparations are owed by states and why we prosecute political and military leaders for aggression but not privates.

Please treat this as an audit rather than a literature review. Which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, and how badly — rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them flat. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest fix would be and what that fix would cost me elsewhere in the set. Also flag any tensions that are only apparent and dissolve on inspection, so I know which parts of the chapter can stay as they are. Finally, tell me which single revision, if I made it, would clean up the most. I'm not attached to any particular one of these, so don't pull your punches about where the real trouble is.
