Next month I'm presenting a paper at a small workshop on consequentialism and formal decision theory — twenty minutes of talk, then forty minutes of questions, and the audience is the sort I most dread: roughly half hard-headed consequentialist moral philosophers who have heard every thought experiment Nozick ever invented and are inoculated against all of them, plus two economists-turned-formal-decision-theorists who believe that interpersonally comparable utility was refuted sometime in the 1930s and that anyone still trafficking in it owes them a measurement procedure, plus one confirmed Rawlsian who suspects the whole example is smuggled Kantianism in disguise. My thesis is deliberately provocative: that the utility monster survives the ordinalist revolution. The standard move in that room is to dismiss the monster as a wheezing antique — a being whose resources yield astronomically more utility than everyone else's only threatens a theory that treats utility as a cardinal, interpersonally comparable quantity, and there is no such quantity; under ordinal preferences or a von Neumann–Morgenstern representation, the monster degenerates into an ordinary person with steep returns, and no aggregation principle is embarrassed by steep returns. My paper concedes the comparability point and then argues the monster never needed it: the case can be rebuilt using only comparative, all-things-considered rankings of outcomes, since its force was always that one individual's gains outweigh everyone else's losses on any way of scoring outcomes a consequentialist could accept, and the residual conflict is with the separateness of persons rather than with any arithmetic. I also lean on a veil-of-ignorance reconstruction, which I admit is dangerous territory given that Harsanyi's aggregation result is usually read as licensing exactly the trade-offs I want to resist.

Prepare my Q&A sheet accordingly. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage each would do, phrased the way these people would put them — with the decision theorists' versions sounding like decision theory and the consequentialists' versions sounding like first-order moral argument, not like textbook summaries. For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is soft, or where it buys me breathing room at a cost I should keep track of. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, with the same treatment. Finally, throughout the sheet, keep settled matters genuinely distinct from contested ones: where something is real common ground among people who work on this — about what the original example was designed to show, or about what the formal results actually establish — say so plainly, and where something is a live interpretive or philosophical dispute, including anywhere my own reading of the literature may be idiosyncratic, flag it as contested rather than presenting it as the consensus view. This audience will know the difference, and a mislabeled consensus claim will cost me more credibility than a conceded soft spot.
