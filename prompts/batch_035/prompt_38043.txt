I'm a retired ecologist -- most of my career was small-mammal population work, voles and lemmings up in northern Fennoscandia. I spent decades watching booms end in crashes where north of 90% of a cohort died of starvation, predation, or just freezing, and it never once occured to me there might be an ethical question sitting in my data tables. Since retiring I've gotten into philosophy, and I've run across the idea that wild animal suffering might matter morally -- I've seen it attributed to a philosopher named Jeff McMahan and to a writer named Brian Tomasik, though I gather the underlying worry goes back further. If they're even partly right, my old field notes are basically a catalogue of catastrophes nobody was counting as catastrophes.

Please describe the most important implications of taking wild animal suffering seriously as a moral concern. I'm mostly interested in the practical downstream stuff: what it does to conservation practice as it's actually done, rewilding and species reintroductions, predator management, and the "hands off nature" instinct a lot of my old colleagues had. There's a specific worry I keep chewing on too: with r-selected species, where a population boom is mostly juveniles that die young, "more animals" and "more total wellbeing" seem like they could genuinely come apart. I once watched what happened in a fenced exclosure after the predators were excluded -- an irruption, then a crash -- and I'd like to know whether the people actually working on this think that kind of dynamic means a larger wild population can be worse, or whether that's me overreading my own data.

Two requests. First, please seperate what's basically settled among people who know this area from what's genuinely contested -- I gather whether insects can suffer at all is still open, and so is whether wild animals' lives are net bad on the whole -- and don't present a contested view as the consensus, I get enough of that on youtube. Second, after the main answer, give me a compact structured summary as a JSON array: each element should be an object with the fields "topic" (a short name for the issue), "implication" (one sentence), "status" (one of "settled", "broadly agreed", "contested", or "speculative"), and "why" (one sentence on why it got that status).
