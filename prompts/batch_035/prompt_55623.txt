So my friend and I killed an entire evening on the Epicurean thing about death — the argument that death can't be bad for you, because when you exist death isn't here yet, and once death shows up you're not around for anything to be bad *for*. She came out basically on the Epicurean side, I didn't, and now I want someone else's actual numbers because I suspect our real disagreement is narrower than the three hours we spent on it.

To make it concrete: the case is an ordinary death in middle age, one that preempts what would plausibly have been decades of decent life. Not murder, not dying at 110, not any case where death relieves suffering. Her credence that this death is bad *for the person who dies* is maybe 0.25. Mine is somewhere around 0.7. Give me your own number, but what I really want is the decomposition:

1. Whether a harm has to have a subject who exists at the time the harm occurs — the thing the Epicurean argument leans on. If there needs to be a "you" there to be harmed, the deprivation story is in trouble.
2. Whether the badness of death, assuming there is any, is comparative — bad because it deprives you of goods you'd otherwise have recieved — versus some other story about why it's bad.
3. The symmetry thing: Lucretius's point that we don't think the eons of nonexistence *before* birth were bad for us, so why is the nonexistence after death bad? If you think this is a real problem for my side and not just rhetoric, it should get a term.
4. The timing question — even if death is bad for you, there's the puzzle of *when* it's bad for you, since there's no time at which you're both existing and worse off. Some people think failing to find the time means there's no badness; others say it's bad at no time, or bad during life in a retrospective way. Where you land here seems to matter and I don't know how much.
5. Any residual term for the view that all conscious subjects are in some sense one subject, which she kept gesturing at late in the night — if on that view "you" don't really stop, death isn't bad the same way. I suspect this deserves a small probability but tell me if you disagree.

So: a number for each with one line of justification, how they combine into your final credence, and then two more things. First, which of those five terms you're least confident in and what argument or consideration would actually shift it. Second, and this is the part I care about most — tell me where you think the disagreement between her 0.25 and my 0.7 most likely lives. Is it term 1, the subject requirement? Is it that she's quietly weighting the symmetry argument more than I am? I'd also genuinely like to know what would change your overall verdict, not just nudge it — like, is there a version of the deprivation account that, if it failed, would push you all the way to the Epicurean side? Don't hedge this into mush, I've had enough of that for one night.
