I'm taking a philosophy of religion seminar, and for our unit on the problem of evil each of us has to write a short position paper stating where we actually stand — not a survey of the literature, but our own view — which the rest of the class will then attack. Before I hand it in, I'd like an outside check on whether my position set even hangs together, because I have a nagging feeling it doesn't. Here is what I plan to write.

(1) I'm a theist in the fairly traditional sense: God is omnipotent, omniscient, and perfectly good. (2) On moral evil, I lean on the free will line: a world containing creatures who can freely choose good is more valuable than a world of well-programmed automatons, and genuinely free choice requires the real possibility of choosing badly and doing real harm. (3) For the worst cases, where the free will line seems to make no sense of things — childhood cancer, the suffering of animals in the wild, the genuine horrors of history — I hold that no evil will be finally unredeemed: whatever is lost will in the end be compensated or overwhelmingly outweighed in the life to come. (4) I also believe God sometimes intervenes in the world: I take petitionary prayer seriously, and I accept that God has acted in history, including at least some miracle claims. (5) Alongside all of this, I'm sympathetic to the skeptical position that we shouldn't expect, from our own vantage point, to be able to work out why God permits each particular instance of suffering — our sense of the space of possible reasons a being like that might have is just too limited. (6) Finally, I firmly reject the soul-making idea: I don't think suffering is generally character-building, and I don't see this world as a vale designed to improve us.

My own half-diagnosis, for what it's worth: I worry that (5) sits badly with (2) and (3) — if I can't grasp God's reasons from here, in what sense do I know that freedom is the reason for moral evil, or that redemption is coming? And I suspect (4) pulls against (2), since a God who sometimes intervenes doesn't look like a God committed to respecting free choices. There's also a smaller itch: (3) seems to smuggle soul-making back in through the back door, which (6) is supposed to bar. But I've been staring at this too long to trust myself — I may be seeing conflicts where there are none, and missing the real one. Please go through the set and tell me: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look that way, and how severe each real tension is. Then tell me what the cheapest single revision would be and what it costs. I'd much rather find out from you than from my classmates on presentation day.
