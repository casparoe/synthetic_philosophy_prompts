I am a science journalist nearing the end of a thirty-year career, largely spent writing explainers of medicine, statistics, and risk for a general readership. I have been invited to give the opening keynote at a national conference of fact-checkers and editors of small newsrooms, and I want the speech built on a knot I have never managed to untangle in my own work.

The situation is this. Over my career I have published, conservatively, ten thousand checkable factual claims. For any single one of them I would testify: it was sourced, corroborated, edited, and issued with appropriate hedging, and I believe it is true. Yet I also know, from three decades of corrections columns and of errors discovered years after publication, that across ten thousand claims some number are false. So I believe each of my claims, and I believe that their conjunction is false — which is, I gather, the structure philosophers call the preface paradox. And the readers I most want to reach behave like the ticket-holder in the lottery paradox: they are entitled to believe of each of my articles that it is reliable, and equally entitled to believe that some article in the oeuvre will mislead them, since I am the first to say so.

The organisers want an inspiring keynote on why the public should keep trusting careful journalism despite the error rate. I refuse to write it by quietly implying the error rate is zero, and I refuse to write it by hedging every sentence into mush ("this claim is probably true, and so is this one...") until nothing is left worth trusting. I want the paradoxes to do real work in the speech: they should shape what can honestly be said about my own reliability, and about the reliability of any institution that publishes at volume.

Please draft the address, roughly fifteen minutes when spoken, for a mixed room — a contingent of statisticians and academics who will spot a mangled probability claim instantly, alongside editors of local papers who have never taken a statistics course and will glaze over at the word "conjunction." The speech must satisfy three conditions. First, it must not resolve the paradox by cheating: no retreat to the claim that individual beliefs are only "accepted" rather than full-out believed, unless you can make that distinction do real rhetorical work for a lay audience rather than serving as an escape hatch. Second, it must give the editors in the room something they can actually use — some concrete guidance on how to communicate to readers the difference between "we are fallible at volume" and "don't believe anything we say." Third, it must end with me making a flat, unhedged assertion about my own track record — and I would like you to explain to me, after the draft, whether that closing assertion is one I am entitled to, given everything the speech itself has conceded.
