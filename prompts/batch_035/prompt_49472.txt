You are a professional philosopher of medicine, so please put that hat on. I'm a retired physiologist (35 years, mostly renal stuff) who got into philosophy after retiring, and I've been going down a rabbit hole on what "disease" actually means. Before I go read more of the literature, I want a spec, not a survey: what would an adequate theory of disease/health actually have to deliver? Please brainstorm a bit first before writing the final list.

Here are the desiderata I'd want you to take seriously -- my career is showing, so push back where I'm being naive. Add others you think belong, and argue against ones you think are prejudices of one camp rather than genuine requirements:

1. It can't contradict evolutionary biology, but it also can't treat whatever selection "built" us for as healthy. Most of what kills my old patients -- hypertension, atherosclerosis, type 2 diabetes -- is plausibly the body doing roughly what it was selected to do, in an environment it wasn't selected for. An account on which the mismatch diseases come out "not dysfunctions" is broken, and so is one on which fitness-maximizing equals health (selection doesn't care if you're miserable after your last kid is raised).

2. It has to place aging. Every kidney I ever saw past 80 had lost a third of its nephrons, quite uniformly. Is that disease, normal function of an old body, or a distinction the theory has to make sense of (and how, without just saying "disease = whatever's common enough"?).

3. It has to distinguish disease from mere disadvantage -- being born tone-deaf, bad at math, unattractive. I remember when homosexuality was in the diagnostic manuals; the normativist side says this shows value judgments all the way down, the naturalists say it shows the psychiatrists were just wrong about the science. The theory has to say which.

4. Asymptomatic conditions. Someone with undiagnosed HIV in 1990, or early glomerulonephritis that will destroy the kidney in 8 years and feels fine now -- healthy or not? Doctors obviously say no, so the theory needs a notion of health that isn't just "feels fine" but also isn't just "will get sick later."

5. Border-line social stuff: deafness, infertility, dental crowding. The theory has to say something principled about why these are contested rather than just shrugging.

For each requirement, give the motivating case in a sentence or two, and -- this is the part I most want -- tell me which pairs of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, because if that's true I'd rather know now than after reading three books. Also mark which requirements are non-negotiable for a clinician vs. which are philosophers' preferences.

Then, briefly: how do the standard contenders do against the list? I mean the statistical-species-normality account (Boorse's biostatistical theory), the harmful-dysfunction hybrid (Wakefield), and something like a needs/goals-based normativist account (Nordenfelt's welfare-ish picture). Keep that short, one paragraph each max -- the criteria are the interesting part, the scorecard I can mostly do myself once I've got the list.

Please think carefully and be concrete -- cases, not jargon. A doctor friend of mine says half of medicine is arguing about what counts as sick and I'm starting to believe her lol.
