My friend Nadia and I have an ongoing game of sorting cases into "self-deception" and "not self-deception," usually over coffee, and I've started keeping a record of her calls because I suspect she's running a single implicit rule, though I can't tell which one. Her rulings so far: a husband who insists his marriage is solid while quietly eliminating everything that could test it — no date nights, no relationship talk, a sudden inability to hear friends ask how things are going — is self-deceived; a woman who believes her health is fine while a stack of unopened lab results sits on her counter and she keeps double-booking her follow-up appointments is self-deceived; a smoker who knows all the evidence that smoking kills, can recite it, doesn't avoid the topic in the least, but says it won't happen to him, is not self-deceived — Nadia called that one wishful thinking, not self-deception; a student who sincerely avows he'll write his thesis chapter over the break, feels no anxiety about it, and simply never sits down, is not self-deceived either — that one she dismissed as plain weakness of will; and a woman who claims she's over her ex while "accidentally" taking routes past his office is, per Nadia, not self-deceived, because, as she put it, the woman knows perfectly well she isn't over him and is only performing being over it.

Two new cases come up next week, and I'd like to predict Nadia's calls in advance rather than argue after the fact. Case one: a chess club member who believes he is the strongest player in the club, keeps demanding rematches, loses every single one, never dodges a comparison, and shows no visible dent in his confidence — his belief is plainly false, he has seen the counterevidence a hundred times, but there is no avoidance anywhere in his conduct. Case two: a small-business owner who believes her partner is honest — and he is; the belief is simply true — but she refuses to look at the books, gets prickly whenever accounts come up, and arranges her week so she never has to be near the ledger. For each case I'd like your prediction of Nadia's verdict, together with an account of the rules that fit her track record so far. It looks to me like at least two do: roughly, self-deception as a false or evidentially irrational belief held against the evidence, and self-deception as a strategy of avoiding the test, whatever the truth of the tested belief; possibly a third requiring some suppressed awareness of the opposite also fits. Say which rule she's most likely running and why, given the whole record rather than any single case. Then describe a further case, not among these, where the leading rules give opposite verdicts — that's the case I should bring to coffee if I want to pin her rule down — and say what each rule would predict about it. I'm less attached to being right about Nadia than to seeing how much her record actually underdetermines her verdicts, so be honest about that too.
