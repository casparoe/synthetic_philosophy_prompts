For my upper-level cognitive-science seminar on concepts, analogy, and categorization, the centerpiece of the final exam should be a question about the Copycat project that no one can answer by reciting the famous letter-string analogies or listing the components of the architecture. I want to build the question around an extension to the microdomain I have been toying with: allow strings to wrap around the alphabet, so that at the z-to-a boundary the successor relation acquires two rival readings — a cyclic successor that continues past z and the ordinary dead-end successor — and pose a new problem such as xyz → xyd; ab → ?. The question should require students to predict, from the architecture itself (Slipnet, workspace, codelets, temperature), how the system would behave and where it would struggle; to argue whether the wraparound difficulty is a fixable engineering matter or a symptom of something deeper — in particular, whether conceptual slippage as Copycat implements it amounts to anything more than movement along hand-coded distances in the Slipnet; and to judge whether this case supports the claim that fluid concepts require a pressure-driven, interactive architecture rather than a model that treats concepts as static points in a fixed similarity space. Assume students have already done the standard reading on the original domain and on the results involving grouped strings. Please write the question itself, a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth 4 marks each out of 20, each accompanied by a one-line note on how candidates who have merely memorized the standard narrative tend to miss it.
