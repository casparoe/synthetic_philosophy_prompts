I'm in my final year of sixth form, and for our philosophy society's end-of-term session each of us has to read out a short position paper and defend it. I've written down my views on when it is acceptable to assert something — roughly, on what rule assertion answers to — and I would like to check that they form a consistent package before I read them to the group.

Here is the set of commitments as I've written them down:

(1) Assertion is governed by a knowledge rule: one is entitled to assert that p only if one knows that p. What convinces me is that certain sentences sound self-undermining when asserted — "The common room is open, but I don't believe it is", or "Your ticket lost, but I don't know that it did" — and the knowledge rule explains this neatly: the speaker violates the very rule whose existence makes their assertion intelligible.

(2) I am a fallibilist about knowledge: knowing that p does not require ruling out every possibility of error. I know that my bus home is the 73, even though in principle the timetable could have changed without my noticing.

(3) In lottery cases the knowledge rule gives the right answer. If a friend holds one ticket among a million and asks me, after the draw but before the results are published, whether they have won, I may not simply tell them "you lost" — however overwhelming the odds, I do not know it.

(4) Ordinary assertions about the future are perfectly proper. Last week I told my mother "the coach will get back at seven" on the strength of the itinerary, and my teacher told me "you will do well in this paper". Neither seems the least bit improper, and I would not want a rule that forbade them.

(5) Nobody can know future contingents. What will actually happen tomorrow is not yet settled in a way that anyone could genuinely know now, however confident they reasonably feel.

(6) Hedged assertions are acceptable even at modest confidence. "I'm not certain, but I think the quiz covers chapter four", said at sixty per cent confidence, is perfectly in order; it would be absurd to refuse to say anything until one was sure.

I have tried to make these fit together, but I cannot shake the feeling that something is off, and I genuinely cannot tell whether the problem is real or whether I am confusing myself somewhere. Please go through the commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only appear to conflict, and what the cheapest repair would be for each real tension — including what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the list. Then tell me whether there is a single revision that would leave the rest standing as a stable position. Please do not be gentle for my sake: the others at the society certainly will not be.
