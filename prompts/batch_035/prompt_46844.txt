I follow the mechanistic interpretability scene, and a certain analogy keeps doing heavy lifting in its pitches — for funding, for research agendas, and above all for optimism. The comparison is to reverse-engineering a program from its compiled binary: you have no source code, no comments, and no documentation, just an enormous file of opaque numbers; but with patience and the right tools you can walk it back up to the structure that was there all along — recover the subroutines, the call graph, the entry points — and once you have that, the artifact is transparent: you can say what it does, predict what it will do, and modify it without guessing. The people leaning on this comparison use it to support two further conclusions: first, that interpretability methods will keep scaling to frontier models, so that we will eventually be able to inspect what a large model represents and is doing rather than merely sampling its outputs; and second, that philosophical hand-wringing about neural networks being irreducibly opaque is misplaced, since the claim that we cannot in principle understand these systems has the same shape as a claim once made, and long abandoned, about binaries.

I'm sympathetic but suspicious, and I'd like a careful audit of the analogy rather than a cheerleading exercise or a debunking.

First, set out the correspondence table explicitly: in the compiled-code story, say what plays the role of the network, the weights, the activations, the individual unit, the feature that interpretability researchers hunt for, the act of decompilation, and the engineer reading the listing at the end. Second, tell me where the mapping breaks — and, since this is the part I most want, rank the disanalogies by whether they matter for the two conclusions the analogy is being used to support. My guess is that some breaks are harmless (compiled code is deterministic in a stricter sense than a sampled language model, but I doubt the scaling claim cares), while others are load-bearing. Candidates for the load-bearing category: a binary's instructions bottom out in a small, fixed, human-designed instruction set whose semantics were specified in advance, whereas whatever counts as a network's primitives — a direction in activation space, a neuron — was specified by no one and may not be discrete at all; and programs are written by humans with a taste for modularity and meaningful names, while networks are grown by an optimizer with no reason to keep distinct functions in separate places, which is presumably why interpretability researchers keep finding units doing several unrelated jobs at once. But please judge these and any others you identify on their actual bearing, not on my framing.

Third, say whether each of the two conclusions survives once the load-bearing disanalogies are on the table. In particular: does the scaling optimism need the analogy at all, or does it survive on independent grounds — say, on the empirical track record of finding shared structure across models of different sizes? And does the response to the opacity worriers survive, or does it turn out that the worriers were never claiming binaries are unreadable, only that transparency in the engineering sense presupposes a kind of designed, discrete, human-legible structure that a grown artifact may lack?

Finally, if the compiled-code picture is the wrong source domain, propose a better one. Some people in my circle suggest the right comparison is to a biology-style anatomy of a grown organ rather than an engineering-style audit of a designed artifact; others suggest something more like deciphering a legal code, where the object is human-generated but cumulatively accidental, or reading a sedimentary record, where the structure is informative but was never intended for any reader. Say which of these, if any, you would endorse, and what your preferred comparison predicts about how far interpretability research can ultimately go — including whether it predicts any point at which the question of whether the model really has representations, rather than merely behaving as if it does, becomes empirically decidable.

Please keep the register analytic: mapping first, breaks second, verdicts third, and a recommendation at the end about whether the field's rhetoric should keep invoking the reverse-engineering image.
