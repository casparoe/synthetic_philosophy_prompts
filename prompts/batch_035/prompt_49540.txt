When environmental groups and public-health campaigns give climate-focused dietary advice, they tell people to cut red meat and, when they name a replacement, to shift toward poultry — chicken production emits a fraction of the greenhouse gases per kilogram that beef does. But per kilogram of meat, the two trades run in opposite directions for animals: a single cow supplies hundreds of meals, while a meal of chicken costs roughly one bird its short life, and beef also generally involves less feed-crop cultivation, whose plowing, pesticide use, and harvesting kill uncounted wild animals. Animal-welfare groups, unsurprisingly, direct their largest campaigns at chicken farming. So the same foodstuff sits near the bottom of one institution's ranking and the top of another's. What theory of value does the climate-style advice presuppose: that animal suffering counts for literally nothing, or only that it falls outside a climate mandate? Is that a coherent division of moral labor for institutions giving advice about diets, given that they know the diets they recommend have effects beyond carbon? Reconstruct the implicit axiology, say whether the practice is underdetermined — could several different weightings of animal welfare still license "replace beef with chicken," or does any nonzero weighting flip the ranking — and describe what the advice would have to look like if animal welfare and wild-animal deaths in crop cultivation were assigned nontrivial weight.
