A while back I read this essay called Meditations on Moloch by Scott Alexander (some rationalist blog thing), and it's been living rent free in my head ever since, because I keep watching it play out at work in slow motion. I'm a tech lead at a mid-size SaaS company and our roadmap meetings basically run on a script: everyone agrees we should slow down and pay down the mountain of tech debt, everyone agrees shipping two half-tested features a week is slowly killing us, and then the quarter starts and nobody can unilaterally slow down, because if we do, our velocity numbers drop while the other teams' don't, and we're the ones who look bad. Same thing with on-call: another internal team started advertising 5-minute pager response in their ops review, so now everyone's scared to admit they aim for 15, even though nobody — including the people doing it — thinks 5 minutes at 3am produces good decisions. It's not that anyone chose this. It's that the choice doesn't exist at the level where any of us sits.

Here's what I want from you: an actual decision procedure, a checklist my team can run when we suspect we're in one of these races, that tells us what kind of situation we're in and what our options realistically are. Kinda like: question one, is there a shared outcome everyone privately prefers to the current trajectory (if yes, look for coordination/escalation moves, if no, stop, it's just hard competition); question two, can we actually tell who we're racing against and is the "race" real or imagined; and so on in whatever order actually makes sense — I made those up, you do it properly, grounded in whatever game theory and political philosophy actually know about these situations (prisoner's dilemma stuff, races to the bottom, that whole literature). The procedure has to be usable in an hour during quarterly planning, by engineers, with no philosophy background. It should have a clear output at the end: exit the race / try to coordinate / escalate above the teams / just compete and accept it / the premise is wrong.

Then run it on three concrete cases for me so I can see how it behaves. Case one: the on-call response time ratchet described above, internal, between two teams under the same VP. Case two: we're rushing a feature to market before a competitor, and the only way to hit the date is to skip the load testing we'd normally do — external competitor, real deadline, uncertain payoff. Case three: every competitor in our space quietly reads customers' usage data in ways the privacy policy sorta technically permits, and our lawyers keep saying we'd be "leaving money on the table" not to — I want to see what the procedure says when the thing being sacrificed is something we think is just wrong. Also, be honest with me about two things: where in the procedure the question stops being one of facts and becomes one of values that engineers can't settle with a checklist, and whether there's a real distinction between a genuine multipolar trap and my team just rationalizing not doing boring work — and if the procedure can't always tell those apart, say so plainly. Ideally include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, I'd rather find out now than in the meeting.
