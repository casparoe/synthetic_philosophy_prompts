I help run a small seminar on the philosophy of mathematical practice, and two of our members have been locked in the same argument for over a year: whether the possibility that our fundamental mathematical theories are inconsistent is a live intellectual possibility that mathematicians and philosophers should prepare for, or a degenerate philosophical curiosity that can be set aside on metatheoretic grounds. One member — call her the classical camp — holds that in classical logic an inconsistency explodes into triviality, that published mathematics is manifestly not trivial, and that this is already excellent evidence that our axioms are consistent, or at least that worrying about inconsistency buys nothing. The other — the paraconsistent camp — holds that explosion is a feature of the proof theory we happen to use, not of rational inference itself, points to historical episodes like the early calculus and naive set theory, which were arguably inconsistent yet supported a rich, non-trivial body of practice for a long time before repair, and concludes that discovered inconsistency in, say, ZFC would be survivable, and that we might even already be living with undetected inconsistency. They have lately been talking past each other, and I suspect part of the dispute is verbal (about what "inconsistent" and "trivial" mean when applied to a practice rather than to a formal object) while another part is genuine (about whether explosion governs actual mathematical reasoning, and about what our failure to date to derive contradictions shows inductively).

Acting as an expert in philosophical logic who has moderated adversarial collaborations, draft the joint statement the two of them should have produced. Structure it as follows: first a numbered section of agreed claims — including claims both can sign about Gödel's second incompleteness theorem (which limits what any consistent-enough theory can say about its own consistency, and therefore limits how the classical camp's metatheoretic argument can be run), about the difference between a theory's inconsistency and a practice's triviality, and about what the historical cases do and do not show. Then a table of disagreements, with each residual disagreement stated twice, once in wording the classical camp would accept as fair to its view and once in wording the paraconsistent camp would accept as fair to its view. Then identify the cruxes — I suspect at least these three: (a) whether explosion describes how mathematicians actually license inferences, as opposed to how idealized formal proofs behave; (b) whether the non-occurrence of derived contradictions in centuries of practice is strong inductive evidence of consistency, or weak evidence compatible with merely-undetected inconsistency; and (c) whether there is any observation or result short of an explicit derivation of a contradiction that could count against the other side's position. For each crux, describe a concrete case, thought experiment, or argumentative move both would accept as bearing on it, and write down in advance what each camp predicts about it — for instance, what each thinks would happen to everyday mathematics (a working analyst's or number theorist's daily theorems) if ZFC were tomorrow found inconsistent. If you conclude that some portion of their dispute is merely verbal, say which portion, and propose the disambiguation that would dissolve it. Please think carefully before drafting, do some internal brainstorming about which formulations both sides could sign without wincing, and avoid slanted phrasing that either camp would reject as question-begging. Don't cite specific papers or give a bibliography; refer to arguments and positions by description, and we will attach references ourselves.
