I run a joint seminar on philosophy of history with two colleagues from the history department, and every year, without fail, we run aground on the same argument, and every year it ends with somebody changing the subject over coffee. The flashpoint this year was a case one of the historians brought in from her own region: a town holds an annual commemoration of an industrial disaster from the early twentieth century. Local memory there has it that around two hundred died and that the townspeople pulled survivors from the rubble for days while the company stood by. The archival record — coroner's inquest, newspaper files, company correspondence — says roughly forty dead, and the rescue effort abandoned within a day because of gas. The memorial plaque in the square gives the remembered figure, not the counted one. My historian colleagues want the plaque corrected as a matter of basic integrity toward the dead; the memory-studies people in the group (broadly the Halbwachs tradition, though they'd hate being lumped together) say correction is a category error: memory isn't in the headcount business, the plaque encodes the town's grief and its sense of having been sacrificed by the owners, and on that register the remembered number does its job perfectly. One of them went further and said the remembered figure is in a sense truer than the counted one, since it registers what the event meant to the people it happened to. The historians reply that once you say that you've licensed anything, including versions pushed by people with far worse motives than a grieving town — and there the seminar always stalls.

My own hunch, offered as a hunch and nothing more, is that this isn't really a disagreement about numbers or even about truth, but about what a public inscription is for: one side is treating the plaque as a degraded record, the other as a ritual object that happens to be made of facts, and the two functions come apart exactly in cases like this. But I half suspect that diagnosis is too convenient for everyone, since it would let each side keep its own word "true" and go home. So I'd like a proper diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute about "truth" and "accuracy", or a substantive one that just looks verbal? Is a hidden normative question doing the work — whose standing is it to fix the content of a communal memorial, the descendants, the experts, the local council? Or are the two sides actually answering different questions under one heading, one about what happened and one about what the community owes its past? I'd like you to work from what each side actually claims, as I've described them, and then tell me honestly what would count as progress here: some distinction that could be drawn (say, splitting the record-keeping function from the commemorative one, a plaque plus an archive entry rather than a corrected plaque), or a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the impasse reflects commitments neither side can argue for rather than argue from. And if you think my hunch is wrong, say so — I'd rather know that than be flattered.
