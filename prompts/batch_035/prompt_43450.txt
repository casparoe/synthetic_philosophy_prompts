I teach an upper-level ethics seminar — 14 students, 80 minutes, they've had a solid dose of utilitarianism but nothing of Williams yet. That's deliberate: I want the activity to come before the reading, so the debrief can cash out something that actually happened to them into the texts. Design me the activity.

No lecture up front, and nobody hears the phrases integrity objection or one thought too many until the debrief. I want them to run into both problems before either gets a name.

Two phases, one scenario — and please invent a fresh case rather than reaching for the drowning-wife example or anything out of an anthology. The case needs: a role where one participant has something personal at stake (a long-running project or a close tie — make it something a student could actually care about defending, not a cardboard spouse), and a role that works the same choice as an impartial optimizer, with real numbers to put up on the board. Plus a mechanic I specifically want: whoever decides has to state their reasons out loud, one at a time, and a designated recorder writes each reason on a separate card as it's voiced. The cards get kept. In the debrief I want to lay the card sequence out in order and ask where, exactly, a thought became one too many — and prep me for the fact that there are two live readings of that remark floating around (roughly: the extra thought is corrupting inside the agent's own deliberation, vs. it's fine there but wrong as a public justification). My students will split across those readings, and I want to be able to referee the split honestly instead of pretending Williams settled it.

Phase two is the integrity half. I want the personal-stake participant's projects and attachments to get picked up and converted into inputs on the optimizer's ledger, and I want the person it happens to feel it — their stuff treated as one more entry in the ranking rather than as theirs. The debrief then has to name that feeling (alienation from your ground projects) and connect it to the objection that a theory which forces you to view your own projects that way has made moral life too impersonal — that it doesn't just constrain your integrity but violates it.

Give me: rules, materials (paper and markers is my budget), how to split 14 people across the roles including what everyone not currently in a role is doing, a minute-by-minute plan for the 80 minutes, the full debrief, what will predictibly go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and an honest note on which part of the whole thing a classroom activity simply cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply myself in the debrief.

Two more constraints. The setup has to be constructible in under five minutes, and the choice has to be live enough that participants actually hesitate — if the case is so obviously stacked that nobody wobbles, the cards are worthless. And I want the optimizer's numbers to run close rather than a landslide: the interesting debrief moments come from narrow margins, where it looks like one or two extra considerations could flip it either way.
