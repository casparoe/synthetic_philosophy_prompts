I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I want to run a real episode through a philosophical debate I've bumped into and can't resolve on my own: methodological individualism versus holism in the social sciences. I'll state my rough understanding of the two positions so you can correct me where I've got them wrong.

As I understand it: methodological individualism says that genuine explanations of social phenomena must ultimately bottom out in facts about individuals — their beliefs, preferences, resources, and the incentives and constraints they face. Any statement about "society," "institutions," or "groups" is at best a compression of those individual-level facts, and at worst a mystification. Holism denies this: it holds that some social entities and forces — institutions, norms, class structures, collective agents — are real in their own right, do causal work that cannot be faithfully reconstructed from the individual level, and therefore must appear as primitives in at least some explanations.

The episode is the GameStop short squeeze of January 2021. The facts as I know them, in case any are load-bearing: GameStop stock was heavily shorted — reported short interest exceeded 100% of the float, meaning more shares were sold short than existed to cover. A large community of retail traders on the subreddit r/wallstreetbets began buying shares and options, partly to profit from a squeeze, partly out of animus toward short-selling hedge funds, and partly, it seems, for the spectacle of it. There was no central direction: one prominent poster, known as DeepFuckingValue, was a focal point and a kind of folk hero, but nobody issued orders. Participants enforced a holding norm on each other ("diamond hands"), shaming and mocking those who sold early. The share price went from roughly $20 to an intraday peak near $480 within weeks. Major short sellers took enormous losses; one fund required a bailout. Then Robinhood and other retail brokers suspended buying (not selling) in the stock, citing margin and clearinghouse deposit requirements, and there were congressional hearings afterward.

Here's what I want, and I want it to run in both directions — the theory applied to the case, and the case used to test the theory.

First, the mapping, done carefully. What does methodological individualism say this episode consists of? Spell out who the individuals are, what each broad type of them believed and wanted (profit, spite, entertainment, belonging), and what the constraints were. Then say honestly whether the aggregate outcome — the squeeze — is in principle derivable from those individual-level facts, or whether something else has to be smuggled in.

Second, tell me where each view strains. My suspicion is that individualism has trouble with at least three features: the norm enforcement inside the subreddit, where individuals were sanctioned for defection by a group that had no membership rules and no officers; the feedback loop of the squeeze itself, where shorts were mechanically forced to buy as the price rose, an aggregate-level dynamic that was no individual's intention; and the way commentators and participants themselves explained events by citing entities — "WallStreetBets," "the hedge funds," "retail" — as actors. But I also suspect holism overreaches: "the subreddit punished the shorts" feels like narrative compression rather than explanation, and I can imagine an individualist saying that everything I've attributed to the group is just the aggregate of individuals responding to visibility and status incentives. Assess both suspicions.

Third, where is each theory simply silent? Don't force either to speak about things it has no resources for — coordination without central direction, the role of clearinghouses and brokers as institutional constraints that no individual trader chose, the fact that the episode seems to have required a crowd that identified as a crowd.

Fourth, the verdict, including on the framing itself. As an engineer, my instinct is to say this is like the relation between a distributed system's node-level state and its system-level behavior: the full node state determines everything, yet the system-level description ("the cluster is degraded," "the swarm is self-healing") is not merely shorthand — it's the only tractable level at which to predict and intervene. Is the philosophy debate just a dispute about levels of description, with both sides right at their own level? Or is that reconciliation too easy — does the individualist owe an account of why system-level explanations predict successfully when we can't actually run the derivation, and does the holist owe an account of what a supra-individual entity is made of? Say which side, if either, the GameStop episode genuinely favors, and what it is about this episode specifically — as opposed to, say, a bank run or a riot — that makes it a fair or unfair test case.

One constraint: I don't want a literature review or a list of who argued what. I want the analysis worked out on the case, with the philosophical positions treated as tools rather than as schools with proper names attached. Where I've mischaracterized a position, correct me inline and continue.
