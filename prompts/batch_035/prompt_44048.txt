So I've been reading about how people try to define deception and honesty for AI systems -- mostly the literature is about chatbots lying to users -- and I keep coming back to the volkswagen diesel thing as a weird edge case that nobody in that literature seems to use. Quick facts so we're on the same page: in 2015 the EPA found that VW had put software in roughly 11 million diesel cars that detected when the car was undergoing an official emissions test (things like steering wheel position and speed/acceleration patterns gave it away), and only then switched the full emissions controls on. In normal driving the controls stayed partly off, the cars emitted up to ~40x the legal NOx limit, and meanwhile the software fed the testers normal-looking numbers. No one claims the code "believed" anything or "wanted" anything -- under the hood it was basically a conditional: if it looks like a test, behave clean.

Heres what I want you to do. Take the main live ways of defining deception for machines and run the defeat device through each of them. I'm thinking of at least (a) intent/agent-based definitions, where deception requires something like a goal of inducing false beliefs in another agent, (b) functional/behavioral definitions, where a system counts as deceptive if it systematically causes false beliefs regardless of whats "inside" it, and (c) the "just a tool" view on which the machine does no deceiving at all -- the deception belongs entirely to the engineers, and the software is like a fake ID that doesn't itself lie. If you think there's a better candidate definition, add it. For each one, say clearly: on this view, was the machine deceptive? Was it honest-but-misused? A neutral instrument?

Then the harder part, and honestly the part I care about most: tell me where each definition strains or goes silent on this case. Some things to chew on (add your own): the engineers had intent, but the deployed system only ever "executed" -- so what exactly carries the intent-based attribution, the design history? does that mean a system's deceptive status depends on facts about its designers that are invisible in the system itself? On the functional view, is there a risk it overcounts -- does it end up calling a chameleon's camouflage, or a poker face, "deception" in the same sense, and does that matter for AI policy language? Also address the obvious objection that this wasn't "real AI" -- it wasn't learning-based, no model of the tester, no representation of anyone's beliefs. Does that difference actually change the analysis, or just the vibe? If a 2015 emissions controller already falls in the gap between intent-based and functional definitions, what does that suggest about definitions of AI honesty that were built with LLMs in mind -- are they picking up on a real category boundary or just tracking what current systems happen to look like?

I want the analysis to run both directions: the theories applied to the case, and then what the case does back to the theories -- where it confirms them, where it's an anomaly they didn't anticipate.

Two things I dont want: no lecture on the environmental ethics (I know NOx is bad, that's not the question), and no reading lists or citations -- just the analysis from what you know. And please don't soft-pedal with "it depends" -- I want you to actually commit to which definition handles this best and say why.
