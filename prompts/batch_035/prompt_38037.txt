My reading group this term is on minds and machines, and the phrase 'competence without comprehension' — always attributed to Dennett, always connected to his pairing of Darwin's and Turing's inversions of the usual order of explanation — has been doing suspicious amounts of work in our discussions. I've now watched it be deployed in at least four different ways, sometimes by the same person:

1. As a historical claim: Darwin showed that the design-quality competence of organisms can be produced by an uncomprehending process of variation and selection, and Turing showed that intelligent-seeming performance can be produced by mechanical symbol-manipulation with no grasp of meaning — so comprehension is simply not the precondition for competence that common sense takes it to be.
2. As an inference-blocker against language models: their fluency is competence, and since competence without comprehension is possible, we are not entitled to infer comprehension from their performance.
3. As a license for agnosticism, by the other side of the table: since, per Darwin, uncomprehending processes can build up competence that eventually adds up to comprehension (as they arguably did in us), competence in a system tells us nothing either way about comprehension — the slogan cuts against confident denial just as much as against confident attribution.
4. As a reduction: comprehension is not merely compatible with being constituted by competences; it just is a particular organization of competences, so 'competence without comprehension' is true both where comprehension is absent and, at the level of the parts, where it is present — and the very contrast the slogan appears to draw is on this reading a kind of illusion.

I suspect that when these uses are stated precisely, some of them collapse into one another, and others collapse into platitudes, while exactly one of them is the interesting and controversial thesis Dennett actually needs for his larger project to go through. But I can't sort out which is which myself.
