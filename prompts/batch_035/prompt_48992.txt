I edit a small philosophy blog for readers with some philosophical background but no specialist training in metaphysics, and I want to run a "which view of material constitution do you actually hold?" quiz as the centerpiece of our month on the statue and the clay. The design constraint I care most about: readers should be sorted by what they say about cases, not by which label they have heard of, so no question may ask, in effect, "is the statue identical to the clay?" or "do you believe in temporal parts?" Every question must be a concrete case with two or three answer options, and each option should be something a thoughtful reader could pick without ever having taken a metaphysics course.

Please write eight to ten questions built from cases of the following shapes, ordered however best serves the diagnostic logic: a sculptor simultaneously brings into being a statue (call it Goliath) and the piece of clay she shapes it from (call it Lumpl), and we ask how many things sit on the pedestal; the clay is later squashed flat, the sculptor says the statue is gone but the clay persists, and we ask what happened; a statue's arm is replaced bit by bit, and we ask at what point, if any, the thing in the gallery stopped being that statue; the statue and the clay appear to have different persistence conditions, since the clay survives being melted down and recast while the statue does not; and a case where two candidate sortals compete for one hunk of matter, such as a piece of clay that is both a pet-shaped blob and a pet. You may invent analogous cases where they discriminate between positions better than the canonical ones do.

Then give me a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the following positions as leaves: (a) the deflationary one-object view, on which there is just one thing there and the statue being constituted by the clay is a genuine non-identity relation rather than a restatement of identity; (b) coincidence or constitution-without-identity views, on which statue and clay are numerically distinct but spatially coincident; (c) four-dimensionalist solutions that dissolve the puzzle with temporal parts; (d) eliminativist views, on which one or both of the candidates does not exist at all; and (e) relative-identity or sortal-relative views, on which sameness is always sameness under a sortal and the question of whether the statue is the clay is malformed rather than false. Where two of these positions cannot be separated by any case you can coherently pose, say so plainly instead of inventing a question to fake a separation.

Two further requirements. First, build the scoring key out of the logical space rather than the names: explain which combinations of answers to the counting question, the persistence-conditions question, and the sortal-dependence question generate each position, and note any combination of answers that nobody defends, with one sentence on whether that combination is unoccupied because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because no one has explored it. Second, design at least two questions whose options will be tempting to readers who profess the deflationary one-object view but are actually inconsistent with it. I have noticed my readers classify themselves as holding that the statue just is the clay because it sounds sensible and unmetaphysical, and then balk at the case where the clay survives and the statue does not; I want the quiz to catch that hesitation rather than paper over it.

Keep the register polished but conversational enough for a blog, and keep each answer option under about thirty words.
