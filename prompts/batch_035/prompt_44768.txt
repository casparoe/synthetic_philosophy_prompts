I work at the boundary of philosophy and game theory, and for several years I've watched the backward induction debate sit perfectly still. I'd like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement it actually is.

The setting, to fix terms: in a centipede game — Rosenthal's construction, two players alternating take/pass with a growing pot — backward induction yields a unique subgame-perfect outcome in which the first mover takes immediately, and this seems at once inescapable and absurd. One camp, following Aumann, treats the conclusion as a theorem: given common knowledge of rationality, the backward induction outcome follows, and the appearance of paradox is a psychological residue, not a formal one. The dissenters reply in two ways I can't fully separate. Pettit and Sugden argued that rational players are not in a position to run the backward induction argument at all — the reasoning requires a player at a late node to believe both that everyone is rational and that the preceding play happened, which, on their view of what belief-formation requires, cannot be managed. The Reny line, as I understand it (I may be garbling details), is that in some games the epistemic assumptions the theorem needs cannot hold at unreached nodes — the game itself refutes them precisely where the theorem invokes them. And then there is the decision-theoretic wing: since arriving at a late node is itself evidence that someone has already failed to be the backward-induction type, a rational player should update on her own survival and pass a while longer — advice that seems to fit what happens in the lab, where McKelvey and Palfrey found subjects passing well into the game.

The camps talk past each other. The theorem-provers respond to objections by tightening the epistemic premises; the dissenters answer that the tightened premises hold, if at all, only along the path of play, where they do no work; the decision-theoreticians are told they have changed the subject from the given game to the given players. Nobody concedes anything.

My hypothesis — offered only as a hypothesis — is that much of this is two questions under one name. One is a question about a mathematical object: what common knowledge of rationality entails in the abstract game tree, where the answer is a theorem and there is nothing to argue about. The other is a question about a situated agent: what a player should do who actually finds herself at node k, holding the evidence that having reached node k itself constitutes. And I suspect the second question is infected by a distinction nobody states cleanly — whether rationality is a property of complete strategies, so that a player must have a sequentially rational disposition toward every node, or a property of acts at nodes actually reached, so that a disposition to pass early can be rational if passing is what one should expect of another rational agent. If that is right, then part of the fight is verbal, turning on whether counterfactual claims about unreached nodes are true, false, or truth-valueless, and part of it is substantive but located somewhere other than where the participants put it.

So: what kind of disagreement is this — verbal, substantive, or two questions under a single heading? Diagnose it using what the parties actually claim rather than by surveying the literature. Tell me whether my hypothesis is right, and if not, precisely where it fails. And tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, experimental evidence, a change of question — or an argument that the impasse should be expected to be permanent, and why. A final request: if you think I have misdescribed the dialectic — that one of the three positions I name does not really exist as a position, or that the debate in fact dissolved at some point I've missed — say so plainly rather than building a diagnosis on my mistakes. I would rather be corrected than politely played along with.
