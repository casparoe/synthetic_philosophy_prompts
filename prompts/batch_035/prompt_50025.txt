I've come to a settled position on the moral status of brain size: gross brain size carries no moral weight of its own. What matters is the capacity for valenced experience and whatever psychological capacities ground moral status, and brain size is at most evidence about those capacities — never itself part of what makes a life count. I want to know what would count against this position, and I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including anything that has arguably already happened rather than merely hypothetical discoveries.

Some directions I've been mulling over, which you should treat as candidates to engage, improve on, or discard: the number-of-subjects problem, namely that if a very large brain, say that of a whale, could implement several partially independent experiencing subjects within one body, then total welfare capacity might scale with brain quantity even if per-subject status does not, and my view as I've stated it speaks only about status per subject — I want to know whether that is a genuine refutation or merely a re-description of what I meant all along. The neuron-count problem: if the best available theory of experience says that cortical neuron number predicts the intensity of valenced experience, and neuron number tracks brain size across most of the tree of life, then brain size irrelevance could be true by definition and false in practice, and I'd like to know whether that distinction does any real work. Human variation: brain volume differs by something like fifty percent between cognitively normal adults, and no one thinks moral status varies accordingly — but is this actually a datum supporting my view, or a case where we have quietly decided not to draw an implication we find monstrous, and does that matter? Evolutionary continuity: since every capacity I care about emerged through gradual scaling and reorganization of primate brains, the view owes an account of where moral status came from without positing a threshold, and I want to know whether a smooth gradient of mattering is something my view can accommodate or something that pressures it.

I'd also like to know what the position predicts that its rivals don't — for instance, about split-brain patients, people who have had a hemisphere removed and function well, artificial systems with no brain at all, and cephalopods whose neurons are mostly in their arms, so that brain size isn't even a well-defined quantity for them. Tell me which of these considerations a committed defender of my position would concede as genuine counterevidence and which they would dismiss, and by what principle they'd draw the line. Finally, give me an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: the retreat from brain size doesn't matter to brain size only matters as evidence seems able to absorb any conceivable result, and I want to know whether that flexibility is a defect in this kind of view, or simply what any view about evidence rather than intrinsic properties must look like, and whether there is a formulation of my position that risks something real.

Please write your answer as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a long letter.
