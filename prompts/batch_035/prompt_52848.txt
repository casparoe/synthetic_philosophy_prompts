I'm an undergraduate taking a course on causal inference in the medical and social sciences, and I'm preparing a presentation for our unit on Simpson's paradox. My classmates already know the arithmetic: everyone can verify that a treatment can look better in every subpopulation yet worse in the pooled data. What I want to argue is that the reversal itself, considered purely as a statistical fact, settles nothing about which comparison to trust — the answer depends on causal facts not contained in the table. For that I need a case built to order, and I'd like your help constructing it.

The specifications are as follows. First, build a single realistic scenario — a hospital ward, a school district, or something comparably ordinary — in which Simpson's reversal occurs in the direction that people find hardest to accept: the treatment looks better within every subpopulation but worse in the pooled data, and yet the pooled comparison is the one that gets the causal story right. Second, nothing in the setup may announce the verdict: no character in the scenario may declare which analysis is correct, and you may not describe the variables in statistical vocabulary (nothing may be labeled a confounder, a collider, or an effect modifier). The causal structure has to come through ordinary institutional detail — who was assigned where, when a measurement was taken relative to treatment — the way it would in real life. Third, produce a minimal variant of the same scenario with the numbers held exactly fixed, in which one fact about the causal position of the third variable changes, and in which the correct conclusion flips to the disaggregated comparison. The pair should demonstrate that identical numbers underdetermine the answer. Use small counts that can be checked by hand, and include the table and the arithmetic verifying the reversal.

After presenting the two versions, please do four things: walk through why the correct conclusion differs between them; identify the single feature of the construction doing the work, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed; explain how someone whose standing rule is "when aggregates and subgroups disagree, always trust the subgroups" would attack the construction, and whether the attack succeeds; and tell me whether the anti-cheating constraint is genuinely met — the verdicts must fall out of the causal structure rather than being stipulated by narrative framing.

Finally, since I will be presenting this to a skeptical audience, please state how confident you are in each of your main claims and single out the point you are least sure of — for instance, whether your counts really produce the reversal, or whether your variant is truly minimal rather than smuggling in a second change alongside the intended one.
