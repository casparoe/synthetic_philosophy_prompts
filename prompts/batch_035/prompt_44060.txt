I've just come away from a long and entirely unresolved argument about the passage in Part Two of Franklin's Autobiography, at the end of the chapter on his bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection, where he reflects on how far short of the mark he fell. He writes that he never actually reached perfection, but that his attempt left him a better and happier man than he would otherwise have been, and he then compares the moral striver to a man who wanted the whole surface of his axe as bright as its edge: the smith agrees to grind it bright, but on condition that the buyer whirl the axe himself on the grindstone, and the buyer, worn out by whirling, agrees at last to take the axe as it stands, speckled with spots of rust on one face, and to like it. Franklin closes by saying that something of this kind has been his fate, and that he has, in the manner he describes, liked his speckled axe — though he notes the envy he has at times escaped, and the vanity he indulges when he thinks of the happiness of his course. I should say that I am working from a close paraphrase rather than the exact wording, but the content is what matters here.

The passage is doing something I can't quite pin down, and my friend and I took diametrically opposed readings of it. He argues that the anecdote is a quiet retraction of the whole scheme: the chart of thirteen virtues, the little book ruled with a page for each virtue and a column for each day of the week, the black marks for faults committed, the sequencing of the virtues so that temperance comes first because it steadies the mind for what follows — all of it, he says, is exposed by the ending as a project that was never meant to be completed, and that Franklin knew it, which is why humility was bolted on as a thirteenth virtue only after a Quaker friend gently suggested that Franklin was generally thought proud, and why Franklin later admits he cannot boast of much success in acquiring the reality of that virtue, only the appearance of it. On my friend's reading, the chapter is essentially a satire of Puritan self-examination recast in the idiom of an accountant's ledger: moral life reduced to bookkeeping, and the speckled axe is the punchline.

I resist this, but not very confidently. It seems to me that Franklin's professions of satisfaction with imperfection are sincere rather than ironic, that the chapter treats moral improvement as a craft to be practised rather than a state to be attained, and that the grinding analogy is meant to make a genuinely defensible point about the diminishing returns of polishing — but I cannot shake the suspicion that a man who frames his concessions to vanity in the language of self-command has smuggled his vice back in as an accomplishment. Can you help me work out what the passage is actually claiming? Specifically: does the speckled-axe comparison concede that the project failed, defend a doctrine of good-enough virtue, or quietly boast about the attempt itself, and what in the surrounding chapter — the design of the book, the ordering of the virtues, the late addition of humility — should settle the question? I would also value your view on whether the chapter is internally consistent in its attitude to its own method, since my friend and I found ourselves arguing as much about that as about the passage.
