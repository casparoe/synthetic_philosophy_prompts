I am grading midterms for an intermediate course on decision theory and rational choice, and I would like help evaluating one student's response. The exam question was:

"State the independence axiom of expected utility theory. Then present the Allais paradox: give the two choices Allais posed, explain why the pattern of answers most people give conflicts with the independence axiom, and what the paradox is supposed to show. Finally, briefly discuss one reply available to a defender of expected utility theory."

The student's answer follows. Please assign a grade (a letter grade or a score out of 100, whichever you prefer) and write the point-by-point comments that justify it, so it is clear exactly which parts of the answer earned or lost credit.

Student's answer:

"The independence axiom says that if you prefer lottery L to lottery M, then you must prefer any mixture αL + (1−α)N to αM + (1−α)N, for any lottery N and any probability α. Equivalently, it says that any mixture of L and M must be ranked between L and M themselves.

The paradox: Allais offered subjects two choices. First, between A, $1 million for certain, and B, an 89% chance of $1M, a 10% chance of $5M, and a 1% chance of nothing. Most people take A. Second, between C, an 89% chance of nothing and an 11% chance of $1M, and D, a 90% chance of nothing and a 10% chance of $5M. Most people take D. But in the first choice the 89% chance of $1M is common to both options (writing A as an 89% chance of $1M plus an 11% chance of $1M), so independence lets us cancel it: choosing A over B means preferring $1M for certain to a lottery that gives $5M with probability 10/11 and nothing with probability 1/11. In the second choice the common consequence is likewise the 89% chance of $1M, so canceling it gives exactly the same comparison, and consistency requires preferring C to D. Preferring A and D together therefore violates the axiom.

This refutes expected utility theory: the choices are systematic, not noise, and they are made by careful subjects, so the theory is not just descriptively false but cannot be the right normative standard either. Allais's own explanation was regret theory: people choose the sure thing in the first case to avoid the regret of gambling away a sure million and ending with nothing.

One reply available to the defender is to bite the bullet: the Allais preferences are irrational even if nearly everyone has them, since the axiom's justification does not depend on majority opinion. But this reply is unavailable, because an axiom that people cannot be argued out of cannot be a genuine requirement of rationality."
