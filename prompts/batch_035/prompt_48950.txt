My philosophy of biology reading group got into a fight we cant settle, and I think it might show the whole free-floating rationale idea is broken. Quick setup for anyone who knows the term only vaguely: Dennett's idea is that evolution produces reasons without reasoners — "free-floating rationales." His stock example is stotting, where a gazelle leaps straight up with stiff legs when it spots a predator. No gazelle ever figured out that this signals "I'm so fit chasing me is a waste of your energy," and no wolf planned it either, but that signalling function is really why stotting spread. So there's a rationale for stotting even though nobody ever entertained it. Reasons floating free of any mind. Fine, we all nodded along.

Then someone asked about cancer, and it went downhill. Here's the argument as we ended up with it:

(1) A trait has a free-floating rationale when the function the trait was selected for can be stated as a reason for having it — i.e., "the reason gazelles stot is to tell predators not to bother."
(2) Whenever natural selection (broadly, including selection at any level) spreads a trait because of what the trait does, condition (1) is met.
(3) Cancer cells ignore the body's self-destruct signals and hijack blood supply, and within the lineage of tumor cells, these traits spread precisely because of what they do for cell replication. That's selection spreading a trait because of what it does.
(4) So by (2), there is a free-floating rationale for ignoring apoptosis signals and becoming cancerous.
(5) The same goes for every gruesome parasite adaptation — the wasp that paralyzes a caterpillar alive so its larvae can eat it fresh has a free-floating rationale for doing exactly that.
(6) A concept of "rationale" that hands one to cancer and to parasitoid wasps exactly as it hands one to eyes and wings is doing no real work — it just relabels "has a selection history." If everything shaped by selection gets a rationale, then "having a rationale" can't explain what was supposed to be interesting about stotting.
(7) Therefore either (6) is false and we bite the bullet, or free-floating rationales are just our retrospective glosses and the notion should be retired.

Half the group is fine biting the bullet: yes, there's a rationale for the cancer cell's behavior, and nobody ever said rationales have to be flattering or good for the organism — the rationale serves the trait's own propagation, not the deer's welfare either, really. The other half says obviously cancer has no rationale, so something broke earlier, probably a sneaky slide between "a reason why the trait spread" and "a reason for the organism to do it." I can feel that there's an equivocation somewhere in (1) and (2), but I can't make it clean, because the bullet-biters keep asking: if you say the stot has a rationale but the apoptosis-evasion doesn't, what draws that line? Levels of selection? Whose good the reason is a reason for? Whether there's an "audience" being signalled to? None of these obviously work.
