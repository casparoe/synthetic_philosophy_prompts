For about two years now I have been arguing with my brother-in-law about a single question: are machines and devices politically neutral until people use them badly, or can the design of a thing settle a political question all on its own? I never went to university — I inspect freight elevators for a living — but I have kept notes on the argument, and since I now help moderate an online forum of hobbyist engineers and industrial designers, I would like to turn the argument into a sorting quiz titled "Which view of technology and politics do you actually hold?" The idea is that members should discover their position from how they judge concrete cases, not by picking the label they like the sound of.

Please write the quiz for me: ten questions, each a short concrete case with two or three answer options, and a scoring key at the end that maps patterns of answers onto the positions below.

The cases must be phrased as judgments about the case itself, never as slogans or self-descriptions, so that someone can answer without knowing any position's name. I insist that the following situations appear among the ten (you may compress or reword them, but not drop them):

1. A nuclear power station: it seems to demand centralized ownership, armed guards, and secrecy about its workings. Does the machine itself require that political shape, or only the institutions people chose to wrap around it?
2. A park bench built with armrests every meter and a half so that nobody can lie down on it.
3. A restaurant that has replaced its paper menus with QR codes, and a subway system whose turnstiles and gates are sized and priced in ways that quietly assume every rider is an able-bodied adult with a smartphone.
4. A technology that has served opposite political masters: the mobile phone used both by dissidents to organize and by authoritarian governments to track them.
5. A design change imposed by law rather than chosen by a designer — curb cuts at every corner, or a government mandating one charging connector for all phones.
6. A case that runs the wrong direction, which I have never seen anyone discuss properly: a city council decides what kind of society it wants — say, neighbors who actually meet — and then bans or mandates artifacts to bring that society about, for instance by barring private cars from the center of town. Here the politics plainly came first and the artifact second. I want a question about whether the artifact nonetheless "has" politics once installed, or whether this is the one situation where the tool really is just a tool.
7. Speed bumps: a street that enforces its own speed limit by physics, with no officer and no ticket.
8. A contrast between politics that is baked into a device and politics that expires with it — the armrested bench torn out and replaced versus, say, a road network that can only be undone at enormous cost and over decades.

The positions the scoring key must be able to sort for are these, described as I have come to understand them from my own thinking; you may supply their proper names if such names exist:

- The tool view: artifacts are neutral, and all politics lies in users and institutions.
- The strong view: certain technologies force certain social forms no matter what anyone intends, and the choice of machine is really the choice of society.
- A softer version of that: technologies do not force anything, but they bias the world — they make some arrangements easy and cheap and others expensive and awkward, and that gradient does most of the work.
- The designed-politics view: an artifact can embody a political decision made by its designer, and that decision persists even when every later user is perfectly innocent.
- A fifth view my brother-in-law keeps retreating to: that the politics is never in the artifact but only in the whole situation of its adoption, so even the armrested bench is political solely through the city government that ordered it, and the same bench on a private patio would be nothing of the kind.

The scoring key should say which patterns of answers place a person at which position, and it must do two further things. First, flag any pair of positions the ten questions cannot genuinely separate, rather than pretending they come apart. Second, note which combinations of answers in your logical space correspond to no position anyone actually holds, and say briefly whether you think nobody is there because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because no one has thought their way into it yet.

Please do not include citations, reading lists, or references to books and articles — our members would not follow them and I would not be able to check them. Keep any technical vocabulary to terms you explain in plain language as you go.
