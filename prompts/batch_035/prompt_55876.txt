My favorite philosophy podcast did an episode on pragmatic encroachment and I've been turning it over ever since. The host's summary, roughly (paraphrasing from memory): pragmatic encroachment, which is basically the same thing as epistemic contextualism, is the view that whether you know something depends on what's at stake for you. The go-to example is the bank case — you drive past the bank on Friday, and if nothing much rides on it, you know it'll be open Saturday morning; but if you'd face some disaster by being wrong, the very same evidence suddenly isn't knowledge anymore. This goes back to contextualists like Jason Stanley, who argued that 'knows' works like an indexical — it shifts its meaning with the stakes the same way 'I' shifts with the speaker. So knowledge is interest-relative: it comes and goes as your practical situation changes, even though your evidence and your confidence never budge. And the host's kicker was that since there's always something at stake in real life, clean, context-free knowledge basically never happens — everyday knowing is more like a credit score, something that gets adjusted depending on how risky you're being.

Some of this bugs me but I can't tell if it's the view that's weird or the summary. The knowledge-coming-and-going bit in particular sounds like a caricature — surely knowing isn't like a lightswitch you flip by worrying harder? Is pragmatic encroachment really just another name for contextualism, or are those two different moves that happen to care about the same examples? Does the indexical part fit either one?
