For the past year or so I've been recording my honest reactions to cases about how much weight the distant future should have in our decisions, and I'd like help working out what principle, if any, actually fits them. A research program that would reduce this century's chance of human extinction by a hundredth of a percentage point strikes me as clearly more important than a health program saving five thousand lives now, and not marginally so. Yet when the same risk reduction could only be bought with a policy that sharply cut living standards for a decade, I find myself unwilling to pay, even though any straightforward arithmetic says the future gains dwarf the present losses. A project that would delay extinction by exactly one generation — ten billion people live decent lives, then humanity ends anyway — seems to me worth far less than saving ten billion people within a civilization that continues, even though the number of decent lives preserved is the same. A choice that merely changes which particular people will exist later, with everyone living a good life under either option, seems morally indifferent to me even when one option contains many more good lives. And although the thought that the future could contain trillions of people moves me, I can't bring myself to care about humanity's potential in a scenario where I've stipulated that no later generation will ever live better than ours does. Is there a single principle behind these reactions — concerning who can be benefited, how tiny probabilities and long delays should be handled, and what exactly is valuable about there being more future — and if not, which of my verdicts should I give up, and in what order? Please also say what the surviving principle recommends on a case I haven't mentioned, such as funding asteroid detection rather than present-day aid. Finally, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present one side of an ongoing argument as the consensus view.
