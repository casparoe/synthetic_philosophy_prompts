Hey, quick bit of context before the ask. I teach an upper-level undergrad course on punishment theory, mixed philosophy and law students, mostly 3rd/4th year. The final is a 90-minute in-class essay on an unseen question, worth 20 marks. I'm tired of compare-and-contrast prompts where anyone who memorized the textbook can coast -- you know the genre, "Explain the differences between retributivism and consequentialist theories of punishment and evaluate each." Every student writes the same paragraph about desert versus deterrence and I genuinely can't tell them apart afterwards.

So here's what I want instead. One essay question built around a pair of cases, roughly these (tweak them if you can think of sharper versions):

Case A: a murderer, convicted beyond any doubt, is already dying -- he'll be dead within the month no matter what we do. Punishing him accomplishes literally nothing going forward: no deterrence (nobody will ever even know the punishment happened), no incapacitation (he's bedridden), no rehabilitation, no message sent to anyone. Do we punish him?

Case B: same man, same crime, same certainty of guilt -- but this time punishing him would cause real forward-looking harm, say it would trigger riots, or eat up enormous resources that would otherwise go to crime prevention measures that would save actual lives. May we simply decline to punish him?

The question has to force students to notice that intuitions pull in opposite directions across the two cases and then figure out what each theory says about that. More specifically, it should get at what the desert claim is actually DOING in each theory -- is it a necessary condition on justified punishment, a sufficient reason to punish, a goal we're maximizing, a side constraint? In my experience a student who can't say what role desert is playing hasn't understood the theory at all, even if they can recite every definition perfectly.

Please give me, in this order and with these rough lengths:

1. "The Question" -- the exam question text itself, around 120 words max
2. "Model Answer" -- 550-700 words, written the way a genuinely top student would write under exam conditions (not a polished journal article, and no citations or named authors -- I want to see the reasoning, not the bibliography)
3. "Marking Scheme" -- five must-hit points, marks for each adding up to 20
4. "Where B Answers Go Wrong" -- one or two lines each, on the specific mistakes that separate a B from an A

Last thing: I'd like the marking scheme to leave room for students who bring in named views from the literature (censure theories, hybrid theories, whatever) as a way to earn marks, but it shouldn't be the only route to a top mark -- the question has to be fully answerable by someone who only attended my course and did the readings I assigned, without name-dropping.
