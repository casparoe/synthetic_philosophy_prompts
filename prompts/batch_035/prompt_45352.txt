so I've ended up with a package of views on whether the earlier self gets to boss the later self around, and I want to know if it actually hangs together before I have to use it in real life. background, because it matters: my dad has Lewy body dementia, I'm the one holding his advance directive (he wrote it years before the diagnosis, after reading the same blog posts about cryonics and psychological continuity that I did), and in my spare time I argue about Parfit in the kinds of forums where arguing about Parfit is normal. between the two of us, my dad and I have assembled the following, and I genuinely can't tell if it's one position or two positions wearing a trenchcoat:

(1) advance directives bind in the strong sense — if the competent earlier self writes no feeding tube in advanced dementia, that settles it, and not because it's a useful prediction of what the later self will want, but because it's his call, an exercise of authority over his own life. (2) that authority comes from autonomy, and specifically from what Dworkin calls critical interests — the enduring, character-level values about who you want to have been — not from moment-to-moment experiential interests like comfort or pleasant afternoons. (3) at the same time I hold the Parfitian line that what matters in persistence is psychological connectedness, and I take seriously the thought that by late stage he and the person in the chair might not be the same person in the sense that matters. (4) but the current resident's welfare counts directly for me: he's placid these days, loves music, hates being poked at, and I want any decision about the next intervention to weigh how it actually lands on him now. (5) I'm firm that the answer to hard cases is not whatever the family finds least upsetting and not whatever the doctor defaults to. and (6) I think a directive can be set aside when its factual assumptions just don't apply — like if it was written assuming a hopeless prognosis and the actual condition turns out treatable — but not merely because the current patient would have consented to what it forbids.

various people in my life keep telling me different parts of this are in tension and they don't agree with each other about which, and honestly I can't tell which commitments are load-bearing and which only look like they're fighting. like, is there a real problem between (1)+(2) and (3), if the person the directive governs isn't, by my own lights, the person who wrote it? do (2) and (4) actually conflict, or is there a clean division of labor between the questions the directive answers and the questions present-tense experience answers? is (6) compatible with (1)'s strong sense of "settles it," or is that a contradiction I've talked myself out of noticing? go through the set, rank whatever tensions you find by severity rather than flagging everything equally, say which ones dissolve on inspection, and for the real ones tell me what the cheapest revision is and what it costs. I'd rather fix the package than own a tidy theory, but I also don't want to quietly give up the one thing that makes the whole exercise mean anything. typos above are mine, dont read anything into them.
