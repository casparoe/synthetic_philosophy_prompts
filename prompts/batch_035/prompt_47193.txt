Around the 2008 financial crisis, macroeconomics took a well-publicized beating. The workhorse DSGE models — a representative household, rational expectations, financial frictions largely assumed away, the whole economy described as small shocks around a steady state — had warned nobody, and critics inside the discipline and out located the failure precisely in those unrealistic assumptions. The familiar defense, when one was offered, had a Friedmanian shape: the unrealism of a model's assumptions is no objection at all; assumptions are to be judged solely by the predictions they generate. That makes the episode look like a live test of the F-twist, and I would like it analyzed as one — carefully, and in both directions.

Map the case onto the doctrine. What counts as the prediction, in Friedman's sense, when these models were used less to forecast than to run counterfactual policy experiments and to underwrite the complacency of the Great Moderation? Do the canonical examples transfer — the expert billiard player who plays as if he had solved the physics, the tree whose leaves are placed as if to maximize exposure to sunlight — given that here the idealization deleted the very mechanisms, leverage and heterogeneity, in which the crisis actually lived? And did the episode refute the F-twist, or did it show that the F-twist was never the defense the modelers needed, since Friedman's standard presupposes a predictive track record, and these models had never delivered one?

Then run the analysis back the other way. What does the episode teach about the doctrine itself: does it survive, and if so with what added condition separating harmless idealization from idealization that assumes away the phenomenon of interest? Where the F-twist is silent on this case, say so plainly rather than stretching it to speak. Throughout, indicate how confident you are in each major claim, and single out the one point about which you are least sure.
