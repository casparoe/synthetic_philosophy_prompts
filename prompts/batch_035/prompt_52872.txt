I'm co-teaching a reading group on personal identity that spends its final three weeks on Buddhist no-self, and I'd like to close the last session with a poem rather than a summary — the discussion always gets abstract when we talk about the aggregates, and I want something the students can sit with. My idea is a sestina, and I want the form to do philosophical work rather than just decorate the theme. You are a formalist poet with graduate training in Buddhist philosophy, so please bring both halves of that to the task, and start with a few paragraphs of private brainstorming before committing to any lines.

The six repeating end-words should be the five aggregates plus one more: render rūpa, vedanā, saññā, saṅkhāra, and viññāṇa as plain single words at line-end (form or body; feeling; perception; something for the formations, which is the hardest — habit, or the wanting, whatever earns its place), and let the sixth end-word be self. The hope is that the sestina's fixed spiral — the same six words returning each stanza in a new order, taking on a new sense each time from the lines around them — enacts the orthodox claim that what you call a person is just the aggregates recombining moment to moment, with nothing over and above them. So the recurring words should genuinely be made new by their new contexts, the way the aggregates are said to arise dependently rather than persist. And the word self should recur like the others but never pick anything out: in each stanza, read on its own, it should seem to point at whichever aggregate it happens to stand next to, so that by the final stanza the attentive reader notices it has been borrowing a referent all along. For the envoi, use three of the six end-words as tradition requires, and let self be one of them — but as the word for what is only a designation over the parts, in the spirit of the chariot analogy from the dialogue between King Milinda and the monk Nagasena, which you may gesture toward without quoting any translation.

Constraints on execution: the poem should be accurate enough that a specialist in Pali Buddhist thought would find it right rather than merely name-dropped, and clear enough that a student who missed the last three weeks still gets a good poem; avoid both sermon and textbook. No verbatim borrowing from any sutta or translation, and no Pali terms except possibly the five end-words themselves if you think they help — explain briefly after the poem how you chose each end-word and how the envoi resolves the sixth. When you're done, check your spiral permutation against the standard sestina order before you present the final version.
