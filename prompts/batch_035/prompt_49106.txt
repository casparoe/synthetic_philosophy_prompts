I'm a novelist working on a near-future book where two old friends, both ex-neuroscience researchers, reunite at a private clinic where one of them is about to undergo an experimental procedure: a permanently implanted device that directly stimulates the reward circuitry, tuned to feel like a continuous, low-grade but utterly stable satisfaction (the engineers colloquially call it "parking," as in parking the mind somewhere pleasant). She's signing the consent forms; he's come to talk her out of it one last time. I want their final conversation as the closing scene of the chapter.

Could you write this as a dialogue between the two of them? Her position, roughly: she's read enough reinforcement learning literature to know that "reward" in a trained system is just a signal that evolution or the engineers happened to use as a proxy, and she thinks human desires are no different — our everyday goals are satisfactions we chase because they ping the signal, so cutting out the middleman and going straight to the signal isn't a corruption of what she values, it's the honest version of it. She can also point out that "reward tampering" is only called a failure when the designers' goals diverge from the agent's, and in her case she IS the designer — there's no one to be misaligned from. His position, roughly: she's smuggling in an assumption that she, now, pre-procedure, is in a position to make a binding deal with her future self, when the whole point of the device is that it will edit the very machinery that made the deal; also that stable satisfaction forever involves giving up the contrast and striving that made anything feel like anything.

Both should be smart and neither should cave — I want real back-and-forth where they actually engage with each other's arguments, and if she lands some genuine hits on him (he takes antidepressants, after all, which is already a mild version of her move) let her. End the scene unresolved, with them still at the clinic, the question of whether she goes through with it left hanging. Medium length, natural speech, no lecture-y monologues please.
