For a school project I am trying to work through Kant's discussion of our treatment of animals in his Lectures on Ethics, and there is a passage I cannot make sense of, so I would be grateful for help.

In the passage, Kant considers a man who shoots his dog once the dog is too old to be of service. Kant says the man does not fail in any duty to the dog, because the dog cannot judge, but that the act is nonetheless inhuman and damages in the man himself that humanity which it is his duty to show toward mankind. Nearby, Kant adds that a person who is cruel to animals will become hard in his dealings with human beings, and so kindness to animals is really an exercise of our duties to humanity.

There are three things I am struggling with. First, what work is the claim that the dog cannot judge supposed to do? Why would the capacity to judge be what makes a being owed duties in the first place, rather than, say, the capacity to feel pain? Second, I do not understand how an act can be morally wrong if it wrongs nobody. Kant says nothing is owed to the dog itself, and no human is harmed, so what exactly is being damaged, and how can I damage humanity in myself? Is humanity supposed to be some kind of property I carry around, like a reputation, that I can erode even when no one is watching? Third, and this is the part that bothers me most: if cruelty to animals is wrong only because it tends to make us crueler toward people, what should Kant say about someone who is cruel to animals in private and is genuinely certain that it will never spill over into how he treats anyone? It seems Kant's own reasoning gives that person no reason to stop, which feels like the wrong answer, but I cannot tell whether this is a real problem for Kant or whether I am misreading him — for instance, whether the claim about becoming hard toward humans is meant as a rough psychological generalization or as the actual grounding of the duty.

Please explain the passage with those three questions in mind, and at the end tell me directly whether you think the private-cruelty case exposes a genuine gap in Kant's view or whether he has the resources to answer it. Please keep your answer under 400 words.
