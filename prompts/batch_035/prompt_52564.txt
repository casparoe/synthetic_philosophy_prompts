Which of them, if either, poses the deeper threat to the platonist, and why?"

The candidate writes:

"Paul Benacerraf posed two challenges to platonism. The first appears in his 1973 paper 'What Numbers Could Not Be' and concerns the attempt to identify the natural numbers with pure sets. Suppose we identify 0 with the empty set and each successor n+1 with {n}: these are the Zermelo ordinals. Alternatively we identify each number n with the set of its predecessors: the von Neumann ordinals. Both reductions preserve every arithmetical truth. Ernie, taught the first identification, and Johnny, taught the second, will agree about every sum, every prime, every proof, and neither can be faulted mathematically. Yet they will disagree about set-theoretic questions such as whether 1 is a member of 2. Since there is no principled way to privilege one identification over the other, and since identity cannot be settled by stipulation, there is no fact of the matter about which sets the numbers are. And the argument generalises: if nothing settles the identity of the numbers, then there is no fact of the matter about arithmetical identity statements generally, and arithmetic truth is indeterminate. The platonist, who insists on determinate truths about numbers, is therefore refuted. The natural moral is that numbers are not objects at all but positions in a structure — though Benacerraf's own positive proposal was that numbers are human constructions, kept useful by convention.

The second challenge, from 'Mathematical Truth' (1965), is epistemological. Benacerraf argued that all knowledge is causally mediated: we know about tables because light reflected from them reaches our eyes, whereas numbers, being abstract, are causally inert, so if platonism is true we could know nothing about them — yet we plainly know a great deal. The challenge has two horns: an account of mathematical truth should both give mathematics the same semantics as the rest of language, so that '2 < 3' is true in the same way 'the cat is on the mat' is true, and mesh with our best epistemology. Platonism satisfies the first horn but cannot satisfy the second.

Of the two, the epistemological challenge is the deeper, because the identification problem is really just a special case of it: if we cannot know which sets the numbers are, we cannot know anything about numbers at all. The identification problem is thus derivative, and the platonist's real enemy is epistemic access."
