I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and this fall I am delivering the keynote at a symposium marking the intellectual legacy of the Society for Psychical Research, which was founded in 1882 with the philosopher Henry Sidgwick as its first president. My thesis is deliberately double-edged. First, that Sidgwick's years of labor for the Society — including the massive Census of Hallucinations of 1894, in which roughly seventeen thousand people were surveyed about apparitional experiences and about 1,684 reported one — were not an eccentric detour from the author of The Methods of Ethics but its working-out: the celebrated gap he identified between the standpoint of duty and the standpoint of the agent's own good left him needing a universe in which morality and happiness ultimately converge, and he treated the survival of personality after death as an empirical hypothesis that had to be tested rather than assumed or dismissed. Second, and this is the riskier claim, I will argue that the Census deserves a modest but real place in the history of survey methodology, alongside early epidemiology, for its attempt to establish a statistical baseline for an ordinary class of human experience that medicine of the time wrote off as pathology. The audience will be mixed and partly hostile: historians of Victorian science who regard psychical research as a professional embarrassment their field has spent a century living down; utilitarianism scholars who know the Methods inside out but consider the psychic material beneath notice; a delegation of present-day Society members who will welcome the praise of Sidgwick but will bristle at any suggestion that the evidence never came in; and a contingent from a national skeptics' organization. Please prepare my question-and-answer sheet as a memo I can read on the train: the eight or so objections I will actually face, ordered by how much damage they could do, each phrased in the vocabulary of the group likely to raise it, each with a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, and a judgment on what I should concede rather than contest. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Write the whole thing in American English, and please render it as continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a long letter.
