I teach a world-history survey, and I've reached the point where I have to decide whether to keep using the word "collapse" in the course or retire it in favor of something like "reorganization." Before deciding, I want the specification first: what would an adequate theory of societal collapse have to deliver? The cases it must handle are the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Terminal Classic Maya lowlands, and Easter Island, and I want the requirements to come out of the ways these cases pull against each other: palace economies unraveling while populations largely persist and new states eventually form; lowland Maya city-states abandoned over a century and a half while millions of Maya descendants continue to this day; and a revisionist literature arguing that Easter Islanders were managing their environment sustainably and that the real damage came with contact rather than self-inflicted ecocide. The theory should specify what exactly collapses (a political system, a population level, a way of life?), say something causal without reducing to climate determinism or a single mechanism like Joseph Tainter's diminishing marginal returns on complexity, and it must neither imply that the people vanished nor become so elastic that no episode counts as failure. Please say which desiderata are non-negotiable, which are prejudices of particular camps, which pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement does the most to exclude contenders. Finish with a brief scorecard for Tainter-style complexity accounts, Diamond-style environmental narratives, and the resilience/continuity school — brief, since the criteria are what I most want to see.
