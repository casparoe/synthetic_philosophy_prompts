I am grading a midterm for an upper-level undergraduate course on judgment and decision making, and I would like help grading one answer. Below I give the exam question as it appeared on the paper, followed by the student's answer, and then what I need from you.

The exam question:

Question 4 (15 points). At a fundraising event, an auctioneer auctions a one-dollar bill under Martin Shubik's (1971) rules: bidding proceeds in five-cent increments; the highest bidder pays the amount of the final bid and receives the dollar; the second-highest bidder must also pay the amount of their final bid but receives nothing.

(a) Explain why, once two bidders are actively competing, each bidder has an incentive to raise rather than fold, and determine how far these incentives can justify a bidder's raises — in particular, whether a bidder who is currently behind should ever bid more than the prize is worth.

(b) Explain why bidders who are commonly known to be rational, and known to know this of one another, might nevertheless decline to place a bid at all.

(c) Distinguish the phenomenon that organizational psychologists, following Staw (1976), call escalation of commitment from the sunk-cost fallacy, and identify one mechanism, other than simple irrationality, by which real organizations come to exhibit escalation.

The student's answer:

(a) The danger comes entirely from the runner-up rule. Suppose the bidding stands at 85 cents against me and 80 cents for me. If I fold, my 80 cents is gone and I get nothing. If I raise to 90, then either my rival folds, in which case I pay 90 cents for a dollar and finish 10 cents up, or he raises and the same comparison recurs with the roles reversed. The correct analysis is the standard one for decisions involving sunk costs: the amounts already bid are gone no matter what I do, so they should not influence my choice. What matters is only whether the next bid is less than the value of the prize. A rational bidder therefore keeps raising as long as the next bid stays below one dollar, and folds as soon as the required bid reaches or passes it; bidding more than the prize is worth would be honoring sunk costs, which is precisely the error this auction is designed to catch.

(b) If it is common knowledge that everyone is rational, then each bidder can predict the others, and the auction unravels before it starts. Anyone who bids must expect that someone else will bid after them, since overbidding remains profitable for the second bidder as long as the next bid is under a dollar; and once two bids are in, neither bidder can exit without losing their last bid. Each can foresee that the runner-up rule will eventually push the total paid past the value of the prize, so no rational bidder is willing to open, and the auctioneer should expect to collect nothing. In effect this is backward induction applied to the decision to enter.

(c) Escalation of commitment is just the sunk-cost fallacy at the level of organizations: it is the name organizational psychologists use for the same error when it appears in firms, as when a company keeps funding a failing project because so much has already been spent on it. The reason real organizations fall into it is the same reason individuals do: managers are human, and human decision-makers irrationally honor sunk costs.
