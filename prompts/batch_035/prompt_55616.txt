I'm taking a graduate seminar on concepts and categorization, and it has left me about sixty percent persuaded by the Hofstadter-and-Sander line that analogy-making isn't a special reasoning trick deployed on IQ tests but the engine of ordinary thought -- that even seeing a new situation as an instance of an old category is already an act of analogy, so that fluid concepts, metaphor comprehension, and clever puzzle solutions are all the same machinery running at different speeds. I want to write my term paper arguing for or against that picture, but before I evaluate any particular theory I want to be sure I'm asking the right things of one, because I suspect the whole debate is being run on a mix of genuine requirements and prejudices of whichever camp is talking. So please write the desiderata: what an adequate theory of categorization and analogy must deliver, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement, and a flag on which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of one research tradition.

Here are the cases I keep returning to, which should each generate or test a requirement. (1) A big mosquito is not big, and a small elephant is not small: adjectives get silently re-scaled to the category they are applied to, and the same fluidity shows up when a metaphor like my lawyer is a shark conveys predatory rather than aquatic. The theory has to explain how word meanings stretch to fit context without becoming arbitrary. (2) Categorization is graded and purpose-relative: a tomato is a vegetable at dinner and a fruit in botany class, a whale is a fish to a five-year-old and a mammal on the exam, and I don't think either verdict is simply a mistake. (3) We form categories with one member -- summer when I was nine -- and even arguably empty ones -- the career I gave up -- and these guide real expectations and emotions, so a theory that only handles natural kinds with many instances is not a theory of our concepts. (4) The letter-string puzzles Hofstadter's group used: told that abc changes to abd, everyone answers ijl for ijk, but for xyz people start improvising -- wrap the z around to an a, slide the whole string back, declare the question malformed -- and which answer feels right seems to depend on how abstractly one mapped the original transformation onto the new case. A theory should say something about which answers are better and why, or about why that question is ill-posed. (5) Speed and ubiquity: these mappings surface continuously and in milliseconds, so a theory that models each analogy as an expensive explicit search over all prior experiences gets the phenomenon wrong at the scale where it actually lives. (6) Compositionality and productivity: speakers understand sentences they have never heard, and understanding a novel sentence seems to proceed by parts, which looks like a different engine than the fluid, context-driven, all-analogy picture -- I'd like to know whether a single theory can honor both.

The part I most want to see is the joint-satisfiability analysis. In particular: can the analogy-all-the-way-down claim coexist with the compositionality requirement, or does one have to give? And is there any principled escape from the regress worry -- if every act of categorization is itself an analogy to prior situations, analogy to what, exactly, and what keeps the story from circling? Please mark any subset of the desiderata you believe cannot be jointly met, and say which of my six cases you think are load-bearing and which are red herrings. If it helps to close the loop, give a brief scorecard of the classical, definition-based view of concepts, prototype theory, and the analogy-centric picture against your list -- but keep that to a paragraph, since the criteria are the main event.
