I run a little decision-theory-ish blog and want to build a "which Sleeping Beauty position do you actually hold?" quiz. Not for experts — my readers mostly know the puzzle and maybe the words halfer/thirder, but they won't know the taxonomy. So the whole point is to sort them by what they say about cases, before they've picked a team. That means: no question can just ask "are you a halfer?", every question has to be a concrete scenario where the positions genuinely come apart.

Please write:

(a) The quiz: 9 questions, each a concrete case with 2-3 answer options, phrased so someone can answer without knowing where the options lead. These cases must appear among them — I'm told these do the real sorting work:

1. The plain case, asked as what Beauty should say out loud on waking, before any fancy framing.
2. Elga's technicolor setup: Beauty is woken in a room with red curtains on one possible waking and blue curtains on the other, so one glance tells her which day-style she's in. Her answer there is where halfers and double-halfers split — use it accordingly.
3. The duplication variant: on tails, instead of waking the original twice, they scan her on Monday night and wake a copy on Tuesday (and she can't tell she's the copy). Some people's intuitions flip here, others don't — the question should catch that.
4. A version where the coin is tossed a second time on Monday night, so tails leads to a million awakenings. Does her heads-credence shrink toward zero? This one pressures halfers who refuse to move at all.
5. A betting case: a bet offered to her at every awakening that pays out on heads, once. What counts as "fair" for the bet — and here's the subtle thing I want a question to catch: some halfers will say her credence is 1/2 but she should still accept terms that look like thirder odds, because the bet gets offered twice on tails. So include an option that combines "credence 1/2" with "I'd still take the thirder-favorable bet."
6. A bridge-to-anthropics question: does her answer in the lab commit her to anything about, say, reasoning from how many people exist in worlds where a coin created few or many observers? Some want to firewall the lab case from anthropics entirely; the question should separate people who accept the linkage from people who deny it.

The remaining 3 questions are yours to design from the logical space — I'd like at least one about whether the awakening itself is new relevant evidence (the classic Lewis-style "she learned nothing" move vs. its denial), and one that can catch readers whose real view is that the whole dispute is partly verbal (two different questions being run together under one phrase).

(b) The scoring key: map answer patterns onto positions. Must appear as labeled outcomes: thirder, plain halfer, double-halfer, the halfer-who-bets-like-a-thirder, an imprecise-credence outcome (interval, not a point value), an anthropics-linked outcome, and the deflationary "the question was ambiguous" outcome. Build the key from the combinations first and then locate the named positions in it, like I said above. Where a combination of answers is one nobody would coherently give, say so briefly and guess why (incoherent vs. just unexplored). And tell me honestly which leaves the quiz can't separate — I already suspect halfers and double-halfers will only split on the technicolor question, and if that's the only separator, I want the key to admit it rather than pretend otherwise.

One more thing, and it's the part I care most about: after the key, include a short note on where the mapping is most likely to fail — which question-to-position assignments you're least confident in, and what would change your mind about them. Like, "if I'm wrong that answer-pattern X tracks the double-halfer rather than the plain halfer, it'd probably be because ..." That way I know which parts of the key to trust and which to test on readers before publishing. Dont sandbag it with hedges everywhere, just point at the genuine soft spots.
