I'm taking a graduate seminar on free will and writing my term paper on whether historical figures' positions can absorb arguments that postdate them — whether their views have the resources to respond, or whether the later developments expose structural weaknesses that were already there. My case study is Kant, and I'd like your help thinking it through, since the secondary literature I've found tends to either ignore this or assume the answer.

As I read him, Kant's position is more complicated than the "freedom versus determinism" headline suggests. In the Third Antinomy he assigns each agent an empirical character that falls entirely under causal laws and an intelligible character whose spontaneity is somehow outside time altogether, and in the practical works he seems comfortable treating the deterministic description of action as complete at the phenomenal level while still insisting on freedom for the purposes of morality and responsibility. Now suppose we could sit him down and present two developments he never lived to see: first, Libet-style experiments in which a readiness potential apparently precedes the conscious registration of an intention, and which are routinely invoked in neuroscientific challenges to free will; second, manipulation arguments of the sort Pereboom later developed, in which an agent who is covertly designed or manipulated to act exactly as deterministic laws would have made him act anyway is intuitively no more responsible than an ordinarily determined agent. Would either of these actually trouble him, given his architecture? My instinct is that Libet is an easy case for him — everything about the experimental setup lives squarely in the phenomenal realm he never expected to be free — but I'm less sure about the manipulation arguments, since those seem designed precisely to put pressure on the thought that a "timeless" origin of action could ground responsibility when the agent's entire motivational profile is causally fixed by something else.

So here is what I'd like from you. First, reconstruct what Kant's resources actually let him say to each challenge — be explicit about which parts of his system you're drawing on, and flag honestly where you're extrapolating beyond what he wrote. Second, tell me whether he would side with Frankfurtians on the principle of alternate possibilities, or whether his conception of origination requires genuine access to alternatives in a way that puts him closer to the incompatibilists — I keep going back and forth on this, since practical freedom seems to be about the will's structure rather than about options. Third, and most importantly for my paper, tell me where the real weak point is: if transcendental freedom can't be touched by any experiment or any manipulation case because it's defined to be outside the causal order, then it's unclear what explanatory work it does for responsibility at all, and whether that makes it invulnerable or empty. Please don't give me a general survey of the free will debate — I want Kant's machinery doing the work throughout. End with a verdict on which of the two challenges would genuinely worry him more, and why, given his specific two-standpoint position rather than generic compatibilist or libertarian resources.
