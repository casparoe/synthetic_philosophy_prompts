My former moral philosophy tutor has firm views about the difference between lying and merely misleading, and over the years I've collected her rulings on a series of cases: (1) a witness who denies having seen the defendant on the night in question, though he did: a lie, and seriously wrong; (2) a referee who writes a letter of recommendation consisting of true but damning praise, hoping the reader infers that the candidate is incompetent: not a lie, though wrong; (3) a father who tells his son "I've told you a million times," along with other obvious hyperbole and sarcasm: neither a lie nor wrong; (4) parents who tell their small child that Santa Claus comes down the chimney: a lie, though not wrong; (5) a homeowner who, asked by an intruder where the family keeps its valuables, silently points toward the train station: wrong, and she says she would call it lying too. She has now been asked about a sixth case: a club treasurer caught on video taking money from the till who, at a meeting where every member has seen the footage, looks them in the eye and says "I never took any money" — knowing full well that no one present will believe him, but refusing to give the members an admission on the record. What will she say about whether this is a lie, and about how wrong it is? Please set out the candidate rules that could fit her first five rulings, say where those rules diverge on the sixth case, give your best prediction of her verdict together with your reasons for preferring that rule, and describe a further case that would best tell the remaining candidate rules apart.
