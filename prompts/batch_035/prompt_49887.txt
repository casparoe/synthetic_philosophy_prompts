My book club is doing a month of Dr. Seuss, and I got assigned to bring discussion questions for The Sneetches. Lately I've been reading about multipolar traps — the idea, associated with Scott Alexander's well-known blog essay on Moloch and the general "race to the bottom" literature, that whenever many agents compete, the selection pressure optimizes for whatever wins the competition rather than for what's actually good, so participants end up worse off than if they'd somehow coordinated, and the benefits leak out to whoever is positioned outside the competition. You don't need to have read the essay — the idea is just what I described. And it struck me that the Sneetches story is almost a textbook case: plain-bellied Sneetches are excluded from frankfurter roasts and bonfire parties, so they pay Sylvester McMonkey McBean to stamp stars on their bellies, then the original star-bellies pay him to take theirs off, back and forth, until every Sneetch is broke and nobody can tell who's who, while McBean drives away rich.

So read the story through this lens for me. Where does it genuinely dramatize the multipolar trap, and where does it cheat? The two cheats I can already see: real races to the bottom don't usually terminate just because the participants run out of money (the trap finds new fuel), and the story is resolved by a preference change — the Sneetches simply stop caring about stars — rather than by any coordination mechanism, which feels too easy, like the trap lets go the moment anyone wants out. Does the story know the difference? Is the happy ending earned, or is it Seussian wish-fulfillment? And a question I keep going back and forth on: is McBean supposed to be Moloch, or is he better read as something the Moloch framework doesn't really have a name for — the external parasite who feeds the trap and profits from keeping it going? Also worth asking whether the moral about stars being a purely positional good — valuable only because others lack them — is the philosophically interesting bit, and whether noticing that is a genuine escape route from races to the bottom or only works for goods you can simply stop wanting.

Which scenes would you point the club to, and is there something the story gets right that the abstract theory misses? Please write the answer in American English, and feel free to bring in other Seussian bits (the ending, with its bit about no one being snooty on the beaches) if they help.
