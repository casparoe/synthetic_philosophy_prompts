You are a professional philosopher specializing in metaphysics and philosophy of language, with particular expertise in the problem of the unity of the proposition, Bradley's regress, and contemporary fact-ontology. Please think carefully and systematically: before committing to a diagnosis, brainstorm the available escape routes, even unorthodox ones, and then evaluate them against the argument below.

Context: I'm a graduate student in a reading group that has been arguing about whether facts have constituents. I assembled the following argument from things group members said, and it now strikes me as proving far too much — if it worked, it would destroy not just the unity of the proposition but apparently all predication, all structure, and all relations whatsoever, including the relations my own semantic theory needs to state the argument in the first place. I cannot find the flaw, though I have my suspicions about one step in particular.

The argument:

1. A sentence is capable of truth or falsity only because its parts are unified: the constituents of the proposition it expresses do not merely sit side by side like items in a list, but hang together as a single claim.
2. Whatever unifies a plurality must relate its members to one another. Call whatever does this the tie.
3. Whatever relates two or more things must itself stand, for each of them, in a relation to it: a tie ties the constituents only if the tie is related to each constituent.
4. If the tie's being related to a constituent is itself something that needs relating, then a further tie is required, and this applies again to that tie, without end.
5. At no finite stage is the original proposition ever put together, and an infinite string of ties none of which completes the job does not complete the job either.
6. So the constituents of any proposition are never unified, and no proposition is ever capable of truth or falsity.
7. By parity of reasoning, nothing is ever related to anything: predication, exemplification, spatial and temporal arrangement, the reference of words — none of it ever gets off the ground.

Conclusion: there are no unified propositions and no genuine relations. This is self-undermining — the argument's own premises are supposed to stand in inferential relations to one another — and I refuse to accept it.

A few constraints and complications:

- I want to keep (1). If someone told me sentences express mere aggregates, I'd want to know what the aggregate of {Socrates, wise} is true in virtue of, and I don't see how they can answer without smuggling a tie back in. So my instinct is that the trouble is somewhere in (2) through (5), but each of those looks like a platitude to me. (3) in particular seems hard to deny: how could something relate to a thing without being related to it?
- A friend of mine in the linguistics department claims formal semantics dodges the whole problem: on her story, predication is function application, and applying a function to an argument is not relating two objects but computing a value, so there is no tie to be tied to anything. I'm unsure whether this is a genuine escape or whether the worry just reappears one level up — after all, a function and its argument still have to be brought together somehow, and the claim that application just happens is exactly the sort of thing (2) denies. Please address her suggestion explicitly.
- Please also weigh the more radical escape routes: that the regress is real but harmless, so (5) is the mistake; that the regress is real and vicious and Bradley was right to conclude that relations are unreal, so I should give up more than I expected; that the whole framing presupposes a constituent-ontology of facts that should be abandoned wholesale; and anything else you can construct.
