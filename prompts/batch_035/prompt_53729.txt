In my reading group we have assembled an argument to the effect that Scanlon's contractualism cannot keep aggregation out, and since the conclusion is one Scanlon plainly rejects, something in it must give — but I cannot see what. (1) On contractualism, whether a principle of action is permissible depends on whether anyone burdened by it could reasonably reject it, where each person's grounds for rejection are the burden imposed on them, considered as an individual complaint, so the strength of the strongest complaint decides. (2) Scanlon concedes that when complaints on each side are roughly equal in strength, numbers may decide: it is permissible to save five people rather than one when the five each face a burden at least as bad as the one's. (3) Between any two burdens, however unequal — a headache and a death, say — there is a finite series of intermediate burdens, each roughly equal in strength to its neighbors. (4) By (2), whenever the two sides of a choice bear burdens that are roughly equal, the side with the greater number may be saved. (5) Applying this along the series: enough people bearing a burden one step short of death may be saved rather than one person facing death; enough people bearing a burden one step short of that may be saved rather than them; and so on down to headaches. (6) Chaining these permissions, enough people with headaches may be saved rather than one person facing death — which is exactly the aggregation contractualism was supposed to block. (7) So contractualism collapses into aggregation, and Scanlon's denial of it is untenable. Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it is worth, I would most like to keep (1) and the anti-aggregation conclusion, and I see no way to deny (3) outright, since between any two burdens there do seem to be further burdens of intermediate strength.
