The exam question was:

"In his paper on internal and external reasons, Bernard Williams argues that all reasons for action are internal reasons. (a) State the internal reasons theory as precisely as you can, making sure to explain the role of the agent's motivational set and of sound deliberation. (b) Explain the example involving gin and what it is supposed to show. (c) Explain why Williams presents but does not endorse the 'sub-Humean model'. (d) Williams later expressed some second thoughts in the paper's Postscript -- what was the nature of the concession, if any?"

And the student wrote:

"Williams's internal reasons theory says that an agent A has a reason to do X only if A can be motivated to do X by a process of sound deliberation starting from A's motivational set, i.e., from the desires and other motivations A actually has. The key point is that the agent has to actually go through this deliberation -- the reason only comes into existence once the agent has really thought it through and arrived at the motivation. So reasons are grounded in what the agent wants plus correct reasoning from what they want.

The gin example: a man wants a gin and tonic in the evening. He'll pass a shop on his way home but it closes soon, so if he wants to drink tonight he needs to stop now and buy the gin. The point of the example is that his reason to stop at the shop derives from a desire he already has -- wanting the drink. Williams contrasts this with a case where someone has no desire at all connected to drinking; such a person simply has no reason to buy gin, and telling them they have a reason anyway is idle. So the example illustrates that reasons must connect up with actual motivation, which is what the internalism condition requires.

Williams argues that external reason statements -- claims that someone has a reason regardless of their motivational set -- are never true, because there is no way for such a reason to explain or produce action. A reason, to be a reason, must be capable of motivating a rational agent, and if nothing in the agent's motivational set can be reached by deliberation, then the alleged reason cannot get a grip. The desire is still necessary; without some motivation in play, the statement can't be true.

The sub-Humean model is Williams's official account of deliberation: the only legitimate moves in practical reasoning are means-end reasoning and working out how to satisfy given desires most efficiently. He endorses it because it captures the minimal core of instrumental rationality.

On the Postscript: Williams conceded that external reasons do exist after all -- a reason is external in cases where it would survive the agent's acquiring full information about all the facts. So the later view is that reasons are whatever a fully informed, rational version of the agent would want."
