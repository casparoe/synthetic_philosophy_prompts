I write about science and ideas for a mid-size paper — mostly book reviews and explainers — and I'm working on a piece about so-called memory laws. There's a puzzle I keep circling and can't quite crack, and I'd like your help actually thinking it through rather then a list of examples.

In maybe a dozen countries — Germany, France, Austria, and others — publicly denying the Holocaust isn't just offensive, it's a crime, and people have really gone to jail for it. In the US the same statement is legal however vile; the government can't punish speech just for being false about history. Meanwhile all of these countries, the European ones included, prosecute ordinary lies all the time — fraud, perjury, defamation — without anyone claiming that's a free-speech violation. So the question that interests me isn't "which side is right" but what the criminalization approach actually presupposes. To jail someone for denying documented history, the legal system seems committed to something about historical truth: that it's the kind of thing a state can declare settled and then enforce, the way it enforces a property line, rather than the kind of thing that stays perpetually open to revision the way a scientific theory does. It also seems to presuppose a theory of harm in which the injury isn't to any particular listener — the denier's audience mostly isn't deceived, they know better, which is exactly what makes it different from fraud — but is something diffuse instead: an attack on public memory as such, or on the dignity of the dead and their descendants. And here's the tension I can't get straight: the same laws carve out room for scholarship and even satire, which seems to concede that "was this history actually like that?" is a question that can legitimately be reopened — just only by the right people, in the right register. So which is it?

Can you reconstruct what view of speech, of truth, and of harm the criminalization approach needs in order to be coherent at all? And please distinguish which commitments are strict requirements versus which it could drop and still stand — like, does it truly need the "settled truth" component, or could the laws run on dignity-based grounds alone? Also tell me where the thing is genuinely underdetermined, such that a couple of quite different philosophical theories would each license the laws just fine. I'm asking because my editor wants a punchy "here's the one deep principle" ending for my piece, and my honest suspicion is there isn't a single principle in there — but I want to check that I'm not just giving up too early before I tell her that.
