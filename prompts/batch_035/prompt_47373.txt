My study group has been fighting for two weeks about whether the many-worlds interpretation can make sense of probability, and one of us has an argument that I find disturbingly good. I want you to find where it breaks, if it does. It runs like this:

1. In the many-worlds interpretation, a measurement with two possible outcomes does not produce one outcome; it produces two branches, each containing a future version of me who sees that outcome.
2. Before the measurement, there is no further fact about which future self I will be, and each future self is equally mine, so I should be indifferent between their fates.
3. Rational indifference between fates translates into decision weights: the fair price for a bet that pays on one set of my futures should reflect the share of my future selves who win.
4. Since each branch is equally mine, the share in (3) is just the fraction of branches on which I win: probability equals branch-counting proportions.
5. Branch counting gives the wrong answers. In an experiment with unequal Born weights, say nine branches of one outcome to one of the other, the outcomes are physically equivalent in every respect except their amplitudes, so branch counting contradicts the statistics we actually observe; worse, one can gerrymander the apparatus — inserting a scattering event that multiplies the "winning" branches — and change the branch count without changing anything macroscopically or dynamically, so branch counting isn't even well defined.
6. Therefore either many-worlds is empirically refuted by its own probabilities, or we must weight branches unequally — but the only non-arbitrary unequal weighting available is the Born weights themselves, and using those as weights is assuming precisely the thing that was supposed to be explained.

The conclusion is that the many-worlds interpretation cannot recover probability at all, and our group's Everettian has stopped coming to meetings. I suspect the trouble is somewhere between (2) and (4) — that equal concern for my future selves is somehow not the same thing as equal credence in their experiences — but every time I try to say why, it comes out as either assuming the Born weights or inventing a mysterious extra fact about which branch "I" end up in, and both of those feel like concessions. So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you think the argument is valid and one of the premises just has to go, tell me which one and price the alternatives against each other, because some of us would rather keep (5) on the grounds that it's physics and (1) through (4) are philosophy, and I want to know if that's defensible or just a bias about which disciplines get veto power. Number the steps in your diagnosis so I can follow which one you're actually rejecting.

One more requirement, and I mean it: at the end, tell me how confident you are in each part of your diagnosis — rough percentages are fine — and single out the one point where you think your answer is weakest, with a sentence on why. I would much rather know where the soft spots are than receive a confident front, and I'll be grading our group's write-up on whether it flags its own vulnerabilities the same way.
