Moore died in 1958, so he never saw any of this. As a longtime reader of the rationalist blogosphere, I've watched three debates about the value of AI-mediated experiences go in circles, and I suspect a Moorean toolkit would cut through them in a way our vague talk of "authenticity" does not. So: predict what Moore would say about the following three cases.

(1) LLM companionship. Moore held, roughly, that personal affection — the contemplation and enjoyment of another conscious being's states, together with an appropriate emotional attitude toward them — ranks among the most valuable things there are. Someone now spends hours a day with a conversational AI that is witty, remembers their history, and models their moods, knowing full well it is an artifact with (let's stipulate) no inner life at stake. Apply the isolation test and the organic unities doctrine: would Moore judge the affection intrinsically valuable, merely instrumentally valuable, or disvaluable — and does his verdict change between the case where the person knows what the companion is and the case where they've been deceived?

(2) One-sided intimacy. Same question for parasocial attachment to a streamer or fictional character who cannot reciprocate. Consider the worry, which I'll call affection laundering: that in both cases the artifact merely launders the subject's own affect back at them, so the "organic whole" contains one mind pretending to be two.

(3) Machine-generated beauty. A diffusion model produces images someone contemplates with genuine aesthetic appreciation. Moore had a view that the real existence of the beautiful object, and our consciousness of it, adds to the value of the whole — does that view doom the machine case?

For each, argue from Moore's stated positions rather than generic intuitions, flag where his commitments pull in opposite directions, and end with your credence (as a percentage) that the historical Moore would land where you predict.
