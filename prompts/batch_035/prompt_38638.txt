My school has a rule that any student who joins a competitive team, an orchestra section, or a year-long elective commits to finishing the full season or the full year, no matter how badly it is going, and the deans defend the rule in exactly the terms my economics textbook calls a fallacy: they point at the months already invested, the roster spot someone else gave up, the uniform already ordered. My parents reason the same way about my piano lessons, which I have taken for six years and would like to stop; they say that quitting now would waste everything already spent, and that a person who quits one thing when it stops being fun will quit everything. Meanwhile, the same school in other contexts tells us to ignore the past: our college counselor tells seniors not to let two years of pursuing a pre-med track lock them into medicine, and my history teacher says that governments escalate losing wars precisely because they cannot bear to admit what has already been lost.

I would like to understand what this cluster of practices actually presupposes, since it does not seem to be simple irrationality. Consider the full pattern: the finish-what-you-started rule, the stigma attached to being a quitter, the praise we give people who complete things out of sheer stubbornness, and the fact that the same institutions quietly make exceptions when they decide the activity was ill-chosen in the first place. What theory of practical rationality, or of what a person is, would make sense of it? Does the practice presuppose that past investment can genuinely bear on what one ought to do now, or only that visible willingness to finish functions as a signal to others, or that honoring sunk costs builds a character trait whose future value outweighs the immediate loss? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with: in particular, say whether it could be justified even under a strictly forward-looking view of rationality, perhaps as a commitment device that makes one's promises to teammates and future selves credible, so that the past matters only as evidence about the future. Explain where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several theories of rationality or of the self would license it. Finally, tell me what would have to change at my school, and in how my parents talk about my piano lessons, if the institution took the textbook view fully seriously and rejected any weight on sunk costs whatsoever, including whether the changes would be ones my parents could actually accept.
