Each spring I teach the abortion unit in our introductory ethics course, and each spring I come away suspecting my own judgments are less tidy than the arguments I assign. I'd like to lay out my verdicts on a set of cases and ask what principle, if any, captures them — I'd want a principle stated precisely enough to have testable consequences, not a slogan.

My verdicts, in roughly increasing order of discomfort:

1. Thomson's famous case: you wake up to find the Society of Music Lovers has connected your circulatory system to an unconscious violinist who will die if unplugged, and nine months of connection will cure him. I judge unplugging to be permissible, even though the violinist is innocent and unplugging kills him.

2. The variant with the drifting people-seeds, where you took reasonable precautions (screens on the windows) and one took root anyway. Removing it: permissible, and my confidence here is actually higher than in the violinist case.

3. A pregnancy resulting from consensual intercourse where contraception was used correctly and failed. Abortion at eight weeks: permissible.

4. A woman who has declined contraception repeatedly over several years and uses abortion as her sole method of birth control, now in her fourth pregnancy, at twelve weeks, healthy fetus, no complications. I still judge this permissible, and yet I cannot shake the sense that something more is asked of her than was asked of the person in case 3 — that the burden of justification rises with each refusal. I'm prepared to be told this is a bias and not a moral datum.

5. A healthy woman, healthy fetus, thirty-eight weeks, and she seeks termination because a fellowship opportunity would be compromised by giving birth. I judge this impermissible, and I judge it so even though the bodily-autonomy considerations at stake in cases 1 through 3 — her body, her ongoing involuntary support — seem formally unchanged from the first trimester.

6. A fetus with anencephaly, which will never be conscious and has, I take it, no future of the kind that makes killing wrong on the deprivation account. I judge terminating that pregnancy permissible. But compare: a patient in a permanent, stable unconscious state who will never wake. Killing that patient seems clearly wrong to me, even though, again, no future of value is available to be deprived of. I cannot easily hold both verdicts if what grounds the wrongness of killing is the deprivation of a future.

7. Contraception, which prevents a future-like-ours from coming into existence just as surely as abortion prevents an existing one, seems to me obviously permissible, with no tinge of regret.

So here is what I'd like from you. First, is there a single principle, or a principled hybrid, that delivers all seven verdicts — or close? If you propose one, state each clause precisely and tell me which case motivates it. Second, apply it to a case I haven't listed: the violinist scenario modified so that you agreed to one week of connection as a favor, and partway through, the connection is quietly extended to nine months without your consent. Third, tell me candidly which of my verdicts your best principle cannot save, and whether the verdict or the principle should give — I am more attached to some of these verdicts than others, and I've flagged where I suspect myself of bias. Finally, since I'll be taking this back to my seminar: say what would change your mind. Which argument or consideration from the other side most threatens your proposal, and where in your reasoning is the proposal most likely to fail? I'd rather know the weakest joint in advance than have a student find it for me.
