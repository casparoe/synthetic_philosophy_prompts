I run a reading group for MSc students on epistemic game theory, and we have spent three sessions stuck on the backward induction debate without anyone moving an inch, so I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another survey of the positions. The debate, as we've been framing it: in the centipede game (two players alternately take a small pot now or pass, doubling the stakes until the pot is enormous), common knowledge of rationality seems to force the first player to take immediately — backward induction rolls up the tree, every pass being irrational given what would happen at the next node. Aumann's theorem says, in effect, that common knowledge of rationality in a game of perfect information does yield the backward induction outcome. But there is an old and, to my students, compelling objection, pressed by Pettit and Sugden in the late 1980s and sharpened by Reny around 1992: the moment play reaches any later node, the common knowledge that was supposed to generate the prediction has been destroyed, because someone's pass refutes it. So the theory is either self-undermining (its own prediction makes the later reasoning it contains unsound) or it can only be applied at nodes that are, on the theory's own terms, unreachable. In the seminar this has hardened into two camps: one holds that the theorem is fine because the epistemic state at later nodes is simply a different game with a different information structure, and the classical view asks what players would do ex ante, not what remains known after a surprise; the other holds that a theory of rational play which cannot be believed at the nodes players actually occupy is not a theory of rational play at all.

Here is my working hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than flatter: the two camps are not disagreeing about games at all — they are disagreeing about what the knowledge operator quantifies over. Camp one reads common knowledge as a syntactic condition on information partitions, something defined over the whole game tree at once, so it trivially "survives" reaching a node because it was never a state of anyone's mind at any time. Camp two reads it as a state of actual reasoners that must be robust to surprise, which is why the counterfactual nodes do all the work — the dispute is really about whether rationality is a fact about hypothetical continuation (what you would do if you found yourself there) or a fact about live deliberation. On my diagnosis the debate about whether common knowledge is "destroyed" off the equilibrium path is partly verbal in exactly the way disputes about material vs strict conditionals are verbal, and the substantive residue is a hidden formal question: which of the reconstruction programmes — belief revision at unreached nodes in the Stalnaker style, hypothetical knowledge in the manner of Samet, or lexicographic beliefs and iterated admissibility in the Brandenburger style — actually captures the "if you found yourself there" reasoning, and whether any of them makes centipede behaviour differ from naive backward induction in an observable way.

To make this concrete, the scenario I've been using with the students: a hundred-move centipede in which the co-player is a machine that passes with probability one minus epsilon for reasons of its own, and a rational player who knows this must pass early to harvest the machine's errors. As epsilon goes to zero the value collapses discontinuously back onto the backward induction solution. I suspect this limit is where the camps come apart in a way that isn't merely verbal — one side says the limit case vindicates the classical analysis, the other says the discontinuity shows the classical analysis was never the limit of anything real. So, three questions, in order of importance. First, is my hypothesis about the knowledge operator's semantics right, or is the disagreement better described as two different questions (what to predict about play versus what a theory could contain) travelling under one name, as Reny's "theory of games" framing suggests? Second, does the epsilon-machine scenario isolate a genuinely substantive dispute about limits and approximations, or does each camp get to interpret it on its own terms? Third, given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a change of question — and be honest if the answer is that we should expect none, and why.
