I TA an upper-level food ethics seminar at a state school — the course is deliberately EA-adjacent, we do utilitarianism, speciesism, welfare science, the works. I've been reading LessWrong and the EA Forum since around 2014 so I'm basically the target audience for this material, which is maybe why I keep second-guessing myself when I grade it. Anyways, it's midterm season and one of our questions keeps making me pause. It reads:

A certain argument, popular in animal-welfare circles, holds that a meat-eater who is concerned about animal suffering should switch from eating chicken to eating beef. (a) Reconstruct the reasoning behind this argument. (b) Explain how animal suffering caused by crop cultivation — wild animals killed by pesticides and harvesting machinery — is supposed to complicate it. (c) Assess whether the complication actually undermines the argument.

Forty minutes, in class, closed book. Before I lock in grades I want a careful second read of one answer, so please go through it point by point: what the student gets right, what they get wrong, how much each mistake should cost given the rubric weights (a), (b) and (c) roughly equally, and then a final grade — letter grade or out of 10, whichever you can justify more cleanly. Dont pull punches just because the answer sounds confident, half the class writes like this.

Here's the student's answer:

"The core of the argument is a headcount. One broiler chicken yields around 1.5 kg of meat and is killed at six or seven weeks old, while a single beef animal yields on the order of 250 kg. That's a difference of about two orders of magnitude in the number of animals killed per kilo of meat. This is basically the argument Tom Regan makes in The Case for Animal Rights — if animal suffering counts morally, the sheer number of animals involved dominates, and chicken comes out vastly worse than beef. One might object that cows are cognitively more sophisticated than chickens, so each cow's suffering deserves greater weight. But even granting a tenfold multiplier for the cow, the headcount gap is a hundredfold, so beef still wins.

The complication comes from the fact that meat doesn't only involve the animals you eat. Crops kill animals too: pesticides and harvesting machinery kill mice, birds, and other field animals. And this actually strengthens the beef argument rather than complicating it, because beef cattle mostly graze, so beef production requires essentially no cropland. Chicken is the most feed-efficient meat, so it needs relatively little cropland as well — but a vegetarian diet, which the beef argument is implicitly being compared to, requires the most cropland of all. So on the crop-cultivation dimension the ordering reverses: vegetarian is worst, chicken is in the middle, beef is best.

Whether this undermines the argument depends on magnitudes, and we simply don't have good numbers for field deaths. Since the uncertainty is unresolvable, the rational move is to set wild animal suffering aside and go with what we can actually count: the farmed animals. By that measure the answer is clear — grass-finished beef involves the fewest farmed deaths and the least cropland, so it beats both chicken and the vegetarian diet, and the concerned meat-eater should eat beef."
