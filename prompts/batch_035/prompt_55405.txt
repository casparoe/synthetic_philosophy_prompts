I serve on the science assessment panel for our national space agency's Mars sample return mission, and the first cores drilled from an ancient hydrothermal vein have produced exactly the result we dreaded: neither clean nor dismissible. The vein material contains membrane-bounded vesicles, a few micrometers across, that sustain a proton gradient across their boundaries when supplied with an iron–sulfur couple, bud off daughter vesicles when the energy source is maintained, and lyse within days when it is withdrawn. Their carbon isotope ratios are fractionated in a way suggestive of metabolism, though serpentinization-style abiotic chemistry could in principle mimic it. Crucially, isotopic labeling shows no selective retention of any component during budding: inheritance appears entirely passive, and a sixty-generation propagation attempt in the presence of a growth inhibitor produced no adaptation, which any system with genuine heritable variation should have managed. Our panel must deliver a written recommendation to the mission directorate within ten days, and the stakes are keyed to a single word: the mission charter's gates for the public announcement protocol, continued high-containment of the samples versus their release to ordinary laboratories, and a fresh planetary protection review are all triggered by a finding of "life," not by any weaker locution. The panel is split three ways. One member holds that the standard working definition — a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution — settles it, and that since there is no evidence of heredity or selection we should announce a non-detection and release the samples. A second argues the system is autopoietic — organized around maintaining and reproducing itself, which she takes to be the deeper mark of the living — and that demanding nucleic-acid-style heredity merely exports Earth's particular solution into a definition. The chair wants to coin a new category, something like "proto-biotic chemical system," to avoid the question entirely, though our counsel warns that the charter offers no such gate and the public will hear "life" or "not life" regardless. Please draft the short written recommendation I will circulate before our final session. It must take a position: whether this is a life detection for the purposes of the announcement and containment decisions, and what operational definition the panel should adopt going forward so we are not improvising this again. It should apply the same reasoning to two further cases we know are coming: a terrestrial biofilm found on the drill bit, and a virus-like particle with heritable capsid structure but no autonomous metabolism of its own. It should engage the strongest argument on whichever side it rejects, rather than a strawman — including the possibility that the chair is right that the definitional dispute is unresolvable and the operational question must be answered some other way. And it should close by stating what would change its verdict: which experimental result or argument, arriving between now and the decision date, would send the recommendation the other way, and at which step its own reasoning is most likely to fail.
