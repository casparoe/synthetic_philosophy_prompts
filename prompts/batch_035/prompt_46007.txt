My two coworkers Sam and Lucia had a proper row about a third coworker of ours, Ren, and I still can't work out who was right, so I'm hoping you can referee.

The background: last spring Ren told our department head, unprompted, that Joy (his main rival for a promotion) had been interviewing elsewhere. This got back to Joy, she got passed over, and Ren landed the role. It later came out — from the department head herself, after a couple of wines at the christmas party — that she'd decided well in advance that if Ren ever looked like he was going to keep mum about Joy, she would have called him in for a "development chat" and leant on him until he told her. As she put it, she has never once failed to get what she wants out of those chats. So the argument at work the next day:

1. Sam said Ren is fully blameworthy, and his being blameworthy doesn't require that he could have done otherwise — because he couldn't have. If he'd kept quiet, the chat happens and she grinds him down. Yet nobody actually pushed him: he told her out of pure career ambition, for his own reasons. So the principle of alternative possibilities is false, and blame doesn't need alternatives.

2. Lucia said that backup plan doesn't remove Ren's alternatives, it just makes them pointless. In the nearby world where Ren hesitates, he still spills — but he spills under pressure, not from his own motives. The whole point of those Frankfurt-style cases was supposed to be that the person *couldn't* have done otherwise, not that doing otherwise would be futile.

3. Sam answered that the only "could have" that matters for blame is one located in his own will. In the actual world he chose to tell her out of ambition; nobody made him; and the existence of some strange nearby world where the department head does the work instead can't subtract anything from what he actually chose.

4. Lucia said then Sam has quietly changed the subject from whether he could have done otherwise to whether he actually chose freely — and PAP defenders are happy to grant the second one. Besides, the flicker world is where his freedom lives: a world where he hesitates and gets leant on is a world where things go differently *through him*, tiny as that difference is.

5. Sam replied that a freedom which exists only in a world where he caves to pressure and betrays Joy anyway is a wierd thing to hang his blame on — you'd be blaming him for what he *would have* done, not for what he did.

6. Lucia finished by saying the case is rigged anyway: "she's never failed to get what she wants" is a prediction about the department head, not a mechanism that ensures anything about Ren. Those cases are meant to involve an intervener who can guarantee the outcome, and a prediction can misfire — so for all anyone knows, there's a world where he holds out even under the pressure.

They both then turned to me and I definately couldn't call it. Can you go through their exchange move by move, say who's right at each of the six points, tell me what if anything both of them are missing, and give a verdict on who's more right overall? Please write your answer in British English.
