I work as a health economist for a public payer, and most of my job involves cost-effectiveness modelling in QALYs: a year lived in full health counts as 1, a year lived with serious impairment counts as some fraction, and preventing a death generates the QALYs the person would presumably have lived had they survived. This has odd downstream consequences I've only recently started to think about philosophically. A vaccine that averts a death in a healthy thirty-year-old generates decades of QALYs; palliative care that makes a dying patient's last six months markedly better generates a fraction of one. Some agencies additionally give extra weight to deaths averted in the young, under an explicit fairness rationale about everyone deserving a normal span of life. And crucially, the scoring counts the future life a dead person loses, but nothing in it corresponds to the years a person "loses" by not having come into existence earlier — prenatal nonexistence earns no compensation and generates no obligations, while posthumous nonexistence is treated as a massive loss.

A friend who studies ancient philosophy pointed out that my scoring rule seems to take a stand on whether death is bad for the person who dies — apparently the deprivation account, on which death harms you by depriving you of goods your continued life would have contained, against the Epicurean view that death cannot harm its subject because when death is present the subject is not, so there is no one for the harm to befall. But I'm not sure the practice really needs the metaphysics it appears to use. An Epicurean agency might justify counting averted deaths on entirely different grounds — grief and bereavement effects on others, obligations to the person's earlier living self who strongly wanted to survive, or social trust — and might arrive at similar numbers for very different reasons. So my questions are these: what theory of the badness of death does QALY-based rationing actually presuppose; which rival theories could license the same scoring, so that the practice is philosophically underdetermined; and are there internal inconsistencies, e.g., between the deprivation logic that gives near-zero weight to saving someone with almost no remaining QALYs and the equity patches agencies bolt on to avoid exactly that result, or between counting posthumous but not prenatal nonexistence as a loss while also insisting the young deserve priority? Finally, if the health system were run on genuinely Epicurean premises, what would concretely change — would averted deaths be scored at all, and at what value?

Please give me a reasoned prose answer that reconstructs the implicit theory, separates what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and diagnoses where it is underdetermined or self-undermining. I'd rather have honest uncertainty flagged than a confident reconstruction that papers over the parts of the practice that don't cohere.
