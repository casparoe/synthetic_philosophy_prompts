I'm preparing a discussion handout for an introductory moral philosophy class on the question of what, if anything, makes promises binding. Please write me a classification instrument for this debate: a decision tree whose branch nodes are concrete cases or claims, and whose leaves are the standard positions on the binding force of promises.

Some constraints on the tree:

First, the diagnostic questions. Each node should be a case a student can answer without knowing which position it funnels them toward, and the cases should be ones on which the actual views genuinely diverge. In particular, the tree should route through some or all of the following: (a) a promise made to a dying person who explicitly releases the promisor and then dies — does the obligation survive the absence of anyone who cares?; (b) a promise extracted at gunpoint; (c) a promise made in circumstances where there is no established social practice of promising — say, castaways from mutually unfamiliar societies on a remote island — to test whether the binding force is conventional all the way down; (d) a promise whose performance would now, because circumstances changed, produce a worse outcome for the promisee than breach would; and (e) a promise the promisee has entirely forgotten about. You may add further cases, but these five must be usable somewhere in the tree, and no node may simply ask the student to self-identify with a label.

Second, the leaves. The following positions should appear, each with a one-sentence note on its most awkward commitment: a convention/practice-based view in the lineage of Hume and Rawls, on which the obligation arises from a social practice of promising; a normative-power view in the lineage of Raz and Shiffrin, on which promising is the exercise of a capacity to impose obligations on oneself for another's benefit; an expectation-and-reliance view on which the wrong in breach is essentially a wrong to someone who was counting on you; a voluntarist/autonomy view on which the binding force flows from the promisor's own will or consent; and a deflationary or skeptical view on which promises carry no distinctive binding force beyond what general moral considerations already supply. Build the tree from the logical space first — decide the branching questions, then locate the positions in the resulting answer combinations — rather than starting from the list of names.

Third, honesty about limits. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any case you can devise — expectation-based and reliance-based views are a likely candidate, but judge for yourself — flag this explicitly at the leaf or in a closing note rather than inventing a spurious distinction. Likewise, if some combination of answers corresponds to no defended position, note the empty cell and say whether you think it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Aim for roughly seven to nine nodes total, and keep the register suitable for first-year students who have read none of the primary literature: no jargon in the node questions, names confined to the leaf descriptions.
