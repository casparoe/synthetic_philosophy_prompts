Write a philosophical essay on the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, organised around a question that the standard presentations of the objection leave unaddressed: what exactly is doing the work in it?

Background you may draw on, stated briefly: the objection, familiar from Rawls and Nozick, is that utilitarianism treats persons' good as if it could be pooled into a single fund and redistributed, ignoring that lives are individuated — that a loss to one person cannot be made good by a gain to another. Most discussions recite this, gesture at the contrast with intra-personal trade-offs (where a present cost can be outweighed by a later benefit in the same life), and stop. Your essay must go further.

First, distinguish three candidate readings of what grounds the objection: (a) the compensation reading — there is no interpersonal ledger, so one person's loss cannot literally be offset by another's gain, whereas within a life the ledger exists; (b) the authority reading — each person has a claim over how her own life goes that others may not set back even for aggregate gains, so the point is not about ledgers at all but about who is entitled to make the trade; and (c) the anti-concentration reading — the objection is at bottom an aversion to distributions in which burdens fall on a few while benefits scatter across the many, an aversion that a prioritarian or sufficientarian could satisfy without abandoning aggregation.

Then test the readings against the following case. A policy maximizes aggregate lifetime well-being by imposing serious early-life burdens on a minority, but each burdened person later receives, within her own life, benefits that she, fully informed and looking back, judges to outweigh those burdens. Every life, burdened and unburdened, goes better as a whole than it would under any alternative policy, and each person's intra-personal ledger is positive. On reading (a), this case should be straightforwardly permissible: whatever the objection forbids, it forbids because one person's loss is uncompensated, and here nothing is uncompensated within any life. If your considered judgment is that the policy could still be wrong, your task is to say what has to change to make it permissible, and whether the residual objection is captured by (b) or (c) — or whether, once (a) fails, the objection collapses into a general complaint about unfairness that any egalitarian already holds, and so threatens utilitarianism no more than any other view that permits trade-offs.

Two further constraints. First, you may bring in the point that whether within-a-life compensation is even available depends on contentious assumptions about personal identity over time — that a life is the sort of thing whose balance can be struck — but do not let the essay become a survey of reductionism about identity; the point must do argumentative work. Second, your concluding section must reach and defend a definite verdict on which of the three readings, if any, states the objection at its strongest, and on whether that strongest version is a threat to utilitarianism specifically or to every aggregative view, prioritarianism included. Ending non-committally — that all readings have something to be said for them — is a failure of the essay. Refer to positions and authors by name where helpful, but do not quote any text verbatim, do not include a bibliography or reading list, and write the essay so that it stands on its own argument rather than on scholarly apparatus.
