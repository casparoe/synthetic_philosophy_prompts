After a career in geophysics and a retirement spent reading astrobiology and the philosophy of science, I have kept a notebook of my own judgments about whether life exists elsewhere in the galaxy. I have begun to suspect there is an implicit principle of evidence behind them — or possibly several that do not fit together. Here are the judgments. I would like the principle, if there is one.

1. If a second, independently originated biosphere were found on Mars or in a subsurface ocean on Europa, I would revise the probability that life is common dramatically upward — this single additional data point would count for more than any theoretical argument about prebiotic chemistry I have ever read. The sample size of one has been the whole problem, and sample size two would change everything.

2. I take the rapidity with which life appeared on Earth — apparently within a few hundred million years of the surface becoming habitable — as strong evidence that abiogenesis is not a rare accident. I resist attempts to explain this away by noting that we would necessarily find ourselves on a planet where life arose early.

3. At the same time, I read the subsequent record as showing the opposite about intelligence: eukaryotic cells, complex multicellularity, and language-grade intelligence appeared only in the final fractions of Earth's history, and I conclude those steps are intrinsically unlikely. Microbial life may be common while technological life is vanishingly rare — the "hard steps" picture.

4. I count the Fermi silence — decades of null SETI results and, more tellingly, the absence of visible large-scale engineering in a galaxy whose typical star is older than the Sun — as genuine evidence against expansive technological civilizations, since on geological timescales a colonizing intelligence should have filled the galaxy many times over. But I do not count it as much evidence against simple life.

5. Arguments from convergent evolution — intelligence is useful, evolution finds useful things, consider crows and cephalopods — leave me unmoved as a way of raising the probability of human-grade intelligence, even though I accept that they show intelligence is not a one-off in principle.

6. A single unambiguous technosignature would, in my book, outweigh a century of further silence. I update asymmetrically: absence of a detection, given how shallow and poorly targeted our searches are, tells us very little; presence would tell us nearly everything.

What principle of evidence, if any, captures all of this? I can see two trouble spots, and I would like you to be blunt about them. First, can one principle sustain both (2) and (3)? It seems to me that the very selection effect I wave away in (2) — observers will find themselves on planets where everything that had to happen happened in time — is doing real work in (3), where the late appearance of intelligence is supposed to be probative rather than an artifact of our own vantage point. Second, can (4) and (6) be held together without the silence collapsing to near-zero evidential weight? If some of my verdicts have to go, say which, and why. Then tell me what the repaired principle says about two cases I have not yet sorted: (a) a shadow biosphere on Earth — a second, independent origin of life coexisting unrecognized here — where I am torn between counting it as nearly as informative as an independent Martian biosphere and discounting it because the two origins share a setting; and (b) an oxygen biosignature on an exoplanet that later acquires a plausible abiotic explanation, and how the initial announcement and its retraction should be handled by the principle. Formalize as much as you like — I did rates for a living and am not frightened of equations.
