In my ethics reading group we have spent two semesters on the question whether lying and merely misleading are morally different in kind or only in degree, and we have stalled completely. On one side, the practice-based view: assertion carries a guarantee of truth, so a lie is a forgery of that guarantee, while a misleading truth merely exploits the hearer's own inference — the liar breaks a norm constitutive of the practice; the misleader leaves it intact and lets you do the deceiving yourself. On the other side, the effect-based view: the wrong of deception is the intent to implant a false belief, that intention is present in exactly the same measure in both, and holding that a careful lawyer's technically true non-denial is clean where a clumsy lie is dirty is a kind of moral magic — in the murderer-at-the-door case, the door is opened either way. Both sides know and concede all the same cases, including the awkward ones (the truthful assertion made in the belief that it is false, where the "liar" deceives no one; the question-dodging evasion that misleads more effectively than a lie would have), and yet nobody has moved an inch in a year. Here is the case that has lately made the stall feel structural to me: imagine a witness at a disciplinary hearing who is under a rule that penalizes only false statements, and who answers every question with truths engineered to leave the panel holding a picture she knows is exactly the reverse of reality. By the rule's own lights she is cleaner than a colleague who lies once, and yet she seems to have done more damage to the hearer's epistemic position than the lie. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is neither verbal nor merely hard, but is a substantive standoff about the locus of the wrong — whether it lives in a norm internal to the practice of assertion or in the effect on the hearer — and that as long as each side treats its preferred locus as the thing to be assessed, they are answering different questions under one heading. Is that diagnosis right? Tell me what each side's actual moves commit them to, whether the debate could be shown to be verbal about the word "lying" rather than about the underlying wrong, whether the witness case is a genuine test that breaks the tie or just intuition-pumping that each side will re-describe to fit its view, and what, if anything, would count as progress here — a distinction, a case, a shift in the question — or whether no progress should be expected and why. Under 600 words.
