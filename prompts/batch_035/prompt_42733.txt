I teach philosophy of history at a UK university and sit on our faculty's curriculum committee, which will vote next month on a proposed first-year breadth module, 'Big History: From Foragers to the Anthropocene'. The idea is to teach the whole sweep of human history through three trade books read in sequence — Jared Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel, Yuval Noah Harari's Sapiens, and Graeber and Wengrow's The Dawn of Everything — closing with a unit on the Anthropocene. The proposers argue that our students arrive with no framework for thinking on the scale of millennia, that these books are the only ones that actually teach long-range causal reasoning in an accessible register, and that the third book already models critical reading of the first two.

Our anthropologists have circulated a hostile response. They concede the books are gripping, but contend that the module would present as settled a chain of just-so stories: Diamond's continental geography as destiny, Harari's cognitive revolution and his claim that the adoption of agriculture left ordinary people worse off, and — in the third book — a revisionist counter-narrative of conscious political experimentation, seasonal switches between hierarchy and equality, and cities before states, a narrative no less sweeping than the story it replaces but more flattering to present sensibilities. One of them put the worry memorably: a course built on three magicians, each exposing the previous one's tricks while performing his own, teaches students that history is whatever the most recent book says it is. They want the proposal rejected in favour of a conventional survey. A third group on the committee accepts the critique of the books but not the conclusion about the module: teaching grand narratives alongside their demolition, they say, is precisely what a first-year course should do, since that is how students learn to read history at all.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It must recommend one of three outcomes — approve as proposed, approve with specified conditions, or reject — with reasons the committee can act on, and it must give an honest hearing to the strongest consideration on the losing side. In the course of the reasoning it should address at least the following: whether the problem with grand narratives is a matter of defective evidence or of genre, and why that distinction matters for what the committee should do; whether the proposers' answer — that the books refute one another — actually works or merely trains students into cynicism about historical knowledge; and what, concretely, a student who has completed the module should be able to do that they could not do before. Begin the memo with your recommendation in its first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble and no restatement of this request.
