My lecturer handed out a revision gloss of Quine's indeterminacy of translation, and I want to check it before I revise from it. Here is the relevant paragraph, condensed:

Quine's argument starts from the field linguist confronting a speaker of an utterly unexplored language, who says "gavagai" whenever a rabbit scurries past. All the linguist can go on is the situations that prompt the speaker's assent and dissent, and the trouble is that many incompatible hypotheses fit that evidence equally well: "rabbit", "undetached rabbit part", "rabbit stage", and so on. Because no further observation could ever decide between these hypotheses, Quine concluded that translation is inherently uncertain: any manual the linguist produces is at bottom a guess. He did allow that observation sentences, taken as unanalysed wholes, are pinned down by the evidence, so the indeterminacy affects only word-by-word translation and the theoretical superstructure built on top of it. In this respect the thesis is essentially a special case of the underdetermination of scientific theory by data, which Quine had already argued for. His moral is a cautionary one: since meaning outruns all possible evidence, we should be more tentative about attributing precise meanings to others — and, by extension, to ourselves.

I should say that this gloss strikes me as broadly right, and I have no instinct for where it might go wrong. But I have reason to be suspicious: the same handout contained at least one error elsewhere that I caught, concerning a different topic.
