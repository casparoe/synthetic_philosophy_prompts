I'm a PhD student in development economics, and I do part-time evaluation work for a small effective-giving nonprofit that advises individual donors on global health and development interventions. Most of the time it's plain cost-effectiveness stuff, DALYs averted per dollar, the usual. But lately we keep getting proposals where the intervention plausibly changes the size and identity of future populations, not just the welfare of a fixed set of people: child survival programs (fewer deaths, but plausibly fertility responses that change total births), voluntary family planning programs, lead remediation that might shift parental decisions, etc. I did some reading in population ethics on my own — Parfit's repugnant conclusion, total vs. average views, person-affecting constraints — and I get why there's no settled answer, but that doesn't help me write our evaluation guidelines.

Please turn whatever population axiology actually has to offer into a decision procedure our eval team can run on a proposal: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure defaults to reporting rankings conditional on axiological views rather than one number, and where it has to hand the decision to our board because the question is one of values rather than facts. It has to be honest about the fact that, e.g., a totalist will score some of these interventions very differently than an averagist or a person-affecting theorist, and it should tell us whether our donor-facing writeups should ever present view-conditional rankings or whether that just confuses everyone.

Then run it on three proposals:

1. A child survival program (vaccination plus neonatal care) in a setting where the best evidence suggests a fairly complete fertility response — fewer deaths lead to fewer subsequent births over a generation, the classic quantity-quality substitution — so the population effect mostly washes out but not entirely.
2. A voluntary family planning program in a region with high unmet need for contraception, where the direct effect is people having the number of children they say they want rather than more. For the record, our board loves this program, so if the procedure gives an awkward answer here I want to see it do that.
3. A lead paint remediation program whose main effect is making existing people healthier but which has a small, badly-estimated effect on population through fertility timing — I want to know where the procedure throws up its hands and says this one can't be settled by us.

Constraints: the eval team is four people with econ and stats backgrounds and zero philosophy; each proposal gets maybe a day of analyst time; the writeups are public so we can't just punt everything as "deep uncertainty." And please flag the places where the procedure will misfire — I'm told the non-identity problem is a landmine here, where the question order itself can smuggle in a substantive axiological commitment, and I want those spots marked rather than buried.
