My reading group has been stuck for basically the whole semester on whether the better-than relation is transitive, and I've started to suspect we're reproducing a stall that's in the literature itself, so I want a diagnosis of the actual disagreement, not another round of arguments.

Here's the shape of it. On one side you have the Temkin-style spectrum arguments: you start with some outcome, make a tiny change that arguably leaves it no worse (or even makes it a bit better in some respect), then repeat this over and over until you arrive at something that is obviously much worse than where you started. If 'no worse than' is transitive, you've proven the end state is no worse than the start, which nobody accepts. So either you accept that betterness (or at least 'no worse than') is intransitive, which is Temkin's own conclusion and feels like biting a big bullet, or you deny one of the small steps, which feels ad hoc — the tiny changes each really do seem permissible. On the other side you have the people who say 'better than' is a comparative just like 'longer than' or 'heavier than', that comparatives are transitive more or less by their very nature, and that any appearance of intransitivity is really an illusion produced by equivocation: we slide between better for someone vs better impersonally, or between different views or outcomes while holding different background facts fixed, or between better all-things-considered and better in some respect. On that view the spectrum arguments are just sneaky equivocation machines, and once you carefully individuate the relation, transitivity is restored.

My working hypothesis, which might be totally wrong, is that the two camps aren't even answering the same question: the transitivity defenders are doing something analytical about the logic of the comparative itself, while the spectrum people are making a substantive claim about how value is structured in the world, and these can come apart — the concept could be transitive by construction while the evaluative facts it's meant to track refuse to be ordered the way the concept demands. Or maybe the real hidden driver is incomparability: if some outcomes aren't comparable at all, then the spectrum arguments exploit gaps in comparability rather than failures of transitivity proper, and everyone is fighting about the wrong formal property. So — what kind of disagreement is this, really? Verbal, substantive, two questions under one heading, or clashing bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually claims, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a sharper individuation of the many 'better than' relations, something formal like the impossibility-theorem results in population ethics, a worked example everyone agrees on, or an argument that this is one of those debates that shouldn't be expected to end.
