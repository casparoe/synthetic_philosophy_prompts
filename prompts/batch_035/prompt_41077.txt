For years I've held a fairly standard knowledge-norm view of assertion: an assertion is proper iff the speaker knows what they assert, and the distinctive way you owe justification when challenged is explained by knowledge being the thing you were supposed to have. But my version has a wrinkle I've come to think is load-bearing: I ground the norm in the function of the practice rather than treating it as a primitive rule. On my story, assertion exists to transmit evidence — asserting a proposition puts it into the common evidence pool available to your audience, and knowledge is exactly the status that entitles a proposition to enter that pool, so the knowledge norm falls out of what assertion is for. This also gives me a tidy treatment of explicit credence reports: when I say I'm 70% confident it will rain, the knowledge norm is simply silent, because that's a different practice governed by its own requirement that the reported credence match the speaker's actual one.

I'd like a map of this position's exposure. Which cases, arguments, or considerations should genuinely move me, and how do they rank from fatal through serious to merely awkward? I'm aware of the usual pressure points — lottery propositions, assertions by people who know but can't muster confidence, speakers who assert content they don't believe — and I'll admit up front that the last of these bothers me least, since a schoolteacher asserting evolution while privately doubting it seems to me to be asserting perfectly well; tell me whether that confidence is warranted. Beyond the individual cases, I want to know whether the evidence-transmission grounding is doing any real work: could it be a decorative story that I'd keep revising to protect the knowledge norm whatever happens, and if so, is that kind of insulation a defect for a view about a social practice, or just what a mature position looks like? Relatedly, which of these defeaters would committed knowledge-norm theorists themselves concede as counting against them, versus dismiss as intuitions the theory was never meant to capture? And finally: is there a result or a consideration that would show the entire norm-of-assertion framework is ill-posed — that asking for the rule of the practice rests on treating a heterogeneous cluster of speech acts as a single thing — and if that's right, what should a retraction of my view look like rather than a mere qualification of it?
