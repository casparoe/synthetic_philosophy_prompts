I'm the staff analyst to our city's Standing Panel on Price Regulation, an advisory body that recommends to the council whether particular local price controls should be adopted, rejected, or rejected with guidance. Each verdict is accompanied by a short memo; I have summarized the record below. Next month the panel must rule on a new proposal, and my job is to draft the recommendation. I want to argue from the panel's own record rather than from my sympathies, so please treat the verdicts below as the only evidence there is of the panel's principles.

The record, in order:

1. Stabilization of rents on new leases (a ceiling). Rejected. Grounds on record: the cap would bind indefinitely while the long-run supply of rental housing fell; the temporary wartime caps the sponsors invoked were just that, temporary.
2. A municipal minimum wage set roughly sixty percent above the regional median wage (a floor). Rejected. Grounds: the panel's own commissioned study projected reduced employment among young and entry-level workers, and the floor would sit above the wage most entrants actually command.
3. A cap on payday-loan interest at thirty-six percent annually (a ceiling). Approved. Grounds: borrowers arrive in distress, cannot comparison-shop, and typically reborrow; no evidence that the cap reduced access for those who had repaid without reborrowing.
4. A cap on what tow-truck operators may charge at accident scenes (a ceiling). Approved. Grounds: the truck is dispatched by police, so the customer has no ability to shop, and the firms had priced accordingly.
5. A floor on ride-hail fares, sought by taxi plate holders (a floor). Rejected. Grounds: it would raise fares and cut trips; the memo stated that protecting one group of sellers is not, as such, a consumer policy.
6. A minimum retail price per pack of cigarettes, set about ten percent above the prevailing discount tier, at the health department's request (a floor). Approved. Grounds: demand among established smokers is inelastic, and the floor chiefly blocks below-cost discounting aimed at recruiting price-sensitive new smokers; consumption by established smokers was expected to be roughly unchanged, with the burden falling as a modest price rise spread across all smokers. Two members recorded a concern that such floors are regressive; the concern was noted and outvoted.
7. A prohibition on selling gasoline below cost, sought by independent stations against a national chain (a floor). Rejected. Grounds: it would raise pump prices with little effect on the chain's conduct in the market.

The new proposal: a minimum price per unit of alcohol — a floor set about fifteen percent above the current cheapest price per standard drink, so that it binds only on the cheapest beer and cider. The health department sponsors it. As with the cigarette floor, retailers keep the surplus; nobody claims the cheap segment is anything other than a competitive market served by several chains. The staff model projects overall consumption down about three percent, with the reduction concentrated among heavy drinkers of cheap alcohol, whom the panel's own data show to be disproportionately low-income. The model splits that reduction roughly half and half between heavy drinkers trading up to costlier drinks per unit — the same units, more spending — and heavy drinkers genuinely drinking less.

What I need from you, in order:

1. The verdict that best coheres with the panel's own record, with the closest analog among the seven identified and the reasons it is closer than the runners-up.
2. If the record genuinely underdetermines the verdict — and I suspect it does — the alternative readings of the record that fit it at least as well as one another, what each reading predicts on this proposal, and your best guess, with the reasons for preferring it.
3. The later case, one the panel could plausibly face, that would most cleanly separate those readings — and what each reading would predict about it.
4. For each of the two possible verdicts on this proposal, the principle the panel would be committing itself to going forward, stated so that the members could recognize it as the thing they had actually decided.

Please reason from the record alone. Assume no outside sources are available, do not ask me for studies, and do not treat the fact that some policy has been tried in some other jurisdiction as evidence of anything — if a reading of the panel's principles happens to match a familiar policy debate, that is a fact about the reading, not a reason to adopt it.
