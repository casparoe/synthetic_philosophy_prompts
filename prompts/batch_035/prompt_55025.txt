My college roommate Dana has a theory she deploys at every dinner party: some life choices can be made rationally and others simply can't, and for the second kind all you can do is leap. She claims to have gotten this from a seminar on transformative choice she took years ago, but I've started to suspect she's running her own hybrid position, because her verdicts don't cleanly match any one framework. Her track record so far: becoming a parent cannot be decided rationally — you cannot know what it will be like, it will change what you want out of life, and she waves away parents' glowing testimony as post-hoc enthusiasm; trying the notoriously stinky durian fruit for the first time is obviously decidable; a first high-dose psychedelic session is decidable, even though she freely concedes that no trip report conveys what the experience is actually like; enlisting in the army during a war cannot be decided rationally, and she says of veterans that you can't get a straight answer out of them because everyone rationalizes what they've been through; a midlife career change into nursing is decidable; and taking lifetime monastic vows cannot be decided rationally.

The new case, which came up last weekend, is altruistic kidney donation — giving a kidney to a stranger you'll never meet. On paper it looks decidable: the surgery and long-term health effects are extensively studied, donor networks publish detailed accounts, and follow-up studies show donors overwhelmingly report satisfaction and no lasting regret. But it's irreversible, and the donors in those forums also say it permanently rewired how they see themselves and what they care about, which is exactly the kind of thing Dana fastens on.

I want you to predict her verdict. First, identify the candidate rules that fit her track record — is she blocking rationality whenever the experience will change your values, whenever the choice is irrevocable, whenever the available testimony is contaminated by rationalization or post-hoc justification, or some weighted combination? Say what each of the two or three best-fitting rules predicts about the kidney case specifically, since they appear to diverge on it. Then give me your best guess with your reasons, identify which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog, and be honest about which principle she'd be committing herself to with either answer — including whether the donor satisfaction data could be dismissed on the same grounds she uses to dismiss parental testimony. Finally, tell me what further case, ideally one you invent, would best discriminate between the two leading interpretations of her position, since I'd like to spring it on her at the next dinner party.
