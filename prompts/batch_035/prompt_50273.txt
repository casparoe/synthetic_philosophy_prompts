I am the senior class president at my high school, and next week I have to give a ten-minute address at an all-school assembly about a cheating scandal: someone photographed the answer key for the junior chemistry midterm and leaked it into a class group chat, and by now about thirty students have either used it or quietly passed it along. The administration asked me to speak because, as they put it, the students will actually listen to me.

Here is my problem. Last spring I submitted a take-home essay that a friend had written the better part of, in violation of the same honor code I would be standing up there defending. No one ever found out; only the two of us know. When I sat down to draft the speech, I found that I genuinely don't know what I am entitled to say from that stage. My ethics teacher once mentioned that philosophers distinguish between a criticism being true and the critic being in a position to make it — that a hypocrite's complaint isn't false, exactly, but something about it is illegitimate. I don't know the literature behind this; I'm seventeen. But I think my speech lives or dies on that distinction, so I need it worked out concretely enough to shape actual sentences.

Please write the speech. It has to name the cheating as wrong, speak to the students who used the key and to the ones who only watched it circulate, and be deliverable by me — someone who has done a version of the same thing and gotten away with it. You will need to decide several things on my behalf: whether I should confess from the stage, and if so, whether a confession delivered to five hundred people is an act of honesty or a performance of it; whether the ritual disclaimer — the "I'm not perfect either" paragraph — restores my standing or is just a way of purchasing it at a discount; and whether it is possible to condemn the act without claiming any authority over the people who committed it. If you think the honest move is to aim the speech at the practice rather than at the students — to describe what the leak did to the class, to the teachers' trust, to the curve, without blaming anyone sitting in that gym — then say so plainly, but also tell me what that costs, because the juniors will hear it as a dodge, and they will be right to.

The audience is roughly five hundred teenagers and twenty teachers. It needs to be serious without being a sermon, and it cannot rely on any authority I don't have. After the speech itself, please add a short note explaining the choices you made about my standing to blame: which lines are doing blame, which are doing something adjacent that I am hoping will pass for it, and what you think happens to the speech if someone in the audience knows what I did last spring and raises a hand during the question period.
