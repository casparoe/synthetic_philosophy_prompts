I keep a running list of my own gut verdicts on variants of Molyneux's problem, and I would like someone to tell me what principle, if any, captures them. The original question — posed by Molyneux and made famous by Locke — concerned a man blind from birth who knows a cube and a sphere intimately by touch: if his sight were restored, could he tell which was which before ever touching them? I am not asking for a tour of what Locke, Berkeley, Leibniz, and Reid each said, and I am not asking for references. I want my own case-by-case judgments treated as data, in the manner of reflective equilibrium, and an answer that supplies the principle behind them, applies it forward, and tells me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save.

Here are the cases, with my calls:

1. The classic case: static view, cube and sphere on a table, no touching yet. He cannot tell which is which.
2. The same man, same moment, but the objects are a sphere and a many-pointed metal star. I think he can pick out the spiky one — it looks like something that would hurt to grab.
3. Cube and sphere again, but he is allowed to move: to walk around the objects, or they slowly rotate on the table. Now I think he can tell them apart by sight alone, before touching — which makes me suspect the problem was never about correlating sight with touch, but only about how much one can see from a fixed, frontal view.
4. While blind he handled two cups, one warm, one cool; they look identical and sit across the room. Asked which is warm, by sight alone: he cannot. (Stipulate no visible steam or glow.)
5. A man who could see until age two, then blind for thirty-eight years, sight restored at forty: he can, and I do not know whether to credit faint stored images or quick relearning.
6. A congenitally blind geometer of the kind Reid imagined, who has proved theorems about surfaces of revolution and knows exactly which surfaces have constant curvature: still cannot. Conceptual mastery seems not to enter the channel at all.
7. A solid ball versus a ring of the same outer size. I think he can identify the ring, and my reason feels like my reason in case 2 — the hole is visible as the place a finger would pass through.

Notice that 1 and 2 sit badly together: both are shape identification, touch first then sight, at the very first moment, and yet I call the second one a success. And 3 threatens to relocate the whole question. My tentative principle is this: what transfers at first sight is exactly what touch already encodes in terms of the perceiver's own possible actions, together with what vision can encode about how appearances transform under his own movement. A spike is where moving meets resistance and pain; a hole is where a finger passes through; a walked-around object displays, in vision, the same changes a tracing hand would feel. Warmth, in touch, is a passive sensation with no counterpart in anything he would do, and the geometer's knowledge is not sensory at all. This gets me verdicts 2, 4, 6, and 7, and reframes 1 as a fact about impoverished static input. But it makes me uneasy in two ways: it may quietly expand into the claim that all genuine perception is enactive, which seems far too strong; and I cannot decide whether it preserves verdict 3 in the way I intend, or explains it away.

So here is what I would like from you. First, state the principle as precisely as you can, clause by clause, naming the case that motivates each clause, and repair it where it stumbles — for instance, on whether it can accept the surgical finding from India, where children who gained sight after years of congenital blindness initially failed to match seen objects with felt ones but improved markedly within days. Second, tell me what the principle predicts for cases I have not listed, especially two: the slope case (of two ramps he climbed repeatedly while blind, can he tell by sight alone which is steeper) and the face case (he has traced his mother's face with his hands since childhood; on the day his sight arrives, can he pick her out of a lineup). Third, tell me plainly which of my seven verdicts must go if a simpler principle is right — for example, that no cross-modal identification at first sight ever occurs, and my verdicts in 2 and 7 are illusions of emotional salience, with 3 not far behind. If you think no single principle can capture the pattern, say so, offer the two or three best candidates, and identify exactly which verdict each one sacrifices.
