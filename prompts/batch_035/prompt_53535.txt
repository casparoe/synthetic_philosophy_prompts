A friend recommended a history-of-philosophy podcast episode on pragmatism that I suspect flattens some important distinctions, and I want to know how badly before I repeat any of it in a seminar paper. The host's segment on the pragmatist theory of truth went roughly like this (I've transcribed his main points from memory, so the wording is close if not exact):

"Peirce and James agreed on the core idea: truth isn't correspondence to reality, it's whatever inquiry and practice settle on. Peirce's version defines truth as whatever opinion the community of scientific investigators is destined to converge on, which really means that truth is just scientific consensus — whatever the experts eventually all agree on is thereby true, and if they converged on something false it would be true anyway. James took the same insight and made it more honest: a belief is true exactly to the extent that holding it is useful to the believer. So if believing in God comforts you, that belief is true for you. Notice that neither version leaves any room for reality to push back on inquiry — the pragmatists give up on the world mattering to truth altogether. The obvious consequence is relativism: two people for whom different beliefs are useful have different truths. Peirce saw this coming, and it's why he repudiated pragmatism late in his career — he disowned the whole approach once he realized where it led."

Something about this feels off. My friend says the Peirce part in particular is doing something different from the James part, and I've also heard that James's actual position had qualifications the host seems to have dropped. But the host is a professional historian of ideas, so I don't want to dismiss his summary just because it's unflattering.

Please audit this passage for me. Go claim by claim and separate what a careful scholar of pragmatism would accept, what they'd wince at as a common oversimplification, and what is straightforwardly wrong. I'm especially interested in: whether Peirce's convergence account and James's usefulness account really are the same theory, or whether keeping them apart changes the story; whether the host's reading of Peirce — truth as whatever scientists happen to agree on — captures Peirce's view or distorts it, and what Peirce's own commitments about the real would say about a false consensus; what James actually required of a belief before its usefulness made any difference to its truth status; whether the relativism charge follows once the accurate parts are kept; and finally, whether the host's account of why Peirce turned against the word pragmatism (as opposed to the doctrine) is itself a misreading.
