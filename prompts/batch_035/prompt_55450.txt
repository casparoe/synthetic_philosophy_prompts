My friend Talia has never read any confirmation theory, but she has firm instincts about evidence for the hypothesis that all ravens are black, and so far her verdicts have been steady. Seeing a black raven while out birdwatching: confirms the hypothesis, and strongly. A wildlife photographer's photo of an albino raven: refutes it, full stop. Inspecting white shoes on a factory tour: confirms nothing whatsoever, and she finds the suggestion funny. An ornithologist's exhaustive census of an island's raven colony, all black: confirms strongly. And when I pressed her on the fact that the albino raven surfaced only because a genetics lab was deliberately collecting unpigmented birds — a procedure that could never have turned up a black raven — she was unmoved: it refutes regardless. Next week she starts volunteering at a natural history museum, cataloguing a large unsorted drawer of specimens: songbirds, corvids including ravens, beetles, and pressed plants, all mixed together. She will draw specimens one at a time. Predict her verdict on whether drawing, say, a white dove from that drawer confirms that all ravens are black. I care less about the answer than about the rules she seems to be running: at least two plausible rules fit all five of her verdicts, and they diverge on the dove. So lay out the candidate rules, say what each predicts for the new case, give your best guess about her verdict together with the reasons for preferring that rule, and identify the further case — beyond the dove — that would most cleanly tell the candidate rules apart, including any two rules that agree on everything so far but would come apart under the right sort of hidden complication.
