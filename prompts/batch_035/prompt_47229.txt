Role assignment: you are a professional epistemologist who specializes in social epistemology and has spent years thinking about testimony. I'm a retired geophysicist (35 years of argon-argon dating, so please don't be shy about getting technical) who got into philosophy about two years ago after retiring, and I've been chewing on the reductionism vs. anti-reductionism debate about testimonial justification. As a lab guy my instinct is reductionist — show me the track record or I don't believe you — but the arguments against it keep nagging at me, so I want to see the views forced apart by a concrete case rather than by definitions. Most of what I've read recycles the same two or three scenarios (stranded traveler in an unfamiliar country, the small child, that whole family), and by now I can recite the verdicts from memory, so I want something fresh.

Here's the assignment. Construct a thought experiment from ordinary life — something like a first week in an amateur naturalist club, a new neighbor, a specialist appointment, your choice — engineered so that three views deliver three different verdicts: (1) global reductionism, where justification from testimony always bottoms out in the hearer's own inductive evidence about the speaker's or the domain's reliability; (2) local reductionism, where there's a default entitlement to trust within domains where testimony is basically our only possible source (history, geography, what people had for lunch), but positive reasons are required once there's a defeater or where we could in principle check for ourselves; and (3) anti-reductionism, where there's a general prima facie entitlement to trust whatever we're told, defeasible but not restricted by domain. The scenario should contain at least two assertions that pull the views apart — ideally one where all three disagree, and one where only two of them do. Hard constraints: the speaker must be sincere and actually correct, so the fight is purely about justification and not about truth; the verdicts must fall out of the theories rather than being stipulated ("and obviously he had no reason to believe her" is cheating); and no desert islands, no uncontacted tribes, no memory-erasing scenarios.

Please think carefully and start with a short brainstorm of where the three views actually diverge before writing the case — write down the design of the experiment before building the apparutus, as I'd put it. Then present the scenario, walk through what each view says about each assertion and why, identify which feature of the case is doing the work, tell me what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed, and give me the best attack a defender of whichever view comes out worst would make on the case. One more thing: my hunch is that once you allow defeaters on both sides, local reductionism and a moderate anti-reductionism get squeezed so close together that no everyday case can split them — please tell me honestly whether that's right or whether I'm missing a divergence, and if you think they really can be split, the case you build is where it should show up.
