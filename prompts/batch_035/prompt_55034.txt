I mostly write about science and ideas, but I read a ton of political coverage, and there's this habit in it that keeps bugging me: every party, politician, and voter gets located on one axis, left to right. Pollsters ask people to place themselves on a 0-10 scale, newspapers deploy "left-wing" and "right-wing" like they're basic physical properties, the way I'd use mass or charge in a physics story, and "centrist" gets used as a compliment, as if moving toward the middle of this single axis is the same thing as being reasonable. Meanwhile the very same coverage is full of stuff the axis obviously can't handle: libertarians who are right-wing on economics and liberal on social questions, greens whose positions don't seem to come from either end, populists on the far left and far right who agree with each other about elites more than they agree with their supposed neighbors on their own side.

So I want to write a column about what this whole practice presupposes. Like: does pinning everyone to a single left-right axis commit you to the view that politics has one underlying dimension, that knowing where you stand on taxes lets me predict your view on immigration? Is the "far-left"/"far-right" talk even compatible with that, or does the fact that we give the two extremes different names while they often behave alike show the coverage is quietly confused? And what does calling someone a "moderate" presuppose — that the midpoint of the axis is somehow epistemically special, or just that it's crowded?

Reconstruct the implicit theory for me: what would have to be true about political belief for the practice to make sense, which rival pictures (politics as shifting coalitions with nothing underneath, politics as identity groups lining up) would it rule out, and where is the practice underdetermined — could you think the left-right distinction is basically fake and still find this style of coverage useful? Bonus points if you can predict what my editor will push back with, since she thinks the distinction is just obviously real.
