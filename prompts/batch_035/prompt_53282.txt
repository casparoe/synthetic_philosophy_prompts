A member of my philosophy of physics reading group maintains that Thomson's lamp is not merely under-described but outright impossible, and she has an argument I cannot fault, even though its conclusion seems to me to prove too much. As she presents it:

(1) In any physically possible arrangement, the state of a system at a time is fixed by the arrangement's complete physical specification of the system prior to that time, together with the laws.
(2) The Thomson arrangement admits a complete specification prior to t = 1: the lamp is on throughout [0, 1/2), off throughout [1/2, 3/4), on throughout [3/4, 7/8), and so on, the switching instants 1 − 1/2ⁿ converging to 1; the switching mechanism can be idealized so that each individual flip is unproblematic.
(3) As t approaches 1, the on/off states alternate and admit no limit.
(4) So nothing in the pre-1 specification, together with the laws, entails that the lamp is on at 1, and nothing entails that it is off at 1; the prior facts leave the state at 1 entirely open.
(5) Any completion of the description must therefore add, as an independent further fact, that the lamp is on at 1 or that it is off at 1 — a fact about the arrangement grounded in nothing else in it.
(6) A possible arrangement cannot contain a fact grounded in nothing; every state a system is in must be grounded in the remainder of the arrangement.
(7) Hence the Thomson arrangement is impossible: the supertask cannot be completed.

She is careful about what she is not claiming. She concedes that the familiar description is incomplete; her thesis is that the incompleteness is ineliminable — no completion is available that does not contain an ungrounded fact, and ungrounded facts cannot occur in a possible arrangement. I tried the obvious counterexample: a lamp that is on throughout [0, 1) and whose switch is flipped exactly once, at t = 1, so that it is off at 1 and thereafter. Read strictly, (1) says the pre-1 facts (a constant sequence of on-states) fix the state at 1, which would make the single flip impossible; since it plainly is not, (1) seemed to me to collapse. But she parries: in the single-flip case the flip at t = 1 is itself an event in the arrangement, so the state at 1 is fixed once the flip is included in the specification; in her case nothing is available to include, since the switching instants converge to 1 without reaching it — there is no last flip, and hence no event at 1 to do any fixing. The parry strikes me as sound, and that is what leaves me stuck.

For my part, I would most like to keep (1), which looks like nothing more than ordinary determinism, and I cannot accept the conclusion, since the same reasoning appears to show that no arrangement could have initial conditions: the state of a system at the first moment of any arrangement is exactly as ungrounded in prior facts as the lamp's state at 1. Which step should be rejected, why did that step look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost — for her, and for me?
