I'm part of a small reading group that has spent the past few months on meritocracy, and we keep getting stuck on a recurring disagreement: whether meritocratic institutions are justified by desert at all, or whether desert and merit are two different things that we lazily treat as one. I finally decided the honest move was to write down my actual gut verdicts on cases rather than the theory I think I ought to hold, and to see whether they add up to anything. Here they are. A brilliant colleague who works thirty-five hours a week and closes every deal gets the promotion over a grinding, mediocre colleague who works seventy: I'd make that call without hesitation, and yet I can't shake the sense that the grinder deserves something the promotion track isn't giving her. A trader whose fund happened to be positioned for a war nobody foresaw and who collects a seven-figure bonus: I think she deserves the payout, since that was the deal, but doesn't deserve the fortune, if that's even coherent. An heir to a fortune he did nothing to earn: he doesn't deserve it, though I'd stop well short of saying he has no right to it. Two equally hardworking students, one from a collapsing public school with a 1300, one from an elite prep school with a 1350: I'd take the first, even though the second worked no less hard, so apparently effort matters but the luck of one's inputs matters too. And a founder whose business succeeded even though ninety-nine out of a hundred equally competent people executing the same plan would have failed: I still think she deserves her success, which sits very badly with what I said about the trader.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures these verdicts -- and where it can't, I want to know which verdicts have to go, since I suspect at least the trader/founder pair is not jointly satisfiable. The case I keep going back and forth on is the author whose novel is identical in every world, but which only becomes a bestseller in the world where a talk-show host happens to mention it: does she deserve the success in that world and merely fail to get it in the others? That feels both obviously yes and obviously no depending on the hour.

Please reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the single best principle behind my verdicts, stated precisely; (2) the one verdict it captures only with real strain; (3) the repair, whether that is an added clause or the overturning of a verdict, and why; (4) what the repaired principle says about the author case; and (5) what it says about a case I haven't listed -- a programmer who lied about having a computer-science degree to get hired, then spent a decade demonstrably outperforming every honest colleague in the department, and whether she deserves the career she built on the lie.
