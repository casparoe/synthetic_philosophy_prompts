I serve on the editorial board of a university press, and I need your help drafting the decision memo for a dispute that has split us down the middle. I am asking you to act as a consulting philosopher with deep expertise in translation theory, the philosophy of language, and aesthetics — someone who has thought hard about what fidelity to a literary work can mean. Please do not jump straight to the recommendation: begin with a brief brainstorm of the competing considerations on each side, weigh them, and only then produce the memo. What I need most is the reasoning, visibly on display, not just a bottom line.

The case: our board must choose, at Thursday's meeting, between two completed and competent manuscripts for a new bilingual edition of Verlaine's Romances sans paroles, with the French on the facing page.

Manuscript A replicates the French formal apparatus as closely as English permits. The verse forms become English meters, the rhyme schemes — including Verlaine's interlaced and enclosed rhymes — are preserved, and the famous vowel music, like the drowned, sobbing o-sounds of the autumn song, is echoed through deliberate assonance. Where sense and music conflict, the translator chooses music, and does so openly, invoking Verlaine's own programmatic injunction in the Art poétique: "De la musique avant toute chose." Some lines consequently drift away from their French meaning, and the translator's preface defends this not as license but as a strict reading of what the poems are made of.

Manuscript B is in free verse, semantically exact, with clear annotations. Its translator argues that reproducing the constraints in English manufactures a foregrounded artifice that the originals never had: French verse conventions in the 1870s were a living background system readers processed as nearly invisible, whereas the same schemes executed in English announce themselves as a performance — so the "form-preserving" version actually alters the register it purports to preserve, recasting a fluid, evanescent poet as a metrical technician. On this view, copying the features of a form across languages does not copy the effect of the form, because that effect was indexed to the conventions of a particular literary culture at a particular moment.

Last night I argued about this for three hours with a close friend who is a poet, and we ended nowhere. Her position is essentially Hofstadter's in Le Ton beau de Marot: in poems like Verlaine's, form is not a container that meaning happens to occupy but a constituent of the meaning itself, so a semantically accurate free translation hands readers the propositional residue of the poems — the label with the wine poured out. She pointed to Hofstadter's long experiment of rendering one slight rhymed get-well note by Clément Marot in dozens of ways under wildly different formal constraints, arguing it demonstrates that form is portable in spirit when a translator treats it as an objective rather than a casualty. My counter was that Hofstadter's showcase is a tiny occasional bagatelle, and that scaling constraint-matching to a whole book risks converting lightness into monotony: preserve every local formal feature over a hundred pages and you may still lose the global effect, which depends on form operating as a barely noticed ground rather than as figure. She landed the last blow, which I cannot answer: I could not say what that global effect is if it is not, somehow, the sum of those features.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday. It should (1) recommend a concrete course of action — publish A, publish B, a hybrid arrangement (for example, one translator's text with the other's versions of selected poems appended, or a commissioned revision of one manuscript under constraints the board specifies), or rejection of both with guidance for a reissue; (2) state the criteria board members should actually apply when comparing the manuscripts poem by poem or section by section, rather than resting on a slogan about form versus content; and (3) treat the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly — if you side against my friend, say plainly what her position gets right that our decision should not forfeit. Finally, the memo should not avoid the question underlying everything: what could fidelity even mean for a work whose meaning is not separable from its form? Is the choice before us really a choice between two different kinds of infidelity, and if so, which infidelity is less damaging for the readers this edition is meant to serve — the general reader without French on the one hand, and the student with the facing-page French on the other? The memo will be read by people with no philosophical training, so keep the prose humane, but do not dumb down the argument.
