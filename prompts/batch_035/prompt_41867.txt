I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of action course that's cross-listed with our behavioral economics program, and this term we've been working through weakness of will alongside the psychology of discounting — in particular the way agents often prefer a larger, later reward when choosing in advance but flip toward a smaller, sooner reward as its availability becomes imminent, so that the person's valuations seem to reverse even though no new information has arrived. That framework got me noticing a family of real-world practices I'd like help thinking through philosophically.

The practices I mean are all commitment devices, and specifically the voluntary, legally or institutionally enforced ones. Casinos in most jurisdictions let you sign a self-exclusion form barring yourself from the premises for years or for life; the casino is then obliged to remove you if you're found on the floor, and in some places a gambler who relapses while self-excluded has even sued the casino for failing to enforce the ban. There are apps that block distracting websites for a period you set in advance, with the override key held by a friend or destroyed entirely; deposit contracts where you forfeit money to a cause you dislike if you fail to quit smoking; people freezing their credit card in a block of ice. What strikes me is what these practices seem to presuppose about who counts as the agent whose preferences matter. The setup appears to treat the calm, temporally distant self as the authoritative one — the one whose choice should bind the later, steeper-discounting self — which fits neatly with the diagnosis that hyperbolic preference reversals are failures rather than mere changes of mind. But I can't see why the distant self automatically wins: its preference is just the one formed when both rewards are heavily discounted, and some philosophers seem happy to say that the preference you have at the moment of temptation is as genuinely yours as any. There's also a puzzle about consistency: the same legal systems that enforce self-exclusion also refuse to enforce certain contracts on the ground that they were made against the promisor's real interests or authentic will, and we generally let people revoke decisions made about their future selves in other domains — yet here the whole point is that the later self cannot revoke. Is that coherent, or does it presuppose something substantive about akrasia — say, that the relapse-preference isn't fully endorsed, or that reflective preferences have a special authority?

Could you take on the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in philosophy of action and behavioral decision theory, and think carefully about this? I'd like you to start with some brainstorming: lay out the candidate theories of the self and of preference authority that could be implicit here (privileged-vantage views, endorsement hierarchies, mere predictions of future preference change, something about which preferences survive reflection), and then reconstruct what the practice actually presupposes — distinguishing what it strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, and identifying where the practice is philosophically underdetermined so that several rival theories would each license it. Then tell me what would have to change in these institutions if we instead assumed that each moment's preferences count equally, or that the tempted self is the authentic one. Finally, since I suspect there's genuine unclarity here, please say explicitly how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the point in your reconstruction you're least sure of — I'd rather know where the ground is soft than receive a polished verdict.
