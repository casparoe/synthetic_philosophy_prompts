I'm writing a seminar paper for a course in the philosophy of technology, and what I need is a genuine case analysis rather than another rehash of the literature. The theory is Langdon Winner's, from his 1980 essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" As I read it, the essay runs two distinct theses that usually get blended together in secondhand accounts: first, that people sometimes arrange technical systems deliberately so that a political question is settled by design rather than kept open for ongoing contest — his overpass and tomato-harvester examples, the overpass story having in any case been challenged on the historical evidence — and second, that some technologies are inherently political in the sense that they require or strongly favor a particular kind of social order around them, whatever anyone intends. I want to test both theses against an episode Winner himself never used.

The episode is containerization. The salient facts, none of them obscure: Malcom McLean loaded truck trailers onto a converted tanker that sailed from Newark in 1956; within fifteen years the ISO-standard steel box had collapsed the cost of seaborne freight, and over the following two decades longshore employment in the traditional ports of the United States and Britain fell by roughly an order of magnitude. The container was not designed by anyone with a political program — it is a corrugated box with standardized corner castings and a seal that takes the dockers' hands off the cargo — yet it ended the hiring-hall system and the gang structure of dock work, made it impossible for dockers' unions to control the pace of loading as they once had, and shifted traffic away from old port districts to new sites like Elizabeth and Felixstowe. Waterfront employers embraced it with visible enthusiasm. At the same time, politics kept intruding: the West Coast longshore union signed a modernization agreement in 1960 that traded labor peace for guaranteed royalties on container moves, and unions and governments in different ports extracted very different terms, so that the same hardware produced different political settlements in Oakland, London, and Rotterdam.

Here is what I want from you: a disciplined mapping of the episode onto Winner's two theses, with the theses kept separate. Which thesis, if either, does the container actually satisfy? If shipping lines and employers simply found the box useful for ends they already held politically, that looks like ordinary politics deploying a neutral tool — the boring reading under which the answer to Winner's title question is a shrug. But is a stronger reading available: did the standardization decisions settle, in design, questions — who controls the pace of work, how large the waterfront workforce is, where the work happens — that had previously been live political contests, so that contestation now has to route through an international standards body instead of a bargaining table? Then tell me honestly where Winner's framework is silent here. No single designer intended the outcome; the politics was distributed across shippers, standards committees, port authorities, and unions; and different ports got different settlements from the same artifact. Does the royalty-fund episode show that an artifact's politics can be renegotiated downstream, and if so, does that confirm Winner's second thesis or reveal that what has the politics is still the institutions, with the artifact serving merely as the object they fight over? Close by saying what the case does to the theory: confirmation, anomaly, or a category the essay never anticipated — emergent rather than designed or required politics — and be candid if the honest answer is that Winner's framework simply does not decide the question.
