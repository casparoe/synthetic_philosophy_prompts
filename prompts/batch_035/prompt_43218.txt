You're basically a veteran workshop facilitator with a background in philosophy of art -- you've run participatory exercises for non-philosophers alot, and you know how to make an idea show up in what people *do* rather than what they're told. I need you to design one for me. Think it through carefully -- ideally sketch two or three candidate structures first and tell me briefly why you settled on the one you did, then give me the full design.

Here's the setting: I run a philosophy club at a community college. Around 16 students, zero philosophy background, one 75-minute session, a classroom with movable tables and a whiteboard. The idea I want them to feel in their bones: we treat the value of an original as something over and above its perceivable qualities, and this shows up most clearly when the copy is *perfect* -- indistinguishable in every observable respect. On the page, students just say "of course the original is worth more, it has the history," but they say it flatly, like trivia. I want the activity to make them discover that they themselves price provenance, before anyone names the concept.

What I have in mind, roughly -- improve on this, dont just flesh it out: early in the session the students produce small original works themselves (quick doodles or collages, maybe 8 minutes, deliberately scrappy). Then some kind of market or auction with a token currency, where the lots include their originals AND duplicates of some of them -- near-perfect via photocopier plus a bit of re-inking if we do images, or literally perfect if we do haiku/short texts written on identical cards. Two valuation rounds: first with provenance hidden, then revealed. Plus a trade moment where an owner is offered their own piece swapped for its duplicate plus a bonus. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the philosophical distinctions -- perceivable properties vs causal history, why the market gap can't come from anything you can see with your eyes -- and can touch on the autographic/allographic distinction from Goodman if that helps, though the students should hit the intuition before hearing any of that. No lecture up front, and nobody hears the word "forgery" until the debrief.

Deliverables: rules, a materials list, a timeline for the 75 minutes, a payoff scheme that works without real money (tokens, a silly trophy, something with just enough sting), a debrief script that maps moments to ideas, an honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guesses: flat bidding because nobody cares about classmates' doodles; a couple of students strategically bidding on copies to prove they're above all this; owners refusing the swap out of pure sentimentality, which is a confound I actually want you to address head-on), and a frank note on which feature of the real debate the activity simply cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply myself at the end. The one I'm already worried about: real forgery cases involve works with art-historical weight and a betrayal-shock that lands years after the acquisition, whereas my students know copies exist going in.

One more ask: in the debrief material, keep a clean separation between what's basically settled among people who know this literature and what's genuinely contested. For example, dont present the view that a perfect forgery is aesthetically worth just as much as the original as though it were consensus -- I gather it isn't, so flag the live positions as live. And if part of your design leans on a contested reading of any of the ideas, say so explicitly.
