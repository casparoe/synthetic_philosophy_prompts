My brother, who is doing a master's in cognitive science, has been pressing Dennett's four kinds of minds on me since my daughter was born a few months ago, and over Christmas he gave me his summary of the idea. I would like to retell it at my philosophy reading group, but not before I know how much of his version to trust. As I understood him, the picture is this: Dennett arranges minds in a tower of four storeys. Darwinian creatures have all their behaviour hard-wired by their genes; the only way they improve is by natural selection weeding out failures over many generations, and since nothing they do during their lifetimes can be revised, they are stuck with whatever they are born with. Skinnerian creatures are the first to learn within their own lifetimes, by trial and error, keeping whatever behaviour gets rewarded, and because individual learning is so much faster than natural selection, they outcompete purely Darwinian species whenever the two come into conflict. Popperian creatures go one better and test their plans systematically, rather as a scientist tests a theory, so that bad options are eliminated in advance and no risky behaviour need ever be attempted blind — Dennett borrows Popper's quip that such creatures can let their hypotheses die in their stead. Gregorian creatures, at the top, are those equipped with language, which Dennett regards as the only genuine mind-tool, and whose arrival in a species marks the point at which thought becomes conscious. My brother added that since the tower runs from simple to complex, a human baby can be watched climbing it in real time: starting out as a purely Darwinian creature, then conditioning like a Skinnerian, and so on up to language.

I should say that I found this compelling and am inclined to accept it wholesale, which is exactly why I would like another pair of eyes on it. Is this a fair summary of Dennett? Please structure your reply as follows. (1) Verdict: one short paragraph, no more than a hundred words, on how fair the summary is overall. (2) What my brother got right: roughly a hundred and fifty words on the parts a careful reader of Dennett would accept without complaint. (3) Where it goes wrong: the longest section, about four hundred words, taking the claims one by one — the outcompete claim, the characterisation of Popperian creatures, the claims about language and consciousness, and the developmental reading of the tower — and saying for each what Dennett actually holds and where the summary misrepresents, conflates or overstates. (4) What I can safely retell: roughly a hundred and fifty words on what survives the corrections, so that I can present the idea to my reading group without passing on my brother's errors.
