I'm a PhD student in economic history, and something about the professional norms of my discipline has started to bother me. Journals essentially never publish counterfactual narrative history — the "what if Napoleon had won at Waterloo" genre is treated as popular entertainment, not scholarship — and we're explicitly trained to avoid speculating about what didn't happen. Yet causal claims are the very currency of the field: we assert that the blockade caused the famine, that monetary policy tipped the country into depression, that the reform was doomed no matter who held office. Philosophers typically analyze causation counterfactually — c caused e only if, had c not occurred, e would not have either — so every causal claim seems to smuggle in a commitment to facts about non-actual courses of events. And we grade counterfactuals constantly without acknowledging it: calling a battle "a close-run thing" or a crisis "avoidable" ranks nearby ways history could have gone. My question is what view of possibility the discipline's practice presupposes. Does the taboo on counterfactual history require denying that there are determinate facts about what would have happened — something a modal realist like David Lewis, for whom possible worlds are exactly as real as the actual one, would reject outright — or is it merely an epistemic norm about what can be evidenced, compatible with any metaphysics of modality? Is the discipline internally consistent, given that its causal claims appear to rest on the very counterfactuals it refuses to assert? And is the line between licit explanatory counterfactuals embedded in causal claims and illicit standalone counterfactual narrative principled, or an aesthetic taboo misdescribed as methodological rigor? Please take a definite position: I want your actual verdict on whether the discipline's commitments are consistent, and on which view of modality it strictly needs, as opposed to merely tolerates.
