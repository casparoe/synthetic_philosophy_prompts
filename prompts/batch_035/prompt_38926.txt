"The lottery paradox, due to Kyburg, comes from three individually plausible claims: (1) if a proposition is probable enough (say .99), it is rational to accept it; (2) it is rational to accept the logical consequences of what one rationally accepts; (3) rational acceptance must be consistent. Consider a fair lottery with 1000 tickets. For each ticket, the probability that it loses is .999, so by (1) it is rational to accept of each ticket that it loses. By (2) it is rational to accept that every ticket loses. But it's also rational to accept that some ticket wins (probability 1), so (3) is violated. Hence the three principles can't all be true, and the paradox is which one to give up.

The preface paradox, also due to Kyburg, has the same structure. An author has carefully checked every claim in her book, so she rationally believes each claim. But she also rationally believes, from her experience that no nontrivial book is error-free, that at least one claim in it is false. Since rational belief is closed under conjunction, she rationally believes a contradiction. This is just the lottery paradox again, with book claims in place of tickets — the evidence differs but the logic is identical.

The best solution is to reject (2) for conjunction: rational acceptance is not agglomerative. The author can rationally believe each claim and rationally believe that at least one is false, because joining beliefs into a conjunction is a further step that rationality doesn't require. This solves both paradoxes at a stroke, and we get to keep the threshold rule and consistency, which are the two principles that do the real work in our epistemic lives."
