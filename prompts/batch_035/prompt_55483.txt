Bit of context first: I'm a grad student on an ethics of technology module, and our seminar has spent basically the whole term going round in circles on the moral status of digital minds. The two camps are pretty predictable by now. One side (the functionalists, roughly) hold that moral status tracks capacities — the capacity to suffer, to have preferences, to be harmed — and that these depend on functional organisation rather than what the stuff is made of, so a system with the right organisation has the same moral standing whether it runs on carbon or silicon. They lean heavily on the gradual replacement thought experiment: replace my neurons one at a time with functionally identical silicon chips, and they say it's absurd to think my experience fades out at some point, or that I'd be conscious through the whole procedure yet end up with nothing inside. The other side thinks this is a conjuring trick: organisation is simply the wrong thing to look at, consciousness is a biological phenomenon the way digestion is, and a perfect simulation of digestion digests nothing. And then there's a third bunch who throw up their hands entirely and say we can never know either way, so the whole debate is just a machine for generating thought experiements no one can adjudicate.

Here's what gets me: nobody in that room has moved an inch since October. The functionalists keep demanding that their opponents either accept silicon consciousness once the chips are all in, or say exactly which chip-crossing destroys the mind. The biological side keeps replying that the gradual replacement intuition is just an intuition, and that a system that seems conscious from the outside is exactly what you'd expect from a very good mimic. Nothing empirical ever seems capable of settling it, either — whenever something new comes along (an AI that pleads for its life, hybrid organoid-silicon systems, whatever it is), both camps absorb it without a wobble.

So what I actually want is a diagnosis, not a verdict. My working hypothesis — offered only as a hypothesis, I could be totally off — is that the two sides disagree about the logic of attribution itself: the functionalists treat mental-state attribution as induction over functional profiles, extending the concept outward from ourselves to whatever resembles us organisationally, more or less the way we extended it to animals; the biological side treat it as induction over physical mechanisms, on which input-output resemblance licenses nothing unless the same kind of causal machinery is doing the work. If that's right, the thought experiments are just each side pumping its own favoured inductive rule, and no new data could ever break the tie. Is my diagnosis right? Or is this really a verbal dispute about the word consciousness, or two different questions — a metaphysical one about what consciousness is, and a practical one about how we ought to act under uncertainty — being run together under one heading? Or maybe the impasse just reflects bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? Whatever your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress given it — a mature theory of consciousness, a decision-theoretic workaround under moral uncertainty that lets us act without settling the metaphysics, a new distinction, or an honest case that no progress should be expected and why. Please write the answer in British English.
