Next month I'm giving a departmental colloquium defending a two-part thesis about the Frege–Geach problem. First, it's misdiagnosed as a problem about moral language specifically: it's the local face of a general challenge for any semantics on which some embedded clauses do not express plain truth-apt contents, and attenuated versions of the same trouble surface for epistemic modals, probability operators, and mixed conditionals — wherever the relevant generalization of modus ponens is no longer validity-preserving in the classical sense. Second, the way out is a "force-neutral" expressivism on which the attitude-ascriptions get compositional semantic values of the same type as ordinary propositions, so that moral and descriptive clauses embed uniformly and the validity of moral arguments is recovered structurally rather than by stipulation. The audience is mostly formal semanticists and philosophy-of-language people who will want to see the compositionality details made precise, plus two committed expressivists who know the Blackburn- and Gibbard-style machinery inside out and will not accept that my view is a genuine alternative rather than a relabeled one, and one senior logician who is quietly sceptical that anything non-descriptive can be given a coherent model-theoretic semantics at all. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way this audience would phrase it — with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Say explicitly what I should concede up front, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Finally, tell me which single reply has to land, in your judgment, for the talk to count as a success with this particular crowd.
