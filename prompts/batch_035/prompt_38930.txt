my flatmate and I had a proper row last night about whether you can ever give someone rational advice about life-changing choices, and I want to turn the fallout into something useful. She's adamant that when the stakes are high you should weigh everything up carefully, make the expected-value call, and that's just what good advice is. I kept coming back to this idea I half-remember from a philosophy course years ago that for genuinely life-changing stuff — becoming a parent, going to war, emigrating — the decision theory breaks down, because you literally cannot know what the outcome will be like from where you stand now, so you can't assign the utilities you'd need. She thinks that's a cop-out that licenses doing whatever you fancy. By the end we sort of agreed the interesting question isn't whether the theory fails but what an actual advisor should *do* on Tuesday morning with the theory in that state.

Here's my situation. I'm a careers adviser at a mid-sized university, and about a third of my caseload is what I've started privately calling the unrepeatable decisions: a final-year engineer who wants to drop out and apprentice as a chef, a student deciding whether to take a term off to care for her dying father, one who's been offered officer training in the army, one who's pregnant and deciding whether to keep it while doing her PhD. I have thirty minutes with each of them, maybe three meetings total, and they mostly arrive already leaning one way and wanting me to either bless it or stop them. The expected-value playbook my training gave me (list pros and cons, weight them, pick the bigger number) feels actively wrong for these, but I can't just throw up my hands and say philosophy says it's unknowable — that's useless and also not true of all of it.

So: please turn the theory of transformative choice into a procedure I can actually run in a thirty-minute meeting. Questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says no (i.e., this isn't a decision where the hard problem even arises, so do the ordinary thing), and where it has to hand the decision back because what remains is a values question no procedure can settle. Include the distinctions that must survive the simplification — e.g. whether the change is mainly to what you'll know versus what you'll want, and whether other people's testimony can substitute for first-hand experience, and when it can't — and be honest about the steps that are judgement calls dressed up as a checklist. Then run it on three of my real-ish cases: the chef dropout, the army officer, and the pregnant PhD student. On at least one of these the procedure is going to give me an answer I won't like, and I want to see that happen rather than be protected from it.

Format please: sections named "What the procedure keeps and drops" (~200 words, on which bits of the philosophy survive and which you've cut for time), "The procedure itself" (the checklist, questions in order), "The three cases" (worked through, one per paragraph), and "Where it misfires" (~150 words on failure modes and the cases it just punts on). End with a short "Confidence" note: say which parts you're confident are faithful to the actual philosophy, and single out the one point in the procedure you're least sure about. Also, small thing — tell me which of the three cases you're least confident about running, and why.
