I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on formal decision theory, and we've just finished a three-week unit on imprecise probabilities. I'm drafting a term paper on the question of whether an agent with imprecise credences needs a decision rule over and above the representation itself, and my paper needs a worked example as its centerpiece. I'd like you to construct that example to specification.

First, the setup as I understand it, so we're working from the same page (and please flag it if I've misstated anything, since a nonstandard definition would sink the paper): an imprecise probabilist represents an agent's credences not by a single probability function but by a set C of them, taken to be appropriate when the evidence is too sparse or too conflicting to pin down one distribution. Two candidate decision rules for such an agent. Γ-maximin ranks each act by its lower expected utility — the minimum, over every probability function in C, of that act's expected utility — and deems choiceworthy any act whose lower expectation is not exceeded by any rival's. Maximality works differently: an act is inadmissible just in case some rival act beats it on every function in C, and everything that survives this test remains choiceworthy. The two rules are not equivalent, and the difference is what my paper is about.

Your task: construct a decision problem that isolates their disagreement. Specifications:

1. A realistic setting — a farmer choosing among crop varieties ahead of an uncertain season, a homeowner weighing flood coverage, a patient choosing between treatments. No Ellsberg urns, no demons, and no artificial chance device generating the ambiguity; the ambiguity should come from conflicting or sparse real-world evidence, such as two credible forecasting models that give different probabilities for the same weather outcome.
2. Exactly three feasible acts, two states of nature, and a credal set you specify explicitly (an interval of values for the probability of one state is fine). Utilities should be dollar amounts or final wealth, so that the disagreement between the rules cannot be explained away as ordinary risk aversion.
3. The verdict structure: exactly one act must be selected by Γ-maximin; exactly two acts must be admissible by maximality; and the third act must be excluded by both rules, so the case doesn't cheat by making everything permissible. The act in the gap between the rules — permitted by maximality but excluded by Γ-maximin — is the whole point of the paper, so it must be a live option a reasonable person could choose, not an obvious blunder.
4. All numbers small enough that the verdicts can be verified by hand.

The demonstration matters as much as the scenario, and the verdicts must fall out of the numbers rather than be stipulated. So after presenting the case: derive each act's expected utility as a function of the probability of one state, over the credal interval; report the lower expectations; verify each rule's verdicts act by act; and state which act, if any, is E-admissible (that is, maximizes expected utility relative to at least one function in C), noting whether E-admissibility coincides with maximality in this case or comes apart. Then identify precisely which structural feature of the case generates the gap between Γ-maximin and maximality, and run the removal test: if that feature is stripped out — for instance, if the credal set collapses to a single distribution — both rules should reduce to ordinary expected-utility maximization and the gap should vanish. Confirm that they do.

Close with three short paragraphs: (a) how a defender of Γ-maximin would argue that excluding the act in the gap is a feature rather than a bug, and what exactly the agent is being protected from; (b) how a defender of maximality would reply, in particular what she thinks Γ-maximin gets wrong about the significance of the priors on which the excluded act wins; and (c) what a precise Bayesian, who rejects credal sets altogether, would say about the whole exercise — for example, that the gap is an artifact of refusing to commit to a single prior.

One epistemic requirement I need you to respect throughout: keep a clear line between what is settled and what is contested. The formal verdicts, given the definitions and the numbers, are arithmetic, and you should state them flatly. But which decision rule an imprecise agent should use, whether imprecise credences form a coherent ideal in the first place, and whether the ambiguity aversion the case displays is rationally permissible are all genuinely open questions among people who work on this topic. Do not present any of these as the consensus view, and wherever a consideration you invoke presupposes one side of one of these debates, mark it as doing so.
