I've been reading about remedial-right theories of secession (Buchanan-style, where secession is only justified as a remedy for serious injustice) versus plebiscitary theories (where any territorial majority that votes for independence has a pro tanto right to it), and I want to test both against Scotland 2014. What's interesting to me is that the UK government just... agreed to the referendum via the Edinburgh Agreement, making it a case of state-consented secession procedure that neither theory seems built for -- the remedial theorist has to say Scotland lacked the right, the plebiscitary theorist has to say the right existed regardless and the consent was superfluous, and both feel wrong somehow. Analyze the episode through both theories: who had standing, what the franchise decisions (16-year-olds, resident non-Scots, expat Scots excluded) did to the "people" whose self-determination was at stake, and whether "The Vow"-style last-minute promises show the process was really about bargaining power rather than rights. Then tell me where each theory is just silent about a consensual negotiated path -- and does Catalonia 2017, where the state refused consent entirely, confirm that the theories only ever get tested in the hard cases?
