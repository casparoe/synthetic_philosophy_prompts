A friend who's way into physics gave me a pop-science book about what science actually tells us about reality, and there's a paragraph near the end that I keep coming back to but can't quite evaluate. Paraphrasing it from memory, it goes something like: Worrall came up with structural realism to rescue science from the pessimistic induction. When Maxwell's theory of electromagnetism replaced Fresnel's theory of light, the luminiferous ether got thrown out, but the equations survived essentially unchanged — so the moral is that science only ever gets the mathematics right. The physical story we tell about what's obeying those equations is scaffolding that gets torn down at every revolution, and the only thing that accumulates as knowledge is the web of relations between things. The book then pushes this further and says that since only structure is retained, an electron doesn't have any intrinsic nature at all — it just is its relations to other things — and that this is basically why modern physics is happy to be so math-first, since the math is tracking all there is to track. It ends by saying that if you take this seriously, the world is ultimately made of mathematics, which is presented as the natural conclusion of the whole book.

I found this weirdly compelling and also weirdly disturbing, but I have no idea if it's a fair rundown. People online keep mentioning there are two different versions of structural realism that got conflated at some point, and something called the Newman objection that supposedly embarrasses one of them, but the book doesn't mention either. So: you're a philosopher of science, please think carefully and actually brainstorm for a bit before answering — in particular, brainstorm the different things someone might mean by "structure" here (equations? relations? uninterpreted formalism?) because I suspect that's where the paragraph quietly slides from one thing to another.
