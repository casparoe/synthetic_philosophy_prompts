I'm TAing an intermediate game theory course and helping grade the midterm, and I want a second opinion on one answer before i commit to a score. The question was: "Consider a finite two-player game in normal form. Repeatedly delete pure strategies as follows: in each round, delete any pure strategy that is strictly dominated by some mixed strategy over the strategies that have survived so far, where strictly dominated means the mixed strategy yields a strictly higher expected payoff against every surviving pure strategy of the opponent. Continue until no surviving strategy is dominated in this sense. (a) Does the final set of surviving strategies depend on the order in which we delete them? (b) Does every Nash equilibrium (in mixed strategies) of the original game survive this process, and can the process be used to narrow down the set of equilibria? (c) What would players have to know, and know about each other, for this deletion process to be a plausible model of how they actually reason?"

Here is the student's answer, copied verbatim: "(a) The final set does not depend on the order. Deleting a strategy just removes a row or a column; it never changes the payoffs between the strategies that remain. So a dominance relation that exists at any round exists at every round, and deleting can never create a new dominance relation between strategies that are still there. Hence every deletion that ever happens will eventually happen no matter what order you go in, and the final set is the same. (b) Every Nash equilibrium survives. In a mixed equilibrium, each strategy in the support of a player's mixture must be a best response to the opponent's mixture, and a strictly dominated strategy is never a best response to anything, so no equilibrium puts positive probability on a strategy that is ever deleted. So the process can only narrow down the set of equilibria, never destroy one, and it goes the other way too: whenever a game has a unique Nash equilibrium, this process will single it out, since anything surviving alongside the equilibrium would eventually have to be dominated. This is also why iterated deletion solves the prisoner's dilemma in one step. (c) All that's needed is that the players are rational and know their own payoffs. A rational player never plays a strictly dominated strategy regardless of what she believes about her opponent, so the deletion process requires no assumptions about beliefs about the opponent's beliefs or rationality — just rationality of each player, and she can see the same is true of her opponent, so the deletions are justified step by step."
