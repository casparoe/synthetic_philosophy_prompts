I've gotten drafted onto my university's building-services data committee — I'm the token philosopher, since our library is getting an energy retrofit and apparently someone thought that made me qualified. Anyway, the proposal on Thursday's docket: facilities wants occupancy sensors throughout the main library and in a small 24-hour graduate annex, sampling every ten minutes, to tune the HVAC. Their pitch leans hard on "it's just anonymous numbers, like a thermostat" — no cameras, no IDs, nothing ever attached to a person, just counts per zone. The grad students' union has objected (politely, but twice now) that the annex holds maybe thirty people on a good night, so some zones will sometimes contain exactly one person, and a time series of "one occupant in carrel row 3 at 3am, every night this week" is just a description of somebody's life even if no name is ever stored. Facilities calls this paranoid: if the data can't be linked to a name, no privacy interest is even implicated — the "nothing to hide" point, or possibly the "no identification, no privacy" point. They use the two interchangably in their memo and I'm honestly not sure they're the same claim.

Could you draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting? Take a concrete position — approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and if conditions, name them (coarser zones, minimum occupancy thresholds before a reading is logged, retention limits, whatever you actually think follows). Don't just restate the law or the definition of personally identifiable information; the committee has lawyers for that and they already wrote two pages of it. Engage the union's strongest version of the worry — roughly, that identifiability isn't a property of the data alone but of the data plus whoever ends up looking at it, and that habitual patterns can function as signatures even when the raw counts look innocuous — and also give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side: for most rooms most of the time, an occupancy count may really be as anonymous as a thermostat reading, the energy savings are real, and the committee has a carbon reduction target we're supposed to be helping with, not just a privacy charter.

Wherever you come down, I want you to say which principle about the value of privacy is actually doing the work in the recommendation — control over information about oneself, the interest in not being watched, something about information flowing into contexts it wasn't gathered for — rather than hiding behind "on balance" language in the final paragraph. One practical constraint worth keeping in mind: we can push the vendor to change how the system is configured (resolution, aggregation, what gets logged), but we can't realistically walk away from occupancy sensing altogether, so the memo's recommendation has to be something facilities could actually implement.
