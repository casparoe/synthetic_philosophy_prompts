I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on aesthetics, and for my final paper I have to defend a position on whether we can ever gain genuine aesthetic knowledge from testimony — whether a trusted friend's report that a film is moving, or a critic's claim that a poem is elegant, can put us in a position to know the work is moving or elegant, before we have experienced it ourselves. My instructor encourages us to develop our own angle rather than recycle the standard positions, so I want to build mine around a comparison I haven't seen discussed much: aesthetic testimony versus expert testimony about the taste of wine or the flavor profile of a dish. Sommeliers and critics describe experiences that most of us lack the trained vocabulary and perceptual apparatus to verify, and we routinely defer to them, and nobody seems to think this is philosophically troubling. If deference to the wine critic is fine, what exactly is supposed to be different about deference to the art critic? Conversely, if there really is something about first-person engagement that testimony cannot substitute for, the difference has to show up somewhere, and I suspect the wine case might actually be a hard case for my own sympathy toward testimony-based knowledge rather than an easy case for it. I also want to fold in a contemporary twist: recommendation algorithms that predict, with decent accuracy, that I will find a film beautiful or gripping — whether their outputs count as testimony at all is itself something I'd want you to address, since they are not reports of anyone's experience.

Write a polished essay that weighs the main considerations on both sides, treating the wine analogy as the pivot of the argument rather than a decorative aside, and engaging the algorithm case where it genuinely pressures or supports one side. Reach a definite verdict — it is fine for it to be conditional or scoped, but do not end on a non-committal note. I'd like you to state plainly, near the end, which single argument or consideration would be most likely to move you to the opposite verdict, and where your own reasoning is at its weakest. Please write it as continuous prose only, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables of any kind. Keep it at a length appropriate for a term paper's core argument, roughly twelve hundred to sixteen hundred words.
