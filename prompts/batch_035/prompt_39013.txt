My mother died eight months ago, and in the time since I have developed a habit I do not fully understand. Most evenings I rewatch one of a small set of films whose plots turn almost entirely on the death of a parent — films I had seen and merely liked before, and which I now find myself looking forward to all day. The enjoyment is unmistakable: it has the warm, appetitive quality of a favorite meal, not the grim dutifulness with which I go through her old letters. What unsettles me is that it began with the funeral and has grown steadily since; I like these films far more now than I ever did when grief was hypothetical. Two friends have offered diagnoses. One insists that what I am calling enjoyment must really be something else — mood repair, a dose of sadness that habituation makes bearable, or a retrospective glow that misremembers the experience as pleasant — and that genuine pleasure in this material would be perverse. The other says it is essentially exposure therapy: I am approaching the fear in manageable doses, and the enjoyment is just the felt signature of control. Neither diagnosis fits very well, for reasons I can partly articulate. First, I have tried documentaries about real bereavement — hospital footage, recorded last conversations — and I cannot sit through them; they only sicken me, and the difference does not feel like a difference of dose. Second, my favorite moments in these films are the unrewarding ones: the scenes where the grief goes unprocessed, where the protagonist is not consoled, where the music swells toward a resolution that never arrives. Whatever I am after, it does not seem to be the relief on the far side of the sorrow, and it does not seem to be safety, since I go in unarmed. Third, a quieter but more persistent worry: the enjoyment sometimes feels like a betrayal, as though taking pleasure in a mother's death — even a fictional one, even my mother's only by analogy — were a kind of disloyalty to hers. So I have two questions. What is the most plausible account of what I am enjoying, given that it is not comfort, not relief, not real-world information, and not obviously distance? I would like the competing explanations compared against the specific pattern I have described, with a clear statement of which features each one accommodates and which it has to explain away. And separately: is the disloyalty worry a distinct ethical question that survives once the enjoyment is explained, or does it dissolve once we understand what the pleasure actually is?
