The exam question read as follows:

**Question 4 (25 points).** Explain the scenario known as Pascal's mugging and why, if an agent maximizes expected utility under an unbounded utility function, it seems the agent must hand over the wallet. Explain what is supposed to make the case more troubling than an ordinary low-probability, high-payoff gamble. Then critically discuss one proposed response.

And here is the student's answer:

Pascal's mugging, a term coined by Nick Bostrom in a short 2009 paper, works like this: a stranger demands your wallet. Rather than threatening you with a knife, he claims to be able to bring about an astronomically vast number of extra happy days of life — and to do the reverse if you refuse. Whatever tiny credence you assign to his claim, as long as it is positive, the claimed payoff can be made large enough that the expected utility of complying exceeds that of refusing, so expected-utility theory says pay. The point is not the small probability itself but the unbounded utilities: for any payoff the mugger names, he can name a bigger one, so the products never fall below the cost of the wallet. With a bounded utility function the problem disappears, since beyond some point larger claimed payoffs add nothing to expected value.

The case is worse than an ordinary long-shot bet because the payoff figure is chosen adversarially: the number carries no information about the world, since the mugger would have said whatever number was needed to extract the wallet, so our credence cannot sensibly track the size of the claim. The scenario is essentially Pascal's wager with the infinite payoff replaced by a very large finite one, so the standard objections to the wager — that there may be many rival muggers, that expected-utility reasoning with very large numbers is unreliable — carry over directly.

The natural response is to bound one's utilities. The cost is refusing some bets with astronomically favorable odds, but that seems tolerable, and in fact bounding appears mandatory: de Blanc (2007) showed that an agent using a Solomonoff-style universal prior assigns probabilities that fall off faster than any payoff the mugger can describe, so for such agents the expected utilities converge and the mugging never gets a grip. An agent with unbounded utilities that failed to use such a prior would be irrational, so bounded utilities are rationally required; alternatively, one could simply assign the mugger's claim probability exactly zero.
