I've been going round in circles on the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, and I've decided the only way forward is to actually put numbers on things. Here's the proposition: the objection, in its strongest form, defeats classical utilitarianism — meaning total wellbeing is not the only thing that matters morally, and aggregating harms and benefits across persons is sometimes wrong precisely because it treats distinct lives as if they were one.

My flatmate puts this at 0.75 (she's basically Rawlsian about it), I'm hovering around 0.45, but I suspect we're actually disagreeing about several different things at once and papering over it. So give me your own credence, but the interesting part is the decomposition, not the number. Terms I can see in mine, though feel free to restructure: (a) that there's a genuine normative difference between trade-offs within a single life and trade-offs across lives, and not just a difference in psychological salience; (b) that Parfit's reductionism about personal identity undercuts the objection — if the boundaries between persons matter less than we thought, why should those boundaries generate strong constraints on aggregation; (c) that the objection doesn't prove too much, i.e. it doesn't condemn the ordinary case of imposing tiny burdens on millions of strangers to avert a catastrophic harm to one person, which nearly everyone thinks is fine; and (d) that no rule-consequentialist or constrained-aggregation rescue keeps enough of the spirit of utilitarianism to count as a win for it rather than a surrender.

Give me a rough probability and a line of justification for each term, then however you think they combine into the final credence. Then tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and try to locate the disagreement: is someone at 0.75 vs someone at 0.45 most plausibly split on (a), (b), (c), or (d)? And what argument or kind of case, if you encountered it, would move your number the most?

One constraint — I'm not interested in "it depends how you formulate utilitarianism". Formulate it however you think is fairest to the classical version, say briefly what you've assumed, and commit to the numbers.
