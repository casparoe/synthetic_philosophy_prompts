quick backstory: I agreed to help judge our city's annual photo contest (the real judge dropped out last minute and apparently I have "a good eye"). My co-judge and I keep fighting about one portfolio, and at a family dinner my brother-in-law, who did a philosophy MA, watched us go at it and then dropped this on me. It's some old complaint about Hume's standard of taste dressed up for photography, and I've been chewing on it since.

Here's the version I wrote down on the train home:

(1) For a verdict about beauty to be right or wrong — not just liked or disliked — there has to be a standard independent of anyone's actual verdicts.
(2) Hume's standard is basically: the beautiful is what the qualified critics converge on approving, where qualified means practiced, free of prejudice, delicate, widely comparing.
(3) But how do we tell who's qualified? Not by credential, since the whole point is their verdicts set the standard — and whether someone's taste really is delicate and unprejudiced shows itself partly in what they prefer. The traits are read off the verdicts, and the verdicts are validated by the traits.
(4) So "the good photos are the ones qualified judges pick" collapses into "the good photos are the ones picked by the people who pick the good photos." Circular.
(5) So Hume's objectivity about taste is a definitional trick, and my co-judge and I aren't disagreeing about anything — there's no fact of the matter about whose read on the portfolio is better.

The part that makes me think he's proving too much: run the same steps on medical diagnosis. "A good diagnostician is one who diagnoses correctly, and a correct diagnosis is one a good diagnostician makes" — by his logic medicine is circular and empty too. But my doctor isn't just expressing a preference when she says my knee is sprained. So the argument has to break somewhere, and I can't find where.

Which step should I give up? My gut says the circularity in (3)-(4) is somehow okay — it feels like this is just how expertise works everywhere — but my brother-in-law insists that in medicine there's an external check (the biopsy, whether the patient recovers) and in taste there just isn't one, so the circle never touches ground. Is that right? Is there anything in aesthetics that plays the role the biopsy plays — endurance over centuries, verdicts holding up across wildly different cultures, whatever — or is that just stacking up more judges, which would put us back at (4)?

Tell me which premise to reject and what rejecting it costs, including whether the medicine analogy holds or breaks somewhere I'm not seeing. And be upfront about where your own diagnosis is most likely to fail — what would someone who disagrees with you say, and what consideration would actually change your mind? I've got the final deliberation on saturday and I want to know whether I'm entitled to tell my co-judge she's wrong, or only that she's her.
