I am a junior and the facilitator of my school's weekly civics discussion group, and for our final sessions of the year I want to build a short diagnostic quiz titled "Who belongs to the demos?" The seed was a real argument in our group over a hypothetical case: a city votes in a referendum to permit a refinery whose emissions will drift mostly over a neighboring state. Everyone agreed the arrangement felt undemocratic somehow, but nobody could say who exactly had been wronged — the downwind residents, who are affected by the decision but do not live under the city's laws, or nobody at all, since the vote followed the rules perfectly.

I would like you to write the quiz for me. My requirements are as follows:

- Eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete voter-roll dispute with two or three answer options. The cases should include at least: a permanent resident of thirty years who pays taxes but is not a citizen; a sixteen-year-old who works and pays income tax; a citizen under guardianship for severe cognitive impairment; a foreign-owned factory whose value would be halved by the regulation on the ballot; and future generations in a climate referendum. Because I want to stress-test the principles rather than just apply them, include at least one question that forces quiz-takers to confront whether their principle could enfranchise a non-human claimant — say, a multinational corporation, or a language model that a city government has begun letting file public comments on proposed ordinances.

- A scoring key mapping answer patterns onto named positions: the all-subjected principle (whoever must obey the laws gets a say in making them); the all-affected principle in both its bounded and unbounded forms (whether it covers only those affected in some substantial, characteristic way, or everyone affected however remotely); competence- or capacity-based views; membership views on which a "people" pre-exists its decision procedures and the franchise tracks belonging to it; and the deflationist position that no principle demarcates the demos, so that democracy simply is whatever a historically contingent electorate decides. If some combination of answers has no occupant among these leaves, say so and diagnose why — whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

- The quiz must sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they like, so no question may ask anything resembling "do you endorse the all-affected principle?"

- Bear in mind that some disputes about who should vote are really disputes about which decisions should be made collectively at all; if you judge that one of my proposed cases is of that kind in disguise, set it aside rather than forcing it into the key, and tell me you did so.

- I have a suspicion that most of my classmates are competence theorists without knowing it. If the suspicion is testable in the design, build in a way to test it.

After the scoring key, please append a short critical note: where the quiz is most likely to mis-sort someone, which two leaves it genuinely cannot separate with these cases, where the key itself leans on a contested reading of a position, and what a new question or case would have to look like to show that the taxonomy is carved at the wrong joint entirely. Please write everything in American English.
