My friend Marcus and I have this running lunch debate. He's a backend guy, no philosophy or econ background either, but he went down a behavioral econ podcast rabbit hole a couple years ago and now fancies himself the office authority on when following the crowd is smart versus when it makes you a sheep ("sheep" is his word, he uses it alot). He literally keeps a list of verdicts and adds to it whenever a real-life case comes up. His rulings so far: joining the longer line at a taco truck you know nothing about, smart; buying the meme stock after it had already 10x'd and everyone on your feed was in it, dumb; going with Postgres for everything even though he admits some niche database beats it on the benchmarks he actually cares about, smart; bolting for the exit when two strangers near you suddenly sprint, smart; ignoring a handful of one-star reviews on a product with two thousand mostly positive ones, smart; panic-selling his index funds in March 2020 because everyone on TV was, dumb. He claims his rule is something like "follow the information, not the people," but I've stared at that sentence long enough to be pretty sure it doesn't actually explain everything on his list.

Next week three new cases are coming up (long story — someone's raising them at a team retro of all places) and I want to predict him before he rules, partly to mess with him and partly because I genuinely can't: (1) buying an apartment in the neighborhood where prices jumped 40% after the big tech campus anouncement, (2) building his weekend side project in the framework that's suddenly everywhere, even though it's a throwaway and he's the only person who will ever touch the code, and (3) siding with the one senior engineer who dissents against what the other eleven people on the team all agreed on. What will he say on each of the three?

Honestly I care less about the three answers than about the following: what rule is he actually running under the hood, what OTHER rules would fit his track record just as well, and which further case could I spring on him that would genuinely split those rules apart instead of all of them predicting the same thing. Bonus points if you can point at specific entries on his list that pull in different directions depending on which rule you read them through — like whether two strangers sprinting for the exit is the same kind of "smart" as Postgres, or something else entirely. I've tried making him sit down and derive his own rule from his own cases and he just accuses me of being a sheep about methodology, which, fair.
