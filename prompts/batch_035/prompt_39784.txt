I work on AI-adjacent stuff (more policy than math) and I've noticed the "tools vs agents" debate has completely stalled, and I'd like a diagnosis of the stall rather than another round of the debate itself. Here's the shape of it. On one side you've got the tool people: AI should be calculators, autocomplete on steroids, a human stays in the loop and the human's ends stay the human's. On the other side the agent people: systems you hand a goal to and walk away, systems that decompose the goal, pick their own subgoals, and act over long horizons. And it gets messy fast, because the safety camp splits internally — some safety people say tools are obviously safer (short horizon, errors don't compound, less drift between what you asked for and what it does), but others say agentic systems are actually the *alignable* ones, because a thing with an explicit target can at least be pointed at something, whereas a "tool" is just a pile of behaviors nobody ever designed anyone to evaluate as a whole. Meanwhile the ethicists mostly argue that tool-framing preserves human agency and agent-framing erodes it, and nobody on any side has moved in what feels like years.

My hypothesis: this is not really a metaphysical dispute about what the system "is" at all. It's a disguised fight about where responsibility should land. When an AI is framed as a tool, blame flows to the user — you asked for it, you got it. There's even a term floating around for the human in that position, the "moral crumple zone": the person who absorbs the blame for what the machine did, whether or not they actually had the control we pretend they had. When the system is an agent, blame diffuses, nobody is ready for that, and so both sides retreat into the vocabulary of "what the system really is," because ontology feels like safer ground than liability. But I could be wrong, and I can see at least three rival diagnoses: (a) it's basically verbal — "agent" and "tool" have never been given seperate criteria, just paradigms (a calculator over there, a butler over here, and everything real lives in between), so people are fighting over a word with no extension fixed; (b) it's two or three different questions under one heading — safety people asking what's safe to deploy, ethicists asking what preserves human control, product people asking what's useful, each using "agent" to mean their own thing; or (c) there's a hidden empirical question doing the work, something like whether errors compound over long horizons, which no amount of conceptual analysis will ever settle and which would definately change which side of the safety split you land on.

So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? I want you to use what the sides actually say, not a survey of AI products, and be honest about which of the diagnoses fits and which don't. And then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a criterion for agenthood both sides could accept (maybe something like: it counts as an agent iff it selects its own subgoals without your involvement)? a measurement of compounding error rates? a legal shift that makes the responsibility question explicit and drains the metaphysics out of it? Or is this one of those cases where we should expect no progress at all, because the bedrock is intuitions about human uniqueness and control that neither side will argue for out loud?
