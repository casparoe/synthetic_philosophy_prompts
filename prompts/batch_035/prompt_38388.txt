My aunt sits on a citizens' commission in our city that reviews who should be entitled to vote in local referendums, and over the years her rulings have settled into a pattern: permanent residents of thirty years' standing may vote even if they hold foreign passports; citizens who emigrated decades ago but still plan to return someday may vote; tourists and seasonal visitors may not; sixteen-year-olds may not; prisoners serving their sentences may; and commuters who live just past the city line, though they use our hospitals and transit daily, may vote only in their own towns. Next month the commission takes up three hard cases: an absentee billionaire who owns most of our downtown commercial property and whose fortune rides on our zoning decisions; a fifteen-year-old, where the referendum at issue concerns a thirty-year climate plan whose costs will fall almost entirely on people her age; and a farmworker who has returned eight months of every year for two decades but keeps his family abroad and intends to retire there. Predict her verdict on each of the three. Then reconstruct the principles her track record might be tracking — I can see at least two that accommodate all of her earlier rulings, and possibly a third — say which you would bet on and why, and describe the single further case, of your own devising, that would best distinguish between the principles she may be running. Please write your answer as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just as you would in a letter.
