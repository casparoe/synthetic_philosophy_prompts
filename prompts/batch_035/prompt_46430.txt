I take part in a long-running online discussion about machine consciousness, and one participant keeps ending arguments with the slogan "conceivable implies possible." His flagship use of it goes like this: he claims he can clearly conceive of a being molecule-for-molecule identical to a human — same brain, same behavior, uttering the same sincere reports about its inner life — yet with no experience at all, nothing it is like to be it; and from "I can conceive of that," plus the slogan, he concludes that such a being is genuinely possible, and hence that consciousness cannot be a purely physical fact. I don't want to join either side yet; I want to know what I'm actually being asked to accept.

Please lay out, as a list I can quote back into the thread, the distinct precise theses that "conceivable implies possible" could be expressing. Separate the candidates along at least these two dimensions. First, the kind of conceivability in play: is it the mere ability to form a mental image or to suppose something without noticing an immediate contradiction; or the considered judgment that no contradiction is hidden anywhere in the supposition; or the even stronger claim that an ideal reasoner, reflecting fully and indefinitely, could not derive a contradiction from it? Second, the kind of possibility promised: logical possibility, metaphysical possibility, or some weaker modal notion — and note that the two sides of the slogan need not use the same grade. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, whether it is trivial, and, crucially, whether it is strong enough to carry his argument: which readings, if any, license the leap from "I can suppose a physical duplicate without experience" to "there could be one," and which make the slogan true but useless for his conclusion, or useful for his conclusion but very likely false?

To close, tell me which reading he most likely has in mind given the work he wants the slogan to do, and what he would then owe us: specifically, what he would need to establish about his own act of conceiving — not just report its phenomenology — for the inference to go through.
