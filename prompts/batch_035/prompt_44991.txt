I'm doing a masters in health policy and something keeps bothering me that everyone in the economics department treats as a settled technical matter rather than a philosophical one. Basically: whenever a new drug comes up for funding review, agencies like NICE score it in QALYs — quality-adjusted life years — they add up the health gains across all the patients who'd get it, divide by the cost of the drug, and check the result against a rough threshold (something like £20-30k per QALY gained). The number that comes out the other end just is a sum of benefits across different people — mostly old people for some drugs, kids for others, people whose baseline health-related quality of life is already low for others — and the arithmetic treats every one of those units as the same thing. There's a slogan version of this, the idea that a QALY is a QALY no matter who recieves it, and it's been controversial forever (the age weighting debate, end-of-life extensions for people with short life expectancy, the whole special treatment of ultra-rare conditions). But then the same governments that run these QALY budgets flatly refuse to do anything like that when they allocate donor organs or ICU beds in a crunch — there it's waiting lists and urgency and a sort of rough first-come fairness, and even the people who designed the QALY system would be horrified at a straightforward maximize-total-QALYs approach to transplant queues.

So what I want is this: pretend you're a philosopher of economics who got handed this as a case study. Reconstruct the implicit theory of utility and wellbeing that the cost-per-QALY machinery has to presuppose in order to even make sense — cardinality, interpersonal comparability, a common scale for quality of life across patients with wildly different conditions, whatever else you can dig out — and for each commitment say why the practice strictly needs it versus merely fits comfortably with it. Then tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, i.e. which rival theories of utility or wellbeing would license the same arithmetic, and whether the refusal to do QALY-style aggregation for organs and ICU beds shows the practice rests on inconsistent commitments or whether there's some distinction that saves it (maybe something about who's already in the system vs. deciding who gets access, I genuinely don't know). I'd also like a short bit at the end on what would have to change in the day-to-day practice if the implicit theory were replaced by a strictly ordinalist view of utility, since that seems like the live alternative.

A couple of asks about how you go about it: please brainstorm the candidate commitments before locking in your reconstruction, and be honest about confidence as you go — especially flag which of the presuppositions you're least sure actually qualifies as a strict presupposition rather than a convenience of measurement. I'd rather have a hedged, careful answer than a confident list that collapses under scrutiny. No reading lists or citations needed, I can chase references myself later — I want the conceptual reconstruction, argued through.
