I'm a PhD student in experimental philosophy, and for every new study my lab first runs its vignettes past Dana, a labmate who has turned out to be a remarkably clean first-pass subject: she reads the cases cold and gives verdicts, which we treat as a sanity check before preregistering anything. We are now extending a battery on side-effect judgments of intentionality — the Knobe-effect family, the chairman-and-the-environment cases and their kin — and next week Dana sees the new conditions. I want to file predictions of her answers in advance, partly as a calibration exercise for myself, and her track record from the earlier batch is what I have to go on. Her verdicts so far, all delivered without hesitation: (1) in the standard harm case — a CEO adopts a program to increase profits, knows it will also harm the environment, and is entirely indifferent to that — she said he brings about the harm intentionally; (2) in the standard help case, with the same indifference and the same sole concern for profit, she said the benefit is not brought about intentionally; (3) asked, on the harm case, whether he brings about the harm in order to hurt the environment, she said no, and she was adamant about this even while standing by (1); (4) in a neutral version, where the program has no effect on the environment at all and he doesn't care, she said not intentionally; and (5) in a version where the CEO is actually pleased about the harm — he dislikes the environment — though profit remains the only reason he proceeds, she again said intentionally.

The new conditions are: (A) a regret version of the harm case, in which the CEO has no alternative — we stipulate that he has tried every feasible substitute, that none exists at any price the company could pay, that the harm will occur if he proceeds, and that he proceeds because the board has tied his hands, and we describe him as visibly sick about it; (B) a delighted version of the help case, in which the CEO personally cares about the environment and is glad the program helps it, but in which his delight makes no difference to what he does — we stipulate that he would have adopted the program on profit alone; and (C) a chance version, in which the program has an equal chance of helping and of harming the environment, the CEO doesn't care which way it goes, and it happens, on this run, to harm.

What I'd like from you: predictions of Dana's verdicts on A, B, and C; a candid inventory of the candidate rules that fit her five earlier verdicts equally well — at minimum the bare harm/help asymmetry, the view that her intentionality attributions are really tracking her judgments of blameworthiness, and the norm-violation view, though I want to hear about any others that fit — together with what each rule predicts for the new cases; your best guess on each, with your reasons for preferring it; and a statement of which further case would best separate whichever rules survive. On the last point, I have been toying with a version of B in which the CEO's delight is what tips the choice between two equally profitable programs, one that helps the environment and one that doesn't — but if you see a better discriminator, say so and say why. Since these are predictions about one person's judgments rather than the folk consensus, please tell me how confident you are in each guess, and single out the one point in your answer you would be least willing to bet on.
