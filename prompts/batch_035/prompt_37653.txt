I teach intro ethics at a community college -- 18ish students, one 75-minute session. Next week we're doing Augustine on lying, specifically his claim that you may never assert something you believe false, not even to keep an innocent person from being killed, and I'm tired of watching students write the position off as fanaticism five minutes into the discussion. Design an in-class game where they have to actually live inside the rule before they're allowed to dismiss it.

Here's the shape I'm imagining, feel free to improve it: some students play villagers in a vague historical setting where soldiers are hunting down a fugitive who's done nothing wrong. One villager per round has the fugitive hidden in their house. Another student plays the interrogator, whose whole job is to get a straight answer about the fugitive's whereabouts -- they can use promises, threats, appeals to the villager's family, whatever they can think of. The villager may refuse to answer, stay silent, stall, or say anything they believe true, but the moment they assert something they believe false, the round is lost for their side. I want some kind of umpire or appeals mechanic around that rule, since "was that statement technically true?" is going to be half the game. Also score it somehow so the interrogator side and the villager side both have something to win, no real money or prizes needed.

The part I care most about: they'll quickly figure out you can say misleading but literally true things ("I haven't seen him since breakfast") and the game should let them lean on that hard. Then the debrief has to hit the question I actually want them to chew on: under Augustine's way of defining lying (asserting something other than what you have in your mind), is a statement that's literally true but crafted to make the listener believe something false still an act of lying, or is it genuinely different? Does the distinction survive once you've felt firsthand how much deceiving you can do without ever asserting a falsehood? I'd also like the debrief to compare the students who stayed silent or flatly refused with the ones who got clever, and to ask whether those come out morally different from each other.

Please include: full rules, materials, a timeline for the session, the scoring system, a couple of curveball complications you'd build in for later rounds, and an honest note on which feature of Augustine's actual problem the game can't reproduce no matter how I run it. And tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time -- I assume at least one student will discover they can just talk forever without answering anything, and I want a plan for that.
