I've spent a few evenings trying to work out what I actually believe about how luck should matter to distributive justice, and I've gotten as far as a list of gut verdicts on cases, plus a suspicion about the principle behind them that I can't quite make precise. I'd like your help turning this into something coherent. Please take on the role of a political philosopher who works on distributive justice and is well versed in luck egalitarianism and its critics, but who is willing to conclude that my verdicts don't add up to any version of it.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. Two neighbors in identical circumstances are each hit by early-onset Parkinson's, one at 38 and one at 55. I think society owes both full compensation — treatment, income support, the works — and I don't think the timing matters at all.

2. Twins inherit equal sums. One puts hers in a diversified portfolio; the other, after doing what any reasonable person would call adequate research, bets everything on a single biotech stock and loses it all. I don't think we owe the gambler a bailout. I do think she should be kept from destitution — food, shelter, basic medical care — but not brought back toward her sister's standard of living.

3. A homeowner on a flood plain would gladly have bought flood insurance but genuinely couldn't afford the premium on her income; the flood comes. I think she's owed substantially more than the gambler — perhaps full compensation.

4. A lifelong smoker who knew the risks develops lung cancer. My verdict: exactly the same medical treatment as anyone else, no questions asked, no copays aimed at her; but I'm genuinely unsure whether she's owed income replacement during treatment, and I notice my confidence about this case is much lower than about the others.

5. Someone develops an expensive taste for vintage wine and finds that ordinary incomes no longer give her the welfare others get. No subsidy, obviously.

6. Someone's upbringing instilled in her, from before she could reflect on it, an all-consuming ambition to be a concert violinist. She chooses the conservatory over a fallback, knowing the odds, and ends up without an orchestra seat and without a fallback career. I say no compensation here either — but I feel distinctly worse about it than about the wine case, and I can't articulate why. Something about the ambition not being, in any real sense, hers.

7. Two farmers each knowingly buy cheap, drought-prone land instead of expensive, reliable land, both fully informed about the odds, and each accepting a higher expected return for the risk. A drought strikes one farm only. My gut says the unlucky farmer deserves something — maybe partial compensation — even though, ex ante, he looks exactly like the biotech gambler. This is the pair I can't reconcile: 2 and 7 feel different to me, and I suspect they shouldn't.

My tentative hypothesis, stated as a hypothesis: the difference between the cases isn't whether the loss flowed from a choice, but something about the opportunity set behind the choice — the gambler declined a genuinely accessible safe option, while the farmer and the uninsured homeowner didn't have the same access to the alternative that would have protected them. But I can see at least two problems with this myself: it's not clear the farmer's safe option wasn't accessible, and it threatens to collapse into compensating everyone whose bad option luck followed from a constrained menu, which looks like it would swallow case 2 as well.

So here is what I'd like from you. First, brainstorm freely — map the possible principles, including luck-egalitarian ones, hybrid views, sufficientarian ones, and views on which relational equality rather than luck-neutralization does the real work — before committing to any of them. Then give me a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Tell me its verdicts on cases I haven't listed, including at least these two: a worker who takes a riskier job for higher pay and is injured on the job (does her higher past pay count against her claim?); and a person who declines a free, employer-provided insurance benefit out of simple procrastination and then falls ill. Be honest about which of my verdicts your favored principle cannot accommodate, and say whether you would overturn those verdicts or complicate the principle to save them.

Finally, two requests about the epistemic character of your answer. Please write in American English throughout, and at the end, include a short section telling me what would change your mind: which of your arguments is most likely to fail under pressure, what kind of case or consideration would push you toward a different principle, and where, if anywhere, you suspect my verdicts rest on a confusion that no principle should be bent to accommodate. I'd rather have a diagnosis of where my reasoning is weakest than a polished defense of your conclusion.
