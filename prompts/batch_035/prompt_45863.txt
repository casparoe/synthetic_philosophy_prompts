"Szasz argued that mental illness is a myth because real diseases, like cancer or diabetes, involve some demonstrable physical pathology in the body, and psychiatric diagnoses don't. When medicine calls something a disease without finding a lesion, the term is being used metaphorically, and metaphor can't legitimately justify medical intervention. What psychiatrists diagnose are really problems in living -- difficulties people have with how they conduct their lives, their relationships, their circumstances. For Szasz the label of mental illness serves as a cover: it lets society dress up moral and social judgments as medical facts, the way earlier societies dressed them up as witchcraft and demonic possession, which is why he compared psychiatry of his day to the Inquisition. He also believed people with psychiatric diagnoses are basically faking or at least exaggerating their distress, since there's no disease there to cause real symptoms, and he wanted to abolish psychiatry entirely. He was part of the anti-psychiatry movement of the 1960s along with R.D. Laing and Michel Foucault.

The strongest objection to Szasz is that modern neuroscience has shown his central claim to be empirically false. He claimed mental illness has no physical basis whatsoever, but we now know that depression and schizophrenia involve measurable differences in brain chemistry and structure. Since mental illness does have a physical basis, Szasz's premise that disease requires something physical is satisfied, so the argument collapses."
