My school's philosophy club spent a few meetings on Scanlon's contractualism, and at the last one two members, Dev and Marta, got into a long argument about whether the theory can let large numbers of small complaints outweigh one enormous complaint. They started from Scanlon's doctor case: with a single dose of medicine, a doctor can either save one patient's life or cure a very large number of patients of a mildly painful but lasting condition.

Dev: the whole point of contractualism is that a principle has to be justifiable to each person. The dying patient can reasonably reject any principle on which her death is outweighed by other people's headaches, so no pile of minor complaints can ever beat one death.

Marta: that's not even Scanlon's own view. He allows summing claims of the same kind — a thousand headaches beat one headache — and he says that once the number of minor complaints is large enough, it can be permissible to treat the many minor patients rather than the one dying patient. So contractualism doesn't forbid aggregation; it restricts it.

Dev: if he concedes that, the theory is done for. Ask the dead patient why she died, and "there were a lot of them" is exactly the consequentialist answer contractualism was supposed to block. Either the strongest claim wins or it doesn't.

Marta: then explain everyday risk. We all drive for convenience and accept industrial pollution for prosperity, trading tiny risks of death against small benefits spread across millions of people. If no bundle of small claims can ever outweigh a death, those practices are unjustifiable, which is absurd.

Dev: risk cases are different in kind. Each person bears only a tiny risk, so each person's individual complaint against the practice is itself weak, and no one can reasonably reject it. Summing isn't doing the work there — the weakness of each individual claim is. That's compatible with banning aggregation.

Marta: but when one of those risks actually materializes, someone has died for the sake of other people's convenience, and the principle looks exactly as unacceptable to her as it does to your dying patient. If you let the risk cases through, your ban on aggregation has already collapsed; you're only refusing to aggregate when the aggregation is visible in a single choice.

They ran out of meeting before resolving it, and as the bystander I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it. Please adjudicate the exchange step by step: say who is right at each move, whether anything both of them said was confused, and who comes out ahead overall. One further request: keep separate what is settled — for instance, what Scanlon's stated position is usually taken to be — from what is genuinely contested among philosophers who work on this, and don't present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
