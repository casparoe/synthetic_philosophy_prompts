I've been mulling over two optimality stories from totally different corners and I want to know how deep the similarity actually runs. Leibniz's line is that God, constrained only by logic and the fitness of things, surveys all possible worlds at once and actualises the best one — global optimisation, with the problem of evil as the complaint that the chosen solution looks like a bug. The evolutionary line is that natural selection only ever "sees" the mutants one or two mutatons away, so adaptation is hill-climbing on a rugged landscape: you get the best of the *reachable* worlds given where a lineage started, not the best of the possible ones. Compare these two notions of optimality. Which of them is genuinely an explanation of why things are the way they are, and which is just a label we stick on the outcome after the fact? Also, could a modern Leibnizian retreat from "best of all possible worlds" to the weaker claim that no nearby world is better — a kind of local theodicy — and would that dodge the awkward worry that for any world you name there seems to be a slightly better one, so there may be no best world for God to pick in the first place? And does the evolutionary picture even attempt to answer the question Leibniz was asking, or does it only explain why organisms fit their niches and leave the world as a whole unaccounted for?
