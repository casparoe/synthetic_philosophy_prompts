Quick context: I'm a PhD student in comparative constitutionalism, working on a dissertation chapter about church-state arrangements, and I want to avoid the usual laïcité-vs-establishment blah-fest where France and the US get all the attention. I've become weirdly obsessed with the German church tax (Kirchensteuer) because it seems philosophically stranger than anyone gives it credit for, but I can't quite pin down what it actually presupposes.

Concretely the system works like this: if you're a member of the Catholic church or one of the Protestant Landeskirchen — and membership is normally acquired when your parents have you baptized as an infant — the state collects an extra tax from you, a surcharge of roughly 9% on your income tax (a bit less in Bavaria and Baden-Württemberg), and hands it over to your church. To stop paying you don't tell the church; you file paperwork with a state registry office, and only that state act releases you from the church's rolls. A few years back they extended it to capital gains, so banks automatically withhold church tax on your brokerage income, which triggered another wave of exits, and some Catholic dioceses even charge an adminstrative fee for processing the departure. Meanwhile the same constitution has strong free-exercise and neutrality guarantees, and religion is an ordinary graded subject in regular public schools.

Here's what I keep chewing on: what conception of religious membership does all this presuppose? The system doesn't treat religion like a gym membership — you were enrolled as an infant, you can't just cancel your payment method, exiting requires an affirmative bureaucratic act with the *state* rather than the church, and it's state records that determine who owes what. But it also isn't quite citizenship, since you can leave without moving anywhere and nobody treats it as a kind of denationalization. So is it some third category — a public-law corporate quasi-citizenship? And does the state acting as tax collector presuppose that the churches are performing some public function, like quasi-public utilities, or is the state just providing billing services for private associations, and does that distinction even do any work? There's also a tension I keep half-noticing: liberal theory usually says religious affiliation is the paradigm case of free choice (that's supposedly why it's the protected sphere at all), but this arrangement treats it as ascribed at birth, something you have to actively escape at some cost and effort. Is that just a historical relic that the presupposed liberal theory of religious liberty can't actually accommodate, or is there a coherent theory under which inherited membership plus costly formal exit is perfectly fine — maybe something closer to how we think of nationality?

So what I'd love from you: reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) of religious membership and of the church-state relationship that this practice rests on, be clear about which parts are strictly presupposed versus which rival theories it's merely compatible with, and tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Then do the counterfactual: if you redesigned the system around a genuinely voluntary-association model of religion, what concretely would have to change, and would the result even be stable — I suspect the exit friction is a big part of why German churches still have any money. And what would a system built on yet another model, say religion as something like ethnicity or descent (exit being more like changing nationality than quitting a club), look like instead?

Please answer in American English, and keep the German terms where they're just the clearest way of saying the thing. Sorry for the wall of text, this has been rattling around in my head for weeks.
