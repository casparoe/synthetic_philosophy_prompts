For the last six years I have kept the keys, the watering schedule, and the petty-cash tin for a community garden in the town where I live. I have no training in economics or philosophy, and everything below is something I worked out mostly while weeding.

Some years ago a neighbor lent me a paperback collection that included the famous essay about herders sharing a pasture: each herder pockets the whole benefit of adding one more cow while the cost of that cow is spread across everyone, so the sensible move for each of them, one cow at a time, is the move that ruins them all. Reading it felt like reading about my Tuesdays. But it only explained half of them.

Here is the other half, as I've come to understand it. Our garden fails in two directions at once. The shared herb bed gets eaten to death: the last basil disappears because taking it costs the taker nothing and costs the bed everything. The compost heap dies the opposite death: it needs everyone to carry their kitchen scraps out to it, and it starves, because not carrying them also costs each of us nothing. I privately call these the two deaths — the commons eaten to death and the commons starved to death — and what strikes me most is the moral asymmetry. Everyone in the garden can name the person who stripped the basil and privately considers them a thief; nobody considers the person who never once brought scraps to be doing anything at all, though the heap dies just as dead, and I am that person as often as not. Doing nothing doesn't look like taking. It doesn't look like anything. And I suspect the two deaths are really one sentence pointed in opposite directions: I've taken to saying to myself, on the way out with empty hands, "one less pail won't be missed," and I've heard the other kind of person think "one more sprig won't be missed," and those are the same thought wearing different boots.

What I would like is a poem — specifically a pantoum, the form where the second and fourth lines of each stanza come back as the first and third lines of the next, so nothing is ever said only once, and the final stanza folds back around to the poem's opening lines. Six quatrains, plain language, rhyme optional. I want the form to do the arguing, in these ways:

1. Each time a line comes around again, the garden should be one season poorer. No new event should happen in the poem — just the same words accumulating damage, so the reader feels the awfulness of nothing new occurring.
2. The first three stanzas should run on the taker's logic and the last three on the abstainer's, and at least one line should cross over between the halves — meaning "one more won't be missed" early in the poem and "one less won't be missed" later, so the reader sees without being told that the two deaths share a skeleton.
3. Nobody in the poem should be a villain in their own eyes. Every step should be defensible from the inside; the poem should convict the structure, not the neighbors.
4. The ending must not be a shrug or a prophecy of ruin. The truth is that our garden survived — not with fences, but with a chalkboard by the gate where each person's contributions got written down where everyone could see them, and seeing each other did more than any rule ever did. Since pantoums circle back to their opening lines, let the final stanza return them with the doom taken out: the same words, meaning something survivable now.

A reader who knows the herders-and-pasture essay should recognize what the poem is about without the word "commons" ever appearing; a reader who knows nothing of it should simply read a good poem about a garden.

Then, for the newsletter, please add a short table beneath the poem, one row per stanza, with these columns: stanza number; which death is on display (eaten, starved, or, for the final stanza if it fits, cured); the line that has come around again and what it means now that it didn't mean before; and one plain sentence on what the neighbors would have to be able to see for that stanza's damage not to happen. Keep the table simple enough to print beside the poem.
