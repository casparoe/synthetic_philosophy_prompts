For a seminar on moral epistemology, I want a case analysis of Milgram's obedience experiments (the early-1960s Yale baseline studies) as an episode in moral testimony — and, just as much, a test of what the moral testimony debate is actually about. The facts you need: subjects recruited for what they were told was a study of learning and memory were instructed to deliver what they believed were escalating electric shocks, in steps up to a maximum of 450 volts, to a learner (an actor) whenever he made an error; the learner's complaints about a heart condition, demands to be released, and eventual silence were scripted; when subjects balked, the experimenter delivered scripted prods, culminating in the insistence that the experiment required them to continue; roughly two-thirds of subjects went on to the maximum. Now the mapping. The pessimist about moral testimony holds that moral belief is not properly acquired on another's say-so — that deference in the moral case yields, at best, second-hand knowledge without the kind of understanding that acting well requires — while the optimist holds that moral testimony transmits knowledge much as ordinary testimony does. Analyze the episode through this debate: who counts as the testifier, and what exactly is testified to — that the shocks are harmless, that continuing is morally permissible, that this is the sort of situation in which deference is appropriate? And then the hard part, which is what I most want to see: the episode looks like one of authority and directive-giving rather than testimony-that-p at all, and much of what went wrong seems to have happened downstream of intact moral belief — many subjects protested and sweated and dissented aloud while complying, which suggests their failure was not in acquiring their moral views on someone else's word. So say plainly whether the Milgram studies are a case the moral testimony literature illuminates, a case it misclassifies, or a case it never anticipated; state where each view of the debate locates the subject's error, and whether that diagnosis is credible against what we know of the subjects' conduct; and determine what, if anything, the episode gives the pessimist — perhaps that the deference the pessimist fears is real but lives in action rather than belief, or perhaps nothing at all. Be disciplined about the mapping: if a category of the theory has no occupant in the episode, say so rather than forcing one in. End with a final line of the form "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase stating whether Milgram confirms moral testimony pessimism, undermines it, or reveals the debate to concern a phenomenon distinct from what the experiments exhibit.
