I've spent the last two years drifting into formal epistemology, and last week a friend from my decision theory seminar — after a long argument that started over coffee and ended over dinner — told me that my picture of epistemic rationality is not a picture at all but a collision of at least three incompatible ones. I want to know whether she's right before I write any of this up, so let me lay out my commitments and ask for an audit.

Here is what I hold. (a) Accuracy monism: the epistemic value of a doxastic state is exhausted by its accuracy, by how close the credences come to the truth values; anything else we care about epistemically — coherence, calibration, responsiveness to evidence — matters only insofar as it serves accuracy. (b) Strictly proper scoring rules are the right way to measure accuracy, and the dominance argument for the probability axioms is, to my mind, the most satisfying justification of probabilism there is: non-probabilistic credences are accuracy-dominated, so rational credences had better be probability functions. (c) In Ellsberg-style cases — say an urn with 30 red balls and 60 more that are black or yellow in unknown proportions — I think the epistemically correct state is not any sharp credence but a range or a state of suspension, and I take this to be a point about what the evidence supports, not about my psychological aversion to ambiguity. (d) A chemist finishing a monograph of 100 claims, each supported to about 0.95 by its own experiments, who is also highly confident that her book contains at least one error (books like hers always do), is entirely rational as she stands; it would be absurd to shave each individual credence down to whatever level would make the package cohere. (e) Free evidence should always be taken: I accept the argument that conditioning on new information increases expected accuracy, so refusing a free look at a relevant fact is always an epistemic mistake. (f) Yet if a benevolent neuroscientist offered to reset my credences to values she could prove — by the lights of any strictly proper scoring rule — are more accurate in expectation than my current ones, based on information I will never have access to, I think I should refuse: credences should answer to my evidence. My friend also claims that my treatment of known liars betrays me — when a pathological liar tells me the meeting is at three, I discount it, which she says my commitment to always updating on evidence forbids — though I suspect this one is just a confusion about what I'm conditioning on.

Please go through the package and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and what the cheapest repair would be for each real conflict, including what the repair costs elsewhere in the set. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally damning, and be specific about which single revision, if I made it, would clean up the most while preserving what I care most about. My friend is confident the accuracy monism has to go; I'd like to know whether she's right before I concede anything.
