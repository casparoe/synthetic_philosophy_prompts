I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a long piece about how AI alignment researchers think machines should figure out what humans want. The idea I keep coming back to is coherent extrapolated volition — roughly, the proposal that an AI shouldn't follow our current, messy, contradictory preferences but should instead act on what we would want if we knew more, thought faster, and were more the people we wished we were, aggregated across humanity in some way. One researcher I interviewed put his credence at about 0.7 that such a procedure, run over the whole human population, would yield a single coherent preference ordering that the AI could actually act on — not perfect unanimity, but enough of a fixed point that "do what humanity would want on reflection" picks out a determinate direction. My own instinct is more like 0.3, and I'd like to figure out where the disagreement actually lives rather than just quoting us both and moving on.

So I want your number, but mainly I want the decomposition. As I understand it, the overall claim factors into at least a few load-bearing sub-questions, and I suspect you'll see more. First, whether human preferences have the kind of structure that survives idealization at all — whether "what I would want if I knew more" is a coherent limiting notion, or whether learning more and reflecting more typically shatters or multiplies values rather than refining them, so that the extrapolation is torn apart by the very process that's supposed to purify it. Second, whether coherence can be imposed at the aggregate level without discarding what mattered: aggregation across billions of people with partly conflicting reflective equilibria may force the same kind of wreckage that social choice theorists worry about. Third, whether the procedure is determinate enough — different choices of what counts as "more informed," different models of reflection, different starting populations could send the extrapolation in different directions, and maybe the whole enterprise is less like discovering the answer and more like choosing one by stipulation. And then there's the normative sub-question of whether even a cleanly extrapolated volition is what an AI ought to be answerable to, though I'd be inclined to treat that as a separate factor.

Please assign a rough probability to each sub-question and any others you think belong, with a line of justification apiece, then show how they combine into your overall number, and tell me which term the final answer is most sensitive to. Then, given that my source is at 0.7 and I'm at 0.3, tell me which factor you think most likely separates us — whether he's confident that reflection tends to converge rather than fragment, or that a determinate choice of procedure is available, or something I haven't listed — and what evidence, of the sort that might actually arrive in the next decade, would move the two of us toward a shared number. I'd rather be able to say in print "the disagreement reduces to X" than leave it as a vibes-level split, so I need the argumentative anatomy, not just the odds.
