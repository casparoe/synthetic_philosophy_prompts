My book club has been reading about karma, and at the end of the last meeting our host offered, by way of consolation for an injustice one of us had suffered, the proverb that what goes around comes around — his point being that we need not despair, since such things settle themselves in the end. The week before, the same host had used the same proverb to say of an acquaintance who had behaved badly and then lost his job that the loss was deserved. I came away feeling that these two uses pull in different directions, but I cannot articulate why. Would you lay out the distinct precise claims the proverb could be making — for instance: a causal law that wrongdoing brings subsequent misfortune; a normative thesis that such misfortune, whenever it arrives, is fitting; a sociological generalization that people notice and reciprocate; a psychological claim about guilt and self-sabotage; or a merely moralizing platitude — and say for each whether it is trivial, defensible, or false? Then tell me which reading the consolation requires, which the desert claim requires, and whether any single reading can support both uses. Finally: on its strongest reading, is the proverb just the doctrine of karma as a theory of moral desert under an English name, or does karma add commitments — about the mechanism, the timescale, or the bearer of consequences across lives — that the proverb neither makes nor needs?
