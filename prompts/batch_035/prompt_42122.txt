Write a villanelle in the voice of the epistemological dogmatist — the speaker of the dogmatism paradox, who reasons that since he knows that p, he can know in advance that any future evidence against p is misleading evidence, and is therefore entitled to dismiss it the moment it appears. Choose a concrete p for him: something ordinary and earnest rather than crankish — a diagnosis he trusts, a friend's innocence, a result he replicated with his own hands — so the reader feels the pull of the position instead of laughing at it.

The two refrains should be the two legs of the derivation. The first refrain asserts his knowledge outright. The second refrain draws the inference from that knowledge: whatever arrives to testify against p is thereby shown to be misdirection, and may be set aside unread. You have latitude in how the refrains are worded, but each should be recognizably one leg of the argument, so that the alternating pattern of the villanelle — one refrain, then the other, then back — lets the poem trace the closed loop: the knowledge guarantees the unreliability of the counterevidence, and the unreliability of the counterevidence protects the knowledge.

Use the middle line of each tercet to bring in fresh trouble, and the returning refrain to absorb it. Let the challenges escalate across the stanzas: a dissenting expert, a failed replication, a memory of his own past gullibility, finally the thought that this very reasoning might be the mechanism by which he was deceived in the first place. Each tercet should show the same movement: a genuine doubt enters, and leaves reclassified as misleading. The form should make the reader feel how self-sealing the position is — nothing that enters the poem can touch p, because p is what licenses the judgment that nothing counts against it.

Two requirements on accuracy. First, the dogmatist must not come across as merely stubborn or stupid; the poem should make visible that his confidence is the output of a valid argument, not a character flaw. Somewhere in the poem the closure of knowledge under known implication should be doing visible work — the step where he combines what he knows with what he knows about what it entails. A reader who knows the epistemology should be able to spot that step in the rhyme logic and recognize it as the same principle the skeptic uses to reach opposite conclusions. Second, do not resolve the paradox. The final quatrain, where the two refrains stand side by side at last, should deliver the paradox rather than dissolve it: the reader should see that the closing couplet follows, and feel the vertigo anyway. Someone who knows the literature should find the poem philosophically exact; someone who doesn't should find the speaker strangely persuasive — which is the trap the poem is about.
