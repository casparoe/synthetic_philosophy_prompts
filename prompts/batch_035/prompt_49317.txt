Background: I'm a philosophy PhD student, and my advisor just tore his achilles, so I'm covering his upper-level jurisprudence seminar next week — 14 students, 80 minutes. They've all read the 1958 Hart–Fuller exchange in some abstract way, but from sitting in on their discussion sections, nobody actually feels the stakes; it's chestnut-shelling, people recite "law and morality" platitudes at each other. I want to build the whole session around the grudge informer case: 1944, a woman who wants to get rid of her husband denounces him to the Nazi authorities for derogatory remarks about Hitler that he made while home on leave; he gets hauled off under validly enacted Nazi statutes; then a 1949 West German court has to decide whether she can be convicted (the real court reached back to the 1871 Code's crime of "illegal deprivation of liberty" to do it). That's the case that made the debate a real fight instead of a semantic one.

My idea so far: split the room into three "courts," each ruling on her case under different constraints. Court One gets only validly enacted law — the Nazi statute was enacted by procedures that legal order itself recognized, plus whatever postwar statutes existed. Court Two is told to rule as justice requires but must write an opinion an actual trial judge could sign his name to. Court Three gets a fictional handout I'd draft: a "Transitional Justice Act" passed by the postwar legislature that retroactively criminalizes political denunciation between 1933 and 1945. The thing I'm fishing for is that Court Three never has to say anything at all about what "law" is, and then in the debrief they have to work out whether Court Two's natural-law reasoning is doing any work the retroactive statute doesn't do cheaper and cleaner — which I take to be Hart's actual move, that treating the Nazi law as voidable rather than void gets you everything without metaphysics. But I've never taught before, so maybe this whole structure is off.

Design me the full thing: role sheets or instructions for each court, timing across the 80 minutes, materials (the handouts should be sketched for me, including my Transitional Justice Act with whatever loopholes/drafting bugs are actually useful), some payoff or stake that isn't real money, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the actual positions without just lecturing at them. I also want the two honest addenda the examples of this kind of thing always mention: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of the debate the activity flatly cannot reproduce — I'm guessing Fuller's "inner morality of law," the generality and publicity stuff, just doesn't show up in a one-shot courtroom game, but tell me if I'm wrong. And please push back on the setup itself: if you think the grudge informer case is too worn out to make anyone feel anything, or that three courts is one too many, or that role-play collapses into debate club at this size, say that plainly instead of designing politely around my half-baked plan. Better an awkward correction from you than 14 bored students and an achilles-less professor hearing about it.
