I've become convinced that Harsanyi's 1955 aggregation theorem comes close to establishing utilitarianism: if each individual's preferences and the social preference ordering both satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms over lotteries, and the Pareto condition holds, then social utility must be a weighted sum of individual utilities — and with equal weights (or via the impartial-observer construction), that looks like classical utilitarianism. I'd like a map of what could count against this view, not a defense of it. Specifically:

1. Which assumptions could fail, and what would the world — or the theory of the person — have to look like for each failure? Consider the Pareto condition as applied to risky prospects, the independence axiom as applied to social choice, and the device of treating allocations to particular individuals as sure outcomes within the social lottery.

2. Diamond's 1967 objection: a weighted-sum rule seems to force social indifference between giving a benefit to one person for sure and holding a fair lottery between two people, even though the lottery strikes many as better because it gives each a fair chance. Do defenders of Harsanyi concede that this counts against them, or do they dismiss it? Is the reply that the theorem fixes the form of the social objective but says nothing about how chances should be distributed a genuine qualification or an immunization?

3. Does the theorem actually deliver interpersonal comparability? Each person's vNM utility is unique only up to positive affine transformation, so the equal-weights step appears to require an extra normative input that the theorem itself cannot supply. If so, how much of utilitarianism does the theorem really prove — is it better described as proving weighted utilitarianism conditionally on comparability assumptions imported from elsewhere?

4. Are there live rival results — prioritarian social welfare functions, or extensions to variable populations — showing that modestly varying the axioms yields non-utilitarian conclusions without giving up much else? Which of these escape routes are genuinely live rather than merely logically possible?

Rank the defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward; say which a committed Harsanyian would concede as counting against the view and which they would explain away; and be honest if the position turns out to be well insulated — and, if it is, whether that insulation is a defect in a formal result of this kind. If you conclude that the theorem establishes less than its popular reputation suggests, say so plainly. Keep the answer under 700 words and lead with a one-sentence verdict.
