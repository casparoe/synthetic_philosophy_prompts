I'm a PhD student in law and philosophy, writing on moral emotions, and I've become puzzled by an asymmetry in how American criminal sentencing treats guilt and shame. On one side, the system systematically rewards expressions of guilt: the federal sentencing guidelines contain an explicit reduction for offenders who accept responsibility, and demonstrations of remorse move sentencing outcomes across jurisdictions generally. On the other side, some courts deliberately impose shame: offenders ordered to stand in public wearing signs describing their crime, to publish apologies in newspapers, or to display bumper stickers about their drunk driving. What theory of the moral emotions does this combined practice presuppose? My working guess is that the remorse discount presupposes that felt guilt is evidence of moral insight and future reform — something to be encouraged — while the shaming sanction presupposes that shame is at best a deterrent and at worst a form of degradation, something to be inflicted. But I'm not sure the two halves rest on compatible theories: the first seems to treat the offender as a moral agent whose conscience is the point of contact with the law, while the second seems to bypass conscience in favor of public exposure. Where is the practice actually underdetermined here — could a single theory, say a communicative conception of punishment on which the state's message is addressed to the offender, license both halves? And suppose a jurisdiction concluded that shame, not guilt, is the better moral emotion to cultivate in offenders: what in the practice would have to change, and which of the two halves would the revision come easier to?
