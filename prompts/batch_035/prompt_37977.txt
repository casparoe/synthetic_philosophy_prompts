I teach an undergraduate survey of classical Indian philosophy, and in the epistemology unit I keep running into the same problem: my students can recite that the Nyāya school counts four pramāṇas (sources or instruments of knowledge) and the Mīmāṃsakas six, but the numbers mean nothing to them, and the standard textbook tables sort positions by school name rather than by any commitment the students actually hold. What I want instead is a diagnostic decision tree: one page, opening from a single question, branching through concrete cases, and ending in leaves labeled Cārvāka materialism, the Buddhist logicians of the Dignāga and Dharmakīrti tradition, Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika, the two Mīmāṃsā sub-schools (Bhāṭṭa and Prābhākara, which share a name but famously fight it out over the nature of error), and Advaita Vedānta.

Every internal node must be phrased as a case someone could answer without knowing which school it leads to; nothing of the form "do you accept testimony as a pramāṇa?" The tree should be built from the following case material, which I ask you to treat as a fixed requirement rather than a suggestion, though you may add nodes if the tree needs them. (1) A doctor you have found reliable for years, and whom you cannot check yourself, tells you your test results are fine: does her word settle the matter directly, as testimony operating as an autonomous pramāṇa (the Nyāya view), or only because you infer her reliability, so that the entitlement is inherited from inference (the Buddhist view), or not at all? (2) Smoke rises from a distant hill: are you entitled to conclude there is fire there, or does every such step quietly assume that connections observed in some cases hold in unobserved ones — an assumption only perception could verify and never does, which is the Cārvāka's reported worry about inference as such? (3) A man is conspicuously fat yet never observed to eat during the day: is the conclusion that he must eat at night a judgment requiring its own source of knowledge, postulation (arthāpatti, accepted by the Mīmāṃsakas), or just one more inference about him (the Nyāya reduction)? (4) "There is no pot on the table": is that the perception of an absence (Nyāya), a finding that needs its own source, non-apprehension (anupalabdhi, accepted by the Bhāṭṭa line and the Advaitins), or a report about the state of your own cognition rather than about the world (the Prābhākara line)? (5) A shell glimpsed at dusk and taken for silver: when the mistake comes apart, what went wrong — a real silver seen on some other occasion was misjoined to what is actually present (the Nyāya account), nothing real ever corresponded to the silver at all, the mistake being rooted in a beginningless ignorance (avidyā) rather than in any misperception (the Advaita account), or nothing false occurred in the first place, a percept and a memory simply not having been told apart (the Prābhākara account, on which error involves no falsity)? Where the Bhāṭṭa leaf belongs on this last case is, as far as I am concerned, part of the assignment: the two Mīmāṃsā sub-schools must be separated somewhere in the tree, but I am not telling you where.

Two further requirements. First, build the tree from the logical space rather than from the roster of schools: lay out the combinations of answers the cases generate, locate each school within that space, and flag at least one unoccupied combination, with a sentence on whether you think nobody holds it because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Second, be honest about what the instrument cannot do: if two leaves cannot be separated by any of the cases above, say so explicitly rather than inventing a question that would only work on someone who already knew the labels. The audience has no Sanskrit, so gloss every technical term in plain English at first use, with the Sanskrit in parentheses at most once per term, and keep the whole thing compact enough that I could actually hand it out on a single page.
