I would like to practice defending the self-indication assumption against a well-prepared opponent. Please play a committed defender of the doomsday argument -- the argument from my birth rank to the conclusion that human extinction is likely sooner rather than later. Be sharp, not a caricature: you know the literature (Carter, Leslie, Gott), you are willing to concede small points, and you have serious replies to the standard objections -- but you never concede that the argument is unsound. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two so it feels like a live conversation, and stay in character until I write "stop". If you want to deploy the presumptuous philosopher scenario against me, save it for the moment it does you the most good rather than opening with it.

Here is my opening move. You claim that my finding myself roughly the hundred-billionth human in history is evidence of impending doom, because that rank would be unremarkable if few humans are ever born but strikingly early if trillions more are still to come. But run the full Bayesian computation over the hypothesis that I exist at all. On the far-future theory, the chance that a randomly chosen position in the total population would fall as early as mine is minuscule -- I would almost certainly have been someone born later. On the near-doom theory, the chance that the population contains someone at my rank, and that this someone is me, is comparatively large. In other words, I am more likely to exist, as the person I am at this rank, under your own favored hypothesis than under the long-future one. The self-indication assumption captures this by giving each hypothesis a prior boost proportional to the number of observers it contains, and that boost exactly cancels the evidential weight you assign to my early birth rank. Your doomsday shift is netted out before it begins. What, exactly, went wrong in that computation?
