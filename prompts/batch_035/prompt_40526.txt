I'm taking an intermediate philosophy of mind course, and we've just spent two weeks on Dennett's view that the self is not a thing inside the head but rather an abstractum — roughly, a center of narrative gravity, a posit that the brain's subpersonal processes and the surrounding social world jointly create and maintain by telling a more-or-less coherent story about a single agent. The analogy we kept returning to in lecture is the center of mass in physics: no bit of matter occupies the center of mass of a chair, and it is in some sense a calculated fiction, yet it does indispensable work in predicting and explaining how the chair behaves. Dennett's suggestion, as I understand it, is that the self is similarly fictional-but-indispensable: there is no inner CEO, but there is an organized pattern of dispositions, memories, and self-ascriptions that makes talk of a persisting person scientifically and practically respectable.

Our section has since split into two camps, and their disagreement is what I'd like you to adjudicate. One group accepts the picture wholesale and draws a deflationary moral: since the self is an abstraction, talk of a deep inner self that has privileged access to its own nature is a confusion, and first-person authority is mostly an artifact of the narrative we're each best positioned to tell. The other group thinks the view quietly collapses into eliminativism: if the self is a posit like a center of mass, then strictly speaking no one ever acts, suffers, or is responsible — we don't hold centers of mass criminally liable — so Dennett has explained the self away rather than explained it, and our practices of praise, blame, and promising are left resting on nothing. A third worry, raised by our TA, cuts across both: unlike a center of mass, the self is supposed to be a thing that experiences, and it is unclear whether an explanatory posit can be the bearer of consciousness at all.

Could you write an essay that weighs these positions and reaches a definite verdict? In particular, I'd like you to address (a) whether treating the self as an abstractum genuinely preserves everything our concept of a person was doing — especially moral responsibility and first-person authority — or whether it gives something up that matters; (b) whether the analogy to the center of mass is doing legitimate work or whether it conceals a crucial disanalogy, given that the self is also the subject of experience; and (c) what your verdict implies for a concrete case, say, whether someone's sincere, stable, and socially well-integrated life story is enough to make them the proper target of long-term praise and blame. Please don't end on a fence-sitting note: defend one camp (or a reasoned hybrid), and make clear what you think survives of the ordinary notion of a self. Feel free to bring in broader positions from the philosophy of mind literature where it helps, but the argument should stand on its own.
