I have just taken over as grader for an undergraduate seminar on the historiography of the Holocaust, and I am working through my first batch of midterm answers. Since I did not write the exam questions, I want my grading to be careful and even-handed. Please assign one of these answers a letter grade (A through F) and justify the grade with point-by-point comments identifying exactly what the answer gets right and where it goes wrong, misattributes a view, or draws a conclusion its evidence does not support.

The exam question read: Christopher Browning's Ordinary Men (1992) and Daniel Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners (1996) offer competing explanations of why roughly five hundred middle-aged reserve policemen, most of them from working-class Hamburg backgrounds and not selected for ideological commitment, carried out mass shootings such as the massacre at Józefów on 13 July 1942. Characterize each historian's explanation and assess which better accounts for the behavior of Reserve Police Battalion 101, drawing on at least three specific pieces of evidence.

The student's answer, reproduced in full:

Browning and Goldhagen agree on more than their famous quarrel suggests: both describe the perpetrators as ordinary, both accept that the men of Battalion 101 were reservists from Hamburg rather than Nazi volunteers, and both reject the claim that the men were forced to shoot on pain of death. Where they differ is on why the men participated. Goldhagen holds that the battalion was uniquely saturated with a peculiarly German, eliminationist antisemitism that made killing Jews seem natural, even a duty. Browning's explanation is situational: following Milgram's obedience experiments, which showed that subjects obey an authority figure when instructed to harm another person because they fear the consequences of refusing, he argues that the men obeyed because they feared the disciplinary and career penalties Trapp could impose. Conformity, deference to authority, the numbing effect of repeated actions, and wartime propaganda did the rest.

The Józefów massacre exposes the difference between the two accounts. Trapp announced the task and offered any man who did not feel up to it the chance to step out; only about a dozen of the roughly five hundred did, so some ninety-seven percent chose to shoot, which confirms Goldhagen's claim that the men were willing. Browning's difficulty is that his own evidence undermines his conclusion: if a man could refuse without penalty, fear of punishment cannot explain the participation of those who stayed. Browning would have to reply that the offer was not made clearly enough for most men to treat it as real, but nothing in his account supports that. On balance, then, Goldhagen's explanation fits the Józefów evidence better, while Browning is more convincing about the later deportations, where habituation and the routine of roundups plausibly mattered more than conviction.
