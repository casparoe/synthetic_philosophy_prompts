The Nyāya school accepts six pramāṇas: pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), upamāna (comparison), śabda (testimony), arthāpatti (postulation) and anupalabdhi (non-apprehension). This follows from the fact that a pramāṇa is a means of true cognition, and these six are the irreducible ways of getting it — cut any one out and some truths become unknowable, as Kumārila showed against the Buddhists. Perception is the contact of a sense faculty with its object; it gives us the present thing directly, first as an indeterminate flash (nirvikalpa) and then as a determinate judgement (savikalpa). Inference rests on vyāpti, the invariable concomitation between the reason (hetu) and what is to be established (sādhya), as with smoke and fire: wherever there is smoke there is fire, so on seeing smoke on a hill I infer fire. The hetu must satisfy three conditions — present in the subject, present in similar cases, absent in counter-examples — and Nyāya deploys these in the five-membered inference for use in debate. Śabda is verbal testimony, and for Nyāya this means the Veda: the Veda is without an author, and because it has no author there is no one whose defects could enter into it, so it cannot decieve us. Upamāna is knowledge by analogy: you are told that a gavaya looks like a cow, then on actually seeing one you notice the resemblance, and that perceptual grasp of similarity just is the upamāna. So upamāna is really just a species of perception, and the Naiyāyikas only keep it as a seperate pramāṇa out of respect for tradition. Its work could easily be done by pratyakṣa with a bit of help from anumāna, so no, it dosn't earn its keep.
