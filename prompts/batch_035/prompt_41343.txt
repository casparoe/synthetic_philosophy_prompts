I'm a PhD student in political theory, and next month I'm moderating the Q&A of our department's graduate workshop on democratic epistemology. The featured speaker is an invited guest who has built her career arguing that the Condorcet jury theorem supplies the strongest epistemic case for universal suffrage: if each voter is even slightly more likely than chance to identify the correct outcome or the better policy, and votes are independent, then majority rule becomes virtually certain to track the truth as the electorate grows large. She treats the theorem not as a curiosity in the history of social choice theory but as a live argument against epistocratic proposals to restrict or weight the franchise.

Draft for me a sequence of eight to ten questions for that Q&A. They should press on the actual load-bearing premises of her position rather than invite her to restate it, and I want each question paired with a follow-up keyed to the response she is most likely to give. Specifically, I want the sequence to cover the following, at minimum:

1. The competence premise. She defines competence as voters being more likely than chance to judge correctly, but critics charge that voter error is not random noise around truth; it is systematically biased by partisanship, motivated reasoning, and low-information heuristics. Include a follow-up on the familiar "miracle of aggregation" reply — that a small informed minority plus unbiased noise still drives the majority toward truth — and how she reconciles that reply with the empirical finding that political knowledge is itself correlated with the very interests the common-good standard is supposed to neutralize.

2. The independence premise. Ladha's results on correlated votes suggest that even modest correlation among errors, or a common shock that pushes everyone the same wrong way, can destroy the theorem's convergence. Include a follow-up on the standard move of decomposing error into a systematic component (which deliberation and shared media generate) and an idiosyncratic component: ask her to say what effective sample size remains once only the idiosyncratic component counts, and whether she has ever calculated it.

3. The tension between independence and deliberation. Democracy's defenders usually prize public deliberation, but deliberation is precisely a mechanism for correlating beliefs. Ask her to choose: does she weaken the independence assumption to accommodate deliberation, or weaken her commitment to deliberation to preserve the theorem? Anticipate her likely appeal to structured deliberation or deliberative polls, and follow up on whether the sort of small, curated deliberation that improves competence can be scaled to national electorates without reintroducing correlation.

4. The swing voter's curse. Feddersen and Pesendorfer showed that rational, privately informed voters may abstain or vote against their own signal, so the set of voters who actually turn out is selected in ways the theorem's model does not describe. Ask whether this selection strengthens or undermines her argument, with a follow-up on whether she accepts turnout itself as endogenous to competence.

5. The common-interest assumption. The theorem assumes everyone is trying to answer the same question, as a jury does. But democratic politics is arguably constituted by disagreement about what question is being asked. Include a follow-up on whether she takes the List-Goodin style extension to plurality-rules and heterogeneous judgments to be a strengthening of the epistemic argument or a tacit admission that the jury framing was doing illegitimate work.

Beyond those five, use your judgment, but consider a question about the asymptotic character of the theorem — whether its guarantees arrive only at population sizes where no alternative institution could fail — and a closing question about what possible empirical finding she would accept as a refutation of the theorem's applicability to real electorates, since an argument that no evidence could touch is not much of an argument for democracy.

One more constraint: I have been asked by the workshop conveners to keep the Q&A adversarial but professional, so frame the questions as attempts to help her position rather than to trap her, and avoid anything that merely rehearses the standard epistocracy debate — I want the questions to stay inside the formal structure of the theorem itself.
