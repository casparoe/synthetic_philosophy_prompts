My brother-in-law cornered me at a christening a few weeks back and I've been chewing on his argument ever since. Some context on me: I do records management for an NHS trust, so I think about what gets kept and what gets binned more than your average person — most of what we hold sits on a retention schedule, meaning it gets shredded after however many years whether or not anyone powerful wanted it gone, it's mostly just storage costs and policy. Anyway, he's been reading about how archives aren't neutral windows onto the past, and he laid this on me:

(1) Archives are created by institutions — states, churches, companies — and institutions record what serves their purposes. (2) So the records that survive mostly tell us what the powerful wanted recorded, and the gaps fall exactly where the powerless lived. He gave the example that most of what we can know about enslaved people comes from bills of sale and plantation ledgers kept by the enslavers themselves, which seemed hard to argue with. (3) A record made for someone's purposes reflects those purposes, not what actually happened. (4) So the archive can't tell us what happened — only what the powerful wanted said. (5) And before I objected that oral histories and family stories could fill the gap, he said those are no better, becuase they also only survived through whoever had the standing to talk and be listened to. (6) So every source is somebody's purpose wearing a costume, and none of them tell us what happened. (7) And if no source tells us what happened, then no account of the past is better than any other — the historian's version, his nan's version, the version the government puts out, the version of the bloke online who reckons the numbers for the whole trade were made up.

Now I want to push back, because (7) sounds completely mad — he's effectively told me the crank's account is as good as anyone's, and that can't be right — but I honestly can't see which step to deny. (1) and (2) strike me as just true, and I'd rather keep them; they're the part of his reading I actually find compelling, and the plantation ledger thing has genuinely changed how I look at my own storerooms at work. (3) sounds fine too, almost boringly so. So have we proved too much somewhere, or is he right that it's purposes all the way down? Tell me which step is the weak one (if there is one), why it looked fine to me when it isn't, and what rejecting it costs — becuase if the only escape is watering down (1) and (2), I want to know exactly how much watering I'm in for, and whether the crank still doesn't get to sit at the table afterwards.
