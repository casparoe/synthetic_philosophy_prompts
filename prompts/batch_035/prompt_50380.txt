I am setting an exam question for an undergraduate course covering the three-way distinction between negative liberty (the absence of external impediments to action), positive liberty (self-mastery or the effective capacity to act on one's own will), and republican liberty (the absence of domination — not living at the mercy of another's arbitrary power). I would like you to construct a thought experiment for this question in which a single, clearly identifiable institutional change leaves a person with more negative liberty than before and yet newly dominated, so that the negative and republican conceptions deliver opposite verdicts on whether the change made the person freer.

The scenario must satisfy the following constraints. The setting should be realistic and contemporary — a workplace, a tenancy, a platform-economy arrangement, or something comparable — and may not involve literal slavery, imprisonment, or a dictator. The domination must be generated by the institutional change itself rather than by anyone's malice or character: the party who acquires arbitrary power over the person should be entirely benevolent and should never in fact interfere. To keep the case non-trivial, I also want the dominator's day-to-day capacity to interfere to be deliberately disabled — they should hold the standing and legal right to interfere at will, but have no routine channel through which to do so and no track record of doing so — so that any loss of freedom the case establishes cannot be redescribed by a sceptic as threatened, probable, or merely possible interference. Finally, please do not stipulate the verdicts; they should fall out of the conceptions themselves, since the case has to be usable as an exam question.

After setting out the scenario in a paragraph or two, do the following. Walk through what each of the three conceptions says about whether the person's freedom increased or decreased as a result of the change, and explain why. Show explicitly how the scenario meets each of my constraints. Identify which feature of the case is doing the work in pulling the negative and republican verdicts apart, and say what happens to those verdicts if that feature is removed. Then give the best response available to a defender of the negative conception — someone who insists that unfreedom requires an actual or threatened impediment, not a dormant entitlement — and assess whether that response succeeds or merely relabels the domination as something else.
