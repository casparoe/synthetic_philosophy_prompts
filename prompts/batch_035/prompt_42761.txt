I've been asked to circulate a one-page statement of my considered position on the self for a workshop on personal identity, and before I do, I want to know whether the position actually hangs together. Here it is, as I'd state it:

1. I accept Dennett's picture: the self is not a thing inside the person but a center of narrative gravity — an abstract posit, generated by the brain's habit of organizing its own outputs into a story, around which we predict and explain a person's behavior. There is no further fact about who I am beyond what the narrative, disciplined by the behavioral evidence, pins down.
2. I take the confabulation literature — split-brain patients constructing justifications for actions initiated elsewhere, subjects sincerely explaining choices they never actually made — to be strong support for this: self-narratives are partly constructed after the fact, and the narrator is a character in the story rather than its author.
3. I hold that the self is genuinely causally efficacious: when I decide to raise my arm and the arm rises, the decision is mine, attributable to me rather than merely to the neural machinery that generates the narrative, and this is what makes my actions things I did rather than things that happened in me.
4. I believe in robust first-person authority: my sincere avowals of my current attitudes are not evidence on a par with third-person data — they partly constitute the attitudes they express, and others can be wrong about my mind in a way that I cannot.
5. I am drawn to MacIntyre's view that the unity of a life is narrative unity, and that this is what makes a life the kind of thing that can go better or worse, what grounds prudential concern for my future self, and what makes me answerable for the commitments of my past self.
6. I am a thoroughgoing naturalist: whatever is true of selves must be true in virtue of facts about organisms and their doings, with no sui generis mental substances or powers.

My question is whether this set is jointly tenable. I genuinely cannot tell which commitments pull against each other, which are the same commitment stated twice, and which merely look incompatible. I have one suspicion I want checked: (1) and (5) both invoke narrative, and in my head they reinforce each other, but when I try to say precisely what narrative unity is, the deflationary use — a fiction the brain spins, epistemically useful, ontologically thin — and the neo-Aristotelian use — a constitutive, normatively loaded unity that a life either has or fails to have — seem to come apart, and I don't know whether that's a real conflict or just two senses of one word. Beyond that, I have no view about where the problems are.

Please audit the set. Identify which pairs are in genuine tension, rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and say for each what the cheapest repair would be and what that repair costs elsewhere in the set. Note which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, so I can tell the difference between real trouble and mere surface friction. Finally, if I am allowed exactly one revision to the position, tell me which single change cleans up the most. I am not attached to any particular item; I want the position to survive the workshop, not to win an argument.
