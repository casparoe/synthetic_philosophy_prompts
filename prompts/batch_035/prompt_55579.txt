Write a philosophical dialogue between two hedonists, Mira and Ezra, who agree that pleasure is the only thing good for its own sake but disagree about what pleasure is. Mira holds a phenomenological view: pleasures share a distinctive felt quality, so a theory of pleasure is a theory of what that feeling is like. Ezra holds an attitudinal view, in the tradition of philosophers like Fred Feldman and Chris Heathwood: what makes a mental episode a pleasure is not a particular texture of experience but the fact that it is an attitude of enjoying, taking satisfaction in, or being pleased about something.

The dialogue should be structured around three cases they are trying to use as evidence:

1. The chili-chocolate case. Mira notes that the pleasure of eating chili chocolate includes a burn, an edge of irritation, that phenomenally resembles mild pain. The phenomenal-quality view seems to predict this should subtract from the pleasure rather than compose it, while the attitudinal view handles it smoothly. Ezra should press this point hard; Mira must attempt a real response rather than a dodge.

2. The absorption case. A rock climber, looking back on her afternoon, sincerely says she enjoyed it, but insists that while climbing she felt nothing at all—no buzz, no glow, no distinctive quality—only total absorption in the moves. Ezra argues this is a pleasure without any felt pleasure, refuting Mira's view. Mira should reply with the move that one can enjoy something while the pleasure goes unfelt or unattended, and the two should then argue about whether that move saves phenomenology or quietly abandons it.

3. The inverted-hedon case, a thought experiment they invent together in the course of the conversation. Suppose there were a creature neurologically and functionally just like a normal human, whose reports of enjoyment, pursuits, and satisfied desires all came out indistinguishable from ours, but whose introspective character was inverted relative to ours. Mira and Ezra should disagree about what follows: Ezra will claim the case shows pleasure is fixed by attitudes rather than by feel; Mira should try to argue that the case is underdescribed or question-begging, and the discussion should expose what each side is treating as primitive.

Somewhere in the exchange, one of them should raise the general worry—familiar from William Alston—that "pleasure" may simply be a disjunctive or cluster concept with no single common essence, and both should find this suggestion unwelcome for different reasons, explaining why.

Requirements: Both characters must be equally competent and equally willing to concede points; neither is a strawman, and each must make at least one genuine concession to the other by the end. They must respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talking past each other. The dialogue should end with the disagreement still live but sharpened—each character should state, in their final turn, precisely what evidence would change their mind. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words.
