I'm trying to work out my actual position on mind uploading by going with my gut on a series of cases, and I'd like to know whether some principle captures the pattern or whether my gut is just incoherent. The cases all involve a procedure in which biological neurons are, one at a time, replaced by artificial devices while the rest of the brain carries on: I stay awake and conversing during the whole thing, so if anything went wrong with my stream of experience, I'd presumably notice and say so. My verdicts: replacing each neuron with a little chip that simulates its ion-channel dynamics in real time feels clearly fine, and I'd expect to end up still me. The same procedure done in a single night under general anesthesia also feels fine to me, which surprised me. Replacing neurons with devices that merely reproduce each neuron's observed input-output behavior, via a stored lookup table rather than any running dynamics, makes me balk — that doesn't seem like thinking, even though from outside my behavior would be identical. Pausing the procedure at 70% and living like that for a year feels fine. But a variant in which each artificial neuron keeps its state in a shared cloud server, so that every replaced neuron communicates with its neighbors through a round trip with 50 milliseconds of latency, bothers me much more than the local-device version, even though the clinic insists the only difference is that my thought processes run somewhat slower. That verdict seems hard to reconcile with my comfort with the awake, weeklong version, and I can't see what principled feature could distinguish them.

There are two further cases I keep going back and forth on. In one, the artificial devices are reset to a factory state every midnight and reloaded from a continuous log of the day's activity, so the causal chain running through the device itself is severed nightly, though the total system's function is unbroken. In the other, the replacement runs at a thousand times biological speed, so my subjective experience of the procedure should be over in seconds — yet I report, during the weeks it takes the surgeons, that I feel perfectly normal. Is there a single principle — something about functional continuity, or local causal connections, or the identity of the system doing the computing — that delivers all of my verdicts? If not, I'd like to know which verdicts a simpler principle would overturn, and why it's those rather than the others. And if you can state the principle precisely, please tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't mentioned: halfway through the procedure, the clinic proposes switching the remaining neurons to the lookup-table kind, on the grounds that the early replacements show no measurable difference. I want to know not just the prediction but which of my commitments it puts pressure on.
