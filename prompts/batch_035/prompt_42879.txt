I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though my work keeps brushing against it. For the past few years I've built tooling around static analysis: checkers that take a program plus an assertion, hand the constraints to an SMT solver, and report either a proof or a counterexample. Last quarter we shipped a bug that the code review, the test suite, and our analyzer all missed, and in the postmortem a colleague who reads epistemic logic for fun walked me into an argument I can't get out of. He claims it shows that the notion of rationality used in decision theory and economics is an honorific with no bearers, and while I'm sure that's too strong, I can't locate the broken step. Here is the argument as he gives it, numbered:

(1) Rational belief is closed under logical consequence: if you believe p, and q follows logically from p, then, if you are rational, you believe q. (He says this is not a fringe claim — it is what the standard model of belief in the logics we work with assumes, and it is what lets a solver certify an assertion once we hand it the constraints.) (2) How a program behaves on every input follows logically from its source text together with the formal semantics of the language — that is simply what a semantics is. (3) An engineer who can read the code believes the source text, and believes the semantics; we test candidates on exactly this in interviews. (4) So a rational engineer believes how the program behaves on every input. (5) Believing that is already knowing every bug in the program and every property that will hold, since bugs and correctness are just facts about behavior. (6) Engineers are rational in the ordinary sense: they update on counterexamples and don't cling to flat contradictions. (7) Therefore every rational engineer already knows every bug in their code, and code review, testing, and our entire verification stack can only ever be a reminder of what was known all along. From the falsity of (7) — and it is false; we were genuinely surprised, and you can only be surprised by what you didn't know — he concludes that no human satisfies (1), that closure is a norm no finite being can meet, and hence that "rational agent" as used by economists and decision theorists picks out nothing.

Two things I'd like to hold onto, and I suspect they conflict: the right to say my colleague was being irrational last spring, when he insisted a provably broken component was fine until the counterexample was in front of him, and the right to say that the rest of us simply did not know about this bug. If (1) stands, I seem to lose the second; if I reject (1) outright, I seem to lose the first. One more wrinkle I keep turning over: his support for (1) via our tooling looks wrong on its own terms, since the solver does not derive every consequence — it times out, and for some logics there is no procedure that derives all of them — so our tools are, if anything, models of bounded rather than ideal inference. I can't tell whether that cuts against him or, perversely, for him. Please tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine to both of us, and what rejecting it costs; and separately, tell me whether his final conclusion — that rationality as used in decision theory is an empty honorific — actually follows even if (1) does fail, or whether that is a second, independent slide I should be pushing back on.
