I spent most of last weekend arguing with a friend about whether rational credence has to be a precise number. She holds the orthodox line: a rational agent's degrees of belief should be representable by a single probability function, and anything that looks like imprecision — discomfort with Ellsberg's two-urn choices, refusal to bet at either side of a price — is better explained as risk aversion, or uncertainty about one's own utilities, or ordinary ignorance about which of several precise priors is the right one, all of which a sharp Bayesian can model. I pushed back with the standard imprecise-probability picture: credal sets, dilation, and decision rules like maximization of the minimum expected utility across the set. But the argument ground to a halt on a point I couldn't answer cleanly. She said: whatever pattern of choices your imprecise agent exhibits, I can construct a sharp agent with a suitable probability function and utility function who makes exactly those choices; so imprecise probabilities are a bookkeeping device for features that are perfectly representable within the orthodox framework, and our dispute is verbal. I replied that the mimicry cannot work in general, because an imprecise agent's preferences shift under dilation in a way no fixed sharp agent can track. She asked me to exhibit the case. I could not construct one on the spot, and I am still not certain I can.

That failure is the germ of the term paper I need to plan. Please turn it into a research proposal for a one-semester philosophy paper of roughly 6000 words. The question as I can currently state it: is there a pattern of choices, whether static or dynamic, that distinguishes an agent with imprecise credences from every sharp-probability agent, or is the difference in representation without behavioral difference? In the proposal, please do the following. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, saying exactly what counts as a "sharp mimic" and over what class of decision situations the comparison is run. Explain why the standard positions on both sides leave the question open — in particular why the usual Ellsberg-based arguments for imprecise probabilities do not by themselves answer the mimicry challenge. Set out the method concretely: what I would do week by week, what a mimicry result would have to look like to settle the question in her favor, and what a counterexample of the kind I gestured at with dilation would have to be shown to do in order to settle it in mine. Be explicit about the possible conclusions and which considerations would decide among them. Include an honest assessment of what could make the project too big for one semester — I suspect the mathematics of dilation and of updating credal sets could swallow the paper whole, and I would rather know that now. Finally, since my friend will read the proposal and will demand to know whether our argument was substantive, say what actually turns on the answer, for decision theory and for the philosophy of probability more broadly. Do not include citations or a literature review; I will supply references myself.
