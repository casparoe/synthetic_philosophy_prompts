My friend is a representational painter who has spent the last year arguing with me about image generators, and she has now produced an argument whose conclusion she despises and cannot refute. She calls diffusion models "very expensive averages" -- each output a point pulled back toward the center of mass of a hundred million captioned images -- and the argument runs as follows.

(1) For a work to be genuinely creative, its maker must be answerable for it: someone must be able to say why the work is as it is -- why this cadmium rather than that ochre, why the horizon sits where it does -- in terms that justify the choice rather than merely cause it.

(2) A diffusion model cannot be answerable in that sense. Its output is the deterministic endpoint of training data, weights, prompt, and seed, and the only explanation anyone can give of why the image came out as it did is a causal story about gradient descent and denoising steps; there is no justifying story, and no one -- least of all the model -- who could give one.

(3) But the same is true of her, or so she now concedes. Her choices are the deterministic endpoint of genes, culture, years of training, and the state of her cortex this morning; her after-the-fact explanations of why a passage works are post-hoc narratives about what her motor system already settled, as the choice-blindness experiments suggest when people earnestly defend decisions they never made.

(4) So if creative responsibility requires justifying rather than merely causal explanation, and no one -- human or machine -- can provide one, then her painting is no more creative than image generation is.

(5) Since that is unbearable, she concludes that the flaw is in the concept itself: there is no such thing as genuine creativity, only causal processes that we flatter with the vocabulary of answerability, and the difference between her and the model is that we have agreed not to look backstage at her.

She is now half-convinced of (5), which is why I am writing to you. I suspect the slide happens between (2) and (3), or inside (4), but I cannot name it precisely, and I notice the argument equivocates somewhere between there being no justifying explanation of a work and there being no one to demand one from. I would much rather keep (1) than keep (5), but I want to know exactly what I am paying for whichever premise I give up.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, one per premise above, each a sentence or two: say whether you accept that premise as stated and, if you reject it, what rejecting it costs. No introduction, no conclusion, nothing outside the list.
