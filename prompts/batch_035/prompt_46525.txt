I sit on the serious-event review committee at a mid-sized community hospital, and next week we have to issue a written determination in a case that has split the committee down the middle. An overnight patient on a telemetry floor died of worsening hyperkalemia before dawn. The lab's critical-value alert system paged the covering hospitalist twice with escalating potassium results; she acknowledged neither page — she says she was managing a rapid response on another unit and that the pages should have auto-routed to the charge nurse, which the system is not configured to do. The patient's primary nurse noticed the abnormal result but, relying on the standard practice that critical labs go to the covering physician, did not escalate further. And the patient himself had, earlier that evening, refused the repeat blood draw that would have confirmed the trend. Under our state's peer-review framework, how we fill in the "causal contribution" field on the root-cause analysis form determines whether the hospitalist is referred for remediation, whether the nurse faces a practice alert on her license record, and whether the event is reportable up the chain. Three factions have formed: the hospitalist's colleagues insist that only the hyperkalemia caused the death and that filling in any omission as a cause is unfair scapegoating; the nursing representatives argue that if any non-action counts as a cause, then the nurse's failure to escalate counts equally, and they point out that the physician is the one whose omission is being treated as more consequential; and a third group wants every salient omission — including the never-acknowledged pages and the ambiguously worded callback policy — listed as contributing causes, since that's what drives the safety fixes.

I've been reading enough philosophy of causation to know this isn't just a hospital-politics fight, and I want the memo to be philosophically defensible, not just administratively tidy. The live issue is causation by omission: it seems wrong to say the hospitalist's silence caused the death when there's no physical process linking her not-paging-back to the patient's heart rhythm, and it seems equally wrong to say the chief medical officer — who also didn't check the potassium — contributed to the death, even though his omission is structurally the same kind of non-event. I gather the standard moves are that omissions are causes only relative to a contrast class or default, and that treating omissions as causes may just be smuggling in norms about who was supposed to do what. I'd like you to take the role of a philosopher specializing in causation who has been brought in to advise us: before drafting, think carefully about which contrast classes are in play here and who gets to set them, and consider whether the "causal contribution" field on our form is even tracking causation or something more like responsibility.

Draft the two-page decision memo I can circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete determination for the form — which omissions, if any, we list as causal contributions, and in what terms — plus criteria the committee could actually apply to comparable future cases, stated in plain language my clinical colleagues won't roll their eyes at. Engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side: the position that "cause" should be reserved for positive events and that everything we're really doing is assigning duty-violations under another name. And tell me honestly if your recommendation implies that our form conflates two questions the committee should be answering separately, since if so the memo should say that too — I'd rather fix the instrument than keep litigating these cases one by one.
