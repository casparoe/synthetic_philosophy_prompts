I run a philosophy of mind reading group, and at our last meeting two members spent an hour arguing about whether split-brain patients have two consciousnesses. I couldn't referee it, and I'd like your help. Here is the exchange, as close to verbatim as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Elena said the case was nearly settled: after a full callosotomy there are two subjects of experience. The right hemisphere perceives, understands some language, forms preferences, and acts on them independently of the speaking hemisphere. In the classic setups it recognizes images flashed only to it, and in one well-known anecdote a patient, shown an emotionally charged picture to the right hemisphere alone, laughed and confabulated a reason for her mood — which shows the right hemisphere had an experience the left was simply not party to. Two perceivers with no informational bridge between them: two consciousnesses.

(2) Marcus replied that this shows information processing without verbal report, not a second experiencer: the right hemisphere could be an unconscious module, and mute does not mean mindful. The dramatic two-minds behavior, he said, essentially requires the lab trickery — brief lateralized flashes that never occur in ordinary life, where free eye movements give both hemispheres the same input. And he cited recent testing on two callosotomy patients: despite the hemispheres being able to exchange almost nothing, both patients could verbally report an object shown in the left visual field and indicate its location, responding about equally well with either hand. So the brain as a whole sustains a single perceiver, and the textbook picture of two agents each owning one hand and one half of the field was wrong.

(3) Elena called that finding too coarse to be decisive: good performance on presence-and-location questions is compatible with the right hemisphere having its own experiences while the left hemisphere guesses well enough on easy tasks. And she pointed to the cases where the streams plainly diverge: patients who cross-cue, passing signals between hemispheres like two people smuggling notes, and the intermanual conflicts, where one hand undoes what the other has just done. When the two halves of one body hold incompatible intentions at the same moment, she said, that is two agents, and the burden is on Marcus to explain what single subject could simultaneously want opposite things.

(4) Marcus answered that the burden is really on her to supply a counting criterion. Ask each hemisphere separately for its name, age, and hometown, and both give the same answers — but that is exactly what you would expect whether there is one subject with one autobiography or two subjects sharing a biography, so the headcount never gets independent grip on the data. Until we have a criterion for individuating subjects of experience that does more than restate the behavioral evidence, he said, the question "one or two?" is partly verbal, and the underlying reality is probably graded — something like how much of the patient's awareness is jointly accessible — rather than a matter of counting minds.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the four numbered moves, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. Then tell me who is more right overall and on which specific points, whether the recent finding Marcus cites in (2) is as damaging to Elena's position as he takes it to be, and whether Marcus's move (4) succeeds in showing that part of the dispute is merely verbal — and if so, state precisely which part, and what substantive question about these patients remains once the verbal part is stripped away.
