So here's what I want: an essay on interpersonal utility comparisons — you know, the old question of whether we can ever say "the loss to this person is worse than the gain to that person," which is exactly what you need for any real utilitarian tradeoff but which a whole tradition in economics declared impossible in principle.

A bit of setup so we're on the same page, since I want you to go deeper than the standard textbook shuffle. The usual move is: utilities are just numbers representing preferences, each person's scale is arbitrary up to positive affine transformation, there's no common unit and no common zero, therefore comparisons across persons are meaningless. Fine. But notice this argument, if it works, only really threatens the *strong* thing — a full common cardinal scale with a shared zero, the kind of thing you'd need to literally add utilities across people. Almost nobody in ordinary life claims that. What we actually claim, constantly, is weaker: that one person is *worse off* than another (an ordinal cross-person judgment), or occasionally that this person's loss is *bigger than* that person's gain (a cross-person comparison of two changes). The standard skeptic argument, as far as I can tell, just doesn't touch those. So a good essay has to figure out which comparisons are actually on the table before deciding whether they're possible.

Here's the bit I really want you to engage with — I've not seen this put quite this way anywhere and I'm curious what you do with it. Imagine we had what I'll call an "experience duplex": a hypothetical device that lets one person feel a brief, calibrated sample of exactly what another person is feeling, at a fixed intensity, without distorting it. Now ask: is the ordinary claim "she's worse off than him" the kind of claim that would be *settled* by putting them both through the duplex and comparing? Two very different pictures fall out. On one, our comparative welfare judgments are (imperfect, indirect) attempts at measuring something the duplex would measure — the concept points at an intrinsic fact about experience, and the duplex is the ideal instrument our ordinary judgments approximate. On the other, the duplex is simply *irrelevant* to the meaning: "worse off" is fixed by something like the concept's social role — how the judgment functions in deliberation, what it entitles us to do — and no instrument, however perfect, could settle it because there's nothing of that sort to settle. I suspect where you land on the duplex question largely determines where you land on the whole debate, so take an actual position on it.

And one more thread you should weave in: the skeptic about cross-person comparisons owes what I'd call an error theory, or at least a reinterpretation theory. Nurses triage, parents split the last piece of cake "because your brother wanted it more," policy types say the tax hurts the poor more than it helps. If all cross-person comparison is meaningless, then either all this ordinary talk is systematically infected (in which case say what it's actually doing — expressing? coordinating? something else?) or it's meaningful in some way the skeptic's formal argument doesn't capture. Either answer is interesting; dodging it isn't.
