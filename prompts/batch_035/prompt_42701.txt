I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the Overcoming Bias days, and I now help run a local community meetup that has drifted over the years from its early epistemics-hygiene roots toward a membership of working ML engineers. Next month it's my turn to lead the session, and I want to do it on Bongard problems — the little six-by-six grids where you induce the rule separating two classes of figures from a dozen examples — and on what they do and don't show about learning concepts from very few positive and negative instances.

My audience is mostly engineers who came to the meetup through AI-interest channels in the last few years. They are bright, allergic to anything that smells of philosophy of mind, and quietly convinced that the sample-efficiency question was settled the moment large models started picking up new tasks from a handful of in-context examples; their implicit slogan is that priors are what you needed back when you didn't have scale. Two or three old-timers from the early days will also come, and they will treat any appeal to priors or inductive bias as either trivially true or unfalsifiable, whichever is more convenient at the moment.

I don't want you to write the session's content. I want a teaching plan: the order the pieces have to go in and why, given what depends on what — I suspect the audience has to see that a learner needs a hypothesis space before data can mean anything, but I'm not sure how to get there without a lecture they'll tune out. Then: which single Bongard problem should carry the whole session — what properties it needs, and why the canonical easy ones that open Hofstadter's discussion in Gödel, Escher, Bach are probably the wrong choice despite being the ones everyone half-remembers. Then the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they'll actually surface: that this is just clustering or classification with a small dataset; that in-context learning has made the priors question obsolete; that once you say Solomonoff induction the matter is closed and nothing empirical remains; and that these puzzles are a solved benchmark from the seventies, so why care. For each, tell me where in the sequence it should be headed off rather than left for the discussion to stumble into.

I should also say what my own thesis for the evening is, because the plan should be built to defend it: my view is that the deep question isn't how any particular solver cracks a given grid but why the space of humanly learnable rule sets is so strikingly small — Bongard problems are curated, and the curation encodes a substantive theory of which concepts are natural or joints-like, which means the dataset itself is evidence about inductive structure rather than a neutral test of it. If the session works, the engineers should leave seeing the problem-set designer, not the solver, as the interesting object. Include a check I can run at the end to see whether that landed as opposed to just leaving them mildly entertained, and tell me what to explicitly refuse to cover — I assume the formal details of Kolmogorov complexity and the entire literature on analogy-making are on that list, but I'd like to know what else joins them and how to say so without sounding like I'm dodging.
