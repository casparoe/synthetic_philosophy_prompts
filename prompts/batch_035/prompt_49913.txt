So here's where I've landed after thinking about this for a while: I've come to hold the view that satisficing isn't just a patch on rational choice theory — it's not "maximisation with an apology attached" — but the actual correct standard of rationality for real agents. The story that got me here is roughly Herbert Simon's old one: classical rationality assumes an agent that can survey every option, compute the expected value of each, and pick the best, and that's a creature that doesn't exist. Real agents with limited time, information, and compute do something else — they set an aspiration level and search until they hit "good enough," then stop. That's satisficing. And I've been pushed further by the ecological-rationality crowd: simple heuristics that stop at the first option passing some threshold, fast-and-frugal ones like take-the-best, have been shown matching or beating optimisation models on out-of-sample prediction in some domains. To me that suggests the norm isn't "approximate the optimiser as best you can" but "be a good satisficer" — procedural rationality, as Simon would put it, rather than substantive rationality constrained by inconvenience.

- The insulation worry. When a heuristic wins, that's credited to satisficing; when it loses, we're told the aspiration level was wrong or the environment wasn't the one the heuristic was adapted to.
