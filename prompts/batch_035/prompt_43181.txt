So my friend and I had a marathon argument last night (three hours, way too much wine) and I want to turn the fallout into something useful. Quick context: I'm the clinical ethicist at a fertility clinic. Not a philosopher — I did a masters years ago and remember fragments. My job is mostly sitting in consults where doctors and patients disagree about whether it's ok to do some fertility treatment or other, and whether "the future child's welfare" is a reason to refuse or delay treatment. I get two or three of these a week, about twenty minutes each, and the clinicians want an answer they can act on, not a seminar.

The argument: my friend is a doctor and her position is dead simple. If the parents are going to have a kid who they know will have a miserable condition, and the clinic could help them avoid that, then refusing or steering them away is just protecting a child from harm. Obviously. I pulled the philosopher card, the nonidentity problem — Parfit's thing, where if a different decision means a different child exists, then the child you're "protecting" couldn't have existed without the decision you're calling harmful, so who exactly got harmed? She said it was a parlor trick, that by my logic nobody was ever harmed by being born into anything, and that real ethics committees don't have time for it anyway. And honestly she's half right about that last part — thats exactly why I'm writing to you.

You're a professional philosopher who specializes in population ethics and reproductive ethics. Think carefully and do some brainstorming before you commit to a structure — I want to see you work out which parts of the theory actually need to survive into a usable procedure and which can be dropped, not just recite the theory at me with steps bolted on.

What I need: a decision procedure I can actually run in a twenty-minute consult. The questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with "there is no child-welfare complaint here" (and what, if anything, is left of the objection once it does), and where it has to hand the decision back to the people in the room because the remaining question is about values, not facts. The thing my friend got me worried about: almost all my cases involve decisions made weeks or months before conception, and nobody can tell whether the decision changes which child exists or just changes how the same child's life goes. The procedure has to say what to do when identity-dependence is unknowable — it can't just shrug there, because that's the most common case I see, and I suspect it's where the whole thing either earns its keep or collapses.

Then run it on three real-ish cases of mine:

1. A couple who are both carriers of a severe, painful, life-shortening recessive condition. They've refused donor gametes, they want a genetically related child, they understand the odds, they're not naive. The clinician wants to refuse treatment on child-welfare grounds.

2. A doctor wants a patient to delay embryo transfer by three months to clear a teratogenic medication. The delay will mean a different child exists. The doctor keeps saying "we're doing it for the baby," and I keep suspecting that rationale doesn't survive contact with the theory, even if the delay is still the right call.

3. Prospective parents postponing conception for two years for financial stability, asking us whether waiting will "give their child a better life." Same worry as case 2, I think, but coming from the other direction.

At least one of these should end somewhere I won't like — you pick which — and don't soften it. Also tell me straight: where does this procedure misfire, and what can't it do at all. And please don't pad it out with a lecture on Parfit; I've had enough of that for one lifetime, and so has my friend.
