I teach a first-year seminar called Puzzles and Paradoxes — sixteen students, one seventy-five-minute session — and in two weeks we reach the announcement-style paradoxes, including the one about a teacher who schedules an exam for one day next week and declares that the students will not be able to work out in advance which day it will fall on. My students last year nodded along to the backward-induction argument on paper without ever feeling its force, and they also never registered that the "official" resolution we discussed (about whether the announcement undermines itself, confirms itself, or somehow does both) only became urgent once the deduction had actually been run under pressure. So this year I want the paradox to happen to them rather than be shown to them. Design a staged game, with no lecture beforehand and without the paradox being named until the debrief, in which one group of participants plays the school's headmaster (whose job is to fix an exam day, in secret, within a known window) and the other plays the pupils (whose job is to catch the headmaster by publicly committing, each evening of the fictional week, to either "the exam is tomorrow" or "I cannot yet rule any remaining day in or out").

Please specify the full setup: a timeline for the seventy-five minutes, the materials (cards, envelopes, a whiteboard grid for the fictional week, anything else), a point scheme that uses only a running score rather than real money — including what happens to the headmaster's score when an exam lands on a day the pupils have ruled out, and what happens when a pupil commits to "tomorrow" and is wrong — and a two-round structure in which the first round is a deliberately tiny three-day week (so the induction is easy to run in one's head and the class discovers the "then it can't be the last day either" cascade for themselves) and the second round is a five-day week with the announcement stated in writing, in full, at the start. Rotate the roles between rounds so everyone sits on both sides of the announcement.

Then the debrief: have it map specific moments from the game onto the steps of the pupils' argument, isolate exactly which premise each observed event is supposed to vindicate or embarrass (that the last day is no longer a surprise, that the headmaster cannot violate the announcement without proving it false, that an announcement the pupils can fully trust may be one they cannot fully use), and walk the class toward the three standard reactions — the announcement is self-refuting, self-confirming, or subtly misworded — without tipping your hand about which you favor. I'd also like an honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (pupils guessing randomly rather than reasoning; headmasters paralysed by the induction and refusing to choose a day at all; the class noticing by round two that the game is really about how a randomizing headmaster makes surprise trivially cheap, which arguably dissolves the puzzle instead of staging it) and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about the one feature of the real paradox no classroom game can reproduce — the pupils' iterated, self-referential knowledge of the announcement, as opposed to mechanically enforced rules — and where in the debrief I must supply it by hand. Close your response with a single line beginning "Core mechanism:" that states, in one sentence, what participants actually do and why that doing generates the paradox rather than merely illustrating it.
