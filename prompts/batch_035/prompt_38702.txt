I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar in formal epistemology, and we've just finished a unit on diachronic rationality. For my term paper I want to work through what van Fraassen's Reflection principle — roughly, the requirement that your current credence in a proposition, conditional on your future self at some time assigning it credence p, should itself be p — actually implies for how a person should manage their beliefs and plans across time, rather than treating it as a purely formal curiosity. Please describe the most important implications of Reflection for everyday epistemic self-management, considering candidates like the following: whether I should defer to my anticipated future judgments whenever I can predict them; what to do in the well-known counterexample cases, where I can foresee that my future self will be compromised (exhausted, intoxicated, in grief) and hence irrational; whether keeping a prediction journal and being scored against it later generates a Reflection-like pressure, and whether that is a good habit or an invitation to groupthink with myself; and whether committing to a long course of action that I expect to change my outlook — say, a career or a course of study — is rational trust in my future self or a way of binding myself to anticipated irrationality. Rank the implications by importance, defend the ranking, and flag any that don't genuinely follow from the principle or are overrated in the literature.
