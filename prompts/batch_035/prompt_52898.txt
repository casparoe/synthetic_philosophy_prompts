I'm giving a guest seminar at a military academy next month and I want to be ready for the Q&A. The audience is mostly senior officers and JAG lawyers doing a professional military ethics course, plus two or three philosophers who'll actually know the McMahan/Walzer literature, so I can't get away with hand-waving. My thesis is the revisionist one: the traditional moral equality of combatants is a mistake. An unjust combatant doesn't acquire a license to kill just by putting on a uniform and fighting within the rules — the Wehrmacht soldier and the British soldier in 1941 weren't morally on par even if both fought cleanly at the tactical level, because tactical compliance doesn't launder the underlying wrong of fighting for an unjust cause. My main line of argument is about responsibility: combatants aren't mindless tools, they typically have enough information available to form at least a rough view of whether their war is just, and someone who foreseeably kills in an unjust cause is doing something pro tanto wrong even with immaculate in bello conduct. I'm NOT planning to argue that unjust combatants should be prosecuted or lose POW protections — I want to keep the revisionism at the level of fundamental morality and grant that the laws of armed conflict are a separate, conventional layer — but I suspect that distinction is exactly where they'll come at me.

Draft me a prep sheet for this specific room: the objections these people will actually raise, not the ones I'd get at a philosophy conference, ordered roughly by how much damage they'd do if I flub them. Phrase each one the way a colonel or a staff judge advocate would actually put it — think discipline, good order, reciprocity, mission command, what happens to the POW regime and the Geneva framework if we tell soldiers their license to kill depends on whether their war is just. For each, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Several of the officers come from countries with conscription, so make sure at least one objection presses the conscript/duress case hard, since volunteer-army intuitions won't carry me with them. Also include the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks, and be blunt about whether I should concede up front that the conventional equality of combatants has to win in practice even if I'm right about the underlying morality — or whether conceding that guts my whole talk.
