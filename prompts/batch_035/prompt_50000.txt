Write an essay on whether prioritarianism escapes the leveling-down objection more cleanly than egalitarianism does — and, more importantly, whether it escapes it cleanly enough to be worth adopting. Frame the discussion around a hypothetical case. During a severe drought, a water authority finds itself with two unused policy levers. Lever A trucks a small additional ration to the township's worst-off households, leaving everyone else exactly as they were. Lever B does nothing for the worst-off but cancels a subsidy that currently tops up the gardens of the better-off households, lowering their consumption of water. A strict egalitarian has at least one respect in which she can welcome pulling Lever B; the question is whether the prioritarian has any ground, internal to her own theory, for refusing it — or whether the refusal must be smuggled in from elsewhere (say, from a person-affecting constraint, or a general prohibition on benefiting no one by harming some). Weigh the considerations on both sides, including whether prioritarianism's "benefits to the worse off matter more" formula gives it a real advantage over egalitarianism here or merely a rhetorical one, and do not end on a fence: reach and defend a definite verdict about how much damage, if any, the leveling-down objection does to prioritarianism. Present the essay as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
