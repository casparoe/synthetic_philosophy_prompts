I teach philosophy of language, and I've been asked by the editors of our university's student newspaper to help them replace their current practice on contested terms, which is essentially improvisation: whether a slur gets printed in quotation marks, asterisked out, or paraphrased depends on which editor happens to be on shift. They want a fixed procedure an editor can run in twenty minutes before deadline, and they've asked me to ground it in what the semantics and pragmatics of slurs actually teaches rather than in instinct. The stakes cut both ways: sanitizing every occurrence can falsify reporting on racism, while printing everything can put derogation in the paper under the heading of news.

Please draft that procedure. It should translate, for editors with no philosophical training, the distinctions the literature actually supports: between derogating a group and merely offending readers; between using a term and mentioning it, and why use/mention alone doesn't settle anything, since a quoted slur in a headline still does work on the page; between targeted uses aimed at a person or group and non-targeted ones, including occurrences embedded under negation or in reported speech; and the problem of appropriation, where who is speaking may matter to what the utterance does. Give the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure outputs "publish as written" versus "publish only with a change" versus "escalate," and the points where it must stop and hand the decision to the editorial board because the question is one of the paper's values rather than a fact the procedure can establish.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a news report on a slur found written on a student's door, where the reporters have a photograph and must decide what to print; a column by a student from the targeted group arguing for reclaiming a term and using it throughout; and a review of a classic novel that contains the term, where the reviewer wants to quote the offending passages directly. At least one of these should come out in a way the editors will dislike, and you should say so plainly. Finally, be explicit about the procedure's failure modes — where mechanical application will misfire, and what the procedure cannot do even when followed perfectly.
