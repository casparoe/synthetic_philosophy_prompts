I'm an undergraduate taking a semester-long seminar on Machiavelli, and for my term paper I'd like to read his comedy The Mandrake (La Mandragola) through the fortune/virtù framework we've built up from The Prince and the Discourses, rather than writing yet another paper on chapter 25. Since you won't have the text at hand, here is the plot: Callimaco, a young Florentine who has spent twenty comfortable years in Paris, hears of the beauty of Lucrezia and returns home consumed by desire for her. She is the wife of Messer Nicia, a wealthy lawyer of legendary foolishness who despairs of producing an heir. With the help of Ligurio, a professional go-between, Callimaco disguises himself as a famous physician and convinces Nicia that a potion of mandrake root will make Lucrezia fertile but will kill the first man who sleeps with her afterward; the obvious solution is to catch a vagrant boy to absorb the poison, and of course that boy will be Callimaco in disguise. Nicia raises no objection, Lucrezia's mother none either, and the friar Fra' Timoteo, after some haggling over the fee, absolves Lucrezia in advance. After the night, Lucrezia discovers who the stranger really was, takes to the arrangement with enthusiasm, and the play closes with Nicia happily awaiting a child, Lucrezia keeping a lover, and Callimaco installed in the household as a friend of the family.

The reading I want to test goes something like this: the play looks like a comic dramatization of the doctrine that fortune must be met and mastered by audacity — Callimaco and Ligurio channel the flood of circumstance and win. But whenever I try to draft it, the reading falls apart in interesting ways, and I'd like your help working out what survives. Callimaco is no cold virtuoso of action: he is sick with passion, paralyzed by fear, and passive through most of the plot, and his desire strikes him from outside, like something done to him — which sounds more like fortune's victim than her master. Ligurio, the obvious virtù candidate, is a parasite with no stake in the outcome. The scheme succeeds only because everyone else — husband, mother, friar — turns out to be corruptible, which looks less like mastery of fortune than like an unusually favorable hand dealt by it. And the single decisive change in the play, Lucrezia's overnight conversion from its most scrupulous character to its most contented, happens offstage, through casuistry that Callimaco doesn't even perform himself.

So: does the comedy vindicate the fortune/virtù framework in the private sphere, resist it, or expose something the political works repress — for instance, that virtù is always somebody else's passion organized by a schemer, or that it is precisely fortune's power over events that permits the ending to be a happy one rather than the tragedy these materials would naturally make? And does the fact that Machiavelli wrote this while fortune had knocked him out of politics himself — dismissed from the chancery, writing from his farm — do any real interpretive work, or is that just biographical garnish? Please give me the answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, and make the five points cumulative — building from where the framework genuinely illuminates the play, through where the play strains or breaks it, to what the comedy finally adds to the political theory — rather than five disconnected observations.
