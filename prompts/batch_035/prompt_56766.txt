I've been working my way through episodes of a philosophy of mind podcast, and the most recent one was about Dennett's paper "Quining Qualia." The host summarized the central argument roughly like this: "Dennett takes two coffee tasters at Maxwell House, Chase and Sanborn. Chase no longer likes the coffee he used to love, though he insists it still tastes exactly the same to him; Sanborn's palate has been altered by decades of scalding-hot tasting, so the coffee really does taste different to him now, and he happens to still like it. Dennett's point is that Chase and Sanborn face exactly the same predicament: since there is no way to hold the taste of today's coffee up against the taste of coffee as it was years ago, neither of them can ever really know whether his experience has changed or only his reactions have. And if you can never check, then talk of taste qualia does no explanatory work -- they're posits that can't be verified even by the person having them, so Dennett concludes they simply don't exist. In effect he shows that conscious experience, as we ordinarily conceive it, is an illusion, and that each of us is a little bit like a zombie about the fine-grained character of our own sensations. The paper's title is a nod to his teacher Quine, who in 'Two Dogmas of Empiricism' proved that analytic truth doesn't exist; Dennett is doing to qualia what Quine did to analyticity."

I found this pretty persuasive at first, and I'm broadly sympathetic to deflationary takes on consciousness, but the zombie remark at the end struck me as going further than anything I'd associated with Dennett, and a friend of mine who has actually read the paper says the host "flattened" the coffee example somehow, though she wouldn't elaborate. So I'd like a careful audit. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher sympathetic to Dennett would accept as stated, what is a conflation or oversimplification, and what is simply wrong. In particular: are Chase's and Sanborn's situations really the same predicament, or does the host's merging of them miss the point of using two cases? Does the argument really turn on our inability to verify that our qualia have stayed the same, or is it doing something else -- and if so, what? And does anything in the paper support the conclusion that ordinary conscious experience is illusory or that we are partly zombies? Finally, please say whether the gloss on the title (and on what Quine is supposed to have shown) is fair, or whether it smuggles in another misrepresentation. I'd rather be corrected than comforted, so don't soften the verdict where the host got something right, either.
