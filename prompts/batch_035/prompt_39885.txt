I am working through the section of Thomas Hobbes's De Corpore (1655) in which he takes up the old puzzle of the ship of Theseus, and I have hit a point where I cannot decide what his considered view actually is. To keep things self-contained, here is a close modernised paraphrase of the argument in that section rather than a verbatim quotation.

Hobbes asks us to suppose that the planks of the ship of Theseus are, over a long period, removed one by one and replaced with new planks, until none of the original material remains. He claims the ship remains numerically one and the same ship throughout. But he then asks what we should say if, all the while, someone had collected the discarded planks and, at the end, fitted them together into a ship of the same shape. That second ship would, he says, be a different one; and he appears to ground this partly in the fact that the gradually repaired ship retained its place in the harbour, continued to be put to the same uses, and was counted as the same by those who dealt with it. From this he concludes that the identity of the ship — and likewise of a man, who grows from a tiny embryo into a large body by the continual accession and removal of parts — consists neither in the matter nor in the form alone, but in the parts' being joined together into one coherent whole according to a common design, and in the signification that we have assigned to the name.

What confuses me is that the passage seems to contain two different sorts of criterion running side by side, and I cannot tell which Hobbes means to be doing the real work. On the one hand, identity seems to be settled by continuity of the body: the planks' remaining attached to one another within a common design, each new plank being received into the whole in place of a removed one. On the other hand, the closing appeal to the signification "we have assigned to the name" suggests a conventionalist resolution: if identity is ultimately fixed by how we use a word, then the verdict that the reassembled ship is "a different one" looks like a report about our naming practices rather than a discovery about the two ships, and Hobbes's confident pronouncement on the second ship would need some other support.

Could you help me understand the following?

1. Does Hobbes intend the appeal to naming as a further condition on identity (so that continuity of design is necessary but not sufficient), or as a diagnosis that the whole dispute is in some sense about words? If the latter, why does he still commit himself so firmly on the reassembled ship rather than declaring both answers admissible?
2. How exactly is the "common design" meant to function? For the ship the design seems external to the planks — imposed by a shipwright or by the purpose the ship serves. For the man growing from the embryo, Hobbes transfers the same account, yet a developing organism arguably maintains its own design rather than being shaped according to one. Does the transfer go through as it stands, or does the embryo case quietly require a stronger, non-conventional notion of identity than the ship case?
3. A more general question, since I am trying to situate this reading: does Hobbes's way of combining a continuity criterion with a naming-based proviso offer anything that the two standard modern camps — those who look for an objective persistence condition and those who regard the puzzle as resolvable by stipulation — each miss on their own?

Please answer in British English, and do not feel any need to cite secondary literature; I would rather have your own careful reading of the position as I have paraphrased it.
