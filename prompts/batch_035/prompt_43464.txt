I've agreed to lead a fifty-minute session of a lunchtime reading group for mathematics graduate students — bright, patient with formalism, but with no philosophical training beyond a vague sympathy for Platonism — on Benacerraf's dilemma from his 1973 paper "Mathematical Truth": the tension between a semantics on which mathematical statements are about causally inert abstract objects and an epistemology on which knowledge of things requires some access to them. The group will conflate this with the point of Benacerraf's earlier 1965 paper "What Numbers Could Not Be," in which the Zermelo and von Neumann ordinals show there is no fact of the matter about which sets the natural numbers are; they will also arrive half-believing that provability is truth, that abstract objects are simply like physical objects only located "elsewhere," and that a causal constraint on knowledge is a straw man nobody actually endorses. One of them works on ordinal arithmetic and will insist that identity questions about numbers have trivially determinate answers, which complicates the 1965/1973 disentanglement. I don't want the explanation itself; I want a plan for delivering it. Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the order the pieces must arrive in and which piece depends on which, (2) the single carrying example it should all be built around and what that example must have and lack, (3) the misconceptions in the order the students will actually raise them, and the point in the sequence where each should be absorbed rather than merely rebutted, (4) one check-for-understanding question I can pose in the last five minutes to tell whether the dilemma itself — not any particular response to it — has landed, and (5) what I should openly decline to settle in fifty minutes and how to say so without seeming to dodge.
