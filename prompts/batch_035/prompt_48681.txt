I've been in a little online reading group on the foundations of logic for about a year, and we've gone around the same mountain so many times I can predict everyone's moves. The mountain is Lewis Carroll's 1895 tortoise dialogue: Achilles has A and B and wants the tortoise to accept Z, the tortoise says fine, I accept A and B, but you can't force me to Z unless I also accept the hypothetical that if A and B are true then Z must be true — and when Achilles writes that in as premise C, the tortoise just asks for another hypothetical covering A, B, and C, and so on forever. His line is basically "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?" and every response we read is some variation on a move that was already on the table by the 1970s. One camp, roughly following Russell and then Prior in his runabout inference-ticket paper in Analysis, says the tortoise is cheating by treating a rule of inference as if it were another proposition — the "if... then" sentence isn't a premise you can stack with the others, it's more like a ticket you carry, and Prior pushed this by imagining a connective like Belnap's tonk that lets you infer anything from anything if you're allowed to just stipulate the rules, which he took to show meaning can't come from rules alone. The Dummett camp says sure, you can't justify deduction deductively without circularity, but the circularity isn't vicious — you justify the rules by appeal to what the logical constants mean, and the proof-theoretic harmony results were supposed to make that respectable. Then there's the Wittgensteinian wing (Winch's reading is the one people in my group cite) that says learning to infer is learning to do something, not learning a further fact, so the regress never gets started because the tortoise is only mimicking the language of inference without the practice. And a fourth guy in the group just shrugs and says the formal-systems answer settles it: modus ponens is a rule inside the calculus, asking for a justification outside the calculus is a category mistake — though someone always points out that Dummett's rejoinder to that is the model-theoretic soundness proof for classical logic uses classical reasoning to justify classical reasoning, and Susan Haack's complaint that this is still just circularity, vicious or not, never gets answered to anyone's satisfaction.

So here's my actual question, and please don't give me a survey of the positions, we've read them. I want a diagnosis of the standoff. My working hypothesis is that the camps aren't really disagreeing about logic at all — they're disagreeing about what "justification" has to mean, with one side thinking only a proposition can justify and the other side thinking rules and practices can justify in a different, weaker-but-still-real sense, and neither side ever says outright what would settle it. But maybe it's more interesting than that: maybe the disagreement tracks the split between people who think the meanings of the connectives are fixed before and independently of the inference rules (so the rules have to answer to something) and people who think the rules are constitutive of the meanings (so there's nothing left for them to answer to). Is that the real fault line? Is this verbal, substantive, or two questions — one about justification, one about meaning — run under a single heading? And then tell me honestly what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, some formal result like a better harmony or conservativeness theorem, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and everyone should stop? Or is it just hard?
