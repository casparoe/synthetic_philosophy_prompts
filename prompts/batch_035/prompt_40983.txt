so, some context: I write historical novels, mostly set between the wars, and I spent the better part of two years buried in the late 1930s for my last book, which is about a minor official in the Foreign Office who convinces himself the memorandum he's drafting will prevent a war. Researching that period is where most of my political views about the past actually came from, and an editor friend of mine who read the new draft in progress tells me those views don't hang together. I want an audit before I decide whether she's right or just being contrarian.

Here's the package I've ended up with. (1) The lesson of Munich 1938 is basically sound: appeasing an aggressor signals weakness, invites the next demand, and this really does transfer to other situations — it's the one "lesson of history" I'd defend at a dinner party. (2) The "lesson of Vietnam," by contrast — that intervening against insurgents just produces quagmires — was mostly a bad export, and the policymakers who later reached for analogies to it got things badly wrong, which has made me pretty skeptical of "lessons of history" as a whole category. (3) I nevertheless think the Thucydides-trap framing of the US and China (rising power, anxious ruling power, war becomes likely unless both are unusually careful) is a genuinely useful lens and probably right in its essentials. (4) I also hold that every historical situation is unique enough that no two cases are truly comparable, which is why op-eds doing "this is just like 1938" or "this is just like Suez" drive me up the wall. (5) And finally, I think the people who misused the Vietnam analogy were simply sloppy about it, whereas a careful person who knows both periods deeply can apply an analogy well — history doesn't repeat but it rhymes, and an expert can hear the rhyme.

My editor says (1) and (3) are precisely the kind of reasoning (2) tells me to distrust, that (4) flatly contradicts (3), and that (5) is me wanting it both ways: either analogy-thinking is error-prone as such or it's fine when done well, and I can't have each claim for the cases I happen to like. My instinct is that it's not that simple — (4) feels like it's about lazy public rhetoric rather than careful structural comparison, and I want to say (1) is different in kind from the Vietnam case, though when I try to finish that sentence I can't, which is maybe why I'm writing to you. Which of my five commitments are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, and if something has to give, what's the cheapest fix and what does it cost me? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than just listing them as all equally bad.
