I teach an upper-level course in philosophy of mind and am writing the final. I want one essay question on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness that cannot be answered by reciting the standard one-person-or-two discussion, and I have a specific axis along which I want the difficulty to run.

The question should force students to do two things the textbook summaries skip. First, disambiguate unity: at a minimum, separate unity of phenomenal character (there being one what-it-is-like at a time, co-consciousness) from unity of access (all perceptual information being available to a single reasoning system) and unity of agency (one deliberator whose attitudes hang together over time), and then say which of these the surgical cases actually put pressure on — as opposed to which they merely seem to put pressure on because the labels share a word. Second, examine the inference itself: the standard reasoning runs from behavioral dissociation to experiential dissociation, and it assumes that verbal report is a transparent window on experience. There is a familiar observation about these patients — the speaking hemisphere's tendency to construct coherent stories about behavior it did not control — that puts that assumption under strain in exactly the patients it is being applied to. I want students to notice that this observation cuts both ways: it weakens the two-streams conclusion, but it equally weakens any later appeal by a patient to feeling perfectly unified.

I will give the students this scenario as the anchor: a patient with complete callosal section is briefly shown a spoon to the left visual field and an apple to the right; asked what she saw, she says an apple; asked to reach with the left hand for what she saw, she retrieves the spoon; asked why she picked the spoon, she offers a plausible-sounding reason that is not the operative one. Students should also address at least one phenomenon that complicates the clean laboratory picture — cross-cuing, in which the non-speaking hemisphere steers the other through posture, expression, or the timing of small actions; or the fact that these patients live outside the laboratory as unproblematically single agents; or the reports that some patients, asked directly, deny that anything about their experience is doubled. For whichever phenomenon they choose, they must say what it supports and why: whether it is better explained by two subjects, by one subject with impaired interhemispheric access, or by neither — and, crucially, what would have to be observed to decide between those readings, since a phenomenon that comports equally with all of them supports none of them.

Please produce three things: the question as I would print it, in a form a student could reasonably answer in ninety minutes; a model answer of about 600 words that itself makes the moves the question demands; and a marking scheme out of 20 marks built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth, a one-line note per point on how students typically lose it, and an explicit statement of what separates an A answer from a B answer. My expectation is that the B answer will recite the familiar framing — the patient is one person or two depending on your theory of personal identity — and stop there; name the specific additional moves that lift an answer to an A, with the bridging assumption about report and experience as the pivot I most care about.

A final request, and I mean it: if you think the question as I have framed it is ill-posed — if, for instance, the contrast I insist on between phenomenal unity and access already begs something the debate is genuinely about, or if ninety minutes is simply not enough for what I am demanding — say so plainly in a closing note rather than quietly building a marking scheme around a false contrast. I would rather repair the question than grade answers to a bad one.
