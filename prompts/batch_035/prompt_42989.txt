Here's what I want: read the Pottersville sequence in It's a Wonderful Life through the problem of overdetermination and multi-causal explanation in history. That whole stretch of the movie is basically a counterfactual experiment. Clarence shows George what Bedford Falls would have been like if he'd never existed, and the film runs the tape back through George's life subtracting him from each node: Harry drowns because George isn't there to save him (and so the transport full of troops goes down later), Martini never gets his house, Mary ends up a lonely librarian, and the town itself slides into Pottersville. Structurally it's a historian's but-for test run over and over — for each outcome, would it have happened anyway? — and the movie always answers no. George gets treated as a necessary, non-redundant cause of basically every good thing in town, which is a wildly strong causal claim. Historians get nervous right at this point: most real outcomes are overdetermined, and if you subtract one person from a story the other causes usually close the gap — someone else steps in, another savings and loan opens, decent people are rarely that scarce. So the film seems to be running on the "great man" theory of community history while pretending to be a controlled comparison. I half-remember historians of science doing a similar move, subtracting Darwin to see what survives, but honestly that's hazy so don't lean on it — the question works without it.
