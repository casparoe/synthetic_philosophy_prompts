Please compare the economic theory of information cascades, in the Bayesian tradition of Banerjee and of Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch, with conformist transmission as developed in cultural evolution theory by Boyd and Richerson and later extended by Henrich. Although both mechanisms are invoked to explain populations converging on a single behavior, I would like the comparison organized around three specific points of tension rather than around their superficial similarity. First, informational requirements: the cascade models appear to require that agents observe the choices of predecessors in sequence, so that each choice can be read as evidence about a private signal, whereas conformist transmission can operate on nothing more than the population-level frequency of a trait; clarify what each mechanism needs to observe and whether cascades could even get started when later agents know only how many, but not in what order, earlier agents acted. Second, fragility: cascade models are notable for predicting that an entrenched herd can be overturned by a single well-placed public signal, while conformist bias is usually taken to make majorities self-reinforcing; explain what drives this difference and whether it follows from rational inference versus biased copying as such. Third, distinguishability: suppose an outside observer has only aggregate adoption data over time, with no record of who chose what when — could that observer, in principle, separate rational cascade dynamics from conformist copying, or do the two frameworks become observationally equivalent at that level of description? Conclude with a considered judgment about which framework does more explanatory work in that frequency-only setting, and defend it against the most obvious objection from the rival camp.
