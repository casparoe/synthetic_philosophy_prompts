I work in transport policy, with some economics training but no background in philosophy, and I would like to know whether a pattern I keep running into has an established name. If it does, I want to know exactly what the term covers and where my pattern diverges from it; if it does not, I would like you to coin a term, define it carefully, and mark out its boundary cases. I will not be satisfied with a bare label.

Here is the pattern. Whenever I try to compare two options by their indirect, long-run effects, each consideration I add licences an equal and opposite one. Assessing whether to extend a suburban rail line, the direct effects were modestly positive. But more rail may mean more commuting from further out, hence more sprawl — a point against. Unless, by relieving pressure on the road corridor, it reduces car dependency and eventually densifies rather than spreads the city — a point for. Each chain is individually plausible, and the chains branch again whichever direction I follow them. What strikes me is not merely that the outcome is uncertain, but that the structure of what I know is symmetric: for every argument that option A does more good, there appears to be a counterpart argument of comparable strength that B does, so the estimated difference stays near zero whilst my confidence in any estimate keeps shrinking the more I learn. Inquiry is not fruitless; it just does not narrow the gap — it only fattens the error bars.

The same thing happened when I weighed a job in development finance against a much better-paid consultancy post that would let me donate the difference. Every calculation opened a mirror-image consideration: the donation route matters only if the money is well used, which depends on institutions I cannot see; the development route builds skills whose eventual deployment is unknowable; and so on, indefinitely.

Some nearby terms seem wrong to me, and I would want the answer to explain why or why not. Knightian uncertainty is about not having probabilities at all, whereas here I could produce probabilities; they simply come in matched pairs. Moral uncertainty is about not knowing what is valuable, whereas I am more or less confident about the values — roughly, aggregate welfare — and it is the empirical question of which act does more good that will not settle. Risk, in the technical sense, describes situations in which you cannot say which outcome will occur but can still say which act is better on balance; the whole point of my pattern is that the balance never arrives. A good answer might also tell me whether the pattern is meant to cover every far-reaching decision, or whether there is a principled boundary with cases where deepening inquiry genuinely does resolve the comparison.

Please respond in British English.
