I'm part of a reading group that has spent most of a year working slowly through Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, and our final session is on strange loops and tangled hierarchies. I'd like to close it with a poem. The concept we've circled all year, put in my own words: a strange loop is not just any feedback loop but one that crosses levels — you climb up through a hierarchy and, without noticing the seam, arrive back where you started. Escher's Drawing Hands is the canonical image: two hands, each drawing the other into existence, an apparently self-creating system. But the picture is not actually paradoxical, and that is the part I most want the poem to capture. There is a level Hofstadter calls the inviolate level: Escher's actual hand, and the paper, which nothing inside the picture drew. From inside the picture the loop looks closed; step outside it and you see the two hands are being drawn by something they can never reach. The tangledness is real, but it is a tangledness with an outside.

I'd like a Petrarchan sonnet that maps this onto its form. The octave should do the tangled work: envelope rhymes (abbaabba), so that each rhyme pair encloses the other the way each hand draws the other, and heavy enjambment, so the lines keep reaching across boundaries the way the hands do. The volta should be the moment of stepping out — the sestet turns from the hands to the paper, the one thing in the picture that no hand in the picture could have drawn. Structurally, the sestet's rhymes should not interlock with the octave's at all; the clean break is the level boundary made audible. And the final line should be the only fully end-stopped, plain line in the poem — the inviolate level rendered as the one line that doesn't reach outside itself. Someone in my group who knows Hofstadter should be able to identify the volta as the step outside the tangled hierarchy rather than as ordinary poetic reversal, while someone who has never read a word of the book should still come away with a good poem about the picture.

Please take on the role of a poet who has genuinely worked through these ideas rather than skimmed them — think through the structural mapping before writing, and make sure the form is doing the philosophical work rather than decorating it. American English, please. Finally, append a short prose note (a paragraph or so, not a lengthy essay) on where the poem takes liberties: does it lean on a contested reading — for instance, on whether the inviolate level survives when the strange loop in question is the self, where "who draws Escher's hand?" seems to recur rather than terminate — and is treating the rhyme scheme's break as the level-crossing a genuine structural correspondence or a stretch? Say which single interpretive decision the poem most depends on, such that if that decision is wrong the whole structure falls apart — that is, where your rendering of the idea is most likely to have gone wrong.
