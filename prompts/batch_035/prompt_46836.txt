I hold a joint appointment in philosophy and a PPE program, and I came to philosophy from economics: game theory is still the language I think in — strategies and beliefs, equilibrium selection, commitment devices, screening, reputational equilibria, money pumps. Each year in my rational choice seminar I teach the sunk cost "fallacy" alongside the experimental escalation literature and the usual jokes about the Concorde, and each year the philosophers in the room push back. Some say honoring sunk costs can be an expression of integrity or narrative identity rather than a computational error; some say escalation is better explained by self-deception and face-saving than by any fault in the agent's arithmetic; and a few suspect the norm of ignoring sunk costs is itself a piece of ideology for a culture that treats quitting as costless. I have never been satisfied with how that exchange goes, and I suspect it is because the two sides never state their claims in a common vocabulary.

Translate the philosophical debate into mine, as faithfully as you can. First, restate the orthodox claim — that honoring sunk costs is irrational — in decision-theoretic terms, making clear whether the irrationality is a dominance violation, a backward-induction failure, money-pump susceptibility, or something else. Then restate each of the main defenses in the same terms: the commitment and reputation defense (staying the course deters future exploitation or abandonment by partners), the epistemic defense (my past investment is evidence about my own earlier judgment and about the project's value, so updating on it is not a fallacy), and the identity-expressive defense (honoring sunk costs says something about who I am that no payoff matrix captures). Then give me the residue report: which defenses translate cleanly into things I recognize — reputational equilibria, signaling, Bayesian updating — which translate only with distortion, and whether anything in the philosophical debate has no game-theoretic counterpart at all. Where there is no counterpart, tell me whether that reflects a limitation of my vocabulary or an unclarity in the philosophy; I want that judgment argued for, not merely asserted.

Let me tell you my working hypothesis, so you can confirm or refute it: that once the defenses are stated properly, most of them collapse into versions of "the costs weren't really sunk" — the reputational stake, the informational value, and the option value of finishing were all live variables all along — so that the philosophers are either making a correct but unexciting decision-theoretic point in disguise, or they are defending something my vocabulary genuinely cannot see. If it is the latter, I want to know what it is, stated precisely enough that I could tell whether it shows up in anyone's payoff matrix or in nobody's.
