I sit, as the staff-elected member, on a small panel at my employer — a company of roughly six hundred people — that reviews contested uses of employee data. The panel has real authority: any use of employee data that someone formally challenges comes to us, and our chair, a long-serving compliance officer, has issued rulings that now function as our precedents. I joined after the panel decided a case involving my own calendar metadata, which is how this subject became personal to me. A new case is now on our docket, and it falls to me to write the recommendation.

The chair's rulings to date, in brief:

1. Wellness program (voluntary enrollment; wearable step data). Using individual-level data to refer employees automatically to wellness coaching, without a separate opt-in for the outreach: impermissible.
2. The same step data, pooled and de-identified, used to set the company's overall insurance contribution: permissible, with no fresh consent required.
3. Badge-access records used by the security team to establish who was in the building during an equipment theft: permissible.
4. The same badge records used by a department head to see which of his reports, on average, arrived latest: impermissible.
5. A research group's use of de-identified calendar metadata to study meeting overload, publishing only team-level averages, where the group retained the technical ability to re-identify: permissible with conditions (restricted access, deletion on completion).

The new case: the people-operations group proposes to use individually identifiable after-hours VPN login times — originally collected for security auditing — to generate a monthly boundary report for each employee, showing that employee their own after-hours activity. The report would be visible only to the employee, with no manager access and no secondary use, and enrollment would be opt-out: everyone receives it unless they say no.

I need to write a recommendation consistent with our own precedents. Please help me by (a) identifying the candidate rules under which rulings 1 through 5 hang together — I can make out at least a consent-based rule, a harm-based rule, and something like the contextual-integrity approach a friend of mine who studies this keeps invoking — and noting where each rule fits the record only with strain; (b) stating what each rule predicts about the boundary-report case; (c) giving your best recommendation, with the closest analog among the five precedents identified; (d) stating candidly what principle we would be adopting with each available choice, if the precedents genuinely underdetermine it; and (e) proposing one further case, specifiable in a sentence or two, that would best separate the candidate rules.

Two requests about intellectual honesty. First, where the candidate rules rest on assumptions that are genuinely contested among people who know this field — for instance, whether de-identification suffices, whether opt-out enrollment counts as consent, or whether an identifiable flow that serves only the data subject's own interests is intrusive at all — please mark those as contested rather than settled, and do not present any single framework as the consensus of privacy scholars; I want to know where knowledgeable people actually disagree. Second, if you believe the precedents underdetermine the case, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a resolution.

Please write in American English. End with a final line in the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of: approve, approve with conditions, reject.
