I'm a master's student in philosophy with about a year left of my program. My background is strong on probability and logic, pretty weak on the history-of-philosophy canon, and my committee is mostly general epistemologists. I want to write my thesis on the epistemic regress problem, but I'm stuck at the agenda-setting stage: I can't figure out which of the open questions in that area is actually worth my year, and which ones just look important.

You know the basic setup: any attempt to justify a belief either loops back on itself, goes on forever, or stops somewhere that itself isn't justified. Foundationalists say stop, coherentists say loop, and the infinitists (tiny minority) say going on forever is fine actually. Fine. But once you get past the trilemma, the live questions seem to fan out into a bunch of half-connected debates, and I dont know which of them is upstream of the others. As I see it the candidates are roughly:

1. Whether infinitism is even possible for finite creatures -- or whether that whole objection just conflates "having infinitely many beliefs" with "being disposed to keep extending justification if challenged," which are obviously different.
2. What the arbitrariness objection to foundationalism is really claiming. Is the complaint that the stopping point is unjustified, or unexplained, or merely revisable? These come apart under pressure and I can't tell which version is doing the actual work.
3. Whether formal methods can settle anything here. I came across an idea somewhere (cant remember where) that you can model a coherentist's justification structure as a kind of random walk over claims: each inferential step has some small chance of error, but the process's long-run behavior can still concentrate around true claims even though no single step is certain. If that kind of argument works, does it answer the regress or does it just restate the regress in math? I honestly can't tell, and I'm exactly the kind of person who'd be seduced by a formal result that doesn't do what it looks like it does.
4. Whether the regress is specifically a problem about belief transmission, or whether it generalizes to other epistemic phenomena -- assertion, inference, understanding -- in which case the classic trilemma is just one instance of something bigger, and maybe people have been solving the instance instead of the problem.

Given one year, a math background, and no real appetite for textual exegesis, which of these would you prioritize and in what order? I care less about the final list than about the reasoning behind it: tell me which questions the other ones depend on, which are tractable with formal tools versus which secretly need history-of-philosophy work I'd be bad at, and what considerations would move something up or down the ranking. And please tell me if my framing itself is off somewhere -- e.g., that two of my "four questions" are really the same question wearing different hats, or that I'm missing something upstream of all of them. I'd rather find that out now than in month nine.
