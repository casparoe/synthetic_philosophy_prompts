For about twelve years I've been a regular on the rationalist blogs and forums — LessWrong, the Slate Star Codex diaspora, the alignment-adjacent corners of the internet — and I help run a monthly meetup where one of us leads a ninety-minute session on a single idea. Next month it's my turn, and I want to do higher-order theories of consciousness: roughly the family of views, including Rosenthal's higher-order thought version and Lycan's inner-sense version, on which a mental state is conscious in virtue of being the object of some further mental state directed at it. My audience is sharp but idiosyncratic. They've absorbed the essays on dissolving the question, they've argued p-zombies and Mary's room to death and now distrust those thought experiments on sight, and several will arrive already convinced that consciousness is just attention or self-modeling, so they'll be tempted to hear the whole theory as a familiar friend rather than a distinct claim.

I'm not asking you to explain the theories to me; I want a plan for the explanation. Specifically: the order in which the pieces must come, and what depends on what — does the audience need the transitivity intuition (a state's being conscious involves its being represented) before they can see why anyone would posit a second-order state at all, or the other way around? What one concrete, everyday case should carry the whole session, and what properties it must have and lack — noting that anything too philosophy-textbook-ish will get pattern-matched as those standard thought experiments they already distrust, and anything too close to attention risks collapsing the theory into their existing prior. Which misconceptions they will predictably form, and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than left to fester until Q&A — my own guesses are that at least a few will hear "higher-order" as recursion and ask about the infinite regress of awareness of awareness of awareness, and that others will quietly equate the second-order state with attention and then announce the theory is trivial, but you should tell me whether those are the right ones to plan around. A closing question whose answer would show me they grasped the actual distinction rather than nodding along politely. And what I should openly decline to cover at this level and say so out loud — I assume the hard problem is on that list, but I'd like your judgment on what else.

A final request of a different kind: if you think the exercise as framed is misconceived — that this audience's priors make the session futile, that ninety minutes is the wrong length for this material, or that I've misidentified which misconceptions matter and the plan should be built around different ones — say so plainly at the top rather than politely constructing a plan around a broken premise. I'd rather hear the objection now than discover it mid-session in front of twenty people.
