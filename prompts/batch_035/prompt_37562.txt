In my school's philosophy elective we spent two weeks on utilitarianism, and I've come out the other side committed to the aggregating side of the torture versus dust specks case: given the choice between one person being tortured for fifty years and some incomprehensibly vast number of people each getting a dust speck in the eye, I say pick the torture, because enough negligible harms can add up to outweigh one grave harm, and I distrust any moral theory whose arithmetic goes vague as soon as the numbers get big. Tell me what would count against this position — not just the immediate revulsion everyone feels at the conclusion, but the deeper defeaters: the intermediate-chain argument, where you get from specks to torture through a long series of steps each only slightly worse than the last and the aggregator has to say exactly where the trade flips; the possibility that harms are only coarsely comparable across different people, or that the badness of an experience simply isn't the kind of quantity you can sum; the thought that a world with more total suffering but none of it concentrated might be better, not worse, than a world with one torture. Which of these would a committed aggregator concede as genuine trouble, and which would they brush off as intuition misfiring at scales it wasn't built for? And has the position been qualified into something unfalsifiable — and if so, is that even a fair complaint about a moral view, or do moral views answer to different standards than empirical ones? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether you think my position is actually exposed here.
