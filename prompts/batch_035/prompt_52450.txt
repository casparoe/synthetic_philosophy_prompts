I help run a film-and-philosophy evening class at our community centre (mostly retirees and a few students, no prior philosophy), and this term we're doing scepticism — Descartes' demon, Putnam's brain in a vat, that sort of thing. Someone suggested The Truman Show and it went down brilliantly, but afterwards my co-teacher and I got stuck on what the film is actually about, and I want to bring it back next session with something more solid than my half-formed thoughts. Her line is that Truman basically IS a brain in a vat: he's massively deceived about his world by an all-powerful deceiver, Christof stands in for the demon, and the ending where he sails into the painted sky is just Descartes emerging from doubt. My worry is that this is too quick, but I can't quite tell whether it's too quick in a boring way or an interesting way. Truman isn't wrong about most things, is he? His house physically exists, the people have real bodies, he really does drink coffee — the deception is local (his marriage is fake, his father's death was staged) plus one big global lie (he's on telly). The brain in a vat, as I understand it, is supposed to be wrong about nearly everything down to having hands. And if I've understood Putnam at all, the whole force of the vat scenario comes from it being undetectable in principle, whereas Truman's is not only detectable but detected — a stage light falls on him, a raincloud follows only him around, he catches the radio scanning his conversations. That looks less like radical scepticism and more like a paranoid thriller about a conspiracy that happens to be true. But maybe the film knows this, and the interesting reading is about where it deliberately breaks from the vat?

There's a second layer I'd love you to address. One of the students had read ahead and brought up Nozick's experience machine, asking whether Truman's situation is really just that — a life engineered to feel good while cut off from reality — and honestly none of us could work out whether that fits either, since Christof's Seahaven is physically real, just staged and instrumentally arranged around one man. So you've got three structures — the vat, the experience machine, and Christof's set — and they all seem to differ in what's fake and in whether the deception could in principle be uncovered. If you could map the film onto those distinctions for me, showing which scenes illuminate which structure (the falling light, the raincloud, the wedding-photo finger-crossing, the final confrontation with Christof where he argues there's no more truth out in the real world than there is in the one he built for Truman), that would be exactly what the class needs.

The last thing, and maybe the heart of it: by the end Truman gets what no brain in a vat ever gets — an actual door in the sky, a way to step out of the deception and verify everything. Does that make the film unfaithful to the sceptical problem, which is supposed to have no exit, or is the exit precisely the point? It seemed to me watching it that the horror of Truman's life isn't epistemic at all — his beliefs are mostly true — but something about being the object of a scheme, watched and used for other people's purposes since birth. Christof's closing speech only lands as chilling because of that, not because Truman has been wrong about the weather. So maybe tell me what the film adds to the scepticism debate that the thought experiments can't do on their own, and where you'd push back on my co-teacher's reading — she'll be in the room, so please be fair to her strongest points rather than the lazy version of them. Also if there are scenes I've misremembered or that you'd point the class to instead, say so.
