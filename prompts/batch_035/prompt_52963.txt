My department's reading group has been stuck on self-deception for two semesters now, and I've started to suspect its not the arguments that are the problem, it's the question. So you know the terrain: on one side the people who take the paradox seriously — to deceive yourself you'd have to be both the deceiver (who knows the truth) and the deceived (who doesn't), and the Davidson-style crowd says this makes the state conceptually impossible or at least deeply mysterious, so if self-deception happens at all it needs something exotic, partitioned selves or unconscious intentions. On the other side the deflationists, Mele and his followers, who say just describe the actual psychology — motivated reasoning, asymetric scrutiny of evidence you like versus evidence you don't — and you get everything worth calling self-deception without anyone intending anything and without anyone anywhere 'knowing' the truth and hiding it from themselves. The frustrating part is that both sides agree on way more of the empirical picture than their rhetoric suggests, and yet nobody has moved an inch. My hypothesis is that it's basically verbal: 'deception' is borrowed from the interpersonal case, which has an agent who knows and a victim who doesn't, and the two camps are fighting over which half of that structure the word gets to keep — the intentionalists treat the label as fixed, the deflationists treat the phenomena as fixed. Is that right? Or is there a genuine substantive disagreement hiding in there — maybe about whether belief comes in degrees, or about whether 'the self' in self-deception is one system or several? Give me a diagnosis based on what each side actually claims, and then tell me honestly whether anything could end this — a stipulative definition, some new psych data, a conceptual distinction nobody's made yet — or whether the right move is to just pick a terminology and go home.
