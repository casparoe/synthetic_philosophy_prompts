I am a professor of philosophy working at the intersection of ethics and decision theory, and I am preparing a graduate seminar unit on infinite ethics. What I want from you is not another survey of positions but the specification that should come first: the list of requirements any adequate theory of infinite ethics must meet, so that my students can evaluate existing proposals against it rather than argue past each other.

First, some role-setting: you are a professional philosopher specializing in axiology and the mathematics of aggregation, with a working command of the population-ethics tradition from Parfit onward, the infinite-ethics literature in the style of Bostrom, and work on infinite lotteries by Vallentyne and Kagan. Do not begin drafting immediately. Start by brainstorming: sketch the candidate requirements, stress-test each against the cases below, discard those that cannot survive, and only then commit to a list. Think carefully about which requirements are genuine demands on a theory of this kind and which are merely prejudices of one camp (for instance, whether it is a real requirement that the theory deliver a total ordering, or whether admitting incomparability is acceptable).

The context fixing "adequate for what": the seminar is attached to a project evaluating policy options under cosmological models on which the future may contain infinitely many welfare subjects — spatially infinite universes, futures without end, and the like. So the theory must do two jobs: (i) rank or compare outcomes that may contain infinitely many lives, and (ii) agree with our finite axiology on any comparison in which finitely many lives are at stake. A theory that is silent on the first job, or that contradicts settled finite judgments in the second, fails.

Each requirement should come with the case that motivates it. Among the cases I want the list to handle are the following; include them or better ones:

1. Parfit's Egypt case adapted to infinity: adding extra happy lives to a world that already contains infinitely many equally happy lives — must the ranking change, and in which direction?
2. What I call the Corridor: two futures, each containing a countably infinite population living excellent lives, except that one contains a single additional person at a slightly lower welfare level. Every additive sum is undefined, so the theory must either rank these, declare them incomparable, or explain why the question is ill-posed.
3. What I call the Stutter: a construction in which plausible dominance reasoning and cancellation reasoning over matched infinite subpopulations, applied in the usual way, licenses the conclusion that a single world is strictly better than itself. The requirements must jointly block this, or the theory must explain precisely which step of the reasoning fails — and do so without ad hoc restrictions invented only for this case.

Two distinctions the theory must not collapse: equal goodness versus incomparability (a tie and a failure to compare are different verdicts, and conflating them has been a persistent defect of aggregationist approaches), and cardinality versus intensity (that there are more lives, or higher welfare, is one thing; whether the theory may compare outcomes containing different infinite cardinalities of lives — say, countably many versus more — is another, and if it refuses such comparisons, it owes us an account of why this does not paralyze every ranking we care about).

The part I most want to see: flag any subset of your requirements that is jointly unsatisfiable. I suspect that something like the following cannot all hold together — agreement with finite axiology on finite cases, a verdict on every pair of infinite outcomes of the same cardinality, and invariance under how the same population is redescribed or partitioned into individual lives — but you should work this out rather than take my word for it. If some subset is unsatisfiable, say which requirement you would drop and why, since that choice is where the real theory-building happens. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or a camp preference, and tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude popular candidates.

Finally, and briefly — one line each, since the criteria are where the thinking is — check three families against the list: aggregation using nonstandard or hyperreal-style values for infinite totals; discounting approaches of the discounted-actual-value type; and lexicographic or Pareto-style rankings that compare infinite outcomes via representative subpopulations. A table for the requirements is fine, provided each row still carries the motivating case.
