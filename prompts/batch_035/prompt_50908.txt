And here is the student's answer, copied verbatim from the bluebook:

"Futarchy is Robin Hanson's proposal for a new form of government. The idea is that we keep democracy for saying what we want, but we let betting markets tell us how to get it. Elected representatives would formally define and manage a measurement of national welfare, and this is the 'vote on values' part — they decide what counts as welfare, they don't pick policies. Then for any proposed policy, betting markets are run on two conditionals: national welfare conditional on adopting the policy, and national welfare conditional on not adopting it. These can be implemented as called-off bets. The rule is that when the market clearly estimates that a proposed policy would increase expected national welfare, that proposal becomes law. So if the price in the adoption-conditional market is higher than in the non-adoption market, the policy is adopted.

The motivation is that familiar governments frequently fail by ignoring the advice of relevant experts. Ordinary voters don't have money on the line, so they have no incentive to inform themselves, and legislators have all the usual incentive problems. In a speculative market you have to put your money where your mouth is. People who know they aren't relevant experts shut up, and people who don't know this eventually lose their money and then shut up. This is Hayek's point about prices aggregating dispersed knowledge, applied to policy. Another advantage: the market price is basically the median trader's belief about the policy's effect, so it works like a vote where each trader's vote is weighted by how much they're willing to stake, which is more democratic in a sense because it costs the same for a poor trader as a rich one to move the price.

The strongest objection is that opponents with a lot of money could try to manipulate the prices. But since speculative markets are efficient, it's impossible to manipulate prices for long — any manipulation attempt just costs the manipulator money, and other traders profit by correcting the bias. So this objection doesn't really apply to futarchy. A second objection is that the national welfare measure is gameable: whatever variable the representatives define, policies will optimize for the measure rather than the real thing. This one is genuinely serious and futarchy doesn't have a clean answer to it yet."
