I am a novelist, three drafts into a book that follows two families in a single northern mill town from the 1870s to the closure of the last mill in the 1980s. The novel is built as a chain of singular events — a strike, a marriage, a bankruptcy, a run of bad harvests — and I have been content, until now, to let each event be explained by the ones before it: this happened because that happened, person by person. A historian friend who read the draft raised a question I cannot shake. She told me that her discipline spent decades arguing over whether explanation in history works by subsuming an event under a general law, on the covering-law model associated with Carl Hempel, or by tracing the singular narrative that led up to it, the line taken by William Dray and others. Her verdict was that my novel, whatever I intend by it, is taking a position in that argument, and that I should at least know which one.

I write fiction, not historiography, and I have no obligation to referee the dispute. But I suspect it has implications for craft decisions I am currently making half-blindly. For instance: whether my panoramic chapters, in which the town's fate is shown as one instance of a pattern that swept hundreds of towns like it, deepen the novel or quietly withdraw agency from my characters, turning people into specimens; whether foreshadowing, as a device, functions as a covert covering law, promising the reader that the ending was derivable in advance from laws of decline; how much explanatory weight I am entitled to rest on the reader's sense that the events, taken together, form a story rather than a case file; and whether a novel that refuses all pattern owes its readers some other form of intelligibility, or merely owes them an apology.

Please describe the most important implications of the covering-law-versus-narrative-explanation debate for my situation as a novelist, not for historians. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
