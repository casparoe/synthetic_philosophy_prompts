I am a high-school senior who has spent much of the past year reading philosophy outside of class, and our school's philosophy club has spent two terms stuck on a debate I no longer think is making progress. The topic is David Lewis's modal realism, from his 1986 book On the Plurality of Worlds. To make the disagreement concrete: the Lewisian side holds that talk of possible worlds is literally true — there really are other worlds, each exactly as real as ours, differing from ours only in that nothing in them is spatiotemporally connected to anything here. The other side, whose members call themselves actualists, agrees that we need possible worlds as a tool for analyzing counterfactuals, modality, and content, but insists that the worlds should be treated as abstract representations — ersatz worlds built out of, say, sets of sentences or maximal consistent properties — rather than concrete parallel universes. Lewis himself admitted that the price of his view is being met with an incredulous stare from colleagues, and that is roughly where we remain.

The two sides in my club have exhausted their arguments, and the arguments have not changed anyone's mind. The realists point out that ersatz worlds need to be built out of something, and that the actualist's abstraction only works because it quietly presupposes modal facts about which combinations are possible — so the actualist helps himself to what he was supposed to be explaining. The actualists reply that parsimony is on their side: they posit vastly less of what we actually care about, since Lewis's concrete duplicates of every possible person are an ontological extravagance, and they add that Lewis's own principle of charity toward other worlds makes his view objectionably indifferent — every way things could have gone is equally real, so nothing is genuinely lost when things go badly here. The realists answer that parsimony should count kinds of things, not numbers of things, and that a single uniform ontology of concrete worlds is simpler than a zoo of ersatz constructions; the actualists answer back that this is an ad hoc reinterpretation of a standard everyone else uses. And there we sit.

My working hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is that this is not really a disagreement about what exists but a disagreement about what a theory is for: the realists are scoring Lewis's view as a scientific theory, where unobservables are fine if they earn their keep, while the actualists are scoring it as a metaphysical theory, where the baseline of what there is should not be multiplied beyond what our own world already contains. But I am not sure the split runs that cleanly, and I am not sure it explains the emotional heat — people in my club are genuinely frustrated, not just talking past each other. So I am asking for a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this dispute substantive or largely verbal — in particular, does the word 'concrete' do any real work in it, or could the two sides agree on every sentence about what there is while still fighting about whether to call the other worlds 'real'? Are the two sides answering different questions under one name? Is a hidden formal question doing the work — for instance, whether parsimony is a measure of entities or of entity-kinds — and if so, could a precise definition of ontological parsimony settle it, or would each side simply refuse to honor the other's definition? Or is the impasse a sign that the underlying question is ill-posed? Please support the diagnosis with what each side actually says, as I have described it, and then tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction, a reformulated question, a formal result about parsimony, or an argument that no progress should be expected here at all. I am also interested in one comparison you may be able to draw: whether this dispute has the same structure as the debate over unrestricted mereological composition, where one side says there is a table-plus-my-chair and the other side refuses to count it, and whether that analogy, if it holds, favors any particular diagnosis.
