Medical law in most Western countries requires informed consent before treatment, but the practice has a curiously formal shape. A surgeon who fails to disclose even a small risk of a complication has violated the requirement even if the patient would have consented anyway, while consent remains valid even when the patient demonstrably understands almost nothing about the procedure — so long as the disclosures were made and the form was signed. Consent procured by an outright lie ("this procedure has no risks") is void, but consent procured through selective emphasis, confident framing, and the quiet authority of a white coat is perfectly valid. And the same system lets a patient refuse life-saving treatment for any reason at all, however irrational or self-destructive, while forbidding her to sell a kidney or to buy an unapproved drug that she has researched thoroughly. What theory of consent, or of autonomy, does this pattern presuppose? Is it a view on which consent is a transaction whose validity turns on what was offered to the patient rather than on what the patient actually came to understand? And does the asymmetry between what patients may refuse and what they may agree to presuppose that autonomy is a purely negative liberty — a right to say no that carries no corresponding right to say yes on one's own terms — or is that asymmetry grounded in something other than autonomy altogether, such as a distinction between authorizing harm and incurring risk? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say which parts of the practice strictly require it and which merely sit comfortably with it, note whether the various components presuppose consistent things, and tell me what the practice would have to look like if we instead grounded consent in actual understanding rather than adequate disclosure.
