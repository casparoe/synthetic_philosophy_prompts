You are a philosopher specializing in rationality and collective decision-making, writing for an educated layperson. Please think the problem through carefully before answering — but keep your reply to at most 350 words, however long your deliberation.

Some background. I am a retired physical chemist who has lately taken up philosophy in the evenings, and I find myself in a standing disagreement with my granddaughter, a first-year graduate student in economics. She maintains that my voting is irrational, and argues as follows: the chance that my single ballot decides a national election is on the order of one in ten million; when that improbability is weighed against the benefit I would thereby confer on my fellow citizens, the expected payoff of the hour spent at the polls comes out smaller than the value of an hour spent doing almost anything else, including napping. She dresses this up in the same arithmetic I once used to weigh measurements, which I confess flatters me.

Yet I resist, and I want to know whether my resistance is respectable or merely sentimental. In the laboratory, when a calculated difference between two conditions fell below the noise floor of our instruments, we did not say the difference was small but positive; we said no difference had been demonstrated at all. It seems to me my granddaughter is doing something analogous in reverse: she multiplies a probability she cannot possibly know to that precision — turnout shifts with the weather, and the underlying process is surely chaotic — by a benefit that is itself a rough guess, and presents the product as a reason for inaction. A number manufactured from two one-significant-figure inputs strikes me as an artifact of the arithmetic rather than a fact about the world. But I worry I am committing a category error, confusing humility about an estimate with a ground for not acting.

My questions, then: (1) Is the noise-floor objection sound, or does it misapply a point about measurement to a point about choice? (2) If it fails, what is the strongest principled reply to her — one that does not simply change the subject to civic duty, and does not pretend the arithmetic comes out favorable when it does not?
