A friend and I spent most of last weekend arguing about whether the counterfactual and interventionist approaches to causation are genuine rivals, and we eventually ran out of stamina before resolving anything. My own position, roughly the one David Lewis developed, is that causation is to be analyzed in terms of counterfactual dependence: had the cause not occurred, the effect would not have. My friend held that this gets things backwards, and that we only understand which counterfactuals matter by appeal to interventions, in the tradition of Judea Pearl and James Woodward: a variable is a cause of another when wiggling the first, while holding other things fixed in the right way, changes the second. Two of our sticking points deserve special attention. First, I pressed the circularity objection — that interventions are themselves causal processes, so defining causation via interventions is unbearably circular — and she replied with the idea of idealized, hypothetical interventions performed by a "surgical" agent. Second, she pressed me on late preemption and causation by omission, where the standard counterfactual test seems to deliver the wrong verdict, and I did not have a crisp reply. We also circled, without settling, the question of whether structural equations are merely a compact way of encoding a similarity ordering among possible worlds, in which case the two theories might be notational variants rather than competitors.

Write a dialogue between a defender of the counterfactual analysis and a defender of the interventionist analysis, continuing our argument where we left off. Both characters should be equally competent: neither should be a strawman, and each should respond directly to the other's actual points, conceding where a concession is warranted and pressing where the literature presses. The dialogue should engage the circularity objection and the surgical-intervention reply, the treatment of late preemption and omission, the question of whether the two frameworks are rivals or variant formalisms, and — since my friend and I never got to it — how each side accounts for the direction of causation and the asymmetry between causes and background conditions. Keep the exchanges substantive rather than anecdotal; the characters may use worked examples, but the argument should carry the dialogue. Please write the dialogue in American English, and conclude it with the two characters identifying precisely what further assumption or case would decide the matter between them, rather than with an artificial reconciliation.
