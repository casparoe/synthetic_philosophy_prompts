So next month I'm giving a talk at a workshop on ethics and war, defending Anscombe's line on Truman. Quick setup in case you don't know it: when Oxford gave Truman an honorary degree in 1956, Anscombe was one of the dons who protested, and she wrote a pamphlet about it arguing that praising a bad action makes you share in its guilt. Her actual argument was that choosing to kill the innocent as a means to your end is murder, full stop -- not a bad tally in a calculation, murder. And she thought Hiroshima and Nagasaki were exactly that: not foreseen side effects of hitting a military target, but the point of the act. On her use of "innocent" she just means people who aren't fighting, so the standard comeback "the Japanese civilians weren't morally innocent" misses.

The audience is the tricky part. It's a just war crowd: several Walzerians who think the bombing of cities can sometimes be justified by necessity, one guy who has published on double effect for two decades and knows every move in that literature, a couple of straight utilitarians who think the invasion alternative makes the arithmetic obvious, and a historian who will show up with casualty estimates for a land invasion of Japan. Nobody there thinks killing civilians is fine as such -- the room agrees on discrimination as a principle -- which is exactly why the objections will come from inside my own framework and not from outside it.

Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room (not the ones a freshman would raise), ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way that specific person would phrase it. For each give me a two minute reply and be honest about where the reply is soft -- I'd rather know now. Also tell me what I should concede up front. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. I have a feeling I know which one it is (hint: she wasn't exactly keen on Britain fighting that war in the first place, and someone will have looked that up), but tell me if there's a worse one I haven't thought of.
