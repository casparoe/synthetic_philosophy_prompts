so heres something I've been going back and forth on with people in forum threads for a while. you know the standard paradox of voting thing — the chance of a single vote being pivotal in a national election is absurdly small (estimates vary by an order of magnitude depending on the state and the margin, and there's this whole side argument that real electorates produce exact ties way more often than naive models predict), so even after multiplying by billions of dollars of stakes you get this expected value that people fight about endlessly. fine. but there's another framing that gets way less airtime, and it's the decision theory angle. if the people similar to you — same feeds, same temperament, roughly the same reasoning process — are running basically the same computation you are, then your choice to vote looks like evidence about their choices too, in the way your choice to one-box is evidence about what's in the box, or the way your choice in the twin prisoner's dilemma is evidence about your twin. under evidential decision theory that can make voting rational even when the causal expected value looks terrible, becuase conditioning on "I vote" also conditions on "a bunch of people reasoning like me turned out, mostly for my side."

anyway, heres what I actually want from you: construct a thought experiment where a causal decision theorist abstains and an evidential decision theorist votes — subject to real constraints. (1) realistic setting, an ordinary election in an ordinary country, no sci-fi; (2) the correlation cannot come from a superintelligent predictor or anything exotic — it has to be ordinary human similarity, people persuaded by the same local paper, a sibling who can predict you about nine times out of ten, that level of shrewdness at most; (3) no inflating the stakes to make it easy — if the wrong candidate wins, boring normal bad things happen, not the apocalypse; and (4) the voter is a roughly ordinary person, mildly altruistic, cares about their town, not a utility monster summing all of humanity's welfare into the payoff (that move does too much work and I want it off the table).

then after laying out the scenario, walk through what each theory actually says and why the verdicts split, tell me which feature of the setup is doing the work, and deal with the obvious causal-decision-theorist rebuttal — I can already hear "my vote doesn't cause theirs" — rather than ignoring it. finally: if I strip the correlation out of the case, does it collapse back into a boring pivot-probability expected value argument, or is something left over? the point of the whole exercise, btw, is to figure out whether the paradox of voting is really a fact about tiny probabilities or a disguised disagreement about decision theory — so build the case so it actually answers that, and please derive the verdicts from the theories instead of just stipulating them, otherwise it proves nothing.
