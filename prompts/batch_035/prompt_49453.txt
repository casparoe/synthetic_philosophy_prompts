Deciding how much of my income to divert from present enjoyment into retirement savings has recently made a question I regarded as academic feel practical. My financial adviser maintains that only empirical considerations can justify weighting my well-being at seventy-five less than my well-being at thirty-five: the probability I won't be around to enjoy it, and the opportunity cost captured by market returns. I'm tempted to add a further, purely philosophical discount — some weight reduction warranted simply by the fact that a benefit lies decades in my future, perhaps because psychological connectedness to that distant self will be thinner. But I can't tell whether this is a genuine rational consideration or dressed-up self-indulgence.

Please give me your credence that pure time preference within a single life is rationally permissible — setting aside mortality risk and returns — decomposed into the sub-questions it turns on. Candidates I can see, though add others if you think they belong: (a) whether rationality requires temporal neutrality of concern within one life; (b) whether weaker psychological connectedness across time could justify differential weighting even if neutrality is the default; (c) whether temporal distance is relevantly unlike spatial distance — whether caring less about my future self because it is further away is on all fours with caring less about a person because they live further away; (d) whether the answer differs for experiences the future self will merely have versus events the present self now anticipates, such as dread or savoured expectation. Assign each term a probability with a one-line justification, combine them into an overall figure, then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to and what consideration would move it most.
