I'm a philosophy graduate student presenting at an interdisciplinary workshop on invertebrate welfare. Most of the audience are applied ethologists and laboratory-animal veterinarians, with a few philosophers of mind mixed in. The ethologists come from a tradition that treats attributions of subjective states to animals as suspect unless pinned to measurable behavior, and they reach instinctively for Morgan's canon — the injunction to prefer explanations of an animal's behavior in terms of lower psychological faculties whenever those will do the work. My thesis is that moral consideration should be distributed across taxa not by a sentience threshold that a species either crosses or fails to cross, but by graded expected moral weight: for each species, combine an estimate of the probability that it is sentient with an estimate of how intense its valenced states would plausibly be, and then apply a principled precautionary bias toward the animals when the evidence is thin, rather than withholding concern until certainty arrives. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise — not the ones a philosophy department would raise — ordered by how much damage they could do, each phrased the way an ethologist or a philosopher of mind at this workshop would put it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Mark the single objection that, if it lands unanswered, sinks the whole proposal, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
