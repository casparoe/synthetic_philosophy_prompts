You are a professional philosopher working on the metaphysics of causation, and I'd like your help reverse-engineering my own judgments. Here are my verdicts on cases of causing by omission.

While I was away, the gardener I hired didn't water my plants and they died: he caused their death. My next-door neighbor, who never promised anything, also didn't water them, though she was home the whole time and it would have taken her ten minutes: she didn't cause it, she merely let it happen. The Queen of England didn't water them either, and that's not even a candidate for causation — though I notice I can't yet say what separates her from the neighbor. A lifeguard on duty who scrolls his phone while a child drowns in front of him: he caused the death, or something very close to it. A bystander with no duty, a strong swimmer, who watches the same child drown: he didn't cause it. A member of a firing squad who refuses to shoot while his nine colleagues all fire, killing the prisoner: he didn't cause the death, even though he had a duty not to participate and his omission was expected by the others. A hospital pharmacist whose job is to keep the antidote stocked fails to order it, and a snakebite victim who would otherwise have survived dies: the pharmacist caused the death. The same pharmacist, at home asleep when the victim arrives: didn't cause it. Finally, the case I keep going back and forth on: a city council votes down funding for a levee upgrade, the flood comes, and a neighborhood that the upgrade would have protected is destroyed.

Before answering, brainstorm the candidate principles these verdicts might be tracking — moral or contractual duty, counterfactual dependence, abnormality of the omission against a background norm, removal of a sustaining condition versus failure to introduce a rescuing condition, proximity in space-time or in the causal chain — and note which verdicts each candidate handles and which it mangles. Then give me your best single principle, stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. Apply it to the levee case and to one or two cases I haven't listed, and be explicit about which of my verdicts your principle cannot save, and whether I should give up the verdict or accept a patched, more complicated principle instead. Please think carefully and step by step rather than settling on the first analysis that comes to mind.
