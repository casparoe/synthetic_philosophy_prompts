Here's what's on the docket, three separate items. One: the dinning halls have a cockroach problem and facilities wants to hit them with a residual spray, though they've also floated a slower "integrated pest management" approach that costs more. Two: grounds sprays the quad lawns every spring for crane fly larvae purely so the grass looks nice — zero health issue, just aesthetics. Three: a student group petitioned to stop destroying the paper wasp nests by the dining hall patio and put up "please don't swat" signs instead, on the grounds that wasps pollinate things and, in their words, probably feel something. Half the committee thinks the wasp petition is a joke, the other half thinks killing anything with a nervous system for a nicer lawn is indefensible.

I'm taking an animal ethics elective right now and we just did a week on insect minds, so I keep noticing that the reasoning depends a lot on how sure you are insects can suffer. Like, if there's even a modest chance they feel pain, and you're talking about tens of thousands of grubs, does that just swamp the aesthetic value of a lawn? But I also genuinely don't know how confident to be, and I suspect the answer shouldn't come out the same for all three items anyway — the cockroach thing involves human health so the stakes aren't symmetric, and the wasps at least have the pollinator angle going for them.

Draft the memo I'd circulate: a concrete recommendation on each of the three items (not "form a subcommittee"), with the reasoning laid out so that someone who thinks the wasp petition is a joke could actually be moved, and with an honest treatment of the strongest point on the losing side too. If the natural recommendation ends up being some kind of tiered policy — health-motivated control yes, aesthetic control no, maybe something in between for the wasps — please spell out what that tiering actually depends on, because I want to be able to say in the memo what we'd learn about insect minds that would make the tiers come out differently.
