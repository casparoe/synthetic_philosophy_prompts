I'm putting together a proposal for a three-month summer research grant in population ethics, and I want help turning my half-formed idea into something fundable. Background: in a seminar last term we were discussing the procreation asymmetry, and basically everyone in the room accepted it — creating a child whose life would go badly is a harm, something you have a reason not to do, while creating a child whose life would go well isn't similarly good for anyone (there's no person it benefits, since without the act that person wouldn't exist). But when I pushed and asked whether that same logic extends to aggregation — if the asymmetry holds for creating single lives, why doesn't suffering just dominate the ledger generally? — the room split, and a couple of people admitted they'd bite the bullet on some form of suffering-focused axiology, where preventing suffering has much greater or even lexical weight over producing happiness. Nobody could say where, exactly, the asymmetry stops applying and ordinary add-everything-up reasoning takes over, except by gesturing at how happiness obviously matters too. So the question I want the proposal built around: is there a principled, non-arbitrary stopping point between accepting the procreation asymmetry and accepting a strongly suffering-focused view, or does the asymmetry, plus standard aggregation, commit you much further down that road than most philosophers are comfortable admitting?

A few requirements for the proposal. State the question precisely enough that it could come out one way rather than another, and explain why the existing standard positions leave it open — total utilitarianism just denies the asymmetry, person-affecting views have their own well-known problems (the non-identity stuff), and the move of just pointing at the extreme version of negative utilitarianism, the one implying it'd be fine to painlessly end the world, feels like a reductio of the most extreme form rather than an answer about the space between. The actual work should be working through a ladder of cases: small amounts of suffering vs. huge amounts of happiness, lives that are mostly good with some bad, lives created vs. lives improved, and where on the ladder people's commitments flip. Also say what would count as the project failing — my worry is the honest answer turns out to be "it's vague all the way down and there's no principled line," in which case the proposal should say upfront what the fallback contribution is (maybe mapping the space and showing which pairs of intuitions can't be jointly held, rather than finding the stopping point). And keep the scope realistic for three months, one person, no empirical component.

Don't include a bibliography or citations, I'll slot those in myself — the proposal can refer to positions and arguments in plain language, like the old line about how we should make people happy rather than make happy people, without pinning it to specific page numbers. And please don't pad it with grant-speak ("transformative," "cutting-edge"); the funder for this one is known for liking direct, plain writing. If it matters for tone: I'm a second-year PhD student and this would be my first solo grant application, so I'd rather it be modest and solid than grand.
