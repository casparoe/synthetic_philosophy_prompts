I'm a novelist by trade, and I sit on the curriculum committee of a small international writers' residency: twenty fiction writers, three months, a dozen nationalities in any given cohort. Our feedback culture has become a live institutional problem, and I've been asked to bring a proposal to our January meeting. Last summer, a Dutch participant, extending what she sincerely considered the respect owed to a professional, told a Japanese participant in open session that his manuscript's first hundred pages were unreadable and should be restarted from zero; he took it as a public dismantling and scarcely left his room for a week. The same Japanese participant, meanwhile, had spent two months assuring her that her chapters were very interesting — which she received as encouragement and which he intended as a gentle no. Two committee members now propose a codified norm of frank, professional critique on the Northern European model, arguing that hedged feedback is a tissue of white lies that squanders the residency's one irreplaceable resource, which is time. Two others propose instead teaching an explicit translation layer — a glossary mapping politeness registers onto intended degrees of severity — on the theory that indirectness is itself a channel of information, and that bluntness merely exports one culture's conversational norms under the banner of universal professionalism. A fifth member wants a mandated praise-criticism-praise structure, which I privately think of as the bureaucratic criticism sandwich. My own view, which I cannot yet make coherent, is that the white lies were wrong and the bluntness was also wrong. Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting: it must recommend one concrete policy for next year's cohort, give the strongest consideration on the losing side a genuinely fair hearing rather than a paragraph of throat-clearing before dismissal, and — this matters to me — it must keep clearly separate what is actually settled in our understanding of lying and deception from what philosophers genuinely contest among themselves, for instance whether every lie is wrong, or whether a paternalistic lie can express respect for a person rather than deny it, so that the committee does not mistake one position in an ongoing argument for the settled consensus. Format: exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion.
