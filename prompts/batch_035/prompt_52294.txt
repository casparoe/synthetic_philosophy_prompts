The audience is smart but has no philosophy of probability background at all: they know conditional probability, they've seen Bayes, but they've never thought about what it means for a probability to be indexed to who or when you are. The plan is to get there through Sleeping Beauty, but I don't want to just walk them through the standard halfer/thirder debate and have it land as a fun puzzle with no lesson. The real point I want them to walk away with is that there's a genuine question about what the sample space even is when your evidence can't distinguish centered possibilities, and that this isn't just sloppiness that a careful setup would wash out.

Don't write me the explanation itself -- write me the teaching plan. I want the dependency structure: what has to be established before the Beauty setup even gets stated (my instinct is they need a warm-up where self-location is unavoidable, like being told one of two people is about to have a different experience, before the coin-and-amnesia story makes the issue vivid, but you tell me). I want a single carrying example, and I'm torn about whether it should be the standard "wake up Monday, maybe Tuesday with amnesia" version or something with creation instead of awakening -- I've seen variants where instead of waking Beauty twice, you flip a coin and create one person in one room or two people in two rooms, and I have a feeling that variant forces the question harder, but I'm not sure it doesn't smuggle in its own confusions about what "I" refers to, so I'd want your take on what the carrying example must include and what it must avoid. Then the misconceptions: I can already predict someone saying the whole thing is a verbal dispute you dissolve by choosing a convention, someone else saying Beauty gets no new information on waking so it has to stay 1/2 and refusing to budge, and at least one person trying to settle it with a betting argument that quietly assumes you get paid once per correct answer rather than once per experiment -- I want to know in what order these will actually show up and where in the sequence each should be pre-empted rather than argued with after the fact. Also tell me what I should openly decline to cover in 45 minutes (I'm assuming the Doomsday-style applications and any of the decision-theoretic stuff about payoffs are out, but flag anything else that will eat the session). Finish with the single question I can pose at the end that would let me tell whether they understood self-locating uncertainty as opposed to just having picked a side in the halfer/thirder fight, since frankly I'll consider the session a failure if they leave thinking it was about a coin.
