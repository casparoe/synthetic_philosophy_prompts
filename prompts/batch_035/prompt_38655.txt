Background (in case you haven't encountered this puzzle, and so we're working from the same version of it): a soldier named Judy Benjamin is dropped into a training area at night with no idea where she is. The area is divided two ways: it's split between territory held by her own side (Blue) and enemy territory (Red), and each of those halves is further divided into a Headquarters Company sector and a Second Company sector. She had no prior information at all about how likely she is to be in any particular sector, so she treats the four cells as equiprobable, a quarter each. Over the radio, a voice she has no reason to distrust tells her: "If you are in Red territory, the odds are 3:1 that you're in the Headquarters Company sector rather than Second Company." That is the entire message — the voice says nothing at all about what happens if she's in Blue territory.

I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I've implemented Bayesian updating plenty of times, so I'm comfortable with the machinery. What I can't get straight is what to do with a message like this, and I'd like a careful explanation of the conceptual issue.

Conditioning on the content of the message doesn't work the way I'd naively expect: the message has no probability of being false or incomplete that I can assign in a principled way, it's not evidence I can weigh against a prior, and it seems to only speak about one branch of the possibility space while leaving the other branch untouched. I've read that one standard approach is to keep the untouched branch fixed at its prior value, redistribute probability within the Red branch so that the stated conditional odds come out right, and then choose the overall distribution that changes as little as possible from the prior in some formal sense of "minimal change." But apparently the natural formalization of that intuition (something involving a measure of how far a distribution has moved) yields a result many people find wrong: in the standard version of the numbers above, it apparently tells Judy she's more likely to be in the specific sector the message said nothing about at all than in either Red sector — and, more strikingly, that her probability of having landed in her own side's headquarters sector, about which the message was completely silent, should change from a quarter to something else. That last part strikes me as the real puzzle: why should information that only concerns Red territory tell her anything at all about her own side's territory?

So, three things I'd like you to cover:

1. A clear account of why this kind of message — an uncertain conditional, or advice about a conditional — resists the ordinary conditioning machinery, and how the "minimal change" style of answer is supposed to work.
2. The intuitive case for the objection that such an update is irrational or at least ad hoc, and — since I gather not everyone agrees it's a problem — some sense of the strongest replies on the other side, i.e. whether the counterintuitive result actually is the uniquely rational one or whether the objection has been addressed.
3. A step back to the practical stakes, since this is the part that matters to me: when I build systems that ingest natural-language assertions and translate them into distributional updates over structured state spaces, what should this episode teach me? In particular, is the lesson that "minimal information change" is a genuine norm I should encode, or that it's a useful heuristic with known failure cases, or that the whole framing of translating an isolated assertion into a posterior is a category mistake? I'd appreciate it if you'd be direct about which of these you actually think, rather than just listing options.

Please avoid a bibliography; what I want is the reasoning itself, at a level of rigor that would let me sketch the update procedure in pseudocode if I wanted to.
