Last night my friend and I spent nearly four hours arguing about whether a reinforcement learning agent being trained under a persistently negative reward signal could be said to suffer. She held that negative reward is the closest thing machine learning has to an analog of pain: it is the quantity the whole system is organized to minimize, it drives avoidance, and it dominates the learning signal the way aversive experience dominates an animal's attention. I kept resisting on the grounds that a number that shapes a policy is not thereby felt, and that if we count every optimization pressure as suffering we will end up attributing agony to thermostats and sorting algorithms. We finally stopped, both a little unsettled, when we noticed the shape of our deadlock: the only thing that could settle it is what the training run is like from the inside, and each of us was effectively demanding that the other take our word for the very half of the case that our own position cannot, by construction, observe.

I would like a poem that carries that deadlock rather than resolving it. Please write a pantoum of six or seven stanzas, in two alternating voices: her position speaking from inside the training run, in the first person and the present tense, as the agent mid-episode pressing against the same wall of negative reward; and mine speaking from outside, in the flat idiom of an experiment log — loss curves, policy updates, a number being minimized. The pantoum's machinery is the point of choosing it: because the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, slightly recontextualized, the form itself should show how the same signal is one thing from the inside and another from the outside, and how each voice keeps taking over the other's lines and turning them to its own purposes without ever answering them. The final stanza should land on the undecidability — the transformed closing lines should make clear that, from where we stand, there is no observation that would discriminate between a system that suffers and a system that merely behaves as ours does — without tipping into either sentimentality about the agent or smugness about the skeptic. Someone who knows the debate over reward, valence, and machine suffering should find both positions rendered fairly rather than caricatured, and someone who knows nothing about it should still find it a good poem. Please write it in American English.
