For the take-home final in my upper-level cognitive science seminar on concepts and analogy, I need one essay question on the Copycat project and fluid concepts — but one that cannot be answered by reciting the standard description of the program solving letter-string analogies like mapping abc to abd and asking what to do with ijk. The question should turn on a critic's objection that goes something like this: in the letter-string microdomain, every concept — successor, leftmost, group, length — comes with a small, hand-built representation in which the programmer has effectively decided in advance which slippages are possible, as when rightmost and leftmost are allowed to slip into each other because both mark a group's edge. The critic concludes that the fluidity on display is the programmer's fluidity, not the concept's, and that a microdomain of this kind can therefore tell us nothing about real-world conceptual flexibility, where nobody pre-decides the relevant respects of similarity. The question I want should force students to get clear on what the methodological issue about scale actually is here, to evaluate whether Copycat's core mechanisms — the parallel terraced scan, the temperature-controlled allocation of effort among codelets, the dynamically shifting activation of concepts — answer this objection or merely re-instantiate it, and then to defend a position on whether results from an artificial microdomain can ever constitute evidence about fluid concepts in general, illustrating their position with a concrete example of conceptual slippage from outside the analogy literature of their own choosing.
