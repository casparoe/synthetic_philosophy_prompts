At a happy hour after our first-year game theory seminar, two fellow PhD students — one in formal theory, one in normative political theory — got into a running argument about the paradox of voting, and neither would let it go. I'm a PhD student in political behavior, and while I have views, they asked me to referee and I found that each of them was right about something at each stage, so I'd rather have someone disentangle it properly. The exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Jacob, who works on rational choice models, opened by declaring individual voting irrational. In any large electorate, he said, the probability that a single vote decides the outcome — or even flips it into an exact tie that gets resolved your way — is on the order of one in millions for even a closely contested race. Meanwhile voting carries real costs: registering, standing in line, taking time off. So the expected instrumental benefit is vanishingly small relative to the cost, and a rational agent who compares expected utilities should stay home. Anyone who votes is either innumerate or isn't actually trying to affect the outcome.

(2) Sofia replied that this argument proves far too much. If expected-value arithmetic of this kind is the standard, then almost no one acts rationally in collective contexts: the same reasoning condemns contributing to any large-scale cooperative scheme where an individual's contribution is not decisive — public radio pledges, union dues, even recycling. She added that voters needn't be aiming at causation at all: voting can be expressive, like cheering at a game, and there is nothing irrational about expression. On the expressive reading there is no paradox, just a different activity than the one Jacob's model assumes.

(3) Jacob said the expressive move concedes his point while pretending not to. If voting is expression, then it is consumption, not political action, and the nagging feature of the phenomenon remains unexplained: voters describe themselves as doing their civic duty, and feel they have discharged an obligation — feelings that would be mystifying if the act were just consumption, like preferring one brand of cola to another. He also pressed the free-rider point against her cooperative-scheme analogy: whatever the other contributors do, the election outcome would have been identical had any one voter abstained, so the scheme doesn't need her participation the way a wall needs each of its bricks; she receives the better outcome (if it is one) whether or not she pays the cost.

(4) Sofia answered in two parts. First, the "outcome would have been identical without you" test proves too much: it would equally show that no participant in a mass movement ever matters, and she doubted Jacob was prepared to accept that sweeping a conclusion. Second, she said the tiny-probability premise is not as friendly to Jacob as he assumes: expected value is sensitive to how the stakes are valued, and if a voter regards the difference between the candidates as enormous — possibly even as a matter of basic justice or constitutional order — then even a probability of one in ten million can yield an expected value exceeding the cost of voting. The paradox, she concluded, is not a discovery about citizens but an artifact of assuming that reasonable people value political outcomes modestly and by the same scale the modeler does.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, and justify each verdict. Then tell me who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing — in particular whether the standoff really turns on an empirical question about how voters value outcomes, a conceptual question about what kind of act voting is, or a normative question about whether the standard of individual instrumental rationality is the right standard to apply here in the first place.
