I'm taking a philosophy of physics course this semester and we just wrapped up the block universe, including the Rietdijk-Putnam argument — the one where, because simultaneity is relative, different observers slicing up spacetime disagree about which distant events count as past, so nothing ends up genuinely "open" and the future is fixed in the same way the past is. At the same time my modern lit seminar is finishing Slaughterhouse-Five, and I can't stop noticing that Billy Pilgrim's Tralfamadorians basically state the block universe in plain English. The passage where he explains that all moments, past, present and future, always have existed, always will exist, and that it's just an illusion that one moment follows another one, like beads on a string, reads like tenseless theory of time with the jargon stripped off. And the whole "So it goes" thing looks like the ethical payoff of that metaphysics: if the dead are in bad condition only in one particular moment and are just fine in plenty of others, then crying at funerals is silly.

Here's what I want to write my final paper on, but I keep going back and forth on it: is the novel's famous fatalism actually the block universe's fatalism, or is Vonnegut smuggling in something extra? My physics prof insists the block universe doesn't by itself imply resignation — events being fixed doesn't mean deliberation is pointless, since our deliberating is itself part of what's fixed, and nobody gets "nothing matters" out of tenselessness without an extra fatalist argument that goes back way before Einstein. But the Tralfamadorians seem to draw exactly that conclusion, and Billy's whole postwar life reads like evidence that adopting the view is a symptom of trauma rather than a piece of wisdom.

So what I'm asking for: read the novel through the block universe. Where does the Tralfamadorian picture genuinely match four-dimensionalism (seeing all the moments at once, the dead being fine elsewhere in spacetime), where does it smuggle in a fatalism the physics doesn't license, and does Vonnegut know the difference — is the book endorsing the view or diagnosing the people who hide in it? I want a reading that takes the physics and the novel equally seriously, that admits where the novel resists the whole framework (there's an awful lot of grief in a book where funerals are supposed to be silly), and that ends by saying whether the novel shows us something about the block universe that the arguments don't — maybe why people find it unbarable in the first place. Please start straight in with the reading itself, no intro or setup.
