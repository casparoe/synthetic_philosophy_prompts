I run a small philosophy forum, and for about a year now two members have been going at it over the combination problem. One is a constitutive panpsychist (Russellian-monist flavor: micro-level entities have phenomenal properties, and my consciousness is somehow constituted out of theirs). The other is a strong emergentist about consciousness, dualist-leaning: conscious subjects come into existence as a lawful but brute fact once neural systems cross a complexity threshold, and micro-experience is a fantasy. They've finally agreed to let me draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration, and since you're the closest thing I have to a professional philosopher of mind, you get to write it.

Take a moment to brainstorm before you draft — I want this good, not fast. The document should have: (1) a numbered section of claims both would sign; (2) a table of the precise disagreements, each phrased so that the side holding the view would accept the wording as theirs; (3) the cruxes — subquestions such that settling one would move at least one of them — and for each crux a case, argument, or thought experiment both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

Some threads you should work in (they've fought over all of these, dont skip any):

- The skeptic's favorite complaint: physical quantities compose transparently — masses sum, and "sum" is an operation we can define without remainder. The panpsychist's "combination" of subjects, by contrast, is never defined, only gestured at. Any attempt to state it either smuggles in the very macro-subject it was supposed to build (circularity) or says only that the micro-experiences are next to each other, which is spatial juxtaposition, not experiential unity.
- The panpsychist's counter: strong emergence of subjects is at least as bad — a new law of nature with no micro-level precedent, mysteriously tuned to nervous systems. Micro-experience at least generalizes from the one system we know is conscious (the brain) instead of positing a threshold miracle.
- The boundary problem: what fixes which physical parts get to compose a subject, and why does a subject end where it does? The skeptic thinks this cuts equally against the emergentist (why this threshold, why these parts); the panpsychist thinks she can just borrow whatever answer the emergentist gives and then win on parsimony everywhere else.
- The chord question, which they got into last month and never resolved: when two notes sound at once and you hear a chord, is that one experience with two components, or two experiences owned by a single subject? And is whichever relation that is — call it co-consciousness — doing the same work the panpsychist needs between micro-subjects, or is it a relation within one subject and therefore useless for building subjects out of subjects? Split-brain patients and dissociative-identity cases are their shared stock examples here — use them for one of the cruxes, with each side's prediction about what those cases show.

One epistemic requirement, and it matters more to me than the formatting: the document has to clearly seperate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who actually know this literature. So for every claim in the agreed section, tag it either [settled] — informed people on all sides treat it as established — or [contested], meaning it's genuinely disputed and the wording is the most both can co-sign. And in the disagreements section, don't smuggle in any suggestion that the field's consensus sits with one side; where the field itself is split down the middle, say that plainly. Also please don't pad it with a bibliography or reading list — this should read like something two forum members would actually put their names on, not a term paper.
