I host a small podcast about language, minds, and machines -- listeners are mostly grad students and programmers who think about AI all day, and tbh they're the kind of audience that notices when a question is really just the host showing off. Next week's guest is a cognitive scientist who's spent his whole career on analogy-making, and he's best known for a 2018 Atlantic essay where he ran French, German, and Chinese texts through Google Translate -- poetry included -- and argued the output was fluent garbage: the system knows what words tend to appear near what other words and nothing else. Call his view the shallowness thesis. He loves bringing up an old 1947 letter by the mathematician Warren Weaver, one of the founding cheerleaders of machine translation, who wrote that when he looked at an article in Russian, his attitude was, "This is really written in English, but it has been coded in some strange symbols. I will now proceed to decode." My guest treats that line as the original sin of the whole field, so I'd like at least one question that asks him point blank whether modern statistical translation has really repudiated Weaver or just gotten very, very good at the decryption Weaver described.

He's very good at two deflections, and I want a question sequence that makes each one progressively harder to use. The first is "come back in fifty years" -- once I concede the missing thing is just a matter of more data, the whole interview collapses into a timing dispute. So I want a ladder of cases where scale alone shouldn't help, each forcing him to say whether THAT would count as understanding or not: a system that renders a genuinely fresh metaphor someone coined at a party that afternoon; a bilingual pun that only lands if you know one specific cultural fact; a system that handles the case where a metaphor for grief is alive in the source language but dead in the target language (like how "deadline" was once a literal line around a prison you'd be shot for crossing, but nobody who uses the word fluently knows that -- does the fluent-but-ignorant human understand "deadline" any more than a machine does?). If he keeps answering "still not understanding," the next question should force him to name the general ability that's missing, in terms that don't just restate the conclusion. The second deflection is parasitism: the machine's output is just recombined human understanding, all the real work was done by the humans whose translations and texts it trained on. I suspect the booth test corners this one -- a professional translator in a windowless room rendering a Tamil funeral song about rites he's never seen, working from dictionaries and parallel texts -- and I want a question that presses on whether his understanding does any work in that room that a machine's couldn't, and where exactly it re-enters once he walks out the door. And since a chunk of my listeners will email me anyway if I don't ask: one question about the reply that human understanding is ALSO pattern-matching all the way down, with a follow-up ready for the move I suspect he'll make, which is to say that human pattern-matching is grounded in a body that has actually been hungry, scared, and bereaved.

Eight to ten questions total. Each one should come with a follow-up keyed to the reply he's most likely to give -- you know the literature well enough to anticipate it. End with a real question about what evidence could actually count against the shallowness thesis, not an invitation to repeat it more beautifully. Nothing generic like asking how he got into the field, nothing that requires him to have read specific papers (he treats citations as concessions anyway), and please flag for me which one or two questions are risky because he might find them hostile rather than fun -- I want him back on the show eventually. Keep two of the questions simple enough that my non-philosopher listeners can follow them in the car, and feel free to make the rest as technical as the topic deserves. One more thing: he's a poet at heart and genuinely loves the machines he criticizes, so if any question can give him room to say something loving about language, that's the one my audience will clip.
