For a couple of years now I have been turning the Fermi paradox over in my head — on walks, in the middle of the night, that sort of thing. I have no training in astronomy or biology; I just find the puzzle irresistible, and I have read enough popular treatments to have picked up a few of the standard terms. I have finally written down where I have come out, and before I present this to my amateur astronomy club I would like an honest audit of whether the pieces actually fit together. Here is my position, as a numbered list:

1. Given liquid water and a few hundred million years, life getting started is close to a sure thing — the chemistry wants to happen, and it happened on Earth about as soon as it could have.
2. Intelligence is not a fluke. Eyes evolved independently dozens of times on Earth, and whatever selection pressures keep rewarding cognition here should reward it elsewhere too.
3. The sky is silent because civilizations do not shout, not because they are not there — deliberate broadcasting into the void is a cost with no clear payoff, and any sensible species eventually figures that out.
4. Interstellar settlement never happens anywhere — not because everyone dies young, but because the economics never pencil out, even for very advanced societies; nothing ever gets cheap enough for long enough to make expansion worth it.
5. If there is a Great Filter, it is behind us. I take our own existence as evidence that the genuinely improbable steps are the ones already in our past, like the origin of complex cells.
6. Finding a second, independent origin of microbial life — say, beneath the ice on Enceladus — would be happy news, and I would sleep fine that night.
7. Finding even one dead alien probe, or ruins on some airless world, would tell us something important even though a single artifact is not a civilization.

Some of these feel as though they rub against each other, but I can no longer tell whether that is real friction or just me holding too many opinions at once. Please audit the set rather than lecture me on the paradox in general: identify which pairs or larger groups of commitments are genuinely in tension, rank those tensions from most to least severe, and for each one say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere on the list. Please also flag anything that only looks like a conflict but dissolves on inspection — I do not want a report that treats every pair as equally troubled. Conclude by telling me which single revision would clean up the most if I made it, and what that revision would cost me in terms I care about, not just in consistency.

Please structure your response with the following sections, in this order: "Bottom line" (one short paragraph on whether the set hangs together); "Real tensions" (ranked worst to mildest, roughly a paragraph each, naming the commitments by their numbers); "Merely apparent tensions" (a few sentences each, with an explanation of why the conflict dissolves); "The one repair I would make" (a short paragraph); and "How sure I am" (a brief closing note stating your overall confidence in the audit and singling out the one point you are least certain of). Do not hedge uniformly throughout — commit where you think the reasoning is solid, and mark precisely the places where it is not.
