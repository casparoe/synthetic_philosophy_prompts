I'm in an intro epistemology course and we just did the Clifford–James exchange on the ethics of belief, and it's been living in my head rent-free for a week now. The way my professor set it up: Clifford's shipowner talks himself out of his doubts about his rickety old emigrant ship, acquires what Clifford calls a sincere and comfortable conviction that she's seaworthy, and then, in Clifford's words, "he got his insurance-money when she went down in mid-ocean and told no tales." From this Clifford derives his famous rule that it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence. James pushes back that when a choice between two hypotheses is living (both are real possibilities to the person choosing), forced (you can't just abstain and stand pat — some choices you make by default whether you like it or not), and momentous (a unique, high-stakes opportunity), and the evidence genuinely can't settle it, then we're entitled to let our passional nature decide — and that Clifford's own austere rule is itself a belief held on passional grounds, a kind of faith in caution.

Here's the twist I keep chewing on though, and it's why I'm writing this instead of doing my problem set. My roommate is a competitive climber, and she swears the routes where she goes in believing she'll send it go categorically better than the ones where she's uncertain — and the sports psych stuff I've skimmed seems to back her up, confidence measurably improves performance. So her belief looks partially self-fulfilling: believing she can do it is part of the causal machinery by which she does it. Clifford's shipowner had no such feedback loop — the ship was sound or not regardless of his state of mind. Does that difference actually matter for the ethics of believing? It seems like James's defense is at its strongest in exactly these self-fulfilling cases and at its weakest in his own showcase example, religious faith, where (on most conceptions anyway) God exists or doesn't completely independent of what anyone believes.

So, write an essay on whether the Clifford–James dispute survives the self-fulfilling belief case, and more broadly on who wins the exchange. Weigh both sides seriously, but commit to a verdict at the end — don't cop out with "it depends on how you define belief." One more thing I'd ask: keep clear about what's actually settled versus what's genuinely contested among people who study this stuff. For example, whether James was even responding to Clifford's real position or to a caricature of it seems to be a live scholarly fight, and also whether Clifford's rule is best read as a rule about belief or really about inquiry and action. If you take a side on something like that, flag it as your read rather than passing it off as the consensus.
