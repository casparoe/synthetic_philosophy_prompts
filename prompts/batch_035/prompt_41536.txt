Before I write up my considered position on moral epistemology for a graduate seminar, I want to know whether it hangs together. The package is roughly this. (1) Act consequentialism is true: an act is wrong exactly when some available alternative would have made things go better, where that's a matter of objective betterness, not of what the agent knew or could have known. (2) An agent who does her best on the evidence can still act wrongly; ignorance mitigates culpability but doesn't excuse. (3) For nearly every ordinary choice, the bulk of the value at stake sits in indefinitely far-reaching ripple effects—shifts in who meets whom, which children exist, which ideas get recorded—that dwarf anything we can foresee. (4) About those ripple effects we are mostly symmetrically clueless: for every plausible story in which my act improves the far future there is an equally credible mirror story in which it worsens it. (5) Where the symmetry genuinely holds, I'm entitled to set the ripple effects aside and choose on ordinary near-term grounds, since the expected values cancel. (6) Where the symmetry is broken—above all for reducing extinction risk, since extinction halts the ripple chains rather than redirecting them—I should act on my best subjective probabilities, and I think tiny probabilities of vast differences can then generate demanding obligations. (7) Finally, I accept that people are often appropriately blamed for wrong acts whose wrongness no one could have known. Please audit the set: identify which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be, rank the tensions by severity, and for each say what the cheapest fix costs. Two spots I'm unsure about: whether (5) is really available to someone who accepts (3), given how much smaller near-term effects are than even slight biases in ripple probabilities would be, and whether (6) is stable if the mirror-story reasoning of (4) is turned on x-risk work itself. Please answer in American English.
