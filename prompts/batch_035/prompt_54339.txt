I'm a PhD student in health policy writing a dissertation on the ethics of pain management, and I keep circling an asymmetry in medicine for which I can't find a clean theoretical story. On one side, clinical practice treats pain relief as close to a categorical duty: hospitals screen for pain at every encounter as routinely as they take blood pressure, unrelieved pain is a reportable harm and grounds for complaint or even litigation, and palliative care is permitted to sedate a patient into permanent unconsciousness to relieve otherwise-intractable suffering — trading away consciousness itself for relief from pain. Nothing remotely parallel exists on the positive side. No clinician has a duty to increase a patient's pleasure, nobody sues over a dreary but painless hospital stay, and a general practitioner who prescribed mood-brightening interventions to an already content patient purely to raise his enjoyment of life would be thought eccentric at best. In clinical practice, pain seems to have a kind of priority that pleasure simply lacks.

Yet the very same institution commensurates the two without blinking. Health technology assessment bodies rank treatments by quality-adjusted life years, in which moving a patient from severe pain to mild pain and moving her from mild pain to comfort are scored on a single scale — and, crucially, so are gains above neutral, so a drug that makes painless people feel genuinely good competes on the same footing as one that merely spares them agony. In that apparatus, avoiding suffering and adding delight are just units of one currency.

Please work out what the bedside side of this presupposes about the moral relationship between pain and pleasure. Is it something like a lexical priority — the thought that no amount of added pleasure can outweigh a given suffering, or at least that suffering's claims are prior in a way pleasure's never are — or is the asymmetry better explained by something non-axiological, like a duty of rescue or a patient's veto over what is done to her body, that remains neutral on commensurability altogether? Then assess whether the QALY side is genuinely inconsistent with the bedside side, or whether the two can be reconciled — say, by treating the clinical priority as agent-relative while the QALY scale serves as a utilitarian bookkeeping device for allocation decisions, or by holding that any lexical priority applies only above some threshold of suffering intensity. Where exactly do these practices leave the underlying theory underdetermined — which rival accounts of the pleasure/pain relation would all license the current pattern? And finally, sketch what a health system would have to change if lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism were in fact the right view: would there be a positive duty to enhance happiness in the population, would funding for feel-good interventions in the well-off have to rival funding for pain relief in the ill, and would palliative sedation remain permissible, or would the threshold structure make it obligatory in cases where it is currently merely allowed?
