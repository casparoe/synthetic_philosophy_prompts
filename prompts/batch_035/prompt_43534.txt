I teach a small decision-theory unit inside an undergrad philosophy elective — 18 students, one 75-minute slot, movable desks, no laptops, basically zero budget. I can hand out index cards and poker chips, and the only "real" payoff I can offer is tokens redeemable for chocolate at the end of term. We've covered expected utility and theyve seen Newcomb as a one-liner on a slide, but nothing beyond that — no FDT, no updatelessness, most of them have never heard the term. Last term I tried running a prisoner's-dilemma-style thing and it died in ten minutes because they all just colluded out loud in front of me and I couldn't tell whether that was a failure or the point, so I'm wary of games where cooperation is the obvious move from minute one.

Design me an in-class game that makes them feel the pull of updatelessness — the thought that you should sometimes act on the policy you would have chosen *before* seeing the observation you've now already seen, because your choice is evidence about what your counterfactual selves do in the branches you're not in. I want them to run into the tension themselves rather than be told about it: a stage where they commit to a policy in ignorance, a stage where the information lands and that policy suddenly looks stupid to whoever's stuck holding it (some of them should visibly want to tear up their card — that moment is the whole reason I'm doing this), and a scoring rule under which the branch-rational choice loses to the ex ante policy in a way they can watch play out on the board, round by round.

Give me the rules, materials, a timeline, a debrief that maps what happened onto the formal distinction, and a frank note about which feature of updatelessness no classroom version can actually reproduce — my guess is the infallible predictor, since any human standing in as the predictor will be noisy in a way the theory doesn't permit, but tell me if something worse is lurking. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. My bet is students trying to renegotiate or make side-deals to wriggle out of the policy they wrote down, and honestly that might end up being the most interesting part of the session — so work out whether I should ban it outright or lean into it.
