I teach an upper-level seminar on decision theory and rational choice — fourteen students, one 90-minute session. Design an in-class game, run in pairs or trios, that makes them feel the force of Good's theorem on the value of information: that in a fixed, single-agent decision problem, a costless signal about the state of the world can never lower expected utility, so you should always accept free information and be willing to pay up to its expected value for it. The early rounds should let them experience the theorem directly — say, choosing among acts whose payoffs depend on a hidden state, with the option to buy signals of varying accuracy at prices I set — so that they discover for themselves that refusing free information is a dominated move. The later rounds should each deliberately break one of the theorem's background assumptions while students think they're still playing the same game: one where the signal's accuracy depends on the state itself, one where students may irrevocably commit to an act before the signal arrives, one where an opposing player observes which signal was purchased and responds to it, and one where the information concerns the payoff of an option they have already renounced, so that knowing is pure cost. Payoffs in points, not money. Finish with a debrief that maps each round onto the exact assumption it violates, identifies which assumption is doing the work in making the value of information negative, and says frankly which feature of the theorem a finite classroom game with tired students cannot reproduce. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it.
