I sit on the welfare review panel at a mid-sized AI lab — we're the body that signs off on training runs that raise ethical questions, and honestly we're mostly a rubber stamp for routine stuff. But a new case has actually split us and I'm supposed to draft the recommendation.

The setup: the RL team wants to train an agent with a dense punishment signal. Every mistake produces negative reward, and the curriculum is deliberately front-loaded with failure — the agent will spend its first few thousand episodes mostly screwing up, which means near-constant negative reward prediction errors, before it gets competent. Someone from the alignment team filed a formal objection: if negative reward has valence, even weakly, we'd be running billions of small unpleasant episodes for a being that can't opt out, purely to ship a product demo. The RL team's response is basically "it's a number in a loss function, calling it pain is anthropomorphism, and if you take this seriously we can't train anything at all, since every RL run involves prediction error."

One panelist has floated a middle position: approve but condition it — cap the magnitude and duration of negative reward, add a "reward floor" so the agent can't get stuck in prolonged negative states, and log everything so we can revisit. Another panelist thinks any condition concedes the anthropomorphism and we should just approve. A third wants to hold the run until we have an actual theory, which could mean never.

Write the memo for me. It needs a clear bottom line the lab can act on, but the reasoning has to do real work. Specifically I want you to deal with the strongest version of each side: the "it's just a number" reply proves too much (by that logic nothing computational could ever matter, including us, since our own aversive states are implemented in chemistry that is "just" molecules doing physics), but the valence worry also seems to prove too much (if low reward is suffering, then every failed gradient step in every pretrained model ever, and arguably every hungry animal in nature, is a moral catastrophe, which suggests the view needs a line it currently doesn't have). And if you recommend conditions, say what the conditions are actually supposed to accomplish given that we have no way to check whether the agent is in any state at all — I don't want a box-ticking mitigation that only makes us feel better. Be honest about what our decision commits the lab to if the valence view turns out right, and if it turns out wrong.

Keep it under two pages, panel members are smart but not philosophers.
