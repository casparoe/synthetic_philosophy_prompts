The exam question read as follows. "In this course we have discussed the simple conditional analysis of dispositions, according to which an object x is disposed to give response r to stimulus s if, and only if, were x to undergo s, it would give r. A glass ornament hangs in a workshop, and the owner installs one or the other of two safety systems, never both at once. System A: a sensor which, on detecting an imminent impact, snaps a rigid foam shell around the ornament in the instant before the blow lands, so that the shell absorbs the blow and the glass itself is left entirely unaltered. System B: a device which, on detecting an imminent impact, would instantaneously restructure the glass at the molecular level, so that from the moment of alteration onward the material is not fragile or brittle in any way. For each system, taken separately, say whether it is a fink, a mask (antidote), or neither; explain what distinguishes a fink from a mask; and state, with reasons, whether the system refutes the simple conditional analysis, and whether it threatens the claim that the ornament's fragility is an intrinsic feature of the glass."

The student's answer, exactly as submitted, is the following. "The simple conditional analysis says that x is disposed to give response r to stimulus s iff, were x to undergo s, x would give r. Applied here, the ornament is fragile iff, if it were struck, it would break.

System B is a fink. A fink is a standing condition which, were the stimulus about to occur, would remove the very disposition before it could manifest. So with System B armed, the ornament is fragile, yet if it were struck it would not break: the device would first make the glass non-fragile. This refutes the analysis, since the counterfactual 'if struck, it breaks' is false while the disposition is present. The notion of a fink is due to David Lewis, who introduced it, together with the electro-fink — the device that would alter a wire's live or dead state in the instant before the wire was touched — in his 1997 paper 'Finkish Dispositions'.

System A is a mask, the sort of thing Johnston called an antidote. The difference between fink and mask is one of timing: a fink acts before the disposition is triggered, a mask intervenes afterwards. The shield suspends the ornament's fragility for the duration of the impact and restores it once the danger has passed; during the impact itself, the ornament is not in fact fragile, which is why the counterfactual comes out false.

Since the analysis fails for System A no less than for System B, the moral is that fragility is not intrinsic to the glass: whether the ornament is fragile depends on its surroundings, namely on which safety system happens to be installed. Intrinsic duplicates could therefore differ in fragility, and the conditional analysis should be abandoned in favour of an account framed in terms of the glass's categorical basis."
