I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and I'm currently reviewing a popular book on philosophical paradoxes that devotes a chapter to Fitch's paradox of knowability. The author summarizes it roughly as follows: verificationism, the old positivist creed that every truth must in principle be verifiable, suffered a fatal blow when Fitch proved in a short paper that the doctrine entails that all truths are already known, an obvious absurdity. According to the book, Fitch constructed a cunning self-referential sentence, in the manner of Gödel, one that says of itself that it is true but will never be known; since such a sentence cannot be known without contradiction, there must exist at least one unknowable truth, so the principle of knowability refutes itself, and with it the entire verificationist program. My editor has asked me to flag anything a working philosopher would wince at, but my training amounts to a single undergraduate course in logic. Before I file the review, tell me how much of this summary would survive scrutiny: in at most 400 words, please separate what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification for lay readers, and what is straightforwardly wrong — I am particularly unsure whether the Gödel-style self-reference is actually part of the proof, and whether Fitch established that unknowable truths exist or only something conditional on there being unknown truths — and then tell me plainly whether the book's verdict, that the paradox destroyed verificationism rather than merely forcing its defenders to become more careful, survives your corrections.
