Until recently I treated the free will question as a seminar topic. That ended last spring, when my brother — a disciplined man who had never placed a bet in his life — was prescribed a dopamine agonist for restless legs syndrome, and within eight months had gambled away a large part of his savings. His neurologist was unsurprised: compulsive gambling of exactly this kind is a recognized side effect of this class of drug, and it typically recedes when the dose is lowered. My family has been arguing ever since about whether the man who emptied those accounts is the man we should hold responsible, and I have found myself defending the only position I ever found plausible: compatibilism of roughly Dennett's stripe, on which the free will worth having is not the power to originate actions from nowhere, but a cluster of developed capacities — foresight, self-control, responsiveness to reasons and to one's own future — whose intact functioning is what responsibility tracks, whatever its physical basis. I assumed this handled my brother's case cleanly: the drug compromised his self-control, so his responsibility was diminished, and it returns as the drug leaves his system. But my sister pressed an objection I could not answer on the spot. If a pill can do that, she said, then talk of intact functioning is just talk about brain chemistry, and the line we draw between the man before the prescription and the man after is a line between two drug states — drawn by us, for social convenience, rather than discovered. So I want an honest map of the view's exposure, not a defense of it. Tell me what would count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including developments that have arguably already occurred (results from addiction medicine and neuroscience, and the manipulation-style arguments that ask why an agent whose capacities are shaped by prior causes is any freer than one whose capacities are shaped by an intervening designer). For each, say whether committed compatibilists of this school would themselves concede it as counting against them or would absorb it, and assess frankly whether the view has been insulated by qualifications — whether anything could ever refute it, and whether that is a defect in a view of this kind. What I would find most persuasive is an answer that takes my sister's point head-on: does the view mark a principled difference between the two drug states, or only a useful one, and does the view have the resources to say which?
