For the past month my school's math club has been running an experiment that has turned into an argument none of us can settle, and I would like your help finding the flaw.

The experiment: we play a game (our advisor called it a "centipede" game) with real money from the club treasury. Two players sit across from each other. There is a pot on the table that starts at $1. On your turn you may either take the pot — in which case you keep 80% and your opponent gets the remaining 20% — or pass, in which case I add $2 to the pot and it becomes your opponent's turn. If both players pass through all six turns, they split the final pot of $12 evenly, $6 each. When we actually played, almost every pair passed at least three or four times, and several went to the end. But my friend Daniel thinks all of us were being irrational, and he has an argument I can't beat.

(1) Each player simply wants to walk away with as much money as possible.
(2) On the sixth and final turn, taking gives you $8.80 while passing gives you $6, so you take.
(3) Both players are intelligent, both know the rules, and both know these facts about each other — so whatever conclusion you can reach, you can be sure your opponent can reach it too.
(4) Therefore the player on the fifth turn knows the sixth-turn player will take, leaving her with $2.20; since taking now gets her $7.20, she takes.
(5) The same reasoning repeats backward turn by turn, so the very first player should take on turn one and walk away with $0.80.
(6) And the argument generalizes: in any interaction with a known final move — a repeated prisoner's dilemma with a fixed number of rounds, tipping a waiter on the last day of a vacation in a city you will never visit again — the same backward march says that self-interested people should never cooperate at all, from the very first round.
(7) But then two perfectly rational players end this game with $0.80 apiece, while two players who are merely trusting and a bit naive end up with $6 apiece. If our definition of rationality guarantees that rational people lose, and lose predictably, to somewhat irrational ones in every game of this shape, it looks like we have defined rationality wrong.

Each of steps (1) through (5) seems undeniable to me on its own, and (6) appears to be nothing more than the same procedure applied again. Yet I cannot accept that tipping a waiter on your last day, or passing once in our game, is a mistake. Which step should I reject, and what do I have to give up if I reject it? For what it's worth, I would be most comfortable denying (7) — maybe rationality really does just mean losing these games — but that feels less like an answer and more like agreeing to be poor.
