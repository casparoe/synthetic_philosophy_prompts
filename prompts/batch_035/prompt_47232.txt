I'm advising a small research institute that can fund, say, three or four projects for four years at the intersection of archaeology, paleoclimatology, and comparative history, and I want to use the money on the concept of societal "collapse" rather than on any single civilization. The field has several live open problems, and I'd like a defended prioritization among them, not a survey. Candidates include, roughly: (1) whether "collapse" is even a coherent unit of analysis, given that what archaeologists actually observe are discontinuities in settlement hierarchies, writing systems, administrative apparatuses, and material culture, while populations, languages, and local lifeways often persist — i.e., whether the field should be reoriented around "transformation" or "reorganization" instead; (2) how much of the explanatory work in particular cases (the Terminal Classic Maya droughts, the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean breakdowns, Easter Island) is done by climate versus by internal political fragility, trade-network interdependence, or post-hoc narrativization of patchy evidence; (3) the degree to which generalizing accounts — decline-of-complexity stories, overshoot-and-collapse stories — import value judgments about what counts as a loss and to whom; and (4) whether any of this research can responsibly inform modern concerns about contemporary societal fragility, or whether such extrapolation is mostly rhetorical. For each question you'd prioritize, explain why it sits upstream of the others, how tractable it is with the evidence base archaeology can actually generate (survey data, paleoclimate proxies, settlement pattern studies), and what kind of finding — from excavation, proxy records, or comparative databases — would most change its priority. Also say which of these questions you would deliberately deprioritize, and why the funding would do more good elsewhere.
