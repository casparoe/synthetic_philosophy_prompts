I hold that responsibility gaps for autonomous systems are real in a modest but important sense: when a machine-learning system causes harm in circumstances no designer foresaw and no operator could have averted in the moment, there are cases in which no one — not the engineers, not the deployer, not the user, not the firm as a collective — is blameworthy for that specific harm in the way we blame people for negligence. I'm not claiming victims should go uncompensated; compensation can be handled by strict liability or no-fault schemes. My claim is only that blame sometimes runs out, and that stretching it to cover the nearest person would be unfair rather than merely unconventional. Tell me what would count against this. Would it be a defeater if careful accident analyses always traced harm back to some negligent human decision? Are there general conditions for fair blame whose satisfaction a sufficiently careful engineering process could guarantee, so that gaps would close as a matter of practice? And give me an honest assessment of whether my "blame, not compensation" qualification has insulated the position from refutation — and, if so, whether that insulation is principled or a retreat. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, noting which committed holders of my view would concede as counting against it and which they would dismiss.
