I've been reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum since the Sequences were still coming out, and one debate in that corner of the internet has never resolved itself into anything either side would actually sign: whether iterated amplification and distillation can produce aligned superintelligent systems out of unaligned subhuman pieces. Camp A — call them the compositionality optimists — holds that oversight can be weaker than what it oversees, that humans plus decomposition plus time can supervise tasks beyond any single human's competence, and that distilling the amplified teacher into a faster student preserves the alignment the teacher had, so the process can be iterated to arbitrary capability. Camp B — the non-compositionalists — holds that alignment is not compositional: a system assembled from individually aligned parts, or distilled from a teacher whose alignment depended on being slow, human-legible, and under active supervision, inherits none of it. They point to mesa-optimization, deceptive alignment, reward misspecification, and gradient hacking as mechanisms by which decomposition quietly smuggles the original problem back in at a level where it is harder to see.

Write the joint statement these two camps could produce after a year of good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should contain, in order:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign — about what amplification and distillation are mechanically supposed to do; about which proposed failure modes are real rather than hypothetical; about what it does and does not entail for an overseer to be weaker than the overseen; and about what kinds of evidence would count against each camp's own position.

2. A disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated twice, once in wording the optimists would accept as a fair statement of their own view and once in wording the non-compositionalists would accept as a fair statement of theirs. If you cannot find such a pair of wordings for a disagreement, flag it — that is itself a finding of the collaboration.

3. The cruxes — the subquestions such that settling one would move at least one camp — and for each crux, the argument, experiment, or concrete case both camps agree would bear on it, with each camp's prediction written down in advance. At least one crux should be concrete and testable in a regime short of superintelligence (for example: when a weak overseer trains a stronger student on tasks whose ground truth becomes checkable only later, what does each camp predict about goal drift across successive distillation rounds, and where does the other camp think that prediction equivocates between behavioral indistinguishability and genuine internalization of the objective?).

4. A closing section in which the collaboration reports which residual disagreements it judged to be verbal — for instance, whether inheriting alignment means producing the same behavior, having the same internal objective, or remaining robust under distribution shift — and what each camp actually meant once the words were fixed.

Constraints: the agreed-claims section must not be vacuous — if a claim is signable only because it is trivially true or because it hides the disagreement in an ambiguous word, say so and drop it. Each camp's position must be given in its strongest form, the one the camp itself would endorse, not a strawman. And the document should read like something two people would put their names to after a year of arguing: careful, a little weary, and precise about what was settled, what was not, and who owes whom what evidence. Aim for roughly 1200 to 1500 words.
