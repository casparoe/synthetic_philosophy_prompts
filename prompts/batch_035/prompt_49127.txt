I run a little newsletter for art people (mostly gallery-goers, a few philosophy nerds) and I want to do one of those "which theory of art do you actually hold?" quizzes, but specifically about whether originals matter. Like, everyone SAYS they'd cry if they found out the Vermeer they loved for years was a forgery, but I suspect a lot of them would also say the aesthetic experience was identical while they loved it, and those two answers don't obviously fit together. So I want the quiz to sort them by what they say about cases, not by which label they like.

Please write me 8-10 questions, each a concrete case with 2-3 answer options, plus a scoring key at the end mapping answer patterns onto positions. Cases I'd like to see included (mix these with your own): a perfect duplicate swapped into a museum with nobody noticing; a misattributed painting re-attributed to a famous master with no change in how it looks; an assistant painting in the master's workshop under his supervision; the artist making an exact copy of their own earlier work; a forgery that critics praised for its delicate handling before being exposed. Questions must be answerable without knowing any theory — a case, then "does its value change when X happens?", roughly.

For the scoring key, build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names, so first say what the cutting questions are (does aesthetic value depend only on what can be experienced? does the causal origin matter, and if so the artist's intention or just the physical/artistic lineage? is forgery a fraud in a way that affects the aesthetic properties or only the market value?), then locate the actual positions in that space: the "if you can't tell the difference there isn't one" empiricist view, Goodman's weird middle position (perfect fakes can't exist, at most undiscovered fakes), the view that provenance and history of production are constitutive of aesthetic value, the Dutton-ish line that forgery is fraud on the artist and that this ruins what's expressed, and a deflationary view where originals matter only the way a first edition or a wedding ring matters — sentimental and economic, not aesthetic. Also note any cells in the space nobody occupies and whether that's because the combo is incoherent or just unappealing, and flag honestly which pairs of positions my quiz probably can't separate no matter how I phrase the cases (I suspect empiricism and Goodman's view are like that, but tell me if I'm wrong).

Also please don't make it feel like a philosophy exam — the readers should feel like they're being quizzed about their own taste, and only find out at the end that they've been holding a theory the whole time.
