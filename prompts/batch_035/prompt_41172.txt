As a journalist who covers mathematics and computer science for a general readership, I have followed the same argument for years now, and it never seems to go anywhere: the argument over whether the P versus NP problem might be unprovable from the standard axioms of set theory. The field has produced genuine formal results about the limits of proof techniques — oracle constructions showing that diagonalization-style arguments cannot settle the question, the natural proofs barrier showing that a broad and intuitively appealing class of circuit lower-bound arguments would collapse cryptographic assumptions, and a further barrier covering certain non-relativizing techniques — and yet the two camps on independence have not moved an inch. One side reads the accumulating barriers as inductive evidence that no proof we would recognize as a proof exists, and hence that P versus NP is independent of ZFC; the other side replies that the barriers only characterize the techniques mathematicians have happened to try, and that the history of mathematics is full of proofs that would have been dismissed as unimaginable a generation before they were found. Both camps seem to accept the same theorems and the same historical record, which makes the impasse puzzling to me as an observer. I am not asking you to adjudicate whether P versus NP is independent, or even to handicap it. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is this a substantive dispute about mathematics, a dispute about the epistemology of untried proofs — in effect, about whether induction over the space of possible arguments is legitimate reasoning — or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading, one about formal unprovability and one about the sociology and psychology of mathematical practice? What does each side's actual reasoning commit them to, and where exactly does it fail to engage the other's? And given your diagnosis, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a new barrier result, a proposed independence argument, a sharper distinction between 'unprovable in principle' and 'unprovable by any method we can currently characterize' — or an honest case that this particular question should be expected to remain undecidable in every sense that matters.
