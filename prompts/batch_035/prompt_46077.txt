I'm a software engineer, and for the last year I've been volunteering with a small nonprofit that does cost-effectiveness analysis for charities. The way they compare interventions that help different species is with what they call a "moral weights" table: a human's suffering counts as 1, a chicken's counts as something like 0.03, a shrimp's as 0.006, and so on. Where the numbers come from is a mix of expert surveys and some neuroscience-ish heuristics about neuron counts, but the analysts are upfront that underneath it all they're just morally uncertain — some of them think a chicken's suffering matters nearly as much as ours, others think it barely matters at all, and the number in the spreadsheet is basically an average across that disagreement. That average then gets fed into expected-value calculations that decide where millions of dollars actually go.

It struck me the other day that this is a pretty strange thing to do. When we're uncertain about facts, averaging makes obvious sense — if I'm 50/50 on whether a server has 32GB or 64GB of RAM, expected value is the right tool. But moral disagreement doesn't feel like that. If half the staff thinks factory farming is one of the worst things humans do and half thinks it's basically fine, is the "right" number really the midpoint? That would mean nobody on the staff actually endorses the figure that drives the decisions. And it seems like the spreadsheet quietly rules out people who refuse to play the game at all — someone who thinks you can't trade one person's interests against any number of chicken interests, or that certain things just don't go into a weighted sum no matter what.

So here's my question: what does this whole practice — collapsing moral disagreement into a weighted average and then optimizing over it — actually presuppose? What view of moral uncertainty does it need to be legitimate, which rival views does it rule out just by existing as a spreadsheet, and could the nonprofit keep its current setup while staying genuinely neutral between, say, "moral uncertainty is literally just like factual uncertainty, so averaging is correct" versus "the average is only a pragmatic compromise, a rough patch on our ignorance"? Relatedly, I want to know where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, i.e. where several incompatible underlying theories would still license the exact same table. Please keep it accessible — I have no philosophy backround, just a lot of arguments at lunch.

At the very end, give me your final answer in machine-readable form: a single line reading \answer{...} with exactly one of three labels — "committed" if the practice strictly presupposes expected-value aggregation over moral theories, "underdetermined" if several incompatible theories of moral uncertainty would license it, or "incoherent" if no single theory could justify it — followed by one short clause saying why.
