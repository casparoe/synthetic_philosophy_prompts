Everyone tells me the sunk cost fallacy is the clearest fallacy in the book, but when I go through my gut reactions case by case, they don't line up the way one clean rule would require, and I'd like help working out the principle behind them, if there is one.

Walking out of a movie I paid for and am hating: obviously fine, since the money is gone either way. Uninstalling a video game forty hours in that stopped being fun months ago: also fine — I feel silly about the forty hours, but not guilty. A friend six years into a PhD who no longer wants to be an academic: I keep telling him he should finish, since he's come this far — and I can see this is exactly the reasoning I reject everywhere else, yet I can't shake the feeling that it's right. A government continuing a war so that soldiers already killed won't have died in vain: clearly wrong, and worse than wrong. Staying in a three-year relationship only because leaving would mean the three years were wasted: a real mistake, not just an error of arithmetic. My mother refusing to sell our family's failing restaurant because of the ten years she put into it: I think she should sell, but I admire her for not wanting to, in a way I don't admire anyone for the war reasoning. And the case I can't fit with the movie one: abandoning a 700-page novel at page 500 because I'm not enjoying it. I've done this exactly once, and it felt like quitting in a way that walking out of the movie didn't, though both are just past, unrefundable costs with no further payoff from staying.

Is there a principle that saves all of these verdicts, or close? If not, which should I give up, and what simpler principle do I get in exchange? I'd also like to know what the principle implies for a case I haven't decided: a climber halfway up a mountain, tired and behind schedule, whose group leader keeps insisting they've come too far to turn back.
