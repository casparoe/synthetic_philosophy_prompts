My flatmate and I took a seminar together last term on the evolution of cooperation, mostly built around the iterated prisoner's dilemma and Axelrod's work, and he's been pressing an argument on me ever since that I can't find my way out of. It goes like this. (1) In an indefinitely repeated prisoner's dilemma, once a reciprocating strategy like Tit-for-Tat is common in a population, no purely defecting strategy can invade it, provided the players value future payoffs enough — the shadow of the future is long. (2) In the tournaments and the evolutionary simulations that followed, nice, retaliating, forgiving strategies like Tit-for-Tat beat the more cunning entrants over the long run, and even survive occasional mistakes once you make the game noisy. (3) A strategy's long-run evolutionary success against a field of rivals is a reliable guide to what it is rational for an individual to do, since rationality just is doing what succeeds against the competition one actually faces. (4) Human life is made up almost entirely of repeated interaction: even a seemingly one-off exchange with a stranger is embedded in reputation, social networks, and species-wide patterns of reciprocity, so there is effectively no true one-shot case for us. (5) Therefore defection is never rational for a human being: to defect is to play the strategy that any well-adapted population eventually eliminates. He concludes, for instance, that a trader who knows he will never meet his counterparty again still rationally ought to cooperate, and that the familiar endgame defection you see when people give notice at a job or approach the end of a relationship is just straightforward irrationality, an atavism that evolution hasn't fully cleaned up.

The conclusion seems to me to prove too much: whistleblowing, informing on a criminal conspiracy, cutting one's losses on a business partner who is himself quietly defecting, and the last round of a fixed-term contract all look like cases where defecting is not merely permitted but obviously sensible, and I don't want to bite those bullets. Yet each premise, read on its own, sounds fine — I even defended versions of (1) and (3) in my seminar presentation. When I complained that Tit-for-Tat can't even get a foothold in a population of defectors without some clustering to start things off, he said the argument doesn't depend on how cooperation first arises, only on what's stable and successful once it's there, which made me worry I was objecting to the wrong thing entirely.

Tell me which step to reject, why it looked so plausible, and what rejecting it costs: what the standard positions on rationality in the iterated prisoner's dilemma — backward-induction reasoning, the folk theorem, the evolutionary treatment — each have to pay to deny it, and what, if anything, survives of my flatmate's evolutionary story once the argument is repaired. If it helps you aim your answer: I'd much rather give up (4) than (3), since (3) is the premise my whole presentation leaned on, but I've come to suspect that preference is a bias rather than a judgment.
