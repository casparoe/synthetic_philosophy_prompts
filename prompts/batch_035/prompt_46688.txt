I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on jurisprudence, and I've hit a wall with the retributivism vs consequentialism thing. Instead of reading another paper I decided to just go through cases and record my gut verdicts, and now I have a list I can't make sense of. Help me out: is there a principle (or a combo) that captures these, or do I have to throw some of my verdicts out? I'd rather know the honest answer than be flattered.

Here's the list. For each, note what my gut says:

1. Two people commit identical vandalism on the same night. One gets caught, one never does. I'm fine with the caught one being punished, and I don't think the state owes the uncaught one anything, but I bristle when a judge seems to punish the caught one extra hard to "compensate" for the partner's escape. My gut: desert is per-person, not per-pair.

2. Attempted murder where the gun jams vs. completed murder where it fires — same intent, same act, pure luck decides. My gut verdict here is genuinely unstable. When I look at the two defendants I want the shooter to get more; when I say it out loud I also want to insist that moral luck can't determine desert, and I can't hold both at once.

3. A dictator is put on trial 40 years later, now elderly and demented, barely connected psychologically to the man who gave the orders. Punish him anyway, full stop. But an ordinary criminal who has genuinely reformed by sentencing time? I go soft, maybe too soft. Everyone tells me these two can't both be right and I can't see why not.

4. Speeding fines deliberately set high because detection is spotty, so the *expected* cost of speeding comes out right. Totally fine with me, seems like flat-out consequentialism about enforcement, whatever my official view is. But scapegoating a known-innocent person to stop a riot — even when the numbers are strictly better — is horrifying to me, and it's not clear to me why the line falls where it does, since both are "use the convict as an instrument."

5. Standard practice of punishing attempts less than completions: fine. But I'm also fine with punishing conspiracy and possession before any harm exists, which seems like it should be hard for a desert view to swallow, and I've never felt the tension until I wrote it down.

6. Punishing someone at the top of the range to "send a message" — hate it, feels like pure using. But I accept plea-bargain discounts that exist basically for docket efficiency, which looks like consequentialism smuggled in through the back door, and my gut doesn't object at all.

7. Defendants with 3 months to live getting suspended sentences on compassionate grounds: fine by me, feels almost obvious. But note this cuts against strict desert — dying doesn't reduce what you did.

My tentative fix has been something like: desert sets a ceiling and a floor, and within that band consequentialist stuff gets to operate freely. I can't get that to give me a clean verdict on 2 or 4, though, and it says nothing about whether the band is wide or narrow, which is basically the whole question.

So: is there one principle that captures all this, or close to it? If not, which verdicts do I drop, and why those rather than the others? Please also say what the resulting principle predicts for a case I didn't list — say, a defendant charged at once for a string of crimes he committed undetected over 20 years, where each crime is past the statute of limitations but the last one isn't, and the prosecutor wants the sentencing judge to "take the rest into account." I want to know if my principle handles that or just reveals another seam.

Also, do me a favor: at the end, tell me how confident you are in the overall reading of my verdicts, and single out the point you're least sure of. I'd rather get a flagged guess than a confident smoothing-over.
