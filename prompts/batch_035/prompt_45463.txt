I teach an upper-level seminar on animal ethics and conservation philosophy, and I am setting the final exam: three hours, one essay. Please write me a single essay question on whether habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering, together with a model answer and a marking scheme totalling 20 marks.

I want the question built around a dispute I have constructed for the exam, so that students cannot pass by reciting lecture notes. The dispute is as follows. Early-successional and disturbed habitats are dominated by r-selected species — enormous reproductive output, near-total juvenile mortality, and hence (if a creature's lifetime welfare is dominated by outcomes early in life) plausibly net-negative lives on average. Mature climax communities, by contrast, hold smaller standing populations of longer-lived, K-selected animals with lower per-capita early death. One student, whom the question should call N., argues that because total wild animal welfare is negative and abundance tracks total suffering, converting rich habitat to barren land — pavement, exhausted mine tailings, desertification — reduces total suffering. Call this the Barren-Benefit thesis. A second student, F., replies that the very same premises show that destroying mature forest and leaving it to regenerate is terrible, since regrowth produces decades of r-selected abundance; call this the Regeneration Objection. The TA who will co-mark the exam insists that the N–F dispute is purely ecological and will be settled by field data.

The question as printed on the paper should ask students to assess the TA's claim, and in doing so to determine what would have to be true, both empirically and normatively, for N. and F. to be disagreeing about anything — and whether they can both be right, both be wrong, or only one of them be right. The question must be self-contained and no longer than 180 words; define r- and K-selection in a clause, since the course presupposes only introductory ecology.

The model answer, at most 600 words, should be something a strong student could plausibly produce under exam conditions — not a literature review, and with no citations or reading lists. At minimum it must: separate the empirical premise about the sign of average welfare from the normative premise about how lives are to be aggregated; explain how logic-of-the-larder reasoning makes the sign of average welfare do all the work, and why that sign is epistemically fragile; bring in the non-identity problem, since N.'s conclusion that fewer animals would be better requires treating the prevention of bad lives as an improvement; distinguish the destruction of habitat from the identity of whatever replaces it, which is where N. and F. genuinely part ways; and take a position on whether any quantity of field data could settle the dispute, which is what the TA's claim amounts to.

The marking scheme, no more than 250 words, should be built around five must-hit points worth 4 marks each, each with a one-line note on how students typically fail it. It should also name the single move that separates an A answer from a B answer — my guess is that it is the recognition that the aggregation principle, not the ecology, is what makes N.'s and F.'s arguments rise or fall together, but as the setter you should decide that for yourself. Keep the entire deliverable under 1,000 words.
