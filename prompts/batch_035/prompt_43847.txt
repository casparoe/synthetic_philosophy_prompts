My friend Priya and I play this game at the bar after journal club that we've never managed to end: alive or not alive, and you have to answer fast, no hedging. She's scary good at it, and over the last year her track record has gotten long enough that I've started to wonder if there's an actual rule underneath her snap judgments or if she's just winging it each time. So far she's said: the flu virus, not alive; a mimivirus-type giant virus, still not alive, even after I pointed out it has genes for translation; a mitochondrion sitting inside a cell, not alive; a bacterial endospore that's been dormant for thousands of years, alive, and when I pushed her she said if it can wake up and start dividing again then it never stopped counting; a mule, alive; a worker honeybee, alive; HeLa cells in a dish, alive; the self-replicating RNA molecules from those origins-of-life experiments where you add replicase and let selection shrink them, not alive; frost crystals and candle flames, obviously not.

Last night I tried to trap her with three harder ones and she went quiet and said she'd need to think, which felt like a win. (1) Rickettsia-type bacteria -- they have their own DNA, ribosomes, and cell membrane, and divide by fission, but they can't make their own ATP and have to steal energy and a bunch of other things from the host cell, so they can only exist inside one. (2) A prion -- no metabolism, no genome, but it templates its own replication and there are distinct strains that get selected for, so in some defintely wierd sense it participates in evolution. (3) A paramecium that was alive an hour ago but got fixed with preservative five minutes ago: every structure is intact, but the metabolism is stopped forever and no conditions will ever restart it.

I want you to figure out what's going on with her. Give me a prediction on each of the three, but what I really care about is the underlying rule (or rules) that could generate her whole track record, since I suspect more than one fits, and whether those rules come apart on Rickettsia or the prion or the dead paramecium, or whether I need to come up with yet another case to force them apart -- and if so, tell me what that case would be. She's not a philosopher, so whatever rule she's running is probably some biologist's implicit version of a real position in the what-is-life literature (metabolic autonomy, life as an evolving cellular lineage, autopoiesis, whatever else fits), and I'd like to know which of those her verdicts actually commit her to, not just which one sounds nice. And please be honest about where you could be wrong: which of the three predictions you're least confident in, and what would have to happen -- some verdict of hers on some case, or some consideration about one of these rules -- that would make you abandon your best guess about the rule she's running and go with a different one. I plan to spring that case on her next Friday, so if your fallback is "she'd say X because she's really running theory Y," I want to know that in advance rather than after the arguement goes sideways.
