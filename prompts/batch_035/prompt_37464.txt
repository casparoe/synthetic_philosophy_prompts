I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training beyond what leaks into ML blog posts, and somewhere over the last few years I've quietly settled into holding a version of the language of thought hypothesis. My version is probably unorthodox, so let me state it as precisely as I can. I take the brain, and by extension any sufficiently general learner, to represent the world in an internal system of symbols that is compositional, that has a syntax in some defensible sense, and over which mental operations are defined — so that thinking is literally a kind of computation over structured internal states, not merely an assortment of learned input-output mappings. Working with transformers has pushed me further: I'm inclined to say that when a large model learns to add numbers or track a chess board, the activations inside it are playing the role of the mentalese expressions, and that this is evidence that a language of thought is what general-purpose learning systems converge on, not an accident of human neurology. I find the alternative picture — that all there is to cognition is learned associations plus vector similarity, with compositionality as a useful fiction we project onto the behavior — uncongenial, but I'm aware that uncongenial is not an argument.

I'd like a serious map of what would count against my position, and I'd like it written for someone like me rather than for a philosophy seminar. Concretely, I want the following, as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a long letter. First, what observations or arguments would genuinely threaten the view: not just the standard objections I've half-absorbed through osmosis, but the specific things — experimental results, modeling failures, conceptual arguments, possible cases — that should make me revise or abandon it, sorted by how decisive each would be, from fatal through serious to merely awkward. Second, since my version leans on machine learning, I want an honest treatment of the analogy itself: which findings about how transformers and similar systems actually represent things would cut against the claim that their internal states form a compositional code, and which would be neutral or supportive. Third, where exactly is the line between a version of the view that risks something and a version that has been qualified into saying nothing — I suspect my own habit of saying the internal symbols don't have to look like logic, just be structured in some way, may be quietly moving me across that line, and I want to know whether I'm right to worry, and if so, what commitment I should give up to get back to saying something. Fourth, which of the defeaters you list would a committed defender of the view, in its more orthodox and more demanding formulations, actually concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss, and whether the dismissals would be principled or merely defensive. And finally, a candid verdict: on balance, how exposed is the position I've described, and if you were in my position, which single development or argument would you watch for as the thing most likely to change your mind. I care more about the reasoning being sound than about the view surviving, so don't soften the case against it to spare my feelings; the whole point of my asking is that I can't reliably find the strongest opposing considerations myself.
