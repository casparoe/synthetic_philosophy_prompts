I'm a high school student working on an essay about the School of Names, the group of ancient Chinese thinkers from the Warring States period who delighted in arguments about names, reference, and paradox. The text at the center of my essay is Gongsun Long's dialogue in which he defends the claim that a white horse is not a horse, apparently much to the exasperation of his interlocutor, who keeps insisting that a white horse obviously is a horse. I've read several secondary discussions, but I've noticed that commentators tend to split into two camps, and in the articles I've found these camps mostly argue past each other. Before I commit to a position in my essay, I'd like to see the two camps genuinely confront one another.

Would you write a dialogue between two interpreters, one defending Gongsun Long and one attacking him? The defender should hold roughly this view: the claim is not about individual animals at all but about terms. The phrase "white horse" and the single word "horse" do not cover the same range of things, and Gongsun Long demonstrates this with the asymmetry of requests — if someone asks for a horse, a yellow or a black one will satisfy them, whereas if someone asks for a white horse, neither will. This reveals that the two expressions pick out different classes, so the startling claim merely states, in compressed form, that what the phrase applies to is narrower than what the bare word applies to. The attacker should reply that this renders the doctrine either trivial or dishonest: read as ordinary predication, it is plainly false, since every white horse is a horse, and the argument only appears to work because it slides between saying that the two expressions do not cover exactly the same things and saying that a white horse is not a horse at all — a slide the poor interlocutor keeps protesting. If possible, have them also weigh a third possibility my textbook raises: that because classical Chinese nouns behave more like mass nouns, "horse" may name a kind of stuff rather than a set of individual animals, in which case the dispute is really about whether combining horse-stuff with white-stuff yields something that still counts as horse-stuff. My textbook also mentions Gongsun Long's related puzzle about hardness and whiteness in a stone — that you never perceive the hardness and the whiteness at the same time, since you either see the stone or feel it — and I'd be pleased if they could use that as a test case for the defender's more general view that combined attributes create something new.

Please make sure both interpreters are equally sharp: the dialogue should contain real back-and-forth, with each side responding to the other's actual objections, making the occasional concession, and then pressing a further point, rather than delivering prepared monologues. Let it end without a tidy resolution — I'm less interested in a winner than in seeing where the strongest pressure on each reading lies.
