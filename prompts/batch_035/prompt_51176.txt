Two of my friends got into a long argument at a bbq last weekend about whether the brain is literally a computer, and nobody could settle it. I want someone to untangle the whole exchange move by move.

Some background so it makes sense: Dave (he does ML engineering) had claimed that obviously the brain is a computer — neurons take inputs, do something like a weighted sum, and fire outputs, and that's computation, full stop. Maya (a philosophy grad student) disagreed, and it went like this:

(1) Maya said that if you define computation that loosely, everything becomes a computer. She brought up Searle's old point that a wall, at the molecular level, is undergoing all sorts of complex physical changes, and if you're free to pick whatever mapping you like between physical states and computational states, you can describe the wall as running Word, or any program you want. On that definition, saying the brain is a computer becomes unfalsifiable trivia.

(2) Dave answered that this is a strawman — nobody picks arbitrary mappings. A real computer is a system whose physical dynamics track the formal structure of the program: the voltage patterns causally support the right state transitions under counterfactuals. If you flip a bit in memory, downstream behavior changes the way the program says it should. That's the difference between a wall and a PC, and on that test brains plausibly qualify.

(3) Maya said fine, but try that test on digestion instead of cognition. Is the stomach a computer running a program for breaking down proteins? The gut also has causal structure that supports counterfactuals — inject lipase at one point and the downstream chemistry differs accordingly. Dave would say no, she continued, because there's no interpretation under which the gut's states are symbols being manipulated toward some purpose. But "symbol" and "purpose" are observer-relative — they live in the interpreter, not the physics. Same for neurons: a spike isn't about anything until someone interprets it.

(4) Dave replied that he doesn't need symbols that are "about" things in some deep sense, just the difference between carrying information and merely undergoing change. The gut does chemistry; it doesn't encode anything. The visual cortex, by contrast, encodes retinal contrast in its spike rates in a way that downstream areas decode, and that's a measurable functional difference, not an observer's opinion — you can find out which animals can't tell vertical stripes from horizontal ones by lesioning them, so the "interpretation" is doing real causal work.

(5) Maya granted that the brain processes information in the everyday sense, which nobody denies. Her target, she said, is the strong claim: that cognition is literally software, such that in principal you could run the same program on silicon and get the same mind. That needs more than encoding — it needs the physical medium to be irrelevant, and she didn't see how "brains carry information" gets you there. A simulation of a hurricane doesn't get anybody wet.

Dave rolled his eyes at that and said a simulation of a calculator does calculate, and that minds are more like calculators than hurricanes. Then the grill run out of gas and the thing dissolved.

Please go through the exchange step by step and say who's right about what at each move — including, where it happens, which moves are talking past each other rather than actually clashing — and tell me who comes out more right overall. And if there's a distinction or consideration both of them missed that would actually move the argument forward, I'd like to hear it.
