I'd like a philosophical reading of the television series Severance (Apple TV+, created by Dan Erickson) through Marx's account of alienated labor, and I want it to resist the obvious mapping long enough to say where the mapping fails.

For context, in case it helps frame the analysis: the show is set at Lumon Industries, whose employees in the Macrodata Refinement division undergo a medical procedure that splits their consciousness in two. The "innie" exists only at work, with no memories of an outside life; the "outie" lives the rest of life and agrees to be unconscious for the workday. The innies spend their days sorting streams of numbers into on-screen bins, without ever learning what the numbers mean or what the work produces. Their incentives are trivial perks — waffle parties, finger traps, portrait rewards — administered alongside a corporate culture that reveres the founder, Kier Eagan, with an intensity somewhere between employee-of-the-month kitsch and liturgy. One refiner, Helly R., repeatedly attempts escape and self-harm; another, Mark S., took the job in part because severance promises relief from grief for his dead wife, since the innie cannot remember her.

Here is what I'd like the reading to do:

1. Work through Marx's four species of alienation — from the product of labor, from the labor process itself, from species-being, and from other workers — and say, for each, whether Severance literalizes it, complicates it, or dissolves it. The show seems almost too neat: work whose product is deliberately opaque, work performed under total surveillance and procedure, colleagues who are strangers with no shared life outside, and so on. Is the show dramatizing Marx or merely decorating him with him already true?

2. But then take seriously the ways the procedure differs from alienation as Marx describes it. Marx treats alienation as a condition suffered by a whole person whose labor is estranged from them; severance creates the estrangement by splitting the person, and it is voluntary — the outie contracts into it, often to escape suffering, as Mark does. Can a condition be alienating in Marx's sense if the sufferer doesn't exist until the condition does, and if the person who consents never experiences it? Relatedly: Marx thought alienation could in principle be overcome through a different organization of labor. Does severance, by making alienation surgically permanent and individually chosen, represent something Marx's framework can't quite name — perhaps the transformation of alienation from an economic condition into a consumer product?

3. Consider Helly R.'s resistance. Is she best read through Marx's class struggle — the worker's revolt against capital — or does the fact that she is in effect a person created by and for the corporation, with no life to reclaim outside it, break the analogy? What is she fighting for, in Marxist terms, when there is no unalienated life her labor is being taken from?

4. Finally, address Lumon's culture: the wellness sessions, the Kier Eagan veneration, the perk system as compensation for meaningless work. Marx has things to say about religion and about how capital substitutes pale satisfactions for the goods it strips away. Does the show's satire add anything to that critique, or does it just illustrate it?

I'm writing this for an essay on whether contemporary science fiction can do original work in the philosophy of labor, so I'm less interested in the show as an illustration of Marx than in the places where the two strain against each other. Please conclude with a judgment: does Severance expose a blind spot in the theory of alienation, or does the theory, applied honestly, explain the show down to its smallest details — and what would count as evidence for each answer?
