I am a recently retired fisheries population ecologist who has, for the last few years, been making up for a life spent on logistic growth curves and stock assessments by reading philosophy — some ethics, a great deal of Parfit. I co-lead a monthly discussion group at our town's lifelong-learning institute: eleven members, mostly retired professionals, three of us with biological training, none with formal philosophical background. This winter we worked through the repugnant conclusion and the main reactions to it, and I discovered a problem I did not anticipate: when a case is presented as a comparison between two populations of lives, several of us — myself very much included — answer as ecologists rather than as axiologists. We want to know the carrying capacity, whether the larger population will overshoot and crash, whether the extra lives are sustainable at that welfare level. These are good questions for fish and irrelevant, I take it, to the axiology, but they contaminate our answers anyway.

I would like you to write a diagnostic quiz for the group, titled something like "Which population axiology do you actually hold?" — a sorting instrument, not a teaching document, though a one-paragraph introduction suitable for our members is welcome. Please meet the following specifications.

First, ten questions, each a concrete case with two to four answer options. No question may ask about a position by name or near-name, and no case may simply restate the repugnant conclusion or the mere addition argument in the standard way; the cases must be varied enough that a member cannot pattern-match to a known argument after question three. Where a comparison may reasonably be judged indeterminate or incommensurable, offer that as an explicit option rather than forcing a choice.

Second, build the quiz from the logical space, not from a list of positions. That is: first identify the separable commitments that actually divide the field — whether adding a life worth living makes a world better, other things equal, when no one else is affected; whether welfare aggregates additively across persons; whether losses to existing people are weighed differently from gains and losses to merely possible people; whether there is a critical level of welfare below which additions make things worse even though positive; whether any amount of quality can be compensated by quantity, or vice versa. Then derive the questions from these commitments, and only afterwards locate the named positions in the resulting answer space. The scoring key should map answer patterns onto totalist views, average views, critical-level views, variable-value views, person-affecting restrictions, the asymmetry, and lexical or perfectionist views, plus the "no amount of merely adequate life compensates for lost excellence" family.

Third, at least two questions should be constructed so that a population-dynamics intuition points somewhere none of the named axiologies can accommodate — for example, a case where the intuitive badness of a population seems to come from its trajectory rather than its level, as with a boom-and-bust population whose peak welfare is identical to a stable one's. The scoring key should say explicitly what to do with members whose answers to these questions fit no leaf, since I expect several of ours to land there.

Fourth, flag honestly which pairs of positions the quiz cannot separate and why — I have read enough to suspect that critical-level and variable-value views agree on nearly every pairwise comparison a questionnaire can pose — and note any answer combinations that are coherent but unoccupied by any defended position, with a sentence on whether the emptiness is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

Finally, apply the quiz to one hypothetical member: a retired colleague of mine who spent her career on collapse dynamics in overfished stocks and now insists that any population "above replacement" is presumptively too large regardless of welfare. Tell me which leaf she lands on, or whether she is an unoccupied combination, and how you would score her. I intend to pilot the quiz at our March meeting, so I need it usable on paper, without any member needing to look anything up mid-quiz.
