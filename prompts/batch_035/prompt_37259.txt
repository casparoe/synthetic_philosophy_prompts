My credence that evidential cooperation in large worlds should play a nontrivial role in an effective altruist's career choice — say, in decisions about which cause area to enter or which skills to build, as opposed to merely adding a tiebreaker between otherwise equal options — is currently about 0.35, while a colleague with similar background knowledge sits near 0.05. Please give me your own credence, but more importantly decompose it into the terms that generate the number. I would suggest, at minimum, roughly these: (a) the probability that our universe is large enough in the relevant sense — via inflationary cosmology, many-worlds, or anything else — that causally inaccessible civilizations with roughly human-scale decision-making exist; (b) the probability that some decision theory under which conditioning on your own action is a legitimate input to decision-making is correct, so that your choice genuinely is evidence about what your distant counterparts choose; (c) the probability that the agents you thereby coordinate with have value systems sufficiently aligned with yours that mutual cooperation is positive-sum rather than an accidental subsidy to opposed values; (d) the probability that the aggregate gains from such cooperation are large relative to the direct causal impact of the same career decision; and (e) any correction you think is warranted for the fact that ECL's prescriptions may be extremely sensitive to the fine structure of the situation — for instance, that agents conditioning on cooperation also includes agents conditioning on defection, or that the recommendable set of actions may be unstable under reflection on the recommendation itself. Assign a rough probability to each term, justify each in a sentence or two, show how they combine into your final figure, and then address two further questions. First, which term do you think most likely explains the gap between my 0.35 and my colleague's 0.05 — and is that gap more plausibly a factual disagreement about (a) or (d), or a decision-theoretic disagreement about (b)? Second, note that ECL is often claimed to have a self-referential feature: the expected value of acting on ECL considerations may itself depend on your credence in ECL, since distant agents presumably condition on how you handle exactly this kind of uncertainty. Does that consideration push you to revise your number after computing it, and if so, how do you avoid the regress? I am less interested in whether the decomposition comes out to your exact number than in seeing which terms carry the weight and which you would honestly mark as guesses.
