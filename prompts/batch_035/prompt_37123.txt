I teach an upper-level seminar on institutions and collective choice — 24 students, one 90-minute session, a room with movable desks and a projector. Design an in-class simulation that makes students feel the central bet of futarchy: that a market in predictions about a policy's consequences aggregates what people know, while a vote aggregates what people want — and that these two can come apart in the same room, on the same question, in the same hour.

The policy at stake should be something the class can actually enact for the rest of the term — a real decision with real consequences for them, such as whether the final exam becomes a take-home or whether weekly reading responses become optional. Run both institutions in the same session: first a straight vote on the policy, then a play-money prediction market on a sharply defined outcome conditional on the policy's adoption, with the live price displayed for all to see while they trade. Assign a handful of secret roles before the session — at least one trader with a large budget and an incentive to move the price rather than to be right, and a few insiders whose private behavioural choices partly determine the outcome the market is pricing — and reveal what these roles were doing only in the debrief.

Deliverables: the exact policy question and outcome variable (chosen so that the outcome is measurable within the term), rules and materials assuming no real money and nothing more exotic than index cards or a shared spreadsheet, the token economy and what tokens are worth when the market resolves, a minute-by-minute timeline, the text of the secret role cards, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the futarchist claim that citizens should vote on values but bet on beliefs. The debrief should also stage the strongest objection the class will have just lived through — manipulation, self-fulfilling prices, traders whose interests are entangled with the outcome they are pricing — and say what their experience of that objection does and does not show about futarchy at national scale. Finally, be honest about which feature of futarchy no classroom simulation can reproduce however cleverly it is designed, and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. Please write the whole thing in British English.
