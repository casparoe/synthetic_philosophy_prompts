I referee arguments for a living — quality control at a food plant — but I have no training for this one, and it has been running between two friends of mine for three weeks now. None of the three of us went to university; we're just people who think about this stuff on our own, and I've reached the limit of what I can work out unaided. If you have knowledge that we don't, I'd be grateful if you'd bring it to bear.

It started when Theo mentioned reading about a small country where a currency collapse had wiped out the savings of the richest tenth of the population while leaving everyone else exactly where they were. He called it a silver lining: the country was now more equal, and equality is a good thing in itself, so the collapse made the country better in at least one respect. Maya thought that was absurd, and things escalated. Here is the actual sequence of moves, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Theo: removing inequality is like removing a stain from an otherwise ruined shirt. The shirt is still ruined, but it is better in that one respect; likewise a country can be better in the one respect of being more equal even though no one in it is better off.

(2) Maya: a respect of improvement that nobody can live, feel, or use is a bookkeeping fiction; goodness has to bottom out in how lives go. Then she asked whether he'd say a world where everyone is equally blind is in one respect better than a world where only some are blind — because if inequality is bad in itself, he seems committed to that, and she can't take anyone seriously who accepts it.

(3) Theo, taking the challenge head-on: perhaps what's bad about inequality isn't the gap on paper but what the gap does to lives. The poor feel humiliated, are looked down on, lose political voice, and even the bare knowledge of being at the bottom corrodes a person. In his collapse scenario, all of that is swept away too, so his point survives in a more careful form.

(4) Maya: fine, but now the badness is entirely a matter of effects on how lives go — shame, powerlessness, health — and any straightforward consequentialist can sign onto that. What's left of caring about equality as such? She then pressed the blind world again: if you could somehow erase everyone's awareness of who can and cannot see, would the remaining inequality still be bad? If he says no, the gap itself was never the evil; if he says yes, he's back to the bookkeeping fiction.

(5) Theo: what's left is unfairness. Where the rich–poor difference traces to circumstances of birth rather than anything anyone chose or did, that unfairness is bad even if nobody ever feels a thing. Equality is just the visible face of fairness.

(6) Maya: she thinks what people actually care about, when they care about inequality, is priority: the same euro in the hands of a beggar matters more than the same euro in the hands of a millionaire, so lifting the worst off has moral weight that lifting the well off doesn't. You can believe that wholeheartedly while thinking gaps are irrelevant in themselves. And she pressed him: if a policy made the worst off slightly better off while making the gap astronomically larger, would he really refuse it?

(7) Theo: he asked her where the extra weight on the beggar's euro comes from, if not from the conviction that it's bad for some to be worse off than others — which is his own view in different clothes. And if the weights are just a pragmatic device, she owes a story for why they bend exactly as they do; otherwise her position is moral fashion with a calculator attached.

(8) Maya answered that his fairness talk has the same hole: fair compared to what? Unless he smuggles in a preferred pattern of outcomes, saying the birth lottery was unfair is just saying things could have gone otherwise.

Neither of them will budge, and I honestly cannot tell who is winning, or whether the whole fight rests on both of them meaning different things by the same words. Please adjudicate. Before writing anything, think through at least three distinct ways one might understand what is bad about inequality and keep them in view as you score the moves, since the verdicts may differ depending on which one each friend is implicitly relying on.

Structure your reply as follows:

1. "The real question" (about 150 words): identify precisely what disagreement is actually running underneath the exchange, and say whether some of the moves are talking past each other.
2. "Move-by-move verdicts": take the eight moves in order. For each, rule it basically right, basically wrong, or right in part, with a sentence or two of justification. Don't average them into mush — issue actual verdicts.
3. "What both are missing" (about 200 words): the considerations neither of them raised that someone who has thought hard about this would put on the table, stated in plain language I can relay back to them.
4. "Bottom line" (one short paragraph): who comes out more right overall, and what each of them should concede to the other.

Take the role of a professional moral philosopher who has spent a career on theories of equality and its rivals, and evaluate the arguments on their merits rather than pattern-matching to who sounds generous or harsh — as far as I can tell, both of them are defending positions with serious philosophical standing, and neither is a strawman.
