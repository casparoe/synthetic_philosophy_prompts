I'm a science-and-ideas correspondent at a national daily, and I also sit on our standards committee, which rules on corrections and sets editorial policy for the newsroom. Last month our health desk ran a front-page feature crediting a hospital network's new sepsis protocol with a dramatic improvement: network-wide mortality fell from 12.0 percent to 8.0 percent after the protocol was introduced. This week a biostatistician wrote in to point out what the underlying figures show. The network consists of two hospitals. Before the protocol, the large tertiary hospital treated 600 sepsis patients with 15.0 percent mortality, and the small community hospital treated 200 patients with 3.0 percent mortality; together, 800 patients and 12.0 percent. After the protocol — which also changed triage so that far more patients were routed to the community hospital — the tertiary hospital treated 400 patients at 16.0 percent mortality and the community hospital 800 patients at 4.0 percent; together, 1,200 patients and 8.0 percent. So within each hospital, mortality rose slightly, and the celebrated aggregate improvement is an artifact of case mix. Our health editor's defense is that the network-wide rate is the number that matters to readers deciding where care is delivered, and that any disaggregation can be sliced to tell an opposing story; several colleagues retort that the hospital-level numbers are the real ones and we printed an effect that reversed on inspection. The committee meets Tuesday and must decide two things: what, if anything, we print as a correction, and whether to adopt a standing rule for when an aggregate claim requires a check for reversal across relevant subgroups before publication. Draft the decision memo. It should give a concrete recommendation on both questions — with criteria a reporter without statistical training can actually apply on deadline — and it should engage seriously with the strongest argument for standing by the aggregate figure, including the worry that demanding stratification for every aggregate statistic invites both paralysis and cherry-picking of whichever subgroup framing flatters a preferred conclusion.
