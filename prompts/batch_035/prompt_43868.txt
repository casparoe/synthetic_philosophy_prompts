Two weeks from now I defend, before my department's joint seminar in decision theory and economic methodology, a paper arguing that Keynes's views on probability and uncertainty still constitute a live problem for orthodox Bayesian decision theory rather than a historical curiosity. My sketch is this: in the Treatise on Probability (1921), Keynes allowed that probabilities may be non-numerical — comparable at best via intervals and orderings, not points — and introduced the weight of an argument, the amount of relevant evidence bearing on a probability, as something that can vary independently of the probability itself; in his later reflections on investment and long-term expectation, he claimed that for some questions (the examples involve a European war and the price of copper decades out) there is no scientific basis for any calculable probability at all, so that markets run on convention, confidence, and animal spirits. I want to argue that neither sharp priors nor imprecise-probability machinery captures the weight dimension, and that where weight is very low, rational agents' actual behavior — hoarding liquidity, following conventions, refusing to act on "expectations" in the technical sense — cannot be reconstructed as expected-utility maximization over any prior whatsoever.

The audience: two doctrinaire subjective Bayesians who hold that Ramsey's 1926 critique demolished Keynes's logical interpretation and that representation-theorem and Dutch-book arguments settle what probabilities must be; one formal decision theorist working on ambiguity (Ellsberg preferences, maxmin expected utility) who regards Keynes as a footnote to the literature she already knows; two economic methodologists sympathetic to heterodoxy but hostile to hand-waving; and our historian of economic thought, who will pounce on any anachronistic use of Keynes. Prepare my question-and-answer sheet: the objections these people will actually raise, phrased the way each of them would phrase it, in roughly the order they will come; for each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes plus a note on where the reply is doing less work than it appears to; what I should concede up front to keep the room on my side; and the one question I am quietly hoping nobody asks, with what to say if it is asked anyway.
