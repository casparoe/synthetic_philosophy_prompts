I'm a philosophy of mind graduate student drafting a section on computationalism, and a friend has walked me into a corner I can't find my way out of. He's reconstructed the old triviality worry — the one Putnam pressed in the appendix to Representation and Reality — but strengthened it with the counterfactual clause that Chalmers added in his Synthese paper on whether a rock implements every finite-state automaton, so that the usual first response is blocked. Here is the argument as he gave it to me:

(1) A physical system implements an automaton A if and only if there is a mapping from A's internal states, inputs and outputs onto sets of physical states of the system such that, for every transition of A, if the system is in a state mapped to internal state S and its environment supplies a configuration mapped to input i, the system then enters a state mapped to S′ — and the same holds counterfactually: had the input been different, the system would have traced the corresponding alternative branch.

(2) It is precisely this counterfactual clause that is supposed to block triviality. A system that merely happens to run through the right sequence of states does not implement A, because it would not have done so had the inputs differed.

(3) Now take any open physical system with enough independent degrees of freedom — a wall will do — and any finite automaton A. Over any finite run, designate one region of the wall as a clock, cycling deterministically through distinguishable states, and another as a dial, whose state I set from outside to encode A's input sequence. At each tick, define the mapping so that the clock-and-dial pair is mapped to A's internal state at that step. The construction honours the counterfactuals: had the dial been set differently, the system would have entered states mapped to the corresponding alternative internal states.

(4) So the wall implements A, for every finite automaton A and every finite run — including an automaton that, at some coarse level of description, is functionally isomorphic to my brain over the past hour.

(5) If implementing the right computation suffices for the corresponding mental states, then a stretch of my wall spent the past hour in a state functionally indistinguishable from mine: it felt pain when I stubbed my toe and puzzled over triviality arguments.

(6) The wall did nothing of the kind.

(7) So implementing the right computation does not suffice for mentality; computationalism, in its sufficiency form, is false.

(8) But here is the sting: the same reasoning now rebounds on the brain. The claim that my brain implements my mind's computation, rather than merely being mappable onto it, must be underwritten by something over and above the bare existence of a structure-preserving mapping; yet whatever that something is, either the construction in (3) can be given it too, or the brain may lack it. Either the wall computes in exactly the sense in which the brain does, or nothing does.

(9) Therefore computationalism is either false or trivially true, and in neither case is the brain a computer in the sense the computationalist needs.

I can't locate the faulty step, and my friend insists there may not be one — that the argument is valid and the whole question is which plausible premise is cheapest to abandon. I'd most like to keep (1) together with the claim that my brain genuinely computes; I could probably live with denying sufficiency if that's the least costly exit.

Please tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. In particular, I want you to compare the main escape routes as my friend framed them: (i) tightening (1) so that mapped states must be non-disjunctive, non-gerrymandered states, or so that implementation requires an isomorphism of causal organisation among distinct components — does this repair the wall case, and does it leave the brain's credentials intact, or does the brain's own description at the level of neurons or molecules inherit the gerrymandering problem? (ii) adding a semantic or teleological constraint on computation — that genuine computation must involve representation, or be selected for; (iii) accepting pancomputationalism while denying sufficiency — is computationalism as a theory of mind thereby saved, or hollowed out? Say which of these is cheapest, and say whether you think step (8) bites even after the repair: does whatever distinguishes the brain from the wall do any work that a defender of sufficiency can use, or only work that a defender of the brain's merely computing can use? If you think the argument equivocates or slides somewhere rather than being valid — say, in what 'input' means in (3) versus (1), or in how the quantifiers over finite runs interact with the claim in (4) — name the equivocation and locate it precisely. Please write in British English. Finally, and this matters to me: tell me how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and single out the one point in your answer that you are least sure of — I'd rather know where the ground is soft than have a confident-sounding section collapse under questioning at my supervisor's next meeting.
