I've ended up an illusionist about phenomenal consciousness — the strong kind, Frankish-style: there are no phenomenal properties, no irreducible what-it's-likeness, and the hard problem dissolves once you explain why we're all so strongly disposed to believe we have phenomenal properties (roughly: introspection is a lossy, theory-laden model of our own cognitive processing, and it systematically mislabels relational/functional properties as intrinsic phenomenal ones).

Pretend you're a professional philosopher of mind who has refereed every paper in this debate and is mildly sick of it. I want a map of what would actually count against my view — not the reflexive "but the illusion itself would be phenomenal!" response, unless you think that one can be made to bite in a non-question-begging way. Please think carefully, and maybe start with a short brainstorm of candidate defeaters before you rank anything. Then cover:

1. Empirical defeaters. Is there anything neuroscience or psychology could discover that would specifically hurt illusionism, rather than hurting every theory of consciousness equally? For example: would it be bad for us if a patient turned up whose introspective reports fully decoupled from discrimination and perceptual behavior — or is that just more grist for our mill? Which existing results should make me at least a little uncomfortable? Draw from wherever you think the real pressure is: blindsight, change blindness and inattentional blindness, no-report paradigms, confabulation in split-brain patients, hallucination and delirium research, misperception under unusual conditions, whatever.

2. Structural defeaters. What would have to be true for the view to collapse? A few candidates I want your read on: (a) the illusion problem — explaining an illusion of phenomenality without any phenomenality means saying what the "seeming" is, and I worry the obvious answer (a judgment or disposition to judge) either quietly reinstates a richer seeming or makes the illusion suspiciously thin; under what conditions does that problem become unsolvable rather than just unfinished business? (b) the changes-the-subject objection — is there a version of the complaint that illusionism isn't a theory of consciousness but a theory of something else that a fair referee should uphold, given that we get to redefine the term? (c) self-defeat — we say introspection is unreliable exactly where the phenomenal question is at issue; can that be pressed against our own confidence in the illusionist story, or is that just a debating point?

3. What we'd concede vs. dismiss. Sort the defeaters into three buckets: things a committed illusionist should honestly concede count against the view (even if not decisively), things we can dismiss as begging the question, and things that are merely awkward. Then tell me which single defeater you'd personally lead with if you were arguing against me in a seminar.

Finish with an honest assessment: my worry is that illusionism is nearly embarassingly insulated, because our opponents' best evidence consists of intuitions we've already reinterpreted. Is that a case of a mature research program legitimately reinterpreting anomalies, or a sign the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if the latter, is that fatal for a philosophical view of this kind or just how metaphysics works? Don't go easy on me just because I'm the one asking.
