I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and I've fallen into a rabbit hole about how one might rank possible futures by the lives they contain. The naive rule — count up welfare across everyone who exists — implies that an enormous population whose lives are barely worth living ranks above a small population of excellent lives. Almost everyone finds that revolting, so people propose alternatives, and each alternative gets killed by a purpose-built counterexample. Some rules imply that adding a group of extra happy lives to a world, without touching anyone already there, makes the world worse. Some imply that a smaller world full of lives of constant torment ranks above a larger world of lives just barely worth living. Often the counterexample is built as a chain of tiny steps, each of which the rule seems forced to accept, with the absurdity appearing only at the end. The striking part is that this doesn't look like ordinary "every theory has counterexamples": people in these discussions claim it's provable that no rule whatsoever, if it satisfies a short list of individually obvious conditions, can avoid all of the listed absurd conclusions. Is there a name for results of this shape? I know of Arrow's theorem in voting theory, which smells similar — does the population case have an established label and a canonical result of this kind, how does it differ from an Arrow-style impossibility theorem, and if the general phenomenon lacks a name, what would you call it, in a way that distinguishes it from a mere tradeoff between desiderata?
