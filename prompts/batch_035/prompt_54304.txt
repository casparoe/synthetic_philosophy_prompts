I'm taking a seminar on Strawson's Freedom and Resentment, and I keep running into something I can't find a label for. Excuses and what Strawson calls exempting conditions seem to work selectively: when we learn that a harmful act issued from coercion, severe mental illness, or a childhood that explains everything about the person, resentment and indignation dissolve. But the same kind of causal story, told about a friend's kindness or a benefactor's generosity, seems to leave gratitude untouched — at most it adds understanding, and often it deepens the attachment. The asymmetry feels puzzling: if what defeats resentment is that the act traces back to factors outside the agent's control, then gratitude looks exactly as exposed, since the benefactor's character is a product of the same kind of luck. Yet nobody thinks that learning about someone's fortunate upbringing is a defeater for gratitude, the way the parallel story about an unfortunate upbringing is a defeater for resentment. Does this asymmetry — negative reactive attitudes being hostage to excuses in a way positive ones are not — have an established name? I'm aware of Strawson's suggestion that the reactive attitudes form a web that cannot be selectively abandoned, and I know the moral luck literature, but neither is about the asymmetry itself, and they seem to pull in opposite directions on whether it is even coherent to keep gratitude while giving up resentment. If there is no standard term, please coin one, define it precisely, and draw its boundaries: does it cover third-party indignation as opposed to personal resentment, attitudes like pride and shame, and cases where someone explains their own good deeds causally?
