I teach an upper-level seminar on moral uncertainty and animal ethics and am writing the final. Draft one essay question, answerable in about ninety minutes, on the scale argument for taking insect suffering seriously: the claim that even if the probability that any given insect is sentient is very low, the sheer number of insects alive (and killed in agriculture and pest control) at any moment is so vast that the expected total of suffering cannot be dismissed. The question must not be answerable by reciting the familiar empirical back-and-forth about invertebrate nervous systems. It should instead force students to examine the argument's structure: whether it requires expected-value maximization over moral uncertainty; how its force changes if moral status is a threshold property rather than a matter of degree; whether it survives lexical or risk-weighted approaches under which avoiding a catastrophic moral error takes priority over maximizing expected value; and, as the crux the whole question should turn on, whether the argument treats its two small probabilities symmetrically — the small probability that insects are sentient, and the small probability that any proposed intervention would actually reduce their suffering on net rather than just displace it. A strong student should have to say whether that symmetry is a feature of the argument, an artifact of expected-value reasoning that a different aggregation rule would break, or a defect.

With the question, provide a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks, organized around five must-hit points, with the marks for each stated and a one-line note on the standard way students lose each. Make the boundary between an A and a B answer turn on whether the student recognizes that the scale argument's practical force depends on how moral uncertainty is aggregated, not on how confident they are about the entomology.
