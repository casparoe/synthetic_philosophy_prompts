Write a satirical advice column, roughly 600 words, for the newspaper of a country that has run itself as a futarchy for the last fifteen years — citizens vote only on values (expressed as a national welfare metric) and all actual policy decisions get adopted or repealed based on conditional prediction-market prices. The columnist, call her something like "Dear Martingale," has fully internalized futarchic common sense: for her, market prices are simply what belief looks like, so every letter gets answered by consulting the relevant market, never by her own judgment. Run at least three letters through the column: maybe someone whose town's schools changed overnight because a conditional market on a literacy metric moved, someone wondering whether it's unethical to bet against their own marriage's success metric, and a parent whose kid got rich trading on early knowledge of a pending policy change. The jokes should come from the real open problems of futarchy, not invented ones — Goodhart-style metric gaming (once a metric becomes the target, people optimize straight past the thing you cared about), manipulation that's just a subsidy in disguise, conditional markets telling you correlation rather than causation, and the awkward fact that beliefs get weighted by wealth. The columnist must stay deadpan and internally consistent: she never winks, never doubts the system, and treats each complaint as evidence the asker hasn't internalized that voting on values means you gave up the right to complain about beliefs. But a reader who knows the futarchy literature should be able to name each objection being dramatized. Keep it funny for someone who's never heard of prediction markets too.
