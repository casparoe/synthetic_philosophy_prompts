Two colleagues of mine got into a long argument at the pub after a seminar on technology and society, and I couldn't follow who was winning. They were arguing about whether artifacts themselves can have politics. For context if it helps: the one, Dan, is very taken with the old story about Robert Moses building low overpasses on the New York parkways so that buses (carrying poorer people, often Black) couldn't get to the beaches, while cars could -- politics built directly into concrete. The other, Priya, thinks that whole way of talking is confused.

Round by round it went something like this:

1. Dan said the overpass example shows an artifact can be a political act: the bridge enforces a class policy just as effectively as a statute, and unlike a statute it needs no policeman -- it works whether or not anyone intended it, and it can't be repealed by an election.

2. Priya replied that the story is basically false -- historians went back and checked, and the low bridges didn't actually keep buses out; the bus routes just never went there. So the "politics" wasn't in the bridge at all, it was in a story we later told about the bridge, which rather proves her point: the politics always sits in the people and the narratives, not the object.

3. Dan said the empirical correction is fine but misses the point: even if that particular story is false, the conceptual claim stands. Some arrangements are simply easier to reach by building things than by passing laws -- think of an assembly line that makes it physically impossible to work slowly, or a factory where the boss can see every worker's screen. The design choice is made once, quietly, by an engineer, and then it's locked in before politics proper can get started. Call it legislation by other means.

4. Priya said that just relocates the politics, it doesn't put it in the artifact: if the politics is in the design *decision*, then the politics belongs to whoever controlled that decision -- the planner, the board, the engineer. Saying the bridge has politics is like saying the gun pulled the trigger. At best it's politics by proxy, and loose talk like Dan's makes it sound like things act on their own, which lets the actual decision-makers off the hook.

5. Dan's last word was that some things you simply cannot argue with, and that's exactly the difference: a bad statute can be overturned, a bad bridge is still there in the morning, and everyone who encounters it has to route around it whether they agree with its politics or not. If that's not the artifact itself having politics, he said, he didn't know what the phrase could possibly mean.

Can you go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move -- who's right at each step, not just overall? And if you think both of them are missing something, I'd want to hear that too, because honestly the whole thing went over my head.
