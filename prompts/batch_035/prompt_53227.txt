I sit on the editorial board of an independent foreign-policy institute. A colleague's draft report on China and Singapore argues that a regime which sustains decades of prosperity, competent administration, and public order earns a claim on its citizens' obedience that is not merely prudential — something comparable in kind, if not in strength, to the compliance-claim that elected governments hold — and the draft names this 'performance legitimacy,' treating it as a genuine form of legitimacy rather than a euphemism for effectiveness. Our board is split three ways. Some want it published as it stands; others say it launders authoritarian apologetics in the neutral vocabulary of social science and should be rejected; the philosophers among us say the draft runs together three distinct questions — whether citizens in fact comply, and mostly approve, because of delivered results; whether those results generate pro tanto reasons to comply; and whether such reasons are of the same kind as those generated by fair elections — and that it can only be published once these are separated. One member goes further and contends that 'performance legitimacy' is a category error outright: legitimacy, as the term functions in political philosophy, presupposes a standard for how political authority is entitled to be constituted in the first place, and performance, however impressive, cannot itself supply that standard, so the draft should be rejected on conceptual grounds alone, not political ones. Please draft the board's decision memo — publish, reject, or publish with specified revisions — with reasoning the author could actually respond to, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the side that loses. Write it as continuous prose only, in the manner of an email or letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
