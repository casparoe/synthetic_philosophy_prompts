I'm a graduate student defending my prospectus next month, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. The thesis I'll be defending is a version of Lewis's modal realism from which I have surgically removed Lewis's insistence that possible worlds are concrete. My argument, in brief, is that the concreteness clause does no explanatory work in Lewis's own case for modal realism. The theory's virtues — worlds as unifying entities, the analysis of counterfactuals and of modality generally without primitive modal operators — are all supposed to flow from the internal structure of worlds and the totality they form, not from whether those worlds are concrete. And the clause is a costly liability: it is what earns the theory the incredulous stare, and Lewis himself conceded he could say little more about concreteness than that other worlds are relevantly like ours. So my view keeps everything else — worlds are individuals, spatiotemporally isolated, non-overlapping, with our world just one among them — and simply refuses to pronounce on their concreteness. The controversial part of the prospectus is my further claim: that once this is seen, the difference between my view and a sufficiently rich ersatzism is merely verbal, and that this is a virtue, since discovering that two apparently rival ontologies of modality are notations for one theory is exactly the kind of result modal realism was supposed to deliver.

Now the committee. My primary advisor is a Lewis-trained metaphysician who thinks On the Plurality of Worlds is roughly right, and she will treat the concreteness clause as the load-bearing wall: for her, if worlds are not concrete, the reduction of modality collapses into circularity, because the entities that would replace concrete worlds get built out of modal or intentional materials, and the analysis presupposes what it was meant to analyze. The second reader is a Quinean naturalist who regards the whole enterprise as scholasticism with quantifiers; his objection will be about ontological and ideological parsimony, and he will ask what my theory predicts or explains that a sober nominalism could not, and whether refusing to say what worlds are is a discovery or an evasion. The third reader is a formal epistemologist who has written on measure problems for infinite collections; she will ask how probability talk over an infinite totality of worlds gets off the ground, and whether my refusal of the concreteness clause makes things better or worse, since concrete worlds at least have a candidate principle of individuation, whereas merely world-like ones may leave the totality improperly large, perhaps even a proper class. And the fourth reader is a Kantian who does not care about the ontology; she will ask whether modality as I construe it is mind-independent or a projection of our inferential practices, and she will ask it in a way that exposes that my project silently presupposes an answer.

Prepare the prep sheet as continuous prose, written the way one would write a long, serious letter or email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Order the objections by how much damage they would actually do to the prospectus surviving, not by how likely they are to be asked. For each, phrase the objection the way that particular reader would phrase it, in that reader's vocabulary, then give me a two-minute reply, then tell me honestly where the reply is soft. Take a definite position throughout: tell me which objection I should concede ground on, which I must hold at all costs, and which single reply has to land for the prospectus to survive. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks — and I mean the one that keeps me up at night, namely whether my thesis is anything more than a terminological thesis dressed up as a substantive one — and tell me exactly what to say if my advisor asks it.
