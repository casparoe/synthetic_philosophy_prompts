I'm writing a paper section defending the pragmatic account of slurs — the idea that the derogation isn't baked into literal meaning but arrives the way implicatures do, through what the speaker's doing and what the audience can work out — and I need a matched pair of cases that puts pressure on the other side. The usual complaint trade is boring: everyone knows semantic accounts overgenerate on quotation and mention, and everyone knows pragmatic accounts struggle with in-group reclamation. I want the cases built so the pressure runs somewhere less familiar.

Case one: build me a realistic scenario, no longer than a paragraph, where a term that is not a slur — no taboo attached to the word itself, no history of oppression built into it — produces everything we'd intuitively call slur-like derogation: the same offense, the same silencing effect, the same "you can't just say that to someone" reaction from bystanders. Think dog whistles and sneering descriptors, but the case has to generate the effect, not stipulate it — I want to be able to point at the actual mechanism in the conversation that produces it. Case two is the mirror image: a scenario where an actual slur gets used and none of that happens, and again it has to fall out of the setup rather than being announced as harmless.

Constraints: it'd be nice if both shared a rough setting (a workplace or a family dinner, not a philosophy seminar), the audience in each case must not be confused about what the speaker means, and the speaker in case one must be sincerely derogating while the speaker in case two is not. I don't want the whole thing to colapse into "intent explains everything," because then the cases don't discriminate between the theories at all.

After the two scenarios, walk each of the three main families through both cases: the semantic content view (the derogation is part of what the word literally means), the expressivist view (the word conventionally expresses contempt toward the target group), and the pragmatic view (derogation is a conversational effect, an implicature or something in that neighborhood). Say where each one has to strain, and be honest if a theory can actually handle a case I'm assuming it can't — especially on reclamation, where I suspect the expressivists have a move I haven't fully appreciated. Then tell me: which single feature of the pair is doing the work, what happens if you remove it, and how a committed semanticist would attack the cases. If the pair turns out to secretly favor the semantic side after all, say so — I'd much rather find out from you than from a referee.

For case two, use either a real but mild slur or a made-up placeholder, whichever makes the mechanics clearest.
