I'm in a distributive justice reading group that has spent the last two months on the old "equality of what?" fight, and I've noticed something that's been bugging me: the debate seems completely stalled, and stalled in a specific way. Everyone on each side already concedes the other side's counterexamples. The resourcists concede that two people with identical resource bundles can end up in wildly different shape (Sen's convertibility cases -- disability, or needing more fuel in a cold climate to reach the same level of functioning), and the welfarists and capability people concede the expensive tastes stuff -- Dworkin's guy who can only get pleasure from vintage wine and plovers' eggs is supposed to show that welfare is the wrong currency because he chose his tastes. But nobody actually switches metrics. Cohen's move, that capabilities are just "midfare" -- an arbitrary stopping point on the road from resources to welfare -- gets noted and then ignored. Meanwhile the capability approach basically won in development economics and policy, and in philosophy nobody thinks the metric question got settled; the philosophical energy just drained away into a different fight, the Anderson/Scheffler relational equality stuff about standing and oppression instead of distribution.

So here's what I want, and it's a diagnosis rather than a survey. What kind of disagreement was the metric dispute, actually? My working hypothesis is that it was never really about the metric at all, and I have two versions of that suspicion I can't decide between: (1) the "equality of what?" question smuggles in the assumption that equality is fundamentally distributive, so once the relational egalitarians rejected that assumption, they weren't answering the same question, and the metric debate just dissolved for them rather than being resolved; (2) the metric dispute was a proxy fight for the cut between what you're responsible for and what you're not, so the welfarist/resourcist/capability disagreement is really a disagreement about choice, and the metrics are just downstream of where each theorist draws that line, which would explain why counterexamples about tastes and needs never move anyone. But maybe both of these are wrong and there's a genuine substantive question about the right space in which to measure inequality that's just extremely hard. Tell me which story fits what the participants actually said and did, whether the stall is evidence the question was ill-posed in some way, and what would have counted as progress -- new cases, a theorem-like impossibility result about metrics, a stipulation about the point of equality that settles the metric by fiat, or nothing.

One more thing: as you go, please flag how confident you are in each part of the diagnosis, especially where you're reading the stall as verbal vs substantive, and single out the point you're least sure of. My suspicion is that the whole diagnosis rests on contested readings of what Anderson's critique was supposed to be doing, so if any part of your answer leans on that kind of interpretive choice, say so rather than presenting it as settled. Also, if my two hypotheses are both bad, don't try to rescue them -- say why they fail, that's more useful than a polite version of them.
