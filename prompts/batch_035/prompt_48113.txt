As part of an upper-level seminar on animal ethics, I've found myself persuaded by the scale argument for taking insect welfare seriously: even if the probability that any given insect is sentient is quite low, the numbers involved — something like 10^18 individual insects alive at any moment, against perhaps 10^12 to 10^13 wild vertebrates and tens of billions of farmed ones — are so lopsided that insects plausibly dominate the expected moral arithmetic of how we treat animals. I want to know how much numerical exposure this conviction actually has. Please give me your credence in the following proposition:

P: In expected moral stakes, the welfare effects of humanity's choices on insects (through pesticide use, habitat conversion, and any deliberate insect rearing) are at least as great as the welfare effects of our choices on all vertebrates, wild and domesticated, combined.

Decompose the number. I anticipate at least the following terms, and you should add whatever else belongs: (i) the probability that consciousness requires nothing beyond the right kind of functional organization, so that an insect nervous system could in principle carry it; (ii) the probability, conditional on that, that insects actually satisfy the relevant conditions, given what is known about nociception, flexible trade-offs, and anaesthetic-seeking in some species; (iii) the weight you assign to the intensity or richness of insect experience relative to vertebrate experience, and whether any defensible weighting function could flip the verdict rather than merely shrink it; and (iv) the aggregation step — whether you take a straight expected-value sum across five orders of magnitude of difference in population, or whether you think tiny-probability, vast-stakes reasoning here inherits the standard worries about fanaticism and Pascalian cases, in which case say what alternative aggregation you would use and how it changes the answer.

Two further requests. First, tell me whether the decomposition should use a single sentience probability for all insects or per-taxon probabilities: the strongest evidence comes from bees and, more weakly, flies, while most of the individuals alive are beetles, ants, and other far less studied taxa — does that asymmetry move the final number materially, or does it wash out at this scale? Second, identify which single term your final credence is most sensitive to, and what realistic discovery — something that could actually be found, not a metaphysical revolution — would move that term the most.

Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence: your credence in P as a number, with a one-clause summary of why. Then give the full reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate the question.
