I'm the managing editor of a mid-tier applied math journal (think: strong but not Annals-level, lots of numerical analysis and PDE work). A few years back we started a "constructive and computable methods" section, partly because a big proof-assistant community cites it, and now every paper that touches existence proofs has to be sorted: does it go in the constructive section with that label attached in databases and everything, or the general section. The problem is that our referees are inconsistent in a way that's getting embarrassing. One referee will accept an existence proof as constructive because "the construction is right there in the proof," another will flag it because the argument for termination invokes excluded middle in some sublemma nobody noticed. One author got a report saying their paper was classical "because it uses proof by contradiction," which was doubly annoying since reductio to derive a negation is fine even constructively and the referee apparently didn't distinguish that from deriving a positive existence statement from a contradiction. I myself am not a logician, so I can't adjudicate these fights on my own knowledge.

Please turn whatever the actual substance of constructivism is (Brouwer-Heyting-Kolmogorov-style readings of the connectives, the existence and disjunction properties, what it means for a proof to carry a witness, that whole cluster) into a screening procedure that a non-logician editor with maybe two hours per paper and access to one logicians referee for hard cases can actually run. I want the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each (e.g., what should I accept as evidence that a cited classical theorem has a known constructive version?), where the procedure terminates with "not constructive" even if the paper is otherwise fine, and where it has to hand off to the human logician because it's a genuine judgment call — I'm told things like whether Markov's principle counts as constructive are school-dependent rather than settled, so the procedure should say that explicitly instead of pretending otherwise. It should also have a small set of red-flag patterns a busy editor can grep for mentally: deriving existence from a contradiction, case splits on undecidable predicates, classical intermediate results smuggled in as lemmas, and whatever else belongs on that list.

Then run the procedure on three real-ish cases I actually have on my desk. (1) A paper proving a boundary value problem has a solution via contradiction, but then noting the solution can be located by bisection since the sign change is decidable, and leaning on that to claim constructivity. (2) A paper that's built on a classical minimax theorem, where the author says constructive versions are "well known" and cites nothing, and the algorithms people want to extract stuff from it. (3) A genuinely elegant paper by someone we very much want in the journal, whose proofs are all explicit constructions over finite-dimensional spaces with decidable equality, except the author never claims constructivity anywhere and I suspect would be irritated to be reclassified. And finally, tell me honestly where this procedure will misfire — where the constructivity question isn't mechanical at all and my checklist will produce confidently wrong labels, and what you'd do about a case where the procedure says one thing and the section editor's intuition says another.
