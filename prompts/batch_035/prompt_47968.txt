So I'm a 3rd year PhD student in philosophy, and next month I'm presenting to our departmental colloquium. The paper defends what I'm calling a *local* evolutionary debunking argument, and honestly I'm not sure how it's going to land. The idea: instead of the familiar global move, where you say evolutionary explanations of our moral faculties threaten moral knowledge across the board (Street's Darwinian dilemma style), I only target a slice of our intuitions -- specifically intuitions about partiality toward kin, reciprocity, and punishing free-riders. My claim is that for these intuitions we can tell a pretty detailed selection story where the intuition tracks fitness rather than anything about the moral facts, so beliefs those intuitions support are epistemically undermined, even though intuitions about, say, impartial benevolence or honesty as such come out fine. The upshot I want is that a kind of reflective equilibrium that downweights the partial/reciprocal intuitions is vindicated, not that everything moral goes in the trash.

The audience: our department is mostly metaethicists. There are two Cornell-style naturalist realists who will insist that on their view the selection story can be truth-tracking (moral facts just are facts about the stable points of human cooperation, so of course evolution tracked them), a couple of Parfitian non-naturalists who think the whole genealogical enterprise is epistemically inert unless I show the dependence is specifically *off* the moral facts, one constructivist who's going to say she's happy to concede everything I argue for because the genealogy is just the metaethical view, and a Kantian who always drags things toward whether the argument applies to normativity in general and not just morality. Plus grad students who love regress worries.

Can you draft my Q&A prep sheet? I want roughly eight objections I'm actually going to get from these people, phrased the way they'd phrase them (in their vocab, not generic textbook objections), ordered by how much damage they'd do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Please be honest about which ones I should concede parts of up front rather than fight. And include the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks -- I think I know what it is (something about where I draw the line and why the same selection story doesn't extend to the intuitions I'm trying to save), but I want to see if you agree and how bad it actually is. Also a note on what to say to the constructivist, since "your debunking argument is just my thesis stated menacingly" has me a bit spooked. Keep it in American English please, and casual is fine -- this is for me, not for the proceedings.
