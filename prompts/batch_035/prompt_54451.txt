A friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about biological function, and we ended exactly where we started, so I want a diagnosis of why rather than another round of the argument itself. The terrain, concretely: on the etiological or selected-effect view, the heart's function is to pump blood because pumping is what hearts were selected for; on the causal-role view, a part's function is whatever it does that contributes to some system-level capacity we have chosen to analyze — so pumping counts as the heart's function relative to circulation, while the thumping noises it makes are mere effects because they contribute to nothing we care about. We kept circling the same cases and could not agree on what they showed: a malformed heart that no longer pumps (etiological function intact, causal-role function gone); feathers that first evolved for thermoregulation and were only later co-opted for flight (which is their function now, and according to what?); traits with no recoverable selection history, in organisms or lineages where the historical facts are simply gone, where biologists still assign functions without hesitation; and, most awkwardly, whether cancer cells can be said to have functions — apparently yes on a causal-role reading relative to some capacity of a tumor or tissue, apparently no on the etiological one. My friend's position was that only the etiological account makes function-talk genuinely explanatory, because it answers why the trait is there at all, and that causal-role function is a metaphor wearing the costume of an analysis. I answered that etiological functions are unknowable in most real cases, useless for physiology, and silent about novel capacities that appear in lab-evolved organisms with no selection history behind them, and that causal-role talk is what working scientists actually mean and use. Around midnight it dawned on me that we might have been arguing past each other about something else entirely: whether a theory of function is supposed to capture a concept biologists already possess, or to legitimate the normative-seeming language of function and dysfunction — which is no small stake, since at least one influential line of thought defines disease as selected-effect dysfunction, and that whole picture stands or falls with the etiological side. So my questions are these. Is the disagreement substantive, or verbal, or two research programs answering different questions under a single word? Is a hidden empirical question — what selection in fact did — doing the work, or a normative one about whether biology is entitled to function-talk at all, or a clash of starting intuitions about individual cases that neither side can argue for without circularity? Please diagnose the impasse using what each side actually maintains, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a class of cases, a formal or empirical result — or argue that no progress should be expected and why, and assess honestly whether my suspicion, that the two accounts are tools for different jobs rather than rivals, is a genuine resolution or just a polite way of declaring a draw.
