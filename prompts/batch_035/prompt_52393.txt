I'm a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas, and last month I was at a small workshop on the foundations of machine learning, sitting in on a session that was supposed to be about model selection but quickly turned into a two-man war over Occam's razor. One of the researchers, let's call him Dan, claimed that Solomonoff induction finally gives the razor a rigorous footing: you weight each hypothesis-generating computer program by 2 to the minus its length, and then it's basically a theorem that simple hypotheses end up dominating your predictions, plus the whole setup is robust to the details of what machine you use, up to some additive constant. The other guy, Marcus, basically laughed at that. He said the choice of universal machine is completely arbitrary, and the famous constant that supposedly washes out the arbitrariness can be larger than the number of atoms in the universe, so which hypothesis counts as "short" depends entirely on a choice nobody can justify. On his telling, Solomonoff induction doesn't derive Occam's razor at all, it just bakes the razor in as an assumption and then announces it as a conclusion.

Dan came back with what I thought was a decent point: some bias is unavoidable, this is the no-free-lunch result, any learner that generalizes at all is smuggling in a prior somewhere, and the Solomonoff prior is at least a principled one, since it multiplicatively dominates any computable prior you might prefer instead. Marcus wasn't moved. He said that's like defending a gold standard by saying any currency needs backing, and anyway the whole thing is incomputable, so what practicing people actually do is pick architectures based on fashion and hardware, then retroactively call whatever the architecture happens to find "simplicity" -- and he pointed to overparameterized deep nets, where you can have a billion parameters and still generalize well, as evidence that simplicity has nothing to do with whats actually happening. Dan's last move was that Occam's razor was never about counting parameters in a model, it's about the description length of the function being learned, and in that sense the deep nets example is not even a counterexample. At that point they ran out of coffee and I ran out of ability to follow.

I'm writing a piece about why "simpler explanations are better" is such an enduring slogan and whether the formal work actually settles anything, and honestly I couldn't tell watching these two whether they were disagreeing about math or about words. Can you walk through their exchange move by move and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? I especially want to know whether Marcus's arbitrariness point really undercuts Dan's invariance claim, or whether Dan's dominance point is enough to answer it, and whether theres something both of them are missing that a bystander like me should know about.
