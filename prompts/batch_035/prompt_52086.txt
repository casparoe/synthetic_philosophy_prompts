For roughly a century the debate over future contingents — sentences like "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" — has been running in place. Łukasiewicz built a three-valued logic around them; Prior split the field into Peircean and Ockhamist treatments; Thomason's supervaluationism was supposed to keep classical tautologies while leaving the future genuinely open; and the "thin red line" was devised so that one future could be the future without determinism being true. Yet the exchange has an eerily fixed choreography: the anti-bivalence side insists the future is open in a way that shows up in semantics, the bivalence side cites excluded middle — either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there won't — and asks what a third value does for the future that "unsettled" doesn't already do, and the supervaluationist is told that making future contingents all false-at-a-moment is exactly the determinism they were trying to avoid. Nothing moves. Worse, the medieval foreknowledge version of the problem — where God's infallible belief seems to threaten freedom — appears to stall in the same place despite involving none of the same machinery, which makes me suspect the blockage is structural rather than historical accident.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two camps are not disagreeing about one thing. One side is legislating a use for "true" — a semantic question about what the connectives and truth-functional logic require — while the other is making a metaphysical claim about what reality has already settled, and each camp can concede the other's whole position without giving up anything it cares about. If that's right, the debate is insulated by design and no argument can touch it. But I'm not confident, because the excluded-middle arguments do look like they force the two questions into contact: it's hard to say "the disjunction is settled" without saying something about what settles it.

So tell me: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word "true," or two questions — a logical one and a metaphysical one — traveling under a single heading? Is there a hidden formal question, say about which logic the connectives actually obey or about whether "settled" can be defined without already presupposing bivalence, that is doing the real work, and would settling it dissolve the dispute or merely relocate it? Does the fact that the foreknowledge debate stalls in the same place confirm that the blockage is structural, or is that a coincidence of rhetoric? And given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected and why. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says, not in labels for the positions.
