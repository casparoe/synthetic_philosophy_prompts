In a metaphysics seminar on material constitution, my professor dismissed the two-object account of the statue and the clay — the view that the statue and the lump of clay that constitutes it are numerically distinct objects that nonetheless occupy exactly the same region — with the remark that two things can't be in the same place at the same time. The quip carried the room, and I have since heard it repeated in every discussion of the topic, but I want to know precisely what work it is doing. Lay out the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be expressing, ordered from the least to the most revisionary: for example, a truism restricted to objects of the same sortal (two statues can't fully coincide, but a statue and a lump is another matter); a principle of mereology, that no two objects share all their parts; the unrestricted claim that no two objects whatever are spatially coincident; a stronger thesis that no two objects share all their microphysical properties; and a methodological reading on which the sentence is really a counting heuristic — it tells us how many things to say are on the lectern, not what overlap is possible — plus any other readings you think the sentence can bear. For each, give a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible thesis, or false, and say what presses on it, drawing both on the standard puzzles (the clay surviving being squashed while the statue does not; the object consisting of a cat minus its tail) and on less familiar cases where near-coincidence seems benign, such as interpenetrating fluids, a rope whose strands share fibers, or the swarm and the platoon it is composed of. Finally, tell me which reading my professor actually needed in order to rule out the coincidence view, and whether that reading is plausible or merely begs the question — the defender of two objects cheerfully concedes exact colocation and offers modal differences as grounds of distinctness, so the slogan must be argued for, not asserted. I would especially like to know whether any reading strong enough to exclude the statue and the clay also excludes things the professor presumably wants to keep. Present this as a menu of readings with a verdict attached to each, suitable for raising in the next seminar, rather than a defense of a single interpretation.
