At my school's ethics society, we were preparing for a debate on whether animal-welfare spending should take insects seriously, and two members — Amara and Theo — got into an argument the rest of us couldn't settle. I've written down the exchange as fairly as I can, without favoring either side, and I'd like you to adjudicate it move by move.

The disagreement unfolded in four moves.

(1) Amara opened with what she called the scale argument. Entomologists' rough estimates put the number of insects alive at any one moment at something like a quintillion — several orders of magnitude more than all vertebrates combined. She said that even if the probability that any given insect is sentient is low, the expected number of sentient beings is just that probability multiplied by the quintillion, so even a very small probability yields an expected moral stake larger than that of all factory farming put together. On those grounds, she concluded, insect welfare cannot be treated as a rounding error.

(2) Theo answered that the multiplication is doing more work than it can bear. Sentience, he argued, is plausibly a threshold property: an insect either has a point of view or it does not, and if it does not, then it doesn't experience anything faintly — it experiences nothing at all. A low probability spread across a quintillion such cases doesn't conjure a small amount of experience into existence; it just means that in most of the scenarios we're uncertain between, nothing whatever is at stake. He added that calling our hesitation a bias is a psychological diagnosis rather than an argument: showing that human concern tracks concentration of harm rather than its distribution across victims doesn't by itself show that expected-value arithmetic is the correct correction.

(3) Amara replied that Theo had misdescribed what expectation is for. The entire point of averaging, she said, is to act when you don't know which scenario you're in — one doesn't refuse to price fire insurance on the ground that the house either will burn or won't. Given our uncertainty, a quintillion insects each with some chance of being a "someone" is a situation in which there are, in expectation, a great many someones, and declining to average just because each individual case is all-or-nothing is like refusing to insure a street against flooding because each house either floods or doesn't. She then pressed the normative point: if we refuse to multiply, identical harms come to count more when concentrated in a few visible victims than when spread across billions of invisible ones, and she found that result hard to accept.

(4) Theo's closing move was practical. For the sake of argument he granted the whole expected-value apparatus, and offered it as a reductio: by the same logic, suffering in wild animals generally, and among insects most of all, dwarfs every other cause, so the argument doesn't merely add a line to the welfare budget — it reorders all of our priorities, making whichever population is largest the most urgent cause no matter how speculative the underlying sentience claim. That, he said, is the smell of a method proving too much. He finished with an ecological observation: since most insect suffering is inflicted by other insects, through parasitism, predation, and starvation, interventions that shrink insect populations will shift vertebrate numbers and plant communities too, and whether the net result is less suffering is an open empirical question that no expected-value calculation can settle in advance.

Please adjudicate the four moves in order: for each one, say whether the speaker is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, and give your reasons. Then explain what, if anything, both of them are missing. Finally, please close with a single last line of the form "Verdict: Amara" or "Verdict: Theo", naming whichever of them comes out more right overall, followed within the same line by one sentence giving your most important reason.
