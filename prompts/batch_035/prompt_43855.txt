I'm a distributed systems engineer. For the past fifteen years I've designed and operated services, and the questions that consume my working life are: where state lives, what sits in the critical path, what the blast radius is when a component fails, and which components we own versus merely depend on. I have essentially no philosophy training — I know the phrase "extended mind" from a podcast, and that's the extent of it.

As I understand it, the thesis goes something like this: a man named Otto has an unreliable biological memory and carries a notebook; whenever he needs a fact, he looks it up there, much as another person, Inga, retrieves the same fact from memory. The claim is not that the notebook is a handy tool Otto's mind uses, but that it is literally part of the apparatus doing his remembering — part of his mind. There is also a parity principle in the vicinity: roughly, that if an external process functions the way an internal one does, there is no principled basis for treating them differently. That's the material I want translated.

Please restate the thesis and its main objections in my working vocabulary: hot versus cold state, read-through caches, write-back, consistency guarantees, SLOs, critical path, failure domains, blast radius, owned versus dependent components, vendor dependencies, and incident classification (a defect in our code versus an outage at a provider we call). Be as faithful as you can — don't let the engineering frame quietly settle the question in the course of translating it. Then give me a residue report: what carries over cleanly, what carries over only with distortion, and what has no expression in my terms at all.

I can already sketch some of the translation myself. Otto's notebook looks like a read-through cache whose physical location shouldn't matter to the caller, and the parity principle looks like the rule that we don't classify behavior by which rack a component happens to sit in. What I can't generate from inside my vocabulary is the following. Engineering draws the own/depend boundary for contractual reasons — accountability, and, bluntly, who gets paged — and it is perfectly comfortable with a boundary that is conventional. The philosophers, as far as I can tell, think something more is at stake when they ask whether the notebook is part of the mind, but my vocabulary contains no word for whatever that extra thing is. Two candidates occur to me: (a) the difference between a component being on the critical path and its being constitutive of the service — we only ever track the former, so a debate about the latter may simply have no expression in my terms; and (b) the fact that Otto's notebook can be read, borrowed, or subpoenaed by third parties while his biological memory cannot — a security property, in my idiom.

Tell me whether those two candidates are the genuine residue, or whether there is residue I cannot see from where I stand. And then take a definite stand on the question this is all driving at: is the dispute about the mind's boundary a substantive one that my vocabulary is failing to carry, or is it a boundary-drawing convention dressed up as a metaphysical question? I would rather have a committed verdict with reasons than a survey of positions I could have looked up myself.
