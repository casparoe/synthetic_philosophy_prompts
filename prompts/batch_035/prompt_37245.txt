In the Kant seminar I co-teach, a colleague has taken to ruling on maxims as the students propose them, and I would like to predict her rulings before she delivers them. Her track record so far: securing a loan by promising repayment with no intention of repaying — impermissible; lying about a used car's faults to close the sale — impermissible; answering direct questions about one's own conduct with self-serving denials — impermissible; pursuing one's trade honestly while competing for customers — permissible; giving a candid but unwelcome opinion when asked for advice — permissible; declining invitations one would rather not attend, giving one's real reason — permissible. Two maxims are next on our list: never helping others in need, on the ground that one's time is one's own; and letting one's natural abilities rust in favor of amusement. What should I expect her to say about each? Which rules fit her record equally well — for example, a blanket bar on deception, a test of whether universalization undermines the very practice the maxim relies on, a prudential test, or something closer to Kant's notion of a contradiction in the will — and how exactly do they come apart on these two maxims? Finally, which further maxim, if we added it to our list, would best separate the candidate rules from one another? I am less interested in the predictions themselves than in the method of extracting a rule from a track record, so please also say which rule you would tentatively attribute to her and why.
