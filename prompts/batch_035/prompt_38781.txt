My philosophy blog is aimed at curious non-specialists: readers who know the story of Mary, the colour scientist confined to a black-and-white room, but not the professional jargon. I want a 'which response to the knowledge argument do you actually hold?' quiz for them. It must sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they fancy, so no question may ask 'are you a physicalist?' or 'do phenomenal concepts exist?'.

Construct the classification from the logical space rather than from a list of names. The first split should be: when Mary finally sees something red, does she gain anything epistemically at all? Readers who say no land on one leaf — the Dennett-style response that she could have worked it out in advance. Readers who say yes go on to whether the gain is propositional. The non-propositional branch divides between acquiring abilities (the ability hypothesis associated with Nemirow and Lewis) and acquiring acquaintance (as when one finally meets someone whose file one has memorised). The propositional branch divides over whether the proposition was already known, only under a poorer mode of presentation (the phenomenal-concept strategy associated with Brian Loar), or is a new fact; the new-fact branch divides over whether that fact is physical — in which case the stipulation that Mary already knew all the physical facts has quietly failed — or non-physical, which was Jackson's own conclusion in 1982, before he recanted.

Eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three lettered options. The following cases must figure among them, though you may rephrase: (i) a deaf counterpart who knows all the physical facts about the sound of a violin yet has never heard one — does she know what it sounds like?; (ii) a scientifically illiterate child who has seen red — does the child know what red looks like in the very respect Mary lacks?; (iii) a deceptive presentation in Dennett's spirit, e.g. Mary's first colour experience being of a blue banana while she is asked about ripe bananas; (iv) a meeting-versus-dossier case — you have read everything ever written about a public figure and then meet them; did you learn anything?; (v) Mary's post-release remark 'so that's what they were going on about' — a report of fact, an expression of ability, or something else?

Please answer in British English, with the following sections in this order: 'Preamble' (three or four sentences retelling the case for the quiz header, roughly 80 words); 'The quiz' (the questions, numbered, each with lettered options and no labels); 'Scoring key' (about 250 words or a table, mapping answer patterns onto leaves, naming each position and, where one is standard, a representative defender); 'Blind spots' (about 100 words on pairs of leaves the quiz cannot cleanly separate — at least one such pair must be flagged, for instance the concept-based and the mode-of-presentation ways of being an old-fact physicalist); 'Empty cells' (about 100 words on answer patterns no named position occupies, each with a one-line diagnosis of why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored).
