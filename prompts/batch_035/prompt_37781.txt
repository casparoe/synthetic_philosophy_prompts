I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, but over the last couple of years I've gotten increasingly absorbed in questions about how much the future counts, mostly through climate economics arguments with a coworker and reading assorted blogs on effective giving. After one particularly long argument about discount rates, I tried to write down my actual positions so we'd have something concrete to fight about. Here's where I've landed: (1) the mere fact that a benefit or harm falls further in the future is no ethical reason to count it less — the calendar is not a morally relevant property, so there is no "pure" discounting of the future, only discounting for uncertainty about whether the future will actually arrive; (2) nonetheless I'm comfortable with government cost-benefit analyses applying a positive discount rate to future consumption, since future people will likely be richer (so a dollar then matters less than a dollar now, the way a dollar matters less to a rich person than a poor one), and since capital compounds — that's economics, not ethics; (3) for very long horizons like climate damages I favor a declining discount rate rather than a fixed exponential one, because our uncertainty about growth and catastrophe compounds, and treating each successive century as a repeated coin flip seems wrong; (4) I think rational preferences should be time-consistent — if it's rational for me to adopt a plan now, it shouldn't become irrational to stick with it merely because time has passed, and preferences that flip this way leave you exploitable; (5) relatedly, I think it's not a bias for me to care more about my own next decade than about my own decades after that, because on what I understand to be the standard view of personal identity, "me over time" is really a chain of overlapping psychological stages, and the near-future stages are mine in a much stronger sense; and (6) I donate to near-term global health charities and I think the argument that we should redirect everything toward reducing existential risk, on the grounds that the far future could contain astronomically many people, is a reductio of itself rather than a practical conclusion — though I confess I can't pinpoint exactly where it goes wrong, it just smells like a fallacy of huge numbers.

I'd like an audit of whether this set hangs together. Go through the commitments and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they conflict, and for each real conflict say what exactly has to give and what it costs to give it up. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating everything as equally damning — I want to know where the worst leak is, not a flat list of possible objections. One thing I specifically want checked: I've been assuming that refusing to discount by the calendar (1) and accepting a positive discount rate in policy analysis (2) are consistent, and I suspect (4) and (5) are fine because one is about rationality in general and the other is about my own life, but if either of those assumptions is shaky, say so and explain why. Assume I know no formal philosophy, so wherever a technical term from the literature would be the efficient way to name a problem, define it in a sentence as you go.
