Here is the exam question as the students recieved it:

"John Taurek's 1977 paper 'Should the Numbers Count?' defends the permissibility of flipping a coin between saving a single person, David, and saving five others, in a case where number is the only relevant difference between the two sides. (a) Explain how the coin flip is meant to express equal concern for each of the six people, rather than indifference between the outcomes, and how it is supposed to be consistent with granting that each of the five has a claim on the rescuer no weaker than David's. (b) Taurek accepts pairwise comparisons of losses but denies that five losses, or five claims, combine into something that outweighs the one. Is this position stable, or do the permissions he does accept add up to more than he is prepared to grant? [25 marks]"

And here is the student's answer, verbatim (typos theirs):

"In the central case Taurek can save David or five others but not both — for instance there is a drug David needs all of to survive while the others need only a fifth each. If David is his friend, Taurek says he would save him, and I take the point to be that friendship gives the rescuer a reason of his own, not that David's claim on him is stronger than a stranger's. When all six are strangers, numbers alone do not settle it. Each of the five would lose exactly what David would lose, namely his own life, so each has a claim just as strong as David's. But the five claims do not add up to a stronger claim on that side, because there is nobody who suffers the combined loss — there are just five seperate losses, each no bigger then David's.

The coin flip is how he honours the equal claims. Fairness requires giving each person an equal chance of survival, and that is what the flip does, so nobody is being treated as mattering less than anyone else. It is not indifference: he would obviously rather all six lived, and if he could save the five at no real cost to David he would do that without hesitation. Although there is a tension here, because on Taurek's own view there is no common scale on which losses to different people can be compared at all, so it is unclear what equality of claims even consists in.

On stability: Taurek allows that the rescuer may save any one of the five rather than David — each of those pairwise choices is permitted. But then he is permitted to save each of them, so it is hard to see why he is not permitted to save them all, and once that is granted the coin flip looks like a way of hiding the conclusion his own permissions already point towards. The numbers are doing work inside his view already, just at the level of permissions rather than claims. So the position is unstable: either the pairwise permissions collapse into saving the greater number, or the view cannot explain why a rescuer who saves David shows anyone unequal concern."
