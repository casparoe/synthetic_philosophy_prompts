Health technology assessment bodies like NICE in the UK allocate billions in public healthcare spending using the quality-adjusted life year, or QALY. The QALY scale anchors one year in full health at a value of 1, death at 0, and — because patients in valuation surveys rate some conditions as worse than being dead — allows states to go negative. A new treatment is then judged cost-effective by the cost per QALY gained, against a threshold on the order of £20,000–£30,000 per QALY.

What strikes me is the asymmetry built into this scoring rule. On the downside, the scale is extremely sensitive: an enormous range of suffering states — severe pain, depression, disability — register in fine gradations below 1, some of them well below 0. But on the upside there is a hard ceiling: full health is the maximum, and nothing beyond it counts. A year of robust, joyful, flourishing health scores exactly the same as a year of mere absence of disease, even though most people would take the trade in an instant. So a treatment that takes a patient from agony to discomfort generates measurable value, while one that took someone from "fine" to genuinely thriving would generate none at all. And within the negative region, aggregation is fully linear and commensurable: a year at −1 cancels two years at −0.5, so suffering trades off against suffering at fixed exchange rates — but pleasure above the ceiling never trades against suffering at any exchange rate.

I've been reading about lexical threshold negative utilitarianism — the view that suffering matters in a way that is lexically prior to, or at least not commensurable with, positive experience, sometimes limited by a threshold above which pleasure stops counting. It's struck me that the QALY looks like an accidental implementation of exactly this structure, minus the normative argument. So my questions: What theory of the commensurability of pleasure and pain does the QALY framework actually presuppose, if any? Does the ceiling at full health commit health economists to a negative-utilitarian view of welfare, or is it justifiable as a mere scope restriction — the scale measures health-related quality, and joy is simply out of the instrument's remit — which would be compatible with ordinary hedonistic utilitarianism? Which parts of the practice are strictly required by the presupposed view, and which parts would survive under several rival theories? I'd also like you to consider the emerging alternative of wellbeing-adjusted life years (WELLBYs), which score life satisfaction on a 0–10 scale with no ceiling: what would funding decisions look like under that regime, how would the trade-offs between relieving misery and enhancing happiness be set, and does the fact that policymakers have resisted the switch tell us anything about which theory of value the existing practice genuinely presupposes?
