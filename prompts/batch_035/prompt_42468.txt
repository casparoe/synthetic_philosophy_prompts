A few of us on my team have been reading a popular book about belief formation and product decisions, and the author leans heavily on the classical American pragmatists in one chapter. Since my entire exposure to Peirce and James is that chapter, I can't tell how careful he's being. He summarizes their theory of truth roughly as follows: 'Peirce and James taught that a belief is true exactly when holding it pays off for the believer, so truth is just usefulness by another name, and a belief can be true for you and false for me if it serves you and hampers me. Peirce put this as the claim that truth is simply whatever a community of inquirers ends up finding useful to agree on, and James sharpened it into a license for faith: if believing in God, in free will, or in your own abilities keeps you going, then those beliefs are true for you. Russell demolished this by observing that on James's account Santa Claus would be true for any child made happy by him, and pragmatism never really recovered; today it survives only as the everyday advice to be practical rather than dogmatic.' The book then draws a conclusion for working engineers: that since truth just is what pays off, we should treat a belief about our roadmap or our users as true so long as the team finds it motivating, and drop it once it stops being energizing.

As an engineer, part of me is sympathetic to the first half of this — my tests only ever verify behavior, not some deeper essence, so a 'works in practice' story about truth sounds congenial — but the 'true for you, false for me' part and the roadmap advice feel like they're sliding from a philosophical claim into something like team-building. I also can't shake the feeling that lumping Peirce and James together as one view is doing some work, since I've gathered elsewhere that they had a falling-out over exactly this. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what a scholar of pragmatism would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a genuine misrepresentation? I'd especially like to know whether the 'useful to the believer' reading and the 'true for you' reading are actually what either man held, whether Peirce would have signed onto the version attributed to him, and whether the Santa Claus point is the knock-down the author implies or an objection a defender of the view had a comeback to. Finally, does the author's advice to treat motivating roadmap beliefs as true follow from anything that survives your corrections, or does it depend entirely on the parts that don't?
