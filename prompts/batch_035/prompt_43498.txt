I'm TAing an intro biomedical ethics course this semester, and I've got a stack of midterm answers to grade over the weekend. The unit we just finished was on killing vs. letting die, so the essay question on the midterm was this: "In his 1975 paper 'Active and Passive Euthanasia,' James Rachels uses a pair of bathtub cases involving two men named Smith and Jones to challenge the moral significance of the killing/letting-die distinction. Explain the cases and the argument they are meant to support. Then present what you take to be the strongest objection to Rachels' argument, and assess whether the objection succeeds."

Here's one student's answer, typed out more or less as they wrote it: "Rachels was writing against the AMA's position at the time, which allowed doctors to withhold treatment and let patients die but condemned active euthanasia. To show this distinction is empty, he describes two cases that are identical except for the killing/letting-die difference. Smith wants his young cousin's inheritance, so he sneaks into the bathroom and drowns the child in the bathtub, staging it as an accident. Jones also wants the inheritance, but when he sneaks in he finds the child has already slipped under the water and is drowning, so he just stands there ready to push the child's head back down if needed, and lets him drown. Since the cases match in every other respect (motive, outcome, personal gain), and neither man's death-causing is worse than the other's, the bare killing/letting-die difference can't carry any moral weight by itself. Notably, Smith intends the death while Jones merely foresees it, so the pair is really a test of the doctrine of double effect, and its failure shows that doctrine is in trouble too. The conclusion is that killing and letting die are always morally equivalent, so wherever letting die is permitted, like withdrawing a feeding tube, actively killing must be permitted as well. The strongest objection comes from Philippa Foot, famous for the trolley problem, who argued that if doctors were allowed to actively kill patients, public trust in medicine would collapse, with cascading bad consequences that Rachels never anticipated in the paper. Because of this, Rachels' argument fails and the AMA policy was on solid ground."

I want to give this a fair mark, and honestly it reads pretty convincingly to me, but I've learned from grading before that smooth answers sometimes hide real problems. What grade would you assign out of 100, and could you go through it point by point — what the student gets right, and where exactly they go wrong or overreach — so my comments on the paper are specific enough to justify the number? Don't just give me a vibe, I need the itemized breakdown.
