I've been reading about how three countries handle quotas meant to help disadvantaged groups, and I can't work out whether they're all doing the same thing for different reasons or three genuinely different things. Norway requires company boards to be at least 40% of each sex, so the rule as written protects whichever sex is outnumbered, not women as such. India reserves university seats and government jobs for historically disadvantaged castes, but for some of those castes it cuts out the well-off members — the so-called creamy layer — while making no such income cut for the lowest castes. And American universities, before the Supreme Court struck the practice down, gave an admissions edge to underrepresented racial minorities while also giving one to the children of alumni. What does each practice presuppose about what the preference is actually for: compensating a group for past wrongs, helping individuals who are worse off right now, or getting the group's numbers right for their own sake? And do they presuppose consistent things about whether the group or the individual is the unit that deserves the help? I'm especially stuck on the creamy layer rule: it seems to say the point is individual disadvantage, while the rest of the reservation system seems to say it's group status regardless of individual circumstances. Please answer in American English, and tell me where a practice is genuinely compatible with several of these views rather than committed to just one.
