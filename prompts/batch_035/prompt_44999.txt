Exam question (25 marks):

A child masters a grammar on the basis of finite, noisy, and largely uncorrected linguistic experience. Explain how this observation is developed into the poverty of the stimulus argument for innate linguistic knowledge. Your answer should (i) state the premises of the argument and distinguish its empirical premises from its inferential structure, (ii) say what kind of innateness the argument, if sound, would actually establish — and what it would not, and (iii) assess whether statistical or distributional models of learning answer the argument, being explicit about which premise or premises any such response targets, and whether it targets the argument in its strongest form.

Student's answer:

Chomsky first pressed this argument in his 1959 review of Skinner's Verbal Behavior. The key observation is that the child hears only finitely many sentences, while the grammar they end up with generates infinitely many; since the input is finite and the output is unbounded, the difference must be supplied by something innate. More precisely, the argument runs: (1) children acquire grammars that project far beyond the sentences they have encountered; (2) the data available to them are too sparse and noisy to determine which grammar they acquire — in particular, because parents rarely correct grammatical form, the child receives little evidence that would discriminate among the grammars compatible with what they have heard; (3) children nevertheless converge rapidly and uniformly on the grammar of their community; therefore (4) the constraints that make this possible come from an innate language faculty. The premises are empirical claims, and the inference is an inference to the best explanation, so the argument does not purport to be deductive: its strength turns on whether the rival explanation, general-purpose learning, is genuinely worse at explaining the facts. This much seems right, and empiricists who respond by denying that the argument is valid are attacking a straw man.

What exactly is innate, then? Chomsky's conclusion is that much of the grammar, including many of its specific structural principles, is genetically specified before birth: the child arrives with something close to a complete grammar, with a small number of parameters left open for experience to set. This is why the view is called nativism — the substance of the particular grammar is in the genome, and exposure to a language merely sets a few switches. Defenders concede the argument cannot establish that every detail is innate, only the structural core, but that core is supposed to be specific to language rather than a domain-general capacity like memory, attention, or pattern-finding.

The statistical learning response attacks premise (2). Experiments with infants show that they can track transitional probabilities between syllables and use them to segment words from continuous speech, which means the input contains far more usable signal than the argument assumed. If distributional information of this kind is available, the stimulus is not impoverished, and the argument collapses at its empirical base. Computational models can likewise learn from corpora without any built-in grammatical knowledge, which shows that the inference to innate constraints is not forced: what looked like the need for innate structure was just the assumption that learning had to be explicit and rule-based.

My view is that the argument is a perfectly good abductive pattern but unsound: premise (2) fails once we appreciate the richness of distributional information, so the explanandum it was invoked to explain can be handled by ordinary learning mechanisms. The argument therefore shows at most that learning a language requires some innate endowment, as all learning does, but nothing specific to language.
