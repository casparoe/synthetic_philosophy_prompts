I've been around the rationalist blogosphere since the old LessWrong days, and no analogy is more load-bearing in that corner of the internet than the one between genes and memes: culture, we're told, is a Darwinian system full stop, and ideas survive or perish the way organisms do. My friend -- who runs an online reading group on evolutionary approaches to culture, and is very much the literal-minded type -- has been pressing this on me to support a specific conclusion: if ideas are selected purely for their ability to get themselves copied, then the beliefs that achieve wide currency are optimized for transmissibility rather than for truth. Virality, memorability, making the host evangelize, resistance to disconfirmation -- these are the "phenotypic effects" that memes have been selected for, and so the popularity of a belief is weak evidence of its truth, maybe even negative evidence. He thinks this licenses a standing prior of skepticism toward any belief that has gone epidemic, from religions to the really infectious bits of internet folk wisdom. He's not using the analogy as a metaphor; he insists it's the same mechanism, just running on a different substrate.

The more I read, the more places the mapping seems to fray, though I don't trust myself to sort the fatal cracks from the cosmetic ones. Gene replication is astonishingly high-fidelity; cultural transmission looks like the opposite -- every retelling mangles the content, and someone in my group keeps insisting that most of what we call "copying" is really reconstruction: people rebuild the idea from noisy inputs, and convergence comes from being pulled toward the same natural-feeling endpoint rather than from accurate duplication, which would make the whole copying-and-selection picture a bad description of what's actually happening. Acquired modifications also seem to be inheritable in culture -- a founder revises a doctrine at forty and the revision passes straight to his successors, which biology flatly forbids. And the variation introduced isn't random in the way selection theory needs: it's guided, biased by human psychology, sometimes deliberately designed by people who can anticipate the selection pressures. Add that cultural items have many parents, blend rather than staying particulate, and leap sideways between unrelated lineages constantly, and I can no longer tell which of these disanalogies actually threatens my friend's conclusion and which are just noise.

So here's what I want: a proper audit of the gene–meme analogy as an argument, not as a metaphor. Spell out the correspondence table explicitly -- what's supposed to play the role of gene, phenotype, organism, population, species, reproduction, and selection pressure. Then walk through where the mapping breaks, and crucially tell me which breaks matter for the conclusion my friend is drawing -- the skeptic's prior against epidemic beliefs -- versus which ones are interesting but harmless to it. Does a Darwinian account of culture still predict that surviving ideas are fitness-optimized rather than truth-optimized even if transmission is reconstructive rather than high-fidelity? And if the gene–meme version is too damaged to carry the weight, is there a better analogy -- an epidemiological one, or something in between -- that carries the same or a different conclusion? Please write your answer in British English; the reading group has gone annoyingly anglophile since two of our members moved to Edinburgh, and I want to post it without editorial sniping.
