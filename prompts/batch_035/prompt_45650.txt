I am a novelist some way into a book set during a citywide quarantine, and I would like a careful reading of Camus's The Plague through the contemporary debate over open borders and the state's claimed right to exclude.

The border debate almost always frames the question from inside: may a political community, roughly like a club, refuse membership or entry to outsiders? What strikes me is that Camus approaches the same question from the opposite side. When the gates of Oran close, the people harmed are the insiders. Rambert, the journalist stranded in a town that is not his own, argues that he is a stranger with no stake in its suffering and a life waiting elsewhere; the town's own residents are cut off from the world rather than defended from anyone trying to enter it. So the novel gives us exclusion without membership: the gates bar even natives returning, the measure is meant to be temporary, and it makes no distinctions among those it traps. And then Camus complicates his own case: Rambert, having finally arranged his way out, stays anyway — out of shame, he says, once he learns that Rieux's wife is far away in a sanatorium and Rieux has stayed regardless.

Please give me a reading that is faithful in both directions. Take the border debate seriously: is the closure of Oran the strongest case for a right to exclude, since even most defenders of open borders concede something like quarantine powers? Or does Camus reveal that the concession is larger than it looks — that the ground which justifies excluding outsiders in the plague (collective risk, and being in it together regardless of citizenship) is precisely the ground on which Rambert's claim to leave collapses, so that the novel ends up justifying exclusion at the moment it looks most like solidarity? Does the book suggest that the right to keep people out and the right to keep people in are one right seen from different sides, and if so, which side is doing the moral work? Where the reading strains the text, say so plainly: no outsiders ever try to enter Oran, the quarantine is time-limited where border regimes are permanent, and Rambert's staying may be about shame and love rather than about borders at all — I want to know whether an honest critic can press the analogy anyway. Finish by pointing me to the specific scenes I should hold in view while writing my own quarantine — the closing of the gates, the successive arguments between Rambert and Rieux, the night the gates reopen and the town discovers that it cannot simply resume its old life — and tell me whether the narrator's closing warning, that the bacillus never dies and waits only for the day, supports the border reading or quietly undercuts it.
