I'm a journalist who writes longform features on history and ideas for a general readership, and next month I'm giving the keynote at a mid-sized European university's interdisciplinary workshop on nationalism and memory. My audience will be roughly half historians — two early modernists who work on pre-modern collective identities, a nineteenth-century Eastern Europe specialist who practically owns Hobsbawm and Ranger's edited volume, and an ethnographer who has done fieldwork on commemorative practice — plus a couple of sociologists, a national museum curator, and a scattering of graduate students who will be taking notes. My thesis is this: the modernist canon (invented tradition, imagined communities, print capitalism) is correct as far as it goes, but it's now treated as finished history when it should be treated as an ongoing process. I want to argue that we are living through a second age of tradition-invention, in which digitized archives, algorithmic circulation of origin stories, anniversary culture in the heritage industry, and — most contentiously — AI-generated content quietly backfilling citations for origin myths are manufacturing the appearance of deep continuity at unprecedented speed and scale, and that this deserves the same unsentimental analytic treatment that famous earlier volume gave the kilt and royal pageantry. As evidence I'll draw on my own reporting: a regional food-heritage dispute in which a dish documented only to the 1950s gained protected status on the strength of a widely repeated but unsourced claim of medieval origin; a town's "medieval" festival that turns out to have been revived by a tourism board in the 1970s on the basis of one ambiguous manuscript; and the growing gap between what local archives actually contain and what circulates online as the town's documented past.

I know the modernist literature well enough for my purposes, but I'm a journalist, not an academic, and this room will have read everything I cite plus the counterliterature I haven't. Draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the seven or so objections I'll actually get from this audience — not the strawmen I field from online comment sections when a piece like this runs — ordered by how much damage each would do to my argument, each phrased the way a professional historian would phrase it. For instance, I anticipate some version of the ethno-symbolist point that Anderson and Hobsbawm explain nationalist success too well and can't account for why some invented traditions take root in one population while falling flat in a neighboring one that had the same print markets and the same state schooling; I'd like that phrased properly, plus whatever else is coming that I haven't anticipated. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply I could deliver without notes, a frank note on where that reply is soft, and, where an objection exposes a genuine weakness in my "second wave" framing, a flag for what I should concede up front so that the concession is mine rather than extracted from me. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody in that room asks — the one that would be hardest for my argument specifically, as opposed to the modernist position in general — and tell me why I'm right to fear it.
