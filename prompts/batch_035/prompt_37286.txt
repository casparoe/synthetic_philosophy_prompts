I'm grading finals right now and there's one answer I keep going back and forth on before I commit to a score. Here's the exam question:

"Two after-school math programs were compared; students chose which one to attend. Among students who started the year already strong, Program A's students passed the state test at a rate of 90% (18 of 20) versus 85% for Program B (340 of 400). Among students who started the year weak, Program A's students passed at 40% (160 of 400) versus 35% for Program B (21 of 60). Yet overall, Program B's students passed at 78.5% (361 of 460) while Program A's passed at only 42.4% (178 of 420). (a) Explain how both subgroups can favor Program A while the overall totals favor Program B. (b) For a randomly chosen student, which program should the district trust more, and why? (c) Would your answer to (b) change if students had been randomly assigned to the programs instead of choosing?"

And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"(a) The trick is that the two programs have very different mixes of students. Program A got 20 strong students and 400 weak ones; Program B got 400 strong and only 60 weak. So B's overall pass rate is dominated by strong students, who pass at high rates no matter which program they're in, and A's is dominated by weak students. When you average over mixes like that, the program with the easier clientele can win overall even while losing within each type of student. Check: A's overall rate is (18+160)/(20+400) = 178/420, about 42%, way below its own strong-student rate of 90%, precisely because almost all of A's students are weak. Basically Simpson's paradox is just the ecological fallacy: group-level numbers can point the opposite direction from what's true at the individual level.

(b) Starting ability is a confounder here — strong students disproportionately picked B (it's the expensive one with the reputation), and they'd pass regardless. So the disaggregated comparison is the right one to look at, and A is genuinely better: 90 beats 85 and 40 beats 35. The general lesson is that you should always break your data down on every variable you have available; conditioning on more information can only make your comparison more accurate. The overall numbers are basically meaningless in a situation like this.

(c) If students had been randomly assigned, starting ability would be balanced across the two programs, so the confounder disappears and the overall comparison becomes the right one — and it would now reflect the true difference. With randomization, this kind of reversal can't happen: the subgroups and the aggregate have to agree, since there's no lurking variable doing the mixing anymore. So the answer to (b) wouldn't really change — you'd trust the overall number, and it would agree with the subgroups anyway."

Grade this on a 0–10 scale and walk me through the point-by-point comments that justify whatever score you land on. I want to see exactly which specific claims you'd credit and which you'd take points off for, not just a general impression. For context, we covered confounding and aggregation bias in lecture, and part (c) was intended to be the harder part of the question.
