Retired spectroscopist here — spent 37 years in a lab, got into philosophy a couple years after hanging up my coat, mostly epistemology, and lately I've fallen down the rabbit hole of formal epistemology. I've been chewing on Aumann's agreement theorem, the result that agents who start with a common prior and know each other's opinions can't stay in disagreement once those opinions become common knowledge. And it hit me that 12 Angry Men is practically a controlled experiment on that theorem. You've got eleven jurors voting guilty on the first straw poll, so the group "market" says guilty with near certainty, and juror 8 flatly refuses to update on the other eleven's posteriors. On the theorem's assumptions that refusal is irrational, and the film treats it as the most heroic thing in the movie.

So read the film through the theorem for me. A few things I'd want you to take on:

1. Is the cheap-but-honest reading just that the film denies the common prior — juror 10's prejedice and juror 3's mess with his own son are different priors, so the theorem never applied? Or does the film claim something stronger, that even granting something like shared priors, a lone dissenter who won't budge on consensus can be vindicated?

2. The ending. They converge on not guilty, which superficially looks like the theorem fulfilled — but the convergence is produced entirely by re-examining evidence: the duplicate knife, the old man's hallway timing, the eyeglass marks on the woman's nose. Is re-weighing testimony you already heard "new information" in the theorem's sense, or does the film show convergence happening through channels the theorem says it shouldn't need?

3. Where does the analogy just break? E.g. are the jurors' opinions even common knowledge in the technical sense after that first poll, given that some of them are voting guilty to get it over with rather than from belief?

I want a reading that treats the theorem as actual math and the film as an actual film, not a lecture illustration — and that flags honestly where the film resists the reading. Also, please tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point in your reading you're least sure of. I'd rather know where the ice is thin than get a smooth answer.
