I serve on the allocation panel of a regional health service. We distribute a fixed annual budget for high-cost, non-emergency procedures — joint replacements, dental reconstruction, bariatric surgery, fertility treatment — among far more eligible candidates than the money can cover. Clinicians triage for need, but we're under pressure to add a responsibility-sensitive element, on roughly these grounds: disadvantages arising from circumstances people didn't choose — poverty, genetics, plain bad luck — are the sort of thing a public health system exists to correct, while costs that flow from a person's own informed choices can fairly be left with that person. I gather this family of views is called luck egalitarianism.

Please turn that principle into a decision procedure our panel could actually run. The constraints: we hear a few dozen cases a year in monthly meetings, with under an hour per case; we have only what's in the referral file, which often won't say what a patient knew or could reasonably have known about their risks; and we must give written reasons that can survive an appeal, so every step has to be one we can justify without philosophy jargon. Lay out the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a "no," and where it must hand the case back to the panel's judgment because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts.

Then run it on three candidates: (1) a woman seeking bariatric surgery whose obesity took hold during a childhood in which the affordable calories were the worst ones; (2) a former heavy drinker, sober for two years, who needs a liver-related procedure; (3) a semi-professional rock climber on his third injury, who argues that his sport is central to the life he has reason to value, so charging him for it is like charging someone for their religion or their family. At least one of these should come out in a way you expect me to dislike — the point of the exercise is to see where the principle bites. Finally, be explicit about where the procedure will misfire: where it ends up punishing people for ignorance or addiction it has classified as choice, where verifying "informed choice" would cost more than the treatment, and what the procedure simply cannot do no matter how carefully we build it.
