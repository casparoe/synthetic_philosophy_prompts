I run a weekly politics society at my school — about twenty students aged sixteen to eighteen, one fifty-minute session, an ordinary classroom, no technology. We have just spent a term on how democracies die, and I have noticed a problem in our discussions: everyone uses the phrase "democratic backsliding" confidently, but nobody can say where it starts. One classmate labelled a change to our student-council election rules backsliding simply because it favoured the party she disliked; another insisted nothing counts unless soldiers are in the streets. I want an activity that makes the group feel the difficulty rather than argue about it in the abstract.

Design a game in which the society governs itself for real stakes — the plan for the end-of-term social and a modest budget we genuinely control — under a mini-constitution the players write themselves in the first ten minutes, including a three-person court that rules on disputes and an elected executive who proposes rule changes between rounds. Arrange the rounds so that every change the executive proposes is formally legal under that constitution, but so that the sequence drifts: some proposals are genuine efficiency measures, others quietly shift who counts the votes, who may speak, when elections are held, and who appoints the court. Players must decide, round by round, whether to keep contesting elections, to appeal to the court, or to treat the whole enterprise as no longer worth playing — and the game should end only when enough players simply stop voting, never because anyone announces that backsliding has occurred. If the design can echo the different real trajectories — the slow capture of courts and media, the fast referendum-to-concentrated-power route, the erosion of norms without any constitutional change at all — without naming countries until the debrief, so much the better; the cases I have in mind are Hungary, Poland, Turkey and the United States.

Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the real distinctions: the difference between changing the rules and eroding the informal norms of forbearance and mutual toleration that the rules presuppose; the puzzle of whether a change can be legal and still be backsliding; the question of what fixes the baseline against which decline is measured, and who is entitled to fix it; and the awkward observation that some of us will have cried backsliding only when our side lost — the suspicion that the term is a partisan label dressed up as a description. I want the debrief to force us to say what work the concept does that "our opponents won" does not.

I would also like the honest parts. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — players treating it as a joke, the court being captured in round one, apathy arriving for the wrong reasons — and how I might handle each. Be frank about what the game cannot reproduce: a decade's timescale, genuine fear, the outside anchors such as foreign leverage that mattered in the real cases. And in the debrief notes, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study this: I do not want the group leaving with the impression either that there is an agreed definition, or that it is merely a partisan quarrel, when the truth is somewhere more interesting.
