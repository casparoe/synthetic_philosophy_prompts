After a career spent dating rocks for a national geological survey, I retired and joined a reading group that worked through Darwin and has now arrived at Marx. Having spent four decades among quantities that no political arrangement could alter, I find myself troubled by an argument I have assembled from the 1859 Preface, and I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis of it: which step to reject, and what rejecting it costs.

The Preface, as I read it, holds that the relations of production constitute the economic structure of society, the real foundation on which arises a legal and political superstructure, and that it is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but their social existence that determines their consciousness. From this I have constructed the following:

(1) Every element of social life belongs either to the economic structure or to what that structure brings forth; the doctrine admits of no third category.
(2) Natural science is an element of social life: it is carried on by people, housed in institutions, and funded and directed according to the priorities of whoever rules.
(3) Therefore the content of natural science in any epoch is part of what the economic structure of that epoch brings forth, and would have been different had the structure been different.
(4) But I have measured isotope decay constants for most of my adult life, and no transformation of the mode of production would have made a single one of them come out otherwise; a feudal laboratory and a socialist laboratory find the same half-life.
(5) So either the decay constants are exempt from the foundation — in which case (1) is false and something in social life escapes the economic structure — or historical materialism entails that a different mode of production would have produced a different physics, a conclusion I am unable to accept.

I cannot locate the fault. If I had to bet, I would put it in (3), but (3) strikes me as the whole point of the doctrine, which is precisely what worries me. And the exemption route in (5) has a cost I can see coming: if natural science is exempted because it has a subject matter with a logic of its own, I see no principled way to refuse the same courtesy to jurisprudence and theology, which also have internal logics; the exemption threatens to swallow the doctrine whole.

Our group's resident philosopher offers two reassurances. One is the phrase "relative autonomy of the superstructure," which so far seems to do more work as a phrase than as an explanation. The other is a letter Engels wrote to Joseph Bloch in 1890, in which he says that the ultimately determining element in history is the production and reproduction of real life, and that if somebody twists this into saying that the economic element is the only determining one, he transforms that proposition into a meaningless, abstract, senseless phrase. I take the point, but I notice that Engels also concedes there that superstructural elements often preponderate in determining the form of historical struggles, with the economic movement asserting itself only "finally." This leaves me unsure whether the last instance ever arrives in any given question, and if it never arrives, I do not know what remains of the thesis beyond the truism that material life matters a great deal.

I would like to keep two things: the observational anchor in (4), which I will not surrender on any terms, and the explanatory priority of the economic base, which is what made the doctrine attractive to me in the first place. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked innocent, and what each way out has to pay — including whether the Bloch letter's "ultimately" is a genuine solution or only a postponement.
