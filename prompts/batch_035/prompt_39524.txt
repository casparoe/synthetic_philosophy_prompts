I'm a novelist, halfway through my third book, and I've hit a snag that's less about plot than about how minds work, so bear with me.

The POV character, Maren, is a fairly new birder. Early on a friend tells her a rare warbler has been spotted in the county. After that, Maren starts seeing "the warbler" everywhere — a flick of yellow in the bushes that she'd previously have clocked as a goldfinch, or as nothing at all, now jumps out at her, and twice she genuinely swears she saw the field marks before the bird flew off and it turned out she was wrong. Later she's told the warbler was almost certainly never in the county at all, and the bushes still look warbler-y to her. Her more experienced birding companion, who never believed the sighting, looks at the same hedges and just... sees sparrows.

My agent says these scenes read as either Maren lying to herself or me being lazy with a dramatic device, because perception doesn't work that way — you see what's there, and belief only comes in afterward, when you interpret. But I wrote them intending the opposite: that what Maren was told literally changes what shows up in her visual experience, that the yellow flick becomes more vivid, more salient, more insistently *there*.

So, questions. Is my version actually defensible, psychologically and philosophically? Is there a real difference between "her belief changed what she saw" and "her belief changed what she noticed and concluded" — and should I as a writer care which one is true? If the second one is the right story, how would I even rewrite the scenes so the distortion happens at the level of attention or judgment instead — like, how do you show a character mis-judging on the page without just narrating "she judged"? And related: if perception really can be shaped by what you believe, does that make Maren's visual experience less trustworthy in some principled way, or is that fine — her companion certainly throws "you see what you want to see" at her in chapter nine, and I honestly don't know who's suppossed to win that argument. I want to get this right without the book turning into a psych paper, but I also don't want a reviewer catching me doing something no philosopher of mind would let slide.
