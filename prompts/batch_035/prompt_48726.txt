I'm taking an undergraduate course in democratic theory, and last week we covered the Condorcet jury theorem. The way it was presented, if each member of a large electorate is even slightly more likely than not to judge correctly on some question — even 51% reliable — then as the number of voters grows, the probability that the majority judgment is correct approaches one. My professor mentioned in passing that this result is sometimes treated as the mathematical backbone of epistemic democracy, and that its assumptions do a lot of quiet work, but we ran out of time before saying what that work is.

Afterwards my friends and I got into an argument about what the theorem actually licenses. It seems obvious to me that if a majority of millions of voters is almost certainly right whenever each voter has even a slight edge over a coin flip, then for contested factual questions — say, whether a particular vaccine causes a serious side effect at some low rate, or whether a proposed minimum wage increase would raise unemployment in a mid-sized city — a national referendum should strictly dominate deferring to a panel of a few dozen experts: the theorem guarantees the referendum's near-certain correctness, while a small panel enjoys no such asymptotic guarantee, and the theorem itself tells us that more voters is always better. My friends think this is obviously crazy, but when I pressed them they couldn't point to where the reasoning breaks. Can you settle this: who is right, and if they are, what exactly is wrong with the referendum argument?
