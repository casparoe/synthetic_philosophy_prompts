A friend of mine who took a philosophy of language course last year recently tried to explain Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment to me, and I would like to know how much of his summary I can trust. He put it roughly like this: "Putnam asks you to imagine a planet exactly like ours, except that the stuff filling its rivers, lakes, and rainclouds — call it XYZ — is indistinguishable from water in every everyday respect: how it looks, tastes, quenches thirst, and sustains crops, while having a different underlying chemical structure. Suppose Oscar on Earth and Twin Oscar on Twin Earth are molecule-for-molecule duplicates, so that every internal state of one is shared by the other. When each says 'water is good for you,' the two utterances are psychologically identical, yet the words mean different things, since one tracks H2O and the other XYZ. This shows that the meaning of every word is partly fixed by the environment, so what you mean is never entirely in your head. It also shows that the divergence only happened once chemistry matured: back in 1750, before anyone knew the composition of water, Earthlings and Twin Earthlings meant exactly the same thing by 'water,' and the two meanings only came apart when scientists discovered that Earth water is H2O and Twin water is XYZ. That is why Putnam concluded that what a word means is settled not by individual speakers but by the community of experts — chemists in this case — and hence that a dictionary's definition is strictly speaking obsolete the moment science moves on."

The basic setup sounds right to me, but a couple of his claims felt off, and I don't yet know enough to say where. The remark about 1750 seems to cut against the point of the example: if the meanings were genuinely identical before anyone knew the chemical facts, wouldn't the internalist simply say that meanings are fixed by speakers' descriptions until science arrives, leaving Putnam with a much weaker conclusion than my friend suggested? And the sweeping claim that every word's meaning is partly external does not seem to follow from a case about a natural-kind term like "water" — does Putnam really extend the point to all vocabulary, including logical words like "and" or mathematical terms? I am also unsure whether the "community of experts" part is meant to be a further discovery or just a restatement of the first point.

I would be grateful if you could go through the summary claim by claim and separate what a philosopher of language would accept, what is a common simplification or harmless gloss, and what is a genuine misreading of Putnam. Then please tell me which of the conclusions my friend drew — the universal externalism claim, the pre-chemistry divergence point, and the remark about dictionaries — actually follow from the parts of his summary that are accurate, and which do not. For context, I am a second-year student reading Putnam for the first time, so feel free to introduce the standard terminology (narrow versus wide content, the division of linguistic labor, natural-kind terms) where it does real work, but I would rather have a careful audit than a survey lecture.
