I'm a novelist about halfway through a draft set in a small alpine tourist town (fictional, but modeled on places in the Tyrol). Plot thread: a young local historian digging through parish records discovers that the town's famous midsummer procession -- the one everyone knows has happened since time immemorial, torches carried down from the peak at dusk, the whole identity of the place hangs on it -- was actually cooked up in the 1930s by a hotelier and the parish priest to draw hikers in. Basically a marketing scheme that got sanctified over three generations. My question is about what happens next, sociologically and philosophically, because I don't want to write it as either farce or tradegy.

Do people drop a ritual once they learn it's fake? From what little I've picked up about real cases, they seem to often double down instead, and the thing carries on as though nothing happened. And is the procession somehow less real now? My protagonist (the historian) thinks the whole thing is a fraud and wants to blow it up publicly. Her mother, who runs the biggest hotel in town, says it doesn't matter where it came from: it's the thing that makes them a we. Without it the town wouldn't exist as a town, would just be a bunch a houses on a mountain. I want to stage their argument as a real argument, not have one of them be a strawman -- the daughter shouldn't come off as a pedant and the mother shouldn't come off as cynical.

So: how would you think about who's right here, or what each of them is right about? And any thoughts on the most plausible way the town actually reacts when the news comes out -- quietly ignored, scandal, the founding date quietly re-dated to the 1930s and celebrated anyway? I'm trying to get the texture right, not a thesis. Also curious whether there's a principled difference between a ritual that's three generations old and one that's three hundred, or whether that line is itself something the town just draws wherever it needs to.
