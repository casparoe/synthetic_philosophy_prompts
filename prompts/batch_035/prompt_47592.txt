My seminar reconstructed a version of the classic triviality argument from the probabilities-of-conditionals literature, and its conclusion is obviously too strong, yet every step still looks fine to me individually. I'd like you to find where it goes wrong, say which step I should give up, and tell me what giving it up costs.

The argument: (1) For an indicative conditional, the probability of the conditional equals the corresponding conditional probability: P(A→B) = P(B|A), whenever P(A) > 0. This seems to be just the Ramsey test: to evaluate "if A, B", add A to your stock of beliefs and see how confident you are in B. (2) Conditionals are propositions in the language like any others, so their probabilities combine under the ordinary laws; in particular, for any X with 0 < P(X) < 1, P(A→B) = P(A→B|X)·P(X) + P(A→B|¬X)·P(¬X). (3) The equation in (1) is not a mere coincidence of one's current credences but a constraint of rationality, so it survives learning: after conditionalizing on X, it still holds, giving P(A→B|X) = P(B|A∧X), and likewise for ¬X. (4) Substituting (3) into (2): P(B|A) = P(B|A∧X)·P(X) + P(B|A∧¬X)·P(¬X). (5) But the law of total probability applied within A also gives P(B|A) = P(B|A∧X)·P(X|A) + P(B|A∧¬X)·P(¬X|A). (6) Since B can be chosen so that P(B|A∧X) and P(B|A∧¬X) take any values whatsoever, (4) and (5) can agree only if P(X) = P(X|A) and P(¬X) = P(¬X|A), for arbitrary A and X: every proposition is independent of every other. (7) Universal independence holds only in trivial probability spaces — roughly, ones in which no three mutually exclusive propositions all receive positive probability. (8) Therefore no rational agent can be uncertain among three or more alternatives.

That conclusion is absurd, and the absurdity is not distant: I am about a third confident in each of three suspects, and I am far more confident that if the butler did it, the weapon is in the pantry than that the weapon is in the pantry unconditionally — clause (1) is what gives that contrast its point, so I'd much rather keep (1) than (2), and (3) looked to me like the most innocent of the lot. Still, I'd rather know what each available rejection costs than keep the step I happen to like. Please also say how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of.
