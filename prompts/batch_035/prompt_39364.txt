This came up in my AI safety seminar and I've been going round in circles since. The unit we just did was on control as opposed to alignment, and the guest lecturer kept summarising the whole approach as: treat the model like an untrusted employee. The picture is a new hire at a bank who might be cooking the books — you don't psychoanalyse them, you don't need to know whether they're "really" dishonest at heart, you just make sure there are audits, separation of duties, dual sign-off on transfers, tripwires in the accounts. His claim was that this is exactly why control is a better research bet than alignment: alignment needs you to work out the model's inner motives (which nobody knows how to do), control just assumes the worst about behaviour and designs so it doesn't matter, the way the bank doesn't need a theory of the employee's soul to stop fraud.

I sort of bought it until our tutorial, where the tutor made me try to say what actually maps onto what and I couldn't. The employee has a life outside the job, gets tired, needs the salary, can be fired and sued and prosecuted — all of which is doing real work in how containment for actual employees works. The model can be copied a million times, never gets bored, isn't deterred by threats unless they were somehow built into it, and if it IS misaligned its goals might not connect to any of the levers a bank can pull on a person. Also, honestly, banks accept some fraud as a cost of doing business — the containment is calibrated to "sometimes we lose money", which seems like a very different calibration than "sometimes the model seizes control of everything". And the insider is one person with human-bandwidth abilities, whereas a deceptive model might be coordinating across every deployment of itself at once. But maybe none of that matters, because the conclusion was only ever "behavioural containment is more tractable than motive-reading", and that doesn't obviously need the employee at all?
