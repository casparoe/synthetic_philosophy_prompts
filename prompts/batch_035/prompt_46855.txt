I teach a third-year module on determinism, laws, and free will, and next week's seminar is meant to bridge the metaphysics we have been doing with the philosophy of physics. A student has sent me the opening pages of a popular science book she is reading, and its summary of what chaos theory shows about determinism will, I suspect, do more harm than good in my students' essays if they take it at face value. I would be grateful for a careful audit of it before I decide how to use it as a teaching text. The passage reads:

Chaos theory delivered the death blow to the clockwork universe. Classical physics was deterministic: given the exact state of the world at one moment, the laws of physics fix everything that happens thereafter. Chaos theory showed that this picture is empirically empty, because in chaotic systems the tiniest difference in initial conditions is amplified exponentially, so no measurement, however precise, can pin down the future. This is the famous butterfly effect: a butterfly flapping its wings in Brazil can set off a tornado in Texas, which means small causes have large effects and the future is genuinely open. And since quantum mechanics tells us that nature is irreducibly random at the microscopic level, chaotic systems amplify that randomness into the macroscopic world, so that even a Laplacean demon equipped with the exact laws and exact initial data could not forecast a chaotic system. Determinism, on this view, is not so much false as meaningless: unpredictability is baked into the laws of nature themselves.

Please go through this claim by claim and tell me which parts a philosopher of science would accept as stated, which are defensible simplifications, and which are genuine conflations — I have my eye on the slide from unpredictability to indeterminism, and on what the butterfly effect was originally invoked to show, but I want to know whether I am being too harsh. In particular: does chaos theory pose any threat at all to determinism as an ontological thesis, rather than to predictability as an epistemic one, given that the paradigm chaotic systems are themselves strictly deterministic? Is the claim about the Laplacean demon flatly wrong, or is there a serious line of thought about the amplification of quantum indeterminacy by non-linear dynamics that the populariser is gesturing at but overreaching with? And does the concluding sentence — that determinism is rendered meaningless rather than false — follow from anything true in the passage? Where the populariser keeps two distinctions apart that my students habitually run together, please make those distinctions explicit, since I want to mark their essays against them. Please write your reply in British English.
