I'm preparing a decision-tree handout for a graduate seminar on mechanism design and social choice, provisionally titled "So you want a voting rule that can't be gamed." The handout's purpose is diagnostic: students arrive knowing they dislike the conclusion of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem but have usually never worked out which of its assumptions they are actually willing to surrender, since in casual discussion all escape routes sound alike. The tree should take them from an entry commitment to a specific position, and it should be built from the logical space rather than from a menu of famous names.

The entry point: a designer faces a group of at least three voters who must select one of at least three alternatives, every alternative is in principle admissible, preferences are private information, and the designer wants a rule under which truthful preference revelation is weakly dominant. From that entry point, the tree should descend through branch nodes, each phrased as a question a student could answer without knowing which position it leads to — for instance, whether the designer is allowed to observe or elicit intensities of preference rather than only rankings, whether the voters' preferences can plausibly be assumed to have some structure over a known political spectrum, whether lotteries over alternatives are an acceptable output, whether the identity of the person who benefits from a biased rule matters or only the existence of such a person, and whether occasional profitable misrepresentation, in the sense of a rule that is manipulable in principle but only under conditions of coordination and information that large electorates rarely meet, is a price worth paying. Each branch label should name the theorem's assumption that the question is probing: the ordinal framework, domain richness, single-valuedness or determinism, non-dictatorship, the onto condition, strategy-proofness itself, and any others you think belong on the tree.

The leaves should include, at minimum: the peaks-only rules defined on single-peaked domains (with the median-voter outcome as their most familiar member); the VCG family and its kind, where strategy-proofness is bought with cardinal preferences and real transfers; random dictatorship; the honest acceptance of a dictator; the position that restricts the outcome set so the onto condition fails and lives with what that restriction does to the choice problem; and the position that keeps an ordinal, deterministic rule and simply tolerates manipulation in large electorates. In addition, I want one leaf for a view I have not seen given a name: the rule that is formally strategy-proof on the ballot space it offers voters, but where the real power lies with whoever draws up that ballot space — the agenda-setter's paradise. Include it, and then say in a postscript whether you think it deserves leaf status or whether it dissolves into one of the other branches once examined closely; I genuinely don't know, and I'd rather the handout be honest about it than tidy.

Two further requirements. First, wherever the tree cannot really separate two positions — for example, whatever pair you find cannot be pried apart by any question answerable before the labels are known — flag the pair explicitly rather than inventing a question that only appears to distinguish them. Second, include the unoccupied cells: the combinations of branch answers that lead nowhere, diagnosed in a sentence each as to whether nobody holds that view because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is simply unexplored. I would like at least three such diagnoses, and I am especially curious about combinations adjacent to the agenda-setter's paradise.

Keep the whole instrument to at most twelve internal nodes, with each leaf carrying a name (or "unnamed" if no one defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Assume the students know the statement and proof of the theorem itself; the handout begins after that, at the moment of choosing what to give up.
