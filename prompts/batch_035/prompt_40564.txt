I'm a PhD student in political science, working on nationalist memory, and there is a pattern I keep running into that I can't find a term for. A political cause, while it still had a live chance of success, is a mess: its supporters quarrel among themselves, its leaders make cynical deals, many who fight for it are motivated by ordinary material interests, and some of its own partisans are half ashamed of it. Then it is not merely beaten but finished — defeated so decisively that it never has to govern. And from that point on, the community that identified with it begins remembering it as pure and unified: the internal quarrels drop out, the compromises are never mentioned, the grubby motives get reassigned to outsiders, and the defeat itself is retold as the cause's finest hour. Stranger still, the defeated cause seems to serve its community better than a victory would have: winning would have meant governing, splitting over the spoils, and disappointing people, while defeat keeps the cause permanently young and permanently innocent. I can point at instances: the Confederacy, Masada, the Serbian memory of the battle of Kosovo. Is there an established concept that captures this exact configuration — decisive defeat, retroactive purification, and a cause that is more useful to its community dead than it ever was alive? I know of the "Lost Cause" as a name for the specific Southern American ideology, and of the idea of invented tradition, but the first looks like a case of the pattern rather than the pattern itself, and the second says nothing about defeat being what does the purifying. If nothing established covers it, coin a term, define it precisely, and draw its boundaries — in particular, does the pattern require that the defeat be old and settled rather than recent and still politically contested, and does it survive cases where the cause is partly vindicated later on? Please don't give me a bare name and stop: I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and how it differs from each nearby concept. Answer in American English, and end with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing either the established term or "no established term" followed by your coinage.
