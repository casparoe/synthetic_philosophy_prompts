I am a novelist by trade — thirty years of making things up for a living — and I serve on the jury of a small foundation's biennial fellowship for an unpublished novelist. The fellowship is substantial: two years of funding, a residency, and an introduction to agents and editors. The deed of gift, drafted by a benefactor now dead, says the award shall honor and further the art of the novel, and it leaves it at that. We have two finalists, and the jury is deadlocked, so I have been asked to write the decision memo that will circulate before our final meeting. I would like you to draft it for me to revise and sign.

The facts are these. The first finalist, R., is twenty-nine and the daughter of a celebrated novelist. Her manuscript is, by common agreement on the jury, extraordinary — formally daring, emotionally precise, finished in eleven months, much of it written at a fully funded residency. It emerges that two complete line edits were performed on it by a well-known freelance editor retained, and paid, by her father. Our rules require disclosure of substantial editorial assistance; R. disclosed this as having a friend read the manuscript and give notes, which is true the way a manslaughter is a disagreement. The second finalist, S., is fifty-six, self-taught, and worked the manuscript over nine years on a phone during breaks at a warehouse job. No one helped him. The prose is uneven, occasionally startling, structurally rough. He needs the fellowship in the ordinary human sense; R. does not.

The jury's split is instructive. Our chair argues that the moment we judge anything but the page, we become a hardship agency rather than a literary one, and that the page-judging standard is the only one that can be applied without sentimentality. Others respond that a pure standard of this kind quietly converts inherited advantage into earned distinction — that the finished manuscript is not a self-originating achievement, and that if we reward only what arrives polished, we will mostly reward those who were handed the polishing. A third position, which I find most interesting and hardest to answer, concedes that desert is implicated but doubts that desert favors S. at all: his nine years of persistence, on one reading of the matter, are themselves an unchosen trait of temperament, no more earned than R.'s upbringing, so that the moment we leave the page behind, our reasons for preferring one biographical story to another start to dissolve. Meanwhile the fellowship's forward-looking function complicates everything, since it is meant to launch a career, and the argument about what a career ought to be given is not the argument about what a manuscript deserves.

Please draft the memo: a clear recommendation for one of the two finalists (or, if you genuinely think deferral or a split is defensible, say so, but understand the foundation would resist reopening the cycle), reasons the other jurors could act on rather than merely admire, and a serious engagement with the strongest argument on the losing side rather than a dismissive paragraph. The memo should also propose a concrete, workable disclosure rule for editorial assistance that we can apply in future cycles — one that distinguishes assistance we can live with from assistance we cannot, in terms a jury could actually administer. I would like the philosophical question treated as live rather than settled: the distinction between rewarding a work, rewarding a person's desert, and investing in a future output runs through this case, and the memo is worthless to me if it pretends otherwise. Keep it to something the jurors will read on a Tuesday night — say, under a thousand words.
