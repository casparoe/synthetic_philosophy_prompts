I'm teaching an upper-level epistemology seminar — 14 students, one 90-minute session — and next week is norms of assertion: should we say you may assert p only if you know p, only if p is true, or only if you believe p? I don't want to open with a lecture, because everyone will just pick their favourite on vibes. I want a game where they have to *enforce* a norm against each other before they've read a word of the literature, and then discover in the debrief which norm they were actually enforcing. Design it for me.

Some things I want baked in. The core mechanic should be students making assertions about stuff and other students challenging them, with a payoff scheme that uses tokens, no real money. The assertions shouldn't all be on a par — I want the deck (or cards, or whatever you come up with) to deliberately include at least: a lottery-type case where someone asserts something true that they could not possibly know (something like asserting "my ticket will lose" at 1000-to-1 odds); a Moore-type case where someone asserts something true that they nonetheless don't believe; and a case where someone asserts a falsehood in complete good faith, on what anyone would agree was excellent evidence. Maybe others if you think they earn their place. The point is that each of these should get one norm agreeing and another one dissenting, so the students' actual rulings become data.

The hard design problem, which I want you to actually solve rather than wave at: if payoffs track whether the asserted proposition turns out true, you've smuggled in the truth norm and the whole exercise is rigged. The interesting question is what counts as a *proper* assertion given what the asserter had, not what was true — but you can't score propriety without already taking a side on which norm it is. I've been toying with having a rotating student referee who rules on challenges, and treating the pattern of rulings as the data instead of the token counts — but you tell me whether that works or whether there's something cleverer. Whatever you land on, the group has to run into the question of what a successful challenge even requires — whether the natural response to "why should I believe you?" is "because it's true", "because I believe it", or "because I know it" — and that should come from them, not from me.

Then the debrief: map the specific moments onto the three-way debate, showing which cases cut against which norm, and let people who ruled one way in the heat of the game try to defend it in the cold light afterwards. I'd also like an honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guess: nobody challenges anything for the first ten minutes, and someone will try to tell their friend the answer — build around that), and a frank note on which feature of the debate no game like this can reproduce, so I know what I have to supply by talking afterwards.

One more thing — the students haven't seen Gettier or anything on testimony yet, so don't assume vocabulary they don't have; the game should work on raw intuitions, and any jargon should arrive only in the debrief. Please write the whole thing in British English (behaviour, recognise, and so on), and keep it practical — actual rules, timings, card contents, not a general sketch. I'd rather recieve something I could run tomorrow morning than something philosophically airtight but vague, though obviously I want both if I can get it.
