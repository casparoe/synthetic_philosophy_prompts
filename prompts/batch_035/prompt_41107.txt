I spent most of my working life as a systematic entomologist, forty years of sorting museum drawers and arguing about whether a marginal population is a subspecies or a full species, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of biology to figure out what exactly we were arguing about all those years. The species problem itself I have come to accept as chronic. What I cannot stop chewing on is how the law deals with it. Here in the United States, the Endangered Species Act of 1973 attaches protection, and with it enormous sums of money, land-use restrictions, and litigation, to "species" — including subspecies and, for vertebrates only, distinct population segments of an interbreeding species. Yet every systematist knows that the boundaries between species are drawn partly by convention. When the taxonomic fashion shifts and a committee of my former colleagues lumps two species into one, a population that was endangered on Tuesday becomes part of a common, secure species on Wednesday, without a single organism in it having changed. I remember cases where the split or the lump was actively litigated — where the taxonomists were, in effect, being asked to rule on the legal question under cover of a scientific one.

So my question is: what must a practice like this be presupposing about what species are? The statute seems to require that species be real, discrete, countable, and discoverable — that there is a fact of the matter about whether the beetles in this canyon are a species or a population of a wider one — which sits badly with the conventionalist views many of my colleagues privately held. Can you reconstruct the implicit metaphysics of the listing practice and tell me which accounts of species would actually license it? The biological species concept, the phylogenetic species concept, and the phenetic cluster approach each seem to hand the law a different set of entities, and some of them (the phylogenetic one especially) threaten to multiply entities far past what any administrative process can list. Or is the practice philosophically underdetermined, workable under several of these views so long as a competent committee picks one and applies it consistently? And where do the awkward corners fit — the vertebrates-only clause for distinct population segments, which looks like pure metaphysics-by-insect-order, or the fact that the organisms to be saved are individuals while the thing being saved is an abstraction?

I would particularly like the last part of your answer to be forward-looking. Suppose we conceded the conventionalist point and rebuilt the practice around something other than species rank — say, protecting evolutionarily significant units, or divergent lineages as such, or even something as alien to me as protecting ecosystems and letting the species fall where they may. What would have to change institutionally: who counts as the relevant expert, what would litigation be about, and would the practice gain coherence or just acquire a new set of boundary problems at a different scale? I have my own suspicion, that the law has quietly been operating on a pragmatic nominalism all along and would barely notice the metaphysics shifting underneath it, but I would like to see whether the presupposition analysis bears that out.
