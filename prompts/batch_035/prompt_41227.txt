I run a small furniture restoration workshop, and for the past couple of years I have been reading philosophy in the evenings, mostly on my own. I have become fascinated by Aristotle's treatment of exchange and money in the first book of the Politics, where he separates the kind of acquisition that serves a household from the kind that treats accumulating money as an end in itself. I have drafted the outline and partial text of an essay on this, and I would be grateful for comments on it. I should say up front that I have no academic training in philosophy, so I would welcome corrections of anything I have gotten wrong, not just encouragement.

The working title is "The Ruler That Wants to Be the Longest." My thesis, in brief: Aristotle objects to money-making as an end not merely because of a conservative preference for the "natural" life of the household, but because of something structural about measurement. Money, on his account, exists to make dissimilar goods commensurable — it is the common measure by which a bed and a house can be compared and exchanged. My claim is that his real complaint about the unlimited pursuit of wealth is that the instrument of measurement turns itself into the thing measured and accumulated. The ruler tries to become the longest thing it measures. That, I want to argue, is what makes the practice disordered in his eyes, rather than simply excessive.

The draft proceeds as follows.

Section one sets out the distinction between the art of household management and the art of acquisition, and the further distinction within acquisition between the natural form (getting what a household genuinely needs) and the form oriented at retail trade and, at the extreme, at interest. I follow his thought that money-making for its own sake has no natural limit, unlike acquisition for use, which stops when the need is met.

Section two develops the measurement reading. Aristotle says, if I understand him rightly, that it is need that holds exchange together, and that money is the measure that makes goods commensurable in respect of need. So exchange value, on his picture, is not a property goods carry in themselves but something a convention imposes so that equality can be established between very different things. I then introduce my thesis about the measuring device becoming the measured quantity, and I illustrate it with the case of interest, which Aristotle treats with special contempt as the most unnatural form of acquisition: money, which exists to circulate as a measure, is made to breed more of itself, so that the yardstick not only wants to be the longest thing it measures but claims to reproduce.

Section three is where I am least confident, and I would particularly like your comments here. I worry that the natural/unnatural line leaks. Aristotle seems to allow that money arose naturally out of barter once communities grew and exchange had to go through a common measure. But if unlimited money-making is a natural outgrowth of practices he calls natural, then the distinction between the two forms of acquisition seems to rest on a distinction his own story quietly dissolves. In the draft I am considering conceding this and reframing the essay: perhaps his considered view should be that the disorder is real even though the boundary is not sharp, in the way that someone can acknowledge a spectrum between eating for nourishment and eating as compulsion without being able to name the exact bite where one becomes the other. I am not sure whether that rescue is philosophically respectable or a rhetorical sleight of hand, and I would value your judgment.

Section four is a short comparison with the discussion of exchange in the fifth book of the Nicomachean Ethics, where Aristotle talks about proportionate reciprocity and treats money as making things equated. My question for you is whether these two texts can be read as I am reading them, namely as two presentations of a single thought about measurement and need, or whether the Ethics passage belongs to a different project (justice) and will not bear the weight I am putting on it. I have read that some scholars think the two discussions do not fit together cleanly, and I would like to know whether my use of the Ethics passage is defensible or whether I should drop the comparison and rest on the Politics alone.

Finally, a few general requests. Please comment on accuracy first: I have no one to check me, and I would rather hear that a section rests on a misreading than that it is nicely written. Second, please tell me whether the ruler-as-longest thesis strikes you as a genuine interpretive point about Aristotle or as me attributing to him a thought he never had. Third, if you see places where the essay would be more interesting if it went further — for instance, whether the measurement thesis has anything to say about economies organized entirely around accumulation — I would be glad to know, though I want to keep the essay anchored in Aristotle rather than turning it into a commentary on the present day.
