So I've been going down a rabbit hole for the past few weeks on this question of whether one false theory can be objectively "closer to the truth" than another false theory. I have no philsophy background at all (I fix HVAC systems for a living), so bear with me. It started when a friend and I were arguing about old astronomy. Copernicus still assumed perfect circular orbits, so on actual predictions he wasn't wildly better than the epicycle people, and yet almost everyone feels he was "on the right track" in a way Ptolemy wasn't. Same with Newton vs Einstein — Newton is technically wrong about gravity but obviously not AS wrong as, say, some theory where planets are pushed by angels. I later found out this whole puzzle has a name, verisimilitude or truthlikeness or something, and that Popper guy apparently got obsessed with it and it kind of blew up in his face, but I want to work through it myself rather than read about it.

Here's my own attempt at a measure, and the trap my friend says he found in it. First attempt: closeness to truth = how many observable predictions the theory gets right. But Ptolemy's system predicted planetary positions pretty decently for its day while being flat wrong about the mechanism, so prediction-matching can't be the whole story — you also need the theory's actual content, the stuff it asserts about how things work, to line up with reality. Second attempt: measure how much of the theory's content is true versus false. My friend's trap: take any theory T, and conjoin it with some random TRUE but irrelevant sentence (say, "Paris is the capital of France"). The new theory has all of T's truth plus one extra bit of truth and no extra falsehood, so it should be strictly CLOSER to the truth than T. But that's insane — gluing geography trivia onto your astronomy shouldn't make your astronomy more truthlike. And if you block that by ignoring content entirely and only matching predictions, you're back to rewarding Ptolemy. So maybe every road here ends in weirdness.

Give me your actual credence that there exists a principled, non-arbitrary definition of "closer to the truth" that (a) gets the obvious cases right (Einstein closer than Newton, Newton closer than angels) and (b) doesn't fall into either of the two traps above or some other equally dumb trap. And decompose it for me: like, the probability that "the truth" is even the right kind of target for a structured theory and not just for a list of predictions, the probability that the accuracy intuition and the content intuition can actually be reconciled in one definition, the probability that my friend's Paris-conjunction trap is genuinely fatal rather than just a technical wrinkle that a cleverer formalism dodges, plus any other terms you think belong — give a rough probability and one line of reasoning for each, then combine them into a final number.
