been reading lesswrong and adjacent blogs since like 2011, so i've sat through a hundred versions of this one: instrumental convergence is like money. the pitch goes: people want money no matter what they ultimately care about — a monastery wants donations for the library, a startup wants runway for the product, a dictator wants it for whatever dictators want. money is the convergent instrumental goal because it buys anything, so agents with totally opposed final goals still end up competing for the same stuff. and then the move everyone makes: a superintelligent paperclip maximizer is in the same position. it doesn't care about power or survival for their own sake, but it'll pursue them the way any actor pursues funding, because resources are a universal solvent for whatever end you happen to have. so the conclusion people push with it is: "you can't rely on just giving the AI benign goals, it'll grab resources and self-preservaton capabilities regardless, so the grabbing itself has to be prevented."

i want an actual audit of this analogy, not the usual hand-wave. lay out the correspondence table first: what plays the role of money (raw resources? energy? compute?), of the final ends the money buys, of the market/other agents, of the fact that money has diminishing returns for most people. then the disanalogies. the one i keep chewing on: money is only valuable because there are lots of agents around to trade with, in a roughly stable economy — its value looks like an equilibrium property of a crowd, not something a single agent has all by itself. does that matter for the AI case, where the worry is often about one superintelligence that might not be trading with anyone? also, humans: plenty of intelligent humans with lifelong projects never get around to the "accumulate power" step, and the standard reply is "humans aren't coherent goal-directed agents, that's the whole point." fine, but then the analogy is between AIs and idealized economic agents, not humans, and i've basically never seen anyone say that part out loud — it changes who owes whom an argument.

and the part i actually care about: does the conclusion the analogy is used for — a sufficiently capable AI with basically any final goal will seek resources, self-preservation, and leverage over us — survive once the disanalogies are on the table? or is the money comparison carrying more of the weight than the underlying argument can support? if it doesn't fully survive, say which parts of the conclusion do and what a better argument for those parts would look like, if there is one.
