A colleague over in the economics department is writing a trade book about fairness and meritocracy, and he asked me to sanity-check a paragraph he drafted on luck egalitarianism. I know what I'd say, but I'd be curious how someone else audits it, since he wants it to survive contact with actual political philosophers. Here's his paragraph:

"Luck egalitarianism, the view associated above all with Ronald Dworkin and G.A. Cohen (and really going back to Rawls, whose difference principle is its canonical statement), holds that the only inequalities that are unjust are those traceable to brute luck, while inequalities that flow from people's own choices are fully deserved. So if you chose to surf all day instead of working, justice has nothing to say in your favor, no matter how badly off you end up. But the view cuts both ways: since natural talents are matters of luck, the talented don't deserve their higher earnings at all, and Dworkin in particular thought people should be compensated for lacking talents — his version even extends to compensating people for expensive tastes they knowingly cultivated, since those tastes are likewise unchosen. Elizabeth Anderson famously demolished the whole approach in a single article by pointing out that it would deny medical care to reckless drivers and abandon people who freely chose badly, and no luck egalitarian has really answered her since. What's left is basically a conservatism of outcomes: leave the responsible where they fall and cushion the rest."

Go through it sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a luck egalitarian would accept, which are simplifications they'd grumble about but let pass, and which are flat mischaracterifications — I have my own list, but I'm honestly more interested in whether his conclusion, that the view amounts to leaving the responsible where they fall, actually follows from whatever survives the corrections. My instinct is that the surfing example and the Anderson bit are where he's most slyly stacking the deck, but I'd want to be sure before I send comments back to him.
