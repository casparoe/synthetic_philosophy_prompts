I am preparing a seminar unit on Keynes's vision of the good life, and I want an exercise that forces students to apply his commitments to circumstances he never encountered. Please reconstruct what you take to be Keynes's most probable position on each of the following four developments, none of which postdates—indeed none of which existed in anything like its current form before—his death in 1946.

First, the four-day workweek movement and the broader revival of arguments for legislated working-time reductions, now that the hundred-year horizon of his 1930 essay Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren has lapsed with the fifteen-hour week nowhere in sight. Second, universal basic income as a policy response to automation—the question being not whether Keynes would endorse the macroeconomics, but whether he would regard an unconditional income as an instrument for the life of leisure he anticipated or as a subsidy for the wrong kind of idleness. Third, the attention economy: streaming platforms, algorithmically curated feeds, and what we now call passive digital consumption. Keynes's ideal of leisure, inherited from his youthful Moorean convictions as recounted in My Early Beliefs, was avowedly aristocratic and cultivating—devoted to the enjoyment of art, conversation, and the acquisition of knowledge for its own sake—and it is not obvious whether he would classify most screen-based leisure as a degraded substitute for that ideal or as a democratized form of it. Fourth, "quiet quitting," the Chinese "lying flat" phenomenon, and other explicit refusals of work-centered ambition among the educated young.

For each of the four, give me the view itself, the strongest textual or biographical basis in Keynes's own commitments for attributing it to him, and one consideration that would pull in the opposite direction—some tension in his thought that might make him surprise us. Then answer two meta-questions. Which of the four attributions do you regard as most robust, and which as most speculative, and why? And in which of the four cases is the attribution most likely to be anachronistic in a dangerous way—that is, where the modern concept we are asking about (attention economy, UBI, quiet quitting) has built-in assumptions that simply have no Keynesian analogue, so that "what Keynes would think" is not so much a prediction about Keynes as a disguised statement of our own views?

A note on method for your answer: the point of the exercise is to extrapolate from his actual commitments rather than to sanctify or caricature him. He was a defender of taste, hierarchy in matters of culture, and considerable material comfort; he was also a meliorist who believed economics was a means to living wisely and agreeably. Where those commitments conflict in the cases above, say so rather than smoothing it over. Do not pad the answer with biography I already know; assume the 1930 essay and the 1938 memoir are on the syllabus.
