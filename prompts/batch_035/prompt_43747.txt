A friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about Leibniz's claim that no two things can share all their properties, and the argument turned on Max Black's old thought experiment: a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres, some fixed distance apart, in otherwise empty space. My friend's move was to say the setup is easy to picture: each sphere, he said, is just the other's mirror image, and since a thing and its mirror image are obviously two things rather than one, Black's universe is straightforwardly conceivable and the identity of indiscernibles is simply refuted. I came away uneasy, because the mirror seemed to be doing all the work — it is what made the two-ness vivid — and I could not tell how much of that vividness belongs to Black's universe and how much was imported from the glass. Could you audit the analogy for me?

Give me the mapping first: in the mirror picture, what is supposed to correspond to what — the reflecting plane, the original object, the image, the relation between them? Then the breakdowns. My own candidates, which I would like you to evaluate rather than assume: first, a mirror image is parasitic on its original, so there is a fact about which side is the thing and which is the reflection, whereas Black stipulates a perfectly symmetric universe in which no such fact exists; second, a mirror reverses handedness, so mirrored things are in one respect discernible — the reflection of a left glove is a right glove — whereas spheres have no handedness for the transformation to flip, so the very feature that makes mirror pairs look like two different things is absent here; third, a mirror image is located somewhere else, behind the glass, so the pair is discernible by position, while in Black's universe the 'reflection' is not supposed to occupy any privileged location relative to the original. It also occurs to me that an image is arguably not a physical object at all, in which case the analogy pairs a thing with a non-thing.

Finally, the question that matters to me: once these disanalogies are on the table, does my friend's conclusion — that we can clearly conceive two indiscernible things, and so Leibniz's principle is false — survive on its own, or was the mirror carrying it the whole time? And is there a better analogy that would give him what he wants without the smuggled asymmetries — some perfectly symmetric pattern, perhaps — or does the failure of every such picture tell us something about whether Black's universe can be genuinely conceived at all?
