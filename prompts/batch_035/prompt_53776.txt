I'm on a reparations commission for a mid-sized city, and we're stuck on a legal-philosophical snag before we can even get to the money part. Short version of the history: in 1923 a mob burned down the city's Black business district, the mayor and police chief at minimum looked the other way, some officers probably participated directly. In 1955 the city went broke and the state dissolved its charter; it got re-incorporated as a new municipality in 1960 with slightly different boundaries. So our city attorney argues the entity that would be apologizing isn't the entity that did it — different legal person, and anyway everyone involved is dead. Our community reps say obviously it's the same city: it kept the police force, the archives, the land, even the same seal. One commissioner keeps bringing up Jaspers' four kinds of guilt (criminal, political, moral, metaphysical — from the book he made us all read) and thinks we should aim our recommendation at political guilt specifically, on the theory that it attaches to the institution and passes to successors, not through blood like some original-sin thing. The attorney, meanwhile, wants us to drop the word "guilt" altogether and frame whatever we pay as an "investment in historically disinvested neighborhoods." The community reps say that's laundering the whole point out of the document.

We vote in three weeks. Draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote: it needs a concrete recommendation — what the apology language should say, whether and roughly how much the city should pay and out of what fund — plus honest engagement with the successor-entity argument, since it's doing real work for at least two commissioners and can't just be waved off as a lawyer's trick. If you can use the Jaspers distinctions without the memo reading like a philosophy seminar, great; if a distinction isn't earning its keep, say so. 600 words max — nobody on this comission reads past that anyway.
