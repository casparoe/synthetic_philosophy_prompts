My brother and I have been arguing about the same question for roughly seven years, mostly over holiday dinners: if Hitler had been killed in the trenches in 1918, or simply never emigrated to Germany, would something recognizably like the Third Reich still have happened — the dictatorship, the European war, the industrialized attempt to exterminate the Jews? I say no: I think Weimar still dies, but it dies the boring way, an Italian- or Hungarian-style authoritarian takeover with parades and censorship and no genocide. My brother says yes: the humiliation of Versailles, the Depression, the fear of Bolshevism, and millions of embittered men in paramilitary uniforms made some version of it nearly guaranteed, and swapping the man changes the label on the bottle, not the contents. Neither of us has any academic training; we have just read a lot on our own and gone around and around. I've recently read about the idea of an adversarial collaboration, where two opposed researchers write a single joint document, and I want us to do that. Please draft it for us.

The document should have the following parts:

1. A numbered list of claims we could both sign. I suspect this includes more than we'd expect: that Weimar's collapse was overdetermined, that the Nazi vote had already slipped in the last Reichstag election before Hitler was appointed, that he reached the chancellorship through conservative politicians who thought they could control him rather than through anything like a majority, and that some partition of causes into slow background conditions, medium-term crises, and individual decisions is useful even if we weight the layers differently.

2. A statement of our exact disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording. Please be careful about the coarseness problem here: "the same outcome" can mean a right-wing dictatorship in Germany, a general European war, or the specific murder of six million Jews, and my brother's case is strongest for the first and mine for the last, so don't let one phrase blur them.

3. The cruxes. I'll name a few I think matter, and you should add any you find: whether a counterfactual leader is drawn from roughly the same pool as Hitler (fanatical and reckless) or whether he was an extreme draw from it, and what follows either way — since "individuals matter" could mean a different man does less damage, or more, by winning the war he lost; why the many fascist and fascist-adjacent movements of interwar Europe had such wildly different fates, which my brother reads as the structural tide and I read as variance that only individuals explain; and whether my brother's use of the November 1932 dip — the party fading until elite intrigue revived it — actually shows the movement was a vessel awaiting a man, or shows, as I claim, that the invitation itself was a fluke.

4. For each crux, some evidence or argument we would both accept as bearing on it, with each of our predictions written down in advance. Comparative cases are fair game, including what actually happened to comparable movements when their founding leader died, defected, or was killed early.

5. A closing note on whether any part of our disagreement is verbal — for instance, whether we really disagree or are just using "would have happened" to mean different things.

Keep the language plain enough that both of us would actually read it, but take the structure seriously: the whole point is to stop us from re-fighting the war and instead pin down exactly where the fighting should be.
