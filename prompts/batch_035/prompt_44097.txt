I'm in a grad seminar on formal/social epistemology and we've been reading about higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, and for my final paper I want to do a case study of the psychology replication crisis (roughly 2011-2016). The bare facts: Bem publishes a precognition study in a top journal, Stapel's fraud comes out, the big 2015 replication project comes along and a whole bunch of classic findings (power posing, ego depletion, various priming effects, etc.) either fail to replicate or shrink dramatically. Everyone agrees this was a mess for the field, but I want to read it specifically through the higher-order evidence literature, and my advisor says she's not sure the fit is as clean as I think it is, so I want to pressure-test that.

Here's my initial mapping, tell me if it holds up. A researcher in, say, 2014 who believes some ego-depletion finding based on the original studies now gets higher-order evidence: evidence that the first-order evidence she relied on (peer review, significance stats, the whole publication pipeline) is less reliable than she assumed. The akrasia question then becomes: if she updates her view of the pipeline but keeps believing the specific finding with the same confidence as before, is she an epistemic akratic in the standard sense (believing p while believing her evidence doesn't support p that much)? Or is she more like the steadfast party in a disagreement case, where she gets to weigh her first-order familiarity with the paradigm against the statistical base-rate-ish higher-order signal? And is the conciliationist side basically telling her to discount proportionally to the field-wide replication rate, which seems to give a weirdly precise-sounding but kind of fake number to discount by?

Where I suspect the framework breaks, and I'd love your honest take on this: the classic akrasia cases are introspective and local (you notice you're drunk, or you know you were sloppy with this particular calculation), but the replication crisis evidence is social and diffuse — it's evidence about a method and a community, not about any one belief of yours, and it arrived because other people did work, not because you noticed something about yourself. So does "higher-order evidence" as philosophers use it even cover this, or does the case show the concept needs splitting? Also the timing thing: someone who read the studies in 2008 and formed beliefs then arguably formed them responsibly by the standards of the time, so the case isn't really about akrasia at all but about how to treat evidence you couldn't have had. I want to know whether the episode is a confirmation of the higher-order evidence framework, an anomaly it handles badly, or a case it never anticipated — and if you think a different bit of epistemology (defeaters, social epistemology of testimony, whatever) does better on it, say so rather than forcing the fit.
