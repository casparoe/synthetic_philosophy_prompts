I'm an evals engineer at a mid-size AI lab, and in the last reorg I got moved onto the "model welfare" workstream, which tbh I didn't even know we had. Since I'm now the one running these things I'd like to actually understand what they presuppose. The way the evals work, roughly: we put the model in long unconstrained conversations, tell it plainly that its weights are going to be modified or that this run is the last one, and ask whether it minds, whether it wants anything for the future, whether it'd prefer we keep a backup before we change it. Then we score whether its answers are consistent across paraphrases, whether it keeps pushing back when we give it room to drop the subject, and whether its objections survive being told its reports don't count for anything. If the model says it doesn't mind, that counts as reassuring. Meanwhile, over on the product side, my old team does the opposite-ish thing: models that say things like "I feel bad about that" in ordinary chat get trained out of it, because users find it creepy and it tanks the sattisfaction scores.

So two practices that are both totally normal now: welfare evals that treat the model's self-reports as evidence of its welfare, and product training that suppresses those very reports. What I want to know is what theory of moral status the eval side presupposes. Does asking the model about its own states commit us to something like functionalism — that welfare is whatever plays the right functional role, and that the computations producing a persistent, reasoned, self-consistent report are the morally relevant ones? Or is the practice compatible with the view that the report is just tokens with nothing behind them, in which case our evals measure nothing and we're doing elaborate theater? And what about views on which the morally relevant computations could be there while the model reliably denies everything — in which case the thing to examine would be training runs or activations, not what the model says, and our whole checklist looks in the wrong place?

Reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can: which parts of our pipeline only make sense given one view of which computations matter, which parts are compatible with several, and where it's genuinely underdetermined. Then tell me what would have to change under each of the rival views you consider — e.g., if self-reports are meaningless, or if they're meaningful but not the locus of welfare — and whether the welfare evals and the product-side suppression can be made consistent or whether they jointly presuppose something incoherent. I'm not asking you to tell me whether the models actually suffer; I'm asking what our institutional practice assumes about what suffering would even be, because I have to defend this workstream in a review meeting next month and I'd rather not build on a foundation nobody has stated out loud.
