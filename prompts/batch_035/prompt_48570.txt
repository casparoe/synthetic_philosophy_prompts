At work, whenever one of our distributed systems does something weird and unpredictable, somebody quotes Jurassic Park — life finds a way, ha ha — and we move on. It finally occurred to me I have no idea if that movie is making a real philosophical claim or just doing vibes. So I'd like you to read Jurassic Park (the novel and/or the film, your call on which works better) through the reduction vs emergence debate in philosophy of science.

Here's my half-formed engineer's take: in my world, when a system behaves in a surprising way, you can usually dig down and find the cause — some retry loop, some race condition. It was all there in the code the whole time; we just didn't look at the right level. But Malcolm keeps insisting the park will fail in ways nobody can foresee, and I cant tell if that's supposed to be a claim about emergence — the park as a whole has properties that the dinosaurs and the fences and the DNA sequences just don't have individually, so no amount of knowing the parts tells you what the whole does — or just a claim about our ignorance — we can't predict it, but that's on us, not on reality. Those feel like really different claims to me. One says reduction fails in principle, the other says it fails for beings with limited time and information, which honestly is just a Tuesday in software.

So the reading I want: is the park's collapse actually presented as emergent behavior? But the way everything cascades once it starts — is the story saying that complexity itself defeats the engineer, that no complete specification of the parts constrains the whole?
