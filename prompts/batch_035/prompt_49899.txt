I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade now, and over that time I've absorbed the community's default picture of what an ideal agent looks like: complete, coherent preferences; a utility function; the coherence theorems as enforcers. But the one piece of that picture I've never managed to fully swallow is completeness — the requirement that for any pair of options, a rational agent either prefers one to the other or is indifferent between them. This is, of course, the axiom Buridan's ass is famous for failing: the donkey between two identical haystacks has no preference and no procedure for getting one, and starves. The standard rationalist response has always struck me as too quick: an agent that is merely indifferent can pick arbitrarily or flip a coin and lose nothing, so the failure mode never actually bites, and incomparability collapses into indifference once you allow lotteries.

I'd like a credence, and not a hedge. Specifically: what is your probability that completeness is a genuine requirement of ideal rationality, rather than a mathematical convenience that we have drifted into treating as a normative constraint on agents?

Please decompose the reasoning rather than handing me a bare number. The sub-questions I see, though you should feel free to add or reweight them:

1. Exposure to exploitation. How much of the case for completeness rests on money-pump and Dutch-book style arguments, and do those arguments actually reach completeness, or only transitivity and acyclicity? My working understanding is that an agent with incomplete but transitive preferences can avoid being pumped as long as it declines to choose from pairs it has no ordering over — which suggests the practical argument underdetermines the axioms it's supposed to justify. How confident are you in that claim, and what's your probability that some strengthened pumping or betting scheme closes the gap?

2. The randomization escape. There's a family of moves — descended from Aumann's work on utility theory without the completeness axiom — holding that if an agent prefers flipping a coin between A and B to taking either outright, it has thereby revealed a preference over mixtures, and so the apparent gap in the ordering was never real; the agent's preferences were complete over lotteries all along, just not over the pure options. How much of your final credence rides on whether this move is a proof or a sleight of hand? Note it cuts both ways: if it works, Buridan's ass is saved by coin-flipping; if it fails, indifference and incomparability come apart in a way the standard rationalist story can't accommodate.

3. Idealization versus bounded agency. How much of the intuitive force of completeness comes from facts about actual, computationally bounded agents — dithering, status quo bias, exploitable delays — that are simply irrelevant to what an idealized agent must be like? If completeness is only a pragmatically good approximation, the credence that it's a requirement of ideal rationality should come apart from the credence that it's a good design default. Assign something to each.

4. Indifference versus incomparability. How likely is it that these are genuinely distinct psychological or normative states rather than one state under two descriptions, and does rationality have anything to say about the second one at all?

Give me a rough probability and a one-paragraph justification for each component, explain how you combine them into the final number (I'm as interested in the interaction structure as in the values), and then tell me which single sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to and what argument or thought experiment would move it the most.

One last thing, and it's the part I care about most: flag whether you think my framing smuggles in the rationalist assumption that preferences are things agents have antecedently, which the coherence theorems then constrain — rather than constructs those theorems help us project onto choice behavior. If completeness looks mandatory partly because we've defined preferences as the kind of thing that comes complete, the decomposition should say so.
