I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, so forgive me if I reach for the only analytical vocabulary I have: in my world, when a metric moves, the first question is always what else changed at the same time. That habit is why I've gotten stuck on Max Weber's famous argument from the early 1900s, and I'd like your probability estimate on it — with the reasoning laid out, not just a number.

The thesis, as I understand it: Calvinists believed salvation was predestined and could not be influenced by anything they did. This was supposedly so psychologically unbearable that believers looked for indirect reassurance — evidence of their election in worldly success — and this anxiety-driven need for signs translated into methodical, disciplined, self-denying economic conduct: work hard, don't spend the proceeds, reinvest. That mentality, Weber argued, was a genuine cause of industrial capitalism emerging where and when it did — Holland, England, parts of Germany — rather than somewhere it had every material precondition, and the causation ran from religious values to economic behavior, not the other way around.

Let me state the proposition precisely so it has a truth value. Call it P: religiously transmitted values were a major independent cause of the emergence of industrial capitalism in northwest Europe, where "independent" means not fully explainable as a downstream effect of economic conditions flowing the other direction, and "major" means the emergence would have been substantially delayed or differently configured without them.

Here's what I want: your credence in P, decomposed into the sub-claims it depends on, each with its own rough probability and one line of justification. Some terms I'd expect to see, though you should add or drop whatever you think the argument actually requires: (1) that culture can function as a causal variable at all in historical explanation, rather than being a lagged reflection of material conditions; (2) that the observed association between Protestant regions and early capitalist development reflects culture producing economics, rather than a common cause — say, both fed by political fragmentation or by unusually high literacy from Bible-reading — or economics producing culture; (3) that Weber's specific psychological mechanism, predestination anxiety converting into disciplined accumulation, is the operative channel, rather than some blander effect like literacy, network density among religious dissenters, or exclusion of dissenters from state careers pushing them into commerce; and (4) whatever you think about whether "industrial capitalism" is coherent enough as an effect variable for the question to be well-posed.

Then do three more things for me. Combine the terms into your overall credence in P. Tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to. And tell me what evidence would move that term most — concretely, what would a good natural experiment here even look like, given that we can't A/B test the seventeenth century? For context on my own priors: what bothers me most is that Renaissance Italy and the Hanseatic cities had sophisticated banking and commerce a century before the Reformation, all of it Catholic, which feels like finding the effect present before the supposed cause was introduced. I don't know how much weight that observation deserves, so fold it into your reasoning wherever it fits.
