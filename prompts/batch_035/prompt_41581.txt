I'm on a five-person advisory panel to our ministry of social affairs, and we've been asked for a written recommendation on two rival family-policy packages. Nothing gets cut under either one — the question is purely where the extra money goes.

Package A: broad child allowances plus free childcare, aimed at pushing the birth rate well up. The economic modelling says it works, but with a twist: the birth increase comes mostly from lower-income households, and the per-child public investment gets spread thinner. So the projected outcome is a noticeably bigger population where average wellbeing per person is somewhat lower — more people, each living lives that are decent but pretty stretched (crowded schools, less parental time, tighter household budgets).

Package B: smaller and more targeted, with the birth rate staying roughly flat or dipping a bit, and the savings ploughed into quality of life for the people who exist or will exist either way — better healthcare, parental leave, education. Projected outcome: fewer people than under A, but each with a better shot at a genuinely flourishing life.

Two of our panelists are total-welfare people: they say that as long as each life is worth living, more lives means more total wellbeing, and A wins for the same reason a big pile of small positive amounts beats a smaller pile of bigger ones. One of them literally brought up the repugnant conclusion in our last meeting — as in, if you think A is worse just because each person under it is somewhat worse off, you're on a slippery slope toward having to say a huge population of lives barely worth living beats a small one of excellent lives, and almost nobody will bite that bullet. The other three of us aren't convinced, and one keeps saying a child who is never conceived can't be harmed by not being brought into the world, so extra lives only count insofar as they're good for the people living them — and honestly by that point the debate had gotten pretty tangled, with everyone talking past each other.

Please draft the memo we'd circulate to the ministry's working group. It needs: a clear recommendation between A, B, or a hybrid (and if hybrid, spell out concretely what it would look like, not just "a bit of both"), the reasoning written for a policy audience with no philosophy background, and a genuinely fair shake for the total-welfare side — not a strawman, since the minister will definately hear from people making that case. Also flag one measurable thing the ministry should track over the next five years that would tell us whether we called it right.

Keep it under 800 words or so. And dont stress about making it sound offical — clarity beats formality here.
