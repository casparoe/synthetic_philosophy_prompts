A book on decision-making under uncertainty that I recently finished devotes a chapter to the dispute between frequentist and subjectivist interpretations of probability, and its compressed gloss of the two camps goes roughly as follows:

"Frequentism identifies the probability of an event with its relative frequency in an indefinitely long sequence of repeatable trials. This means the frequentist cannot, on pain of abandoning her own theory, attach a probability to a one-off event — the probability that this particular election produces a constitutional crisis, say — unless she posits a hypothetical ensemble of identical elections, which quietly reintroduces the single-case probabilities the interpretation was invented to avoid. Subjectivism, by contrast, holds that a probability just is a measure of how confident some particular person happens to feel, so on this view any degree of belief between 0 and 1 is as rational as any other. The Bayesian machinery of priors and updating then amounts to a way of dressing up an analyst's personal hunches in quantitative costume; and because two Bayesians who start with different priors are each only ever accumulating evidence consistent with their own starting point, they can never in principle be brought to agreement. The one genuinely deep result in this area is de Finetti's theorem from the 1930s, which shows that if your degrees of belief satisfy a certain symmetry condition, they behave in every observable respect as if there were objective chances — which de Finetti took as proof that objective chance exists after all. Meanwhile the frequentist, who refuses to use prior probabilities on the grounds that they smuggle in bias, must still choose a significance threshold for her tests, and that choice is a prior probability in all but name. So in the end the two camps are not disagreeing about any matter of fact; they mean different things by the same word, and the choice between them is a matter of taste rather than truth."

Something about the chapter bothered me, but I can't locate the problem precisely. The book is by a professional statistician, so I came in inclined to trust it, yet the claim that on the subjectivist view any degree of belief is as rational as any other struck me as too strong to be what philosophers of probability actually maintain. Would you go through the gloss claim by claim and tell me which parts a philosopher of statistics working on these interpretations would accept as correct, which would make them wince, and which are flat misrepresentations? I would especially like your judgment on three points: first, whether the hypothetical-ensemble point is a genuine internal difficulty for frequentism or a standard criticism being presented as a discovery; second, whether de Finetti's theorem is being read correctly here, given what I understand of his wider reputation as the most hard-line subjectivist of his era, which sits oddly with the gloss's reading; and third, whether the closing conclusion — that the whole dispute is merely verbal, a matter of taste rather than truth — actually follows from the accurate parts of the summary, or whether it rests on more than the summary has earned.
