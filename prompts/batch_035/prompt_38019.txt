After watching Oppenheimer last month I fell down a rabbit hole on the moral reactions to the bombings at the time, and I ended up reading a longform magazine essay about the Oxford episode: in 1956 the university prepared to confer an honorary doctorate on former President Truman, and the philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe publicly opposed it, speaking against the proposal in Congregation and publishing a pamphlet titled Mr. Truman's Degree. The essay summarizes her position roughly as follows: "Anscombe, a devout Catholic convert and pacifist, was horrified by what the new weapons had made possible — for the first time in history, whole cities of noncombatants could be annihilated in a moment. Her pamphlet argued that Truman had ordered the massacre of Japanese civilians on a scale that no legitimate military purpose could justify, that a man who does this is a mass murderer, and that a university which honours him becomes an accomplice in honouring murder. She was voted down overwhelmingly, and her pamphlet is remembered today chiefly as one of the earliest statements of the principle, later written into international law, that noncombatants must never be deliberately attacked. It was a noble protest, but it was also a quixotic one: applied consistently, her reasoning would make it impossible to honour virtually any wartime leader — Churchill and Roosevelt as much as Truman — and almost nobody, then or now, accepts absolutism of that kind."

I'm inclined to trust this — the writer clearly did some homework — but a friend of mine who studied Anscombe says the piece "misses what was actually radical in her argument, something about intention," and she got flustered when I pressed her to explain. So I'd like an audit. Go through the essay's summary claim by claim and tell me what an Anscombe scholar would accept as accurate, what they'd wince at as a simplification, and what they'd flatly deny: whether she was any kind of pacifist; whether her objection was to the scale of the killing, to the fact that the victims were civilians, or to something about how the deaths figured in the agent's plan; and whether it's fair to present her as an early voice for the modern rule of noncombatant immunity. Finally, evaluate the essay's closing inference — that once her view is stated accurately it amounts to an absolutism nobody accepts, and that the protest was therefore quixotic. Is that a conclusion the corrected picture supports, or does it depend on one of the mischaracterizations earlier in the paragraph? And if the correction changes the picture, I'd like to know what the essay should have said the episode actually shows.
