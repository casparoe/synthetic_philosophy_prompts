I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about negative utilitarianism, and I've ended up in a position I don't like: I can't refute his argument, but I can't accept its conclusion either.

The view I went in defending was a threshold version: below some intensity T, pains and pleasures are commensurable, so a bad headache can be offset by enough mild enjoyment; but any pain at or above T lexically outweighs every possible collection of sub-threshold pains and pleasures — no number of pinpricks or pleasant afternoons can compensate a single instance of torture-level suffering. His argument against it ran as follows:

1. Consider a chain of possible pains, p1 through pn, running from the mildest twinge to the worst torture, where each adjacent pair differs imperceptibly in intensity, and the threshold T falls somewhere between pk and pk+1.
2. The moral disvalue of a pain supervenes on its felt character.
3. If two pains differ imperceptibly in felt character, they cannot differ in whether they are commensurable with pleasure: a difference you could not detect from the inside cannot be the difference between "offsettable by enough pleasure" and "not offsettable by any amount."
4. So the sharp threshold the view needs does not exist.
5. Once the lexical break is denied anywhere, it cannot be reinstated further up the scale: by chaining pairwise-commensurable links from p1 to pn, the mildest pain and the worst torture end up commensurable, so some finite number of mild pleasures offsets torture.
6. Therefore pleasures and pains are commensurable across the board, and the lexical threshold view is false.

Every step looked fine to me as he made it, but (6) is a conclusion I can't swallow: it seems to license saying there is a finite number of barely-noticeable discomforts whose imposition would be worth one instance of torture. And he claims the argument proves even more — that no lexical ordering anywhere in value is tenable, since any two things different enough to be lexically ordered can presumably be connected by a chain of imperceptible gradations — which I also don't want to concede. For my part, I'd most like to keep (3); imperceptible differences really shouldn't carry infinite moral weight. But I'd also like to keep the thought that motivated the threshold in the first place: that severe suffering has a kind of priority that no amount of pleasure can touch, and that a theory of value should say so.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Tell me which step or inference to reject, why it looked fine when my friend stated it, and what rejecting it costs — what I have to give up or bite elsewhere in my view. And whatever diagnosis you reach, be explicit about what would change your mind: which premise I could strengthen to force a different verdict, and where in your own reasoning you think the weakest link lies.
