So, background: I've been reading rationalist blogs since the Sequences were still coming out, and I swallowed "shut up and multiply" basically whole. If a bet is positive expected value and you can afford the downside, you take it, and anyone who refuses is being squeamish about arithmetic. Except lately I've been reading about Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory, and it's gotten under my skin. The rough idea is that instead of just averaging utilities weighted by probability, you're allowed to weight bad outcomes more heavily than their probabilities alone warrant — so refusing a slightly +EV gamble isn't automatically a bias to be corrected, it can be built into your decision theory from the ground up instead of being smuggled into a concave utility function after the fact.

I want to think this through properly, so write me a dialogue between two equally sharp characters standing at an actual decision point. Setup: two friends at a rationalist meetup are deciding whether to put $15k out of a $60k emergency fund into a friend's startup. It's +EV — say 15% chance of roughly a 10x return, 85% chance of losing everything — but it's genuinely scary for at least one of them. Character A is an orthodox expected-utility maximizer, all "shut up and multiply," deeply suspicious of anything that smells like dodging the math. Character B just finished Buchak and thinks you can rationally refuse the bet without having to claim that dollars are worth less to you when you have fewer of them. Both should be actually smart — no strawmen, and each one has to land at least one real hit on the other.

Things I want the dialogue to actually work through, not just gesture at:

1. A's opening move will be "concave utility already handles this." B has to explain why risk-weighting isn't just double-counting diminishing marginal utility — ideally by pointing at a preference structure that no amount of curving the utility function can capture, and ideally by finding the Allais-style structure inside the startup decision itself rather than reciting the thought experiment from a textbook.

2. A should deploy the "if you're so smart, why ain'tcha rich" argument — REU agents turn down +EV bets, so over a lifetime of decisions they end up with less stuff — plus the money-pump/Dutch-book angle. B definately needs to actually engage with these rather than dodge. (I gather Buchak has replies involving rejecting the independence axiom and denying that the pump bites when you've committed in advance — have B make that case in her own words, not by citing chapter and verse.)

3. Somewhere in there A should make a move I've seen on the blogs a lot: "if what you really mean is that repeatedly betting your whole bankroll is dumb because of compounding and ruin, that's an ergodicity / Kelly-criterion argument, and it's a fact about sequences of bets, not about a single decision — so stop dressing it up as per-decision rationality." This is the point I personally most want to see hashed out, so give it room. B should have to say whether REU can be defended for genuine one-shot decisions, or whether it quietly depends on the fact that a life is a long series of correlated bets.

4. No deus ex machina: neither character gets to win. They end the night still disagreeing, but each has to say concretely what would change their mind — a life circumstance, a proposed experiment, a theorem, whatever.

Make it read like an actual conversation between people who've both done the reading but are also deciding about real money — they can be a little rude about each other's math, reference meetup culture, pause to grab more snacks, whatever. Not a lecture with two names attached. Maybe 1200–1800 words?
