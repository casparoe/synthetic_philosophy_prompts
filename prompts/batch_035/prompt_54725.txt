In our philosophy of science seminar, the professor dismisses worries about the underdetermination of theory by evidence with a statistical analogy: any finite set of data points is compatible with infinitely many curves, yet nobody concludes that curve-fitting is impossible or that statistics is a fraud — we simply add a preference for simplicity, or for the best-supported model, and get on with it. Underdetermination, she says, is just this same banality wearing a fancy name, and the Duhem-Quine problem dissolves once we notice that scientists have always had non-evidential criteria like parsimony, unification, and explanatory power available for exactly this situation. Please audit the analogy for me. First set out the correspondence table: what in actual scientific practice plays the role of the data points, the curve, the free parameters, the noise model, and the simplicity penalty? Then identify where the mapping breaks — which features of the statistical case does the analogy quietly import that theory choice lacks (think about who fixes the variables and the measurement scheme, whether the points arrive independently of the curve being fitted, and whether there is any agreed measure of a theory's simplicity in the way there is for a polynomial's degree)? Crucially, separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine her use of the analogy from those that are merely loose fit. Finally, tell me whether her dismissive conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along — and if it was, say what a better analogy would be, if one exists, for what is really at stake between Duhem-style holism and the scientific realist's confidence that evidence can favor one theory over its rivals.
