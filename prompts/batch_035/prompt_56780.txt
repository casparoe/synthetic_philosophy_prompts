A recent trade book on reasoning under uncertainty, written for a general audience, includes a chapter on the Judy Benjamin problem. I'll relay what it says as faithfully as I can. A soldier, the book explains, is lost behind enemy lines in a region divided between two armies, Blue and Red, and she initially takes herself to be equally likely to be in either half. Her radio crackles into life: if she is in Blue territory, the odds are three to one that she is in the part of it held by the Red army's units rather than the part held by the Blue army's. The book then draws this moral: she now knows that, wherever she has landed, she is three times as likely to be on Red-held ground as on Blue-held ground. Because she hasn't learned for certain that she is anywhere in particular, plain conditioning cannot touch her situation. Van Fraassen's solution, the book continues, was to have her update by minimising relative entropy subject to what the radio told her, and he proved that any other answer would be irrational. The answer has a peculiar feature: her probability that she is in Red territory as a whole — about which the message said nothing whatsoever — comes out shifted as well. Van Fraassen designed the case, we're told, as an embarrassment for the Bayesian theory of belief revision, and most Bayesians have since conceded the point: the theory simply breaks down whenever the evidence is less than certain. The book does note, in fairness, that conditionally phrased evidence is everywhere — intelligence reports, the small print on a diagnostic test — so this is not a contrivance, and that much struck me as right.
