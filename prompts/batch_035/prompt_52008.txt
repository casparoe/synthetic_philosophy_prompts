Each December, the ideas section I edit as a science journalist runs a parody edition — an affectionate roast of the year's intellectual currents, written to the same editorial standards as our serious copy. The pieces that work best are the ones where the joke is load-bearing: the humor comes from getting the substance exactly right, so a specialist in the field laughs in recognition rather than wincing at caricature. This year I want to commission the centerpiece myself, and I'd like your help drafting it.

The piece I have in mind is a satirical press release, roughly 650 words, from a fictional body — call it something blandly authoritative like the International Commission on the Classification of Catastrophes — announcing, with great ceremony, that famine has been formally reclassified from a political event to a natural disaster, in the same administrative family as hurricanes and earthquakes. The target of the satire is the act-of-God framing of famine: the deeply ingrained habit of treating starvation as weather that happens to people, rather than as something done to them. The commission's spokespeople should congratulate themselves on the administrative tidiness this achieves — with famine filed under meteorology, responsibility conveniently evaporates, and the only appropriate response becomes sympathy plus a relief fund, never an audit.

Two requirements on the content. First, the jokes must be drawn from the actual historical record rather than invented, because that's where the bite lives: the commission might note approvingly that Bengal in 1943 behaved very much like a natural disaster, in that food was present in the province while particular classes of people — rural laborers whose wages hadn't kept up with rice prices — lost their ability to command any of it; that Ireland's blight years were, meteorologically speaking, excellent ones for the export of grain and livestock; and that during the Great Leap Forward the weather cooperated beautifully with locally reported record harvests, grain being requisitioned right on schedule from fields that officials had promised were overflowing. Second, the piece should smuggle in the genuine analytical points without stating them: that famines can occur without any overall shortage of food, that they are failures of people's entitlement to food rather than of the food supply itself, and that the commission's reclassification will be easiest to sustain in countries without a free press to file objections, since functioning democracies have proven irritatingly resistant to hosting famines in the first place. The tone throughout should be deadpan institutional prose — press-release boilerplate, subsection headings, a quotable director-general, maybe a line about the reclassification being "effective retroactively" — with no winking at the reader and no explicit editorializing. A reader who knows nothing about the topic should finish the piece feeling that something is terribly wrong with the commission's reasoning; a reader who knows Amartya Sen's work on famines should recognize precisely what is being skewered. Please use only invented spokespeople and organizations, no real living persons, and no citations or references — the piece should stand entirely on its own.
