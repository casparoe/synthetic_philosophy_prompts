I teach a graduate seminar on rational choice and conflict, and this term I have been taking seriously an idea that circulates in the decision-theory literature under the label of safe Pareto improvements. Let me lay out the core idea so that we share a setup, because my question concerns how far you think it stretches.

The point of departure is a familiar inefficiency of commitment. Where agents can bind themselves in advance — publicly and credibly — before a bargaining situation crystallizes, the binding itself can destroy value. The canonical illustration: two parties to a dispute, each of whom does somewhat better by committing to a tough bargaining stance than by remaining flexible; each does better still if neither commits; but each does worst of all if the other commits while she stays flexible. So both commit, and the resulting conflict — the lawsuit, the price war, the war itself — burns resources that both would ex ante have preferred to keep. Since neither can commit to being unable to commit, they appear to be stuck.

A safe Pareto improvement is meant to be a way out that survives this difficulty. Sketch it as follows. Suppose the parties' version of the conflict can be scaled down: there exists a commonly recognizable lower-stakes analogue of the game — a border skirmish rather than a war, a mock trial rather than litigation, a proportional reduction of every payoff — such that winning the small game corresponds to winning a proportionate share of the large one. The parties can then jointly commit in advance to a randomization over which of them will enjoy the (scaled-down) advantage of having committed first. Ex ante, the expected payoffs match those of the original game, since someone would have had the advantage anyway; but the actual destruction — the part of the pie that the race for commitment power burned off — is reduced or eliminated. The arrangement is safe in the sense that it Pareto-dominates the default in every state, so neither party has an incentive to renegotiate once the coin has landed.

Here is my question, and I want it as a number. Consider the claim that, for a substantial fraction of real-world conflicts in which commitment technology generates Pareto losses — interstate disputes, commercial litigation, turf wars within firms — there exists some arrangement of roughly this shape that both parties would in principle accept, were it clearly proposed and understood. My own credence sits around 0.55, though I confess it wobbles. Give me yours, and decompose it: the probability that plausible real-world conflicts admit anything like a commonly recognizable scaled-down analogue at all; the probability, conditional on that, that the parties' valuations are similar enough in structure for the randomization to look fair ex ante; the probability that a party confident it would have won the commitment race can be brought to participate, given that the scheme rewards it only in expectation; and whatever further terms you judge to belong. Assign a number and a line of justification to each, then combine them, tell me which term the final figure is most sensitive to, and say what evidence — a historical case, a formal result, an institutional experiment — would move it most. I should also tell you what unsettles me and what I want you to take a stance on: whether the scheme quietly presupposes that the parties already share something like a common scale for their stakes, which is precisely what the fiercest conflicts are missing. If you think that presupposition is fatal, let it show up in your numbers rather than in a hedged paragraph.
