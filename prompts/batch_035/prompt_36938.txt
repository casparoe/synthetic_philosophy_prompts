I'm a medical student taking a bioethics elective, and our seminar has spent three weeks on the definition of death without getting anywhere. The professor set up the debate as whole-brain versus circulatory criteria, and everyone assumed the brain side had won — until one of the older students, a former ICU nurse, brought in an argument that has the group split down the middle and that I can't answer. She says it shows the whole-brain criterion is not merely incomplete in edge cases but is never a reliable criterion at all. I should say up front that I'd like to keep the whole-brain criterion if it can be kept, because I've watched what abandoning it would do to transplant medicine, but I want to know honestly whether that preference is defensible.

Her argument, as best I can reconstruct it: (1) A criterion of death must be a criterion for the death of the organism as a whole, not of some part of it — we don't say a person dies when their liver does. (2) The whole-brain criterion meets this demand because the brain is what integrates the body into a single organism, so once the entire brain has irreversibly ceased functioning, what remains on the ventilator is not a gravely injured human being but a collection of organs each kept running by machines and staff. (3) A collection of parts running in parallel is not an organism; juxtaposition is not integration. (4) But in documented cases, brain-dead bodies sustained for weeks or months have healed wounds, mounted immune responses, and — in pregnant patients — gestated a fetus toward viability; wound-healing and fever are exactly the sort of thing we mean by integrated functioning of the whole, and none of them is the work of a single organ. (5) So in those cases the ventilated body was alive as an organism, and the whole-brain criterion declared a living organism dead. (6) A criterion cannot be patched case by case: whatever makes those bodies alive must be present in principle in any brain-dead body, even if we don't happen to keep it going long enough to see it. (7) Therefore no brain-death determination is ever safe, and every organ retrieval under the criterion risks taking organs from a living being. She concludes we should return to irreversible circulatory arrest as the sole criterion and accept that transplant volumes collapse.

The group has splintered. One faction attacks step (4) and says healing and gestation are just local, humoral, or placenta-driven processes masquerading as integration — but she responds that the same could be said of any physiological process if you look closely enough, and that calling fever a 'local' process is desperate. Another faction attacks step (3), saying integration can be decentralised, but then struggles to say what integration even amounts to without smuggling the brain back in as its paradigm. A third quietly concedes the conclusion and says we should bite the bullet on transplants. Please tell me which step or inference is the weakest, explain why it looked so plausible to a room full of people who had all passed physiology, and set out honestly what it costs to reject whichever premise you'd give up — including whether the costs fall on the theory of death or merely on transplant practice. If you think the argument is actually sound, say so and defend step (7) against the obvious worry that a criterion can be reliable without being infallible. Please answer in British English.
