I maintain a small interactive site for a reading group on social choice theory, and I want a diagnostic quiz that places each member by what they actually believe about Sen's liberal paradox, not by the label they'd choose for themselves. For orientation in one line: Sen's 1970 impossibility of a Paretian liberal result shows that a minimal condition of individual liberty — each of at least two people is socially decisive over at least one pair of "personal" alternatives — is inconsistent with weak Pareto, where unanimity prevails; the stock illustration is the prude, the lewd, and a single copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover.

Please produce three things: (a) ten diagnostic questions, each a fresh concrete case with two or three plain-language options; (b) a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves; (c) the decision tree that underlies the key, presented with numbered branch nodes.

The requirements:

1. All cases must be new — do not recycle the literature's stock examples. Keep them mundane and specific (flatmates, thermostats, baby names, offensive T-shirts, shared offices), and design the set so that it jointly covers the distinctions that do the real work in this debate: mere meddlesome displeasure at another's business versus being genuinely affected by it; a choice with spillovers versus one without; opposition that is unanimous among bystanders versus unanimous including the chooser; and the same outcome reached by someone exercising a right versus reached without anyone exercising anything. No option text may mention Sen, Pareto, liberalism, or rights theory, so that someone who has never heard of the debate can answer every question honestly.

2. Build the tree from the logical space first, then locate the named responses in it. The leaves must include, at least: disqualifying nosy preferences from the Pareto count (together with the cost this pays — it must scrutinize the content of preferences, not just read them off); the game-form or procedural redefinition of rights associated with Gaertner, Pattanaik, and Suzumura, flagged with the caveat that the escape closes again if one also demands that play of the rights-protecting game produce efficient outcomes; conditional or alienable rights, where my right holds only if everyone else's holds — I believe this line is associated with Gibbard, and if I have misattributed it, please say so plainly; restricting the admissible domain of preferences outright; letting rights yield whenever the opposition is unanimous; and treating rights as side constraints outside the social ranking altogether, individuating states finely enough to distinguish the same outcome reached by choice from the same outcome arrived at otherwise. Give each leaf its conventional name where one exists and "unnamed" where none does, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

3. Note any cell of the logical space that no leaf occupies, and diagnose whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

4. Flag honestly any pair of leaves that your ten questions cannot separate; I would rather publish the caveat than manufacture precision. If one further question would pull a flagged pair apart, identify it and add it as an eleventh.

Do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; names of positions and authors suffice. Because the site parses quiz output automatically, the response must end with a single final line of the form \answer{...} listing the leaf labels, pipe-separated, in the order they appear in the tree.
