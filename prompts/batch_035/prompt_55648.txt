hey, so i run a philosophy club at my school (about 14 kids, 45 min, mostly sophomores and juniors, zero game theory background) and i want to run a session on this thought experiment i found called Parfit's hitchhiker — you're stranded in the desert, a driver will only stop if she expects you to pay her once you're in town, but once you're actually in town you have no reason left to pay. i want them to FEEL the problem, not hear about it, so no lecture up front and don't even say the name until the end.

my rough idea: split them into drivers and hitchhikers, points instead of money, a two-stage game. the first version should be one-shot and anonymous so they discover on their own that no driver picks anyone up and everyone ends up worse off. then i want to layer stuff on top, and this is where i'm stuck. i've read that the interesting bit is that the "irrational" person who genuinely can't bring themself to stiff the driver does better than the rational one — but i can't figure out how to get a real "can't" into a game that everyone knows is fake. my first thought was precommitment options the hitchhiker can buy before flagging a driver down: leave a deposit with me (the facilitator), sign an IOU i actually enforce, make a public promise in front of everyone, or do nothing. but then i worry i've just taught them "commitment devices work," which is econ 101, not philosophy. the actual philosophical part is whether it was rational to pay once you were already safe in town, and whether being the kind of person who pays is rational even when you couldn't bind yourself in advance.

so design the whole session for me. i need: rules, point values (make the numbers such that passing a hitchhiker is clearly the safe move for a driver, and stiffing is clearly tempting once you're in town), how to keep decisions anonymous, which rounds to run in what order, and a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the argument — the backwards-induction reasoning for stiffing, why the honest fool ends up with the ride, and whether "being rational" means doing the best thing at each moment or being glad you're the sort of person who wouldn't. also, please include one mechanic that gets at the difference between changing the situation (deposit, enforceable IOU) and just BEING a certain kind of person even when you can't change anything — even if you have to rig or fake the second part somehow, tell me how. and be honest about what will predictably go wrong the first time i run it (like people paying anyway bc it's fake points and their friends are watching) and how to counter each one. it has to be runnable by one person with paper, index cards, and no budget.

end your answer with a single line starting "Verdict:" — one sentence on whether this design will actually produce students who regret having been "rational," and if not, the one change that would fix it.
