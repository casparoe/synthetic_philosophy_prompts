I've been trying to get clear on the so-called paradox of voting, and I've assembled the following picture, though I suspect I've gone wrong somewhere. As I understand it, the voting paradox originates with Condorcet: when there are three or more options and the electorate's preferences are arranged in a certain way, pairwise majority rule yields cyclical majorities, so there is no determinate fact about what the voters collectively want. It follows from this that the outcome of any election is indeterminate in principle, and hence that the expected payoff of my individual vote is zero no matter what the size of the electorate — it is not merely that my vote is unlikely to be decisive because millions of others are voting; it cannot be decisive, because collective preferences can cycle. I also understand that Riker and Ordeshook, in their 1968 paper on the calculus of voting, effectively conceded that voting is instrumentally unjustified when they added a separate duty term to the expected-utility calculation they inherited from Downs's An Economic Theory of Democracy — a term standing for the intrinsic satisfaction of voting, so that the calculus becomes something like the decisiveness probability times the benefit, plus duty, minus cost. That addition strikes me as rigged: the term was introduced precisely so that the theory could accommodate the observed fact that people vote, which means it can accommodate literally any pattern of turnout and is therefore unfalsifiable.
