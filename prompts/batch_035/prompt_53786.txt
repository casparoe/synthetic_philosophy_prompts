I'm a philosophy PhD on the market, and next month I'm presenting a job talk in a constitutional theory workshop at a law school: roughly a dozen originalist-leaning law professors who take original public meaning originalism seriously, plus two historians of antebellum America who are openly wary of how lawyers use history, and one left legal realist who thinks originalism of any stripe is window-dressing for political preference. My paper defends the following thesis: Frederick Douglass's shift, around 1851, from the Garrisonian view that the Constitution was a proslavery pact to the claim that it was an antislavery document was not political expediency or motivated reasoning but a methodologically serious interpretive position -- one that, read carefully, puts real pressure on originalist method today. My sketch of the argument runs as follows: Douglass, under the influence of Gerrit Smith and Lysander Spooner's The Unconstitutionality of Slavery, reasoned from (a) the absence of the words "slave" and "slavery" from the text, which he took to show the framers deliberately refused to sanction the institution in words; (b) the preamble's appeal to the people as a whole, which he argued could not coherently include the enslaved as parties to a bargain against themselves; and (c) a presumption of liberty under which ambiguous clauses -- the fugitive slave clause, the three-fifths clause -- had to be construed narrowly, since no free people could be presumed to bargain away natural rights without saying so explicitly. I want to claim that (a) through (c) amount to a substantive theory of how a constitutional text's silences, its stated purposes, and background moral presumptions bear on original public meaning, not just a lawyer's trick, and that this makes Douglass an internal critic of originalism rather than an ancestor either side can simply claim.
