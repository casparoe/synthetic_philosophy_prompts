I've been following the AI safety via debate idea since it started making the rounds on LessWrong ~2018ish, and I've slowly drifted into actually believing a fairly strong version of it, and now I want to stress-test it before I get too attached.

My position, roughly: if you set up two AI debaters, each able to quote/cross-examine the other, and let them iterate long enough, you can get to the truth on questions that no human judge could figure out on their own, as long as the debate is long enough. The core intuition is that lying is harder than catching a lie, or at least it's hard *somewhere* in the lie's logical structure, so each round of "which claim do you challenge" zooms in on a spot where one side has to defend something indefensible. Honest answers are decomposable all the way down; dishonest ones eventually have to commit to something checkably wrong. So even a judge who can't do the math can referee the *process*, the same way I can't verify a chess move is best but I can tell who's winning by watching better players fight over it. I'd even bet on this working for math claims about things no human has ever proved.

So: what would count against it? I want this sorted properly, not a list of caveats.

1. What's the *strongest* in-principle objection, i.e., things that could be true about arguments or about debaters that make the scheme fail no matter how long the debate goes? The obfuscated arguments thing -- claims where every decomposition down to human-checkable size is misleading, but the overall conclusion is right (or at least un-refutable in the debate) -- is the one I know about. How bad is that really? Is it a rare curiosity or does it infect basically everything hard? Are there other failure modes of this "no fix exists at any length" type that I haven't even heard of?

2. What would count as *empirical* evidence against it? Like, in actual debate experiments or amplification-style setups, what results should make me update, and how far? Debaters colluding with each other instead of arguing (shared prefixes, steganography in the quotes)? That one worries me because it's not an argument, it's an attack on the whole adversarial setup, and I honestly don't know whether collusion is a "debate is broken" result or just an "engineering is hard" result.

3. And the thing I most want an honest take on: has this position been qualified into unfalsifiability? Because I notice that when people point out a failure, the standard reply is "make the debate longer" or "make the judge decomposition smarter," and when someone points out a failure that longer debate doesn't fix, the reply becomes "well, debate was never meant for *that* class of question." The set of questions it's "meant for" seems to shrink exactly as fast as the counterexamples appear. Is there a crisp version of the claim left that still says something interesting and actually sticks its neck out? And if the crisp version is false, I'd rather know now than after I've spent years treating it as load-bearing for alignment optimism.
