Quick background on me: I'm a backend engineer, zero philosophy training, but I run a lunch-and-learn thing at my company (we get maybe 10-14 people, 60 minutes, and it's voluntary so it dies the moment it feels like a lecture). A couple months back a podcast sent me down a rabbit hole on panpsychism, and the part that actually broke my brain is what people call the combination problem: if tiny bits of matter each carry some tiny flicker of experience, how do a bunch of those add up to one unified consciouness thing like me, with a single point of view? I want an activity where people run into that wall themselves instead of me explaining it at them.

Here's the twist I want baked into the rules, because I think it's where the actual difficulty lives: the activity has to combine participants into one "subject" without any central coordinator. No one gets to see the whole picture, no one plays the boss — the whole point of the problem is that the micro-experiences don't know about each other and there's no homonculus at the top. So whatever setup you design, no single participant should be able to act as the aggregator, and I shouldn't be able to sneak in as one either. Second rule: whatever combined subject emerges, its outputs can never mention or depend on identifying the individual parts — I read somewhere that we're not supposed to be able to notice our own micro-experiences as parts, and I want to see how long a group can actually hold that line before someone cheats.

Beyond that you've got free rein: roles, simple materials, some kind of scoring that doesn't involve real money, a timeline. The debrief should map what happened onto the real philosophical pieces — where does the unity come from, where does structure come from, and crucially the difference between "a bunch of experiences happened side by side" versus "one subject had a complex experience", which I gather philosophers think is doing a lot of work here. Also it should get at whether the group ever crossed from "we all had thoughts at the same time" to "the group as a whole had a thought", and where exactly that line got fuzzy. Don't tell them the word panpsychism up front — I want them to arrive at it, or at least at the problem, cold.

Last two asks: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I've had activities die in minute ten before), and be honest about which part of the real problem no game made of already-conscious humans can possibly reproduce. My hunch is that using full-blown people as my "micro-experiencers" is a cheat somehow, but I can't put my finger on exactly how, and I'd rather hear it from you than find out live.
