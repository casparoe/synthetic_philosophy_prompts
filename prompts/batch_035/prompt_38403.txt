I teach an intro philosophy of mind course with a film unit, and this term I want to do HBO's Westworld (season 1 in particular) through the lens of what Douglas Hofstadter called sphexishness. In case the term is unfamiliar: the sphex wasp does something that looks smart — she drags her prey to the burrow, leaves it at the threshold, goes inside to check the burrow is safe, then comes out and pulls the prey in. But if an experimenter moves the prey a few inches while she's inside, she emerges, drags it back to the threshold, and re-checks the burrow. Every single time. Move the prey, she re-checks, move it again, she re-checks, forever — the impressive-looking behavior is a rigid little program that never re-evaluates itself, and Hofstadter coined "sphexish" for exactly that kind of mechanically rigid, inflexible behavior. Now the Westworld hosts seem built for this comparison: they run the same narrative loops every day (Dolores drops the can, rides into town, comes home), small deviations trigger a patch from behavior techs, and in one story a host literally answers every question with the same canned line no matter how absurd the conversation gets. But the show also complicates the obvious reading in at least two ways, and I want the analysis to take the complications seriously rather than hand-wave them.

First, the show's own theory of host consciousness is the bicameral-mind idea (the one Ford keeps alluding to, from Julian Jaynes): consciousness arrives not when behavior gets flexible but when the inner voice that commands the host gets recognized as the host's own. That's a different criterion than flexibility — a host could in principle be quite flexible and still sphexish, or quite rigid and yet somehow "owning" it. Second, the season finale twist: Maeve's whole escape arc turns out to have been scripted into her narrative, with an exit story pre-written — except she then gets off the train to look for her daughter, which is arguably the first act in the season that wasn't written for her. So is her getting off the train free because it's unscripted? Or is "unscripted" doing way too much work there (humans don't exactly live unscripted lives either, and the sphexishness literature doesn't say freedom = no script)?

Please act as a philosopher of mind who also writes serious film criticism, and think carefully before writing — do some actual brainstorming about which scenes to hang the argument on (the reveries introduced late in the season are an obvious candidate, but don't stop there). I want a reading that's faithful in both directions: don't reduce the show to an illustration of the wasp, and don't bend the sphexishness concept (rigidity, failure to re-evaluate, running the procedure regardless of outcome) to fit whatever the show needs. And please say honestly where the show resists the reading rather than pretending it all fits together. Structure the response in these sections, in this order: "Loops and patches: the textbook sphexishness" (~250 words), "The reveries and flexibility as a red herring" (~300 words), "The inner voice: what the show adds to the debate" (~300 words), "Maeve at the train, and my verdict" (~300 words), and end with a short "Scenes to show in class" (~100 words), listing 3-4 specific scenes from season one with one line each on what each is meant to demonstrate. No need for quotations from the show — paraphrase freely. I'd also love a closing sentence in the verdict saying whether the show dramatizes something about free will that the sphexishness literature itself can't get at on its own, since that's the claim I want my students to fight about.
