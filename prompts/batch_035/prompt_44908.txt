I'm taking a course on Marx this term, and our discussion group has stalled on an argument from a friend of mine — a graduate student in economics who enjoys baiting philosophers — that I can't answer cleanly. It concerns exploitation, and it goes like this. (1) On the Marxian account, exploitation consists in workers performing surplus labor — labor beyond what is needed to reproduce their own consumption — while someone else appropriates the product of that labor. (2) But any society that accumulates, that builds tools, sets aside seed corn, maintains hospitals, requires workers to perform labor beyond reproducing their own consumption: surplus labor is a condition of any economy above static subsistence, whatever its ownership structure. (3) And since a surplus must always be coordinated and allocated by someone — owners, planners, or a cooperative assembly — someone always stands in the position of appropriating it. (4) So if exploitation just is the performance and appropriation of surplus labor, then every society above subsistence is exploitative, including a federation of worker cooperatives that votes to reinvest its surplus rather than pay it out, and even a fully planned economy. (5) But the whole point of the charge was to isolate a distinctive wrong of capitalism; a concept that convicts the cooperative commonwealth as readily as the cotton mill has lost its critical function. Therefore, he concludes, either the Marxian account of exploitation fails outright, or exploitation is not the distinctive feature of capitalism it was advertised to be — the charge proves too much.

My instinct is that performing surplus labor and having it appropriated by another are not doing the same work in (1) and (3), and that the slide happens somewhere around (3), but he has a comeback I find hard to dismiss: even granting that appropriation by a separate class is the real target, who exactly is the "other" in the cooperative? The other members, who outvote me on reinvesting rather than paying out my full product? The planners in the socialist state, whom some defenders exempt entirely and others accuse of running a state capitalism using this very same concept? It looks as though the boundary between exploitative and non-exploitative appropriation has to be drawn by something premise (1) does not contain — ownership of the means of production by a class, unequal initial assets, the absence of an acceptable exit — and each candidate has costs I only half see. He also runs the same move against alienation: if alienation is loss of control over one's activity and product, then any division of labor, cooperative or not, alienates, since my product is always shaped by others' decisions; so that charge too dissolves into a lament about interdependence as such.

I'd rather keep the thought that exploitation and alienation are structural, relational wrongs of a specific form than watch both charges collapse into griping about accumulation in general — but I want to know what I'm paying for that. Tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked fine in the first place, and whether my friend's cooperative and planned-economy cases can be answered without ad hoc boundary-drawing. If you think the argument is actually valid and the real question is which plausible premise is least costly to give up, say that instead, and price the alternatives.
