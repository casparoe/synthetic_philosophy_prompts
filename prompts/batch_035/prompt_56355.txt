AI labs increasingly deploy frontier models under what their safety teams call a control strategy: rather than relying solely on training the model to be aligned, they assume the model may be misaligned and design the deployment so that even a misaligned model cannot cause serious harm. Concretely, the practice involves sandboxing the model away from sensitive tools and data, logging and monitoring its outputs — in some deployments, its chain-of-thought reasoning as well — for signs of deception or of sandbagging during capability evaluations, running staged evaluations in which the model is given real opportunities to subvert oversight without being told it is a test, keeping shutdown authority in human hands, and limiting the blast radius of any single action the model takes. What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Does it, for example, presuppose a broadly behaviorist view of intention, on which it suffices to constrain what a system can do regardless of what it wants — and if so, does monitoring the chain of thought sit oddly with that, since it seems to rely on the model's stated reasoning being evidence of its actual computation, even while the whole strategy treats the model as a potential deceiver? Is there also a tension between building a system through training processes we ourselves chose and then treating it as an adversary to be contained rather than an artifact to be understood? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the practice, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, and say what would have to change in the practice if the presuppositions failed — say, if misaligned behavior were systematically undetectable in outputs, or if deception and sandbagging at evaluation time were indistinguishable from the genuine article.
