I direct a philosophy of mind working group — twelve graduate students and two faculty — that has spent the past year on the knowledge argument. The group has hardened into two camps. The physicalist camp holds that Mary, the color scientist who knows all the physical facts about color vision but has never seen color, gains on release either a new set of abilities, or a new mode of reference to physical facts she already knew — nothing genuinely new in the propositional sense. The anti-physicalist camp holds that she gains genuinely new propositional knowledge, knowledge that is not about any physical fact, and that this shows physicalism false. They have argued past each other for a year: the physicalists accuse the others of conflating knowing with knowing how to think; the anti-physicalists accuse the others of quietly redefining what the original thought experiment was meant to test.

The two camps have now agreed, under some pressure from me, to attempt an adversarial collaboration and to draft the joint statement such a collaboration should produce. I need you to write that document. It should contain the following sections.

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign. These should be substantive, not vacuous — include claims about what the Mary scenario stipulates (the completeness of her pre-release knowledge, the absence of relevant deficits in her visual system), about what both agree the scenario does not assume (e.g., neither side needs Mary to lack concepts, or to be unable to imagine colors), about the structure of the argument both are addressing, and about what would not count as a resolution (mere redescription of "knowing what it's like," or mere insistence on one side's favored vocabulary).

2. A disagreements table. For each remaining disagreement, give one formulation of the physicalist's position that the physicalist would accept as fair to their own view, and one formulation of the anti-physicalist's position that the anti-physicalist would accept as fair to theirs. The formulations should be at cross-purposes only where the disagreement is genuine, not because of equivocation. Where a disagreement turns out to be verbal — for instance, over whether "learns something new" univocally means acquiring propositional knowledge — say so explicitly and locate the residual, non-verbal disagreement underneath it.

3. A cruxes section. Identify the three or four sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp, and for each, describe a concrete case, experiment, or argument that both camps agree would bear on it — with each camp's prediction recorded in advance. To focus this, both camps have agreed to address one further case alongside the standard scenario: a variant in which Mary, before release, is given a complete functional and neural description not only of human color vision but of her own visual cortex, and is then asked in advance to write down her exact reaction — including the wording of her first-person report — at the moment of release. Consider what each camp predicts she will get right and wrong, and what each would take it to show if her prediction is verbatim correct, or verbatim incorrect, or correct in every third-person respect while she still reports being surprised.

4. A closing section in which both camps state, in their own accepted wording, what evidence or argument would lead them to abandon their position — and an honest joint statement of whether anything in the disagreement table could actually be moved by the tests in section 3, or whether the cruxes are, for both camps, insulated from empirical result. If part of the dispute is insulated from any possible test, the document should say which part, and should say so as a shared finding rather than as a point for either side.

Write the whole thing as a single document both camps could put their names to, in the register of a real adversarial-collaboration preprint: precise, a little stiff, and scrupulously symmetrical in how much charity each side's position receives. Do not adjudicate; do not let the joint statement have a winner. The quality bar is that a member of either camp, reading it, would say that their view had been stated exactly as they would state it, and that the residual disagreement had been made exact enough that a newcomer could see precisely where to aim an argument.
