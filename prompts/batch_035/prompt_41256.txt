You are a professional moral philosopher with a specialty in the structure of moral requirements. Please think carefully through the following puzzle before answering; I'd like you to work through it step by step rather than reaching for a stock label.

My study group in a graduate ethics seminar has been circling supererogation — the category of acts that are good to do but not wrong to omit, like the bystander who dives into the river and ruins a laptop doing it. One of us, Mateo, who is a fairly hard-line consequentialist, produced an argument last week that he claims dissolves the category entirely, and none of us has managed to land a clean counter. Here it is, as he states it. (1) An act is supererogatory only if it is permissible, not required, and morally better than some permissible alternative the agent could have chosen instead — its non-heroic counterpart, like walking on by. (2) So if anything is supererogatory, there must be cases where an agent permissibly chooses the morally worse of two available options. (3) But whenever an agent knows that option A is morally better than option B, has both fully within her power, and chooses B anyway, she thereby does wrong: knowingly choosing the morally worse option when the better one is equally available is not merely unfortunate or suboptimal — it is impermissible. (4) In every alleged case of supererogation the antecedent of (3) is satisfied: the agent knows the heroic option is morally better, and it was in her power. (5) So in every such case the non-heroic choice is impermissible, and the heroic option is the only permissible one — which makes it required. (6) Therefore nothing is supererogatory: what we praise as heroism is simply duty discharged, and everyone who declines the heroic option is doing wrong. Mateo finds (6) bracing rather than absurd — he says the "moral slack" we thought we had was always an illusion invented to make mediocrity comfortable.

Here is what bothers me, and why I think the argument proves too much. Premise (3) doesn't apply only in heroic cases. Take any two permissible acts where one is even marginally morally better than the other — spending my Saturday tutoring refugees versus watching television, giving $30 to a food bank versus giving $25. If (3) is true, then choosing the worse of any such pair is impermissible, which would collapse the entire class of permissible acts: everything I do is either required or forbidden, and I have never once permissibly chosen among two decent options. That can't be right, but I can't see how to reject (3) without also licensing someone to choose the known-worse option for no reason at all, which seems to give away the store in the other direction. One member of the group, Priya, suggested that "morally better" might mean different things in (1) and (3) — better for the world, perhaps, versus better all-things-considered with the agent's own stakes counted in — but Mateo says that if the agent's interests can be counted in the moral balance, then it's no longer clear the heroic act is "better" at all, and the category of supererogation starts to look empty for a different reason.

So here is my request. Act as if you were writing the response paper I need to circulate before Thursday's seminar. Tell me which step of the argument you reject, and be honest about whether the flaw is a genuine equivocation or a substantive cost we have to swallow. If rejecting (3) is the right move, explain precisely how to restrict or reinterpret it so that knowingly choosing the worse option is sometimes permissible without its being permissible for no reason — and say what that restriction costs us in other cases. If you think Mateo's argument is actually valid and one of the other premises has to go instead, say which one and price it. I'll confess my own commitments up front: I'd most like to keep supererogation, since it seems incredible that everyone who ever walked past a GoFundMe is a wrongdoer, but I want a real diagnosis rather than an appeal to how strange (6) feels. Please also address the "proves too much" point directly — whether the parity with ordinary permissible choices is a genuine reductio of the argument or whether Mateo could simply accept it and argue that our intuitions about mundane permissibility are the confused ones.
