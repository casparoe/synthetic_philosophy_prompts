I am a novelist at work on a book whose narrator is a retired logician who, late in life, writes a memoir arguing that the incompleteness theorems vindicate her deepest convictions: that the human mind cannot be a machine, that no system of law or constitution can be both complete and internally consistent, and that certain truths will forever lie beyond what any method can establish. My editor warns that readers have seen these invocations a hundred times and will dismiss the narrator as a crank; a mathematician friend of mine says that some of the uses are outright wrong while others are merely adventurous, and that the interesting question is which is which. I need to know the actual philosophical situation before I commit my narrator to a position.

Write an essay on what Gödel's incompleteness theorems do and do not show outside mathematics. Cover the recurrent families of claims: the argument that since we can see the truth of our own consistency, we cannot be the sort of formal system the theorems apply to, and so mechanism about the mind is refuted; the appeal to incompleteness in jurisprudence and constitutional theory, where the inability of any rule system to settle every case is treated as a theorem rather than an observation; and the quasi-Platonic reading on which truth's outrunning proof in arithmetic is supposed to tell us something about the reach of human knowledge in general. Distinguish carefully between what the theorems strictly entail, what they entail only given contentious auxiliary premises, and what they are merely being used as a resonant metaphor for. Treat the strongest forms of the anti-mechanist argument, not just the pop versions, and be equally attentive to the strongest objections to it. Weigh the arguments honestly on both sides, but do not end on a non-committal note: reach and defend a verdict about which, if any, extramathematical claims survive scrutiny, and tell me, concretely, what my narrator could assert about her theorem without losing the patience of a reader who knows the subject.
