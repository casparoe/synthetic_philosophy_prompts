I've got a bunch of gut reactions about equality, and I can't tell if they add up to anything. Thought you could help me find the principle, if there is one.

The cases, all about two-person worlds to keep it clean (call the people A and B, and think of the numbers as wellbeing or resources or whatever, doesn't matter much):

1. A has 10, B has 5. A policy knocks A down to 5 so both end at 5, and B stays put. My gut: that's worse than the starting situation, full stop. Nothing got better for anyone, so how could it have improved? I take it this is basically the levelling-down objection and my gut is on board with it.

2. Same setup, but this time A is only ahead because he swindled B years ago. Knocking A down to 5 (returning the swindled money to nobody, say it gets destroyed) — I still think it's bad on the whole, but I get a flicker of "well, at least now it's fair" about the outcome, which I don't get at all in case 1. That flicker feels like it's pulling towards thinking inequality is bad in itself, which I officially resist.

3. A has 10, B has 5, and the change raises B to 7 while A stays at 10. I think that's an improvement even though the inequality shrinks less than in case 1 — and I'd take this over case 1's outcome in a heartbeat. Suggests what I care about is helping the worse off, not the gap as such.

4. Two worlds: in one, everyone is at 8. In the other, half are at 8 and half at 9. My gut says the second is better, even though it's unequal. But now the version where I half the numbers for both groups in the second world (4 and 4.5): gut says that's worse than the equal world at 8, even though relatively it looks the same shape. So my reaction seems to care about absolute levels somewhere, not just ratios, and I can't work out where.

5. This is the one I keep going back and forth on. A is much better off than B, and there's a policy that brings A down halfway towards B and brings B up a bit as well, ending at, say, 7 and 6 — closer together, both moving, but B gains only a tiny amount while A loses a lot. Sometimes I think "obviously good, gap shrank and B gained", sometimes I think it's just case 1 with cosmetics, since A's loss dwarfs B's gain. I genuinely don't know my verdict here, which annoys me.

So: is there one principle (or maybe a couple that trade off against each other) that gets all of these, or close? I suspect some priorititarian story about giving weight to benefits to the worse off might do it, but I can't make it deliver the flicker in case 2 or the switch-around in case 4. Maybe the flicker has to go — if so, tell me straight, and tell me what it costs. I'd also like to hear what the principle you land on says about a couple of cases I haven't given you: whether it matters if A and B live at different times (am I in an unequal relationship with my great-grandparents?), and whether it applies between species (my cat is worse off than me in every relevant currency — is that bad in itself?).

Please be precise about the final principle — actual conditions, not vibes — and say honestly which of my verdicts you couldn't accommodate and why. Oh, and please write the whole thing in British English.
