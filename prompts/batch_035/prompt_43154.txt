Design a thought experiment aimed at turning the Wilt Chamberlain argument against Nozick's own entitlement theory, subject to the following specifications:

1. The initial distribution must be just by the lights of both theories at once: it satisfies a simple egalitarian patterned principle (equal holdings) *and* the entitlement theory (every holding traces to just acquisition and just transfer, with nothing left for rectification to fix). This removes the standard complaint that Chamberlain-style arguments ride on a dubious starting point.
2. Every step from the initial state to the final state must be a voluntary exchange that the entitlement theory certifies as a just transfer — no fraud, no theft, no force, no mistake, so no basis for rectification ever arises.
3. Nevertheless, the final state must be one in which at least one person can no longer make genuinely voluntary choices within some central domain of life: their continued participation is compelled by economic circumstance rather than by any identifiable rights violation.
4. The loss of freedom must be the product of gradual compounding across many separate exchanges, so that no single transaction can be identified as the one at which the person's liberty was lost.

Further constraints: the setting should be a realistic contemporary economy — think concentrated ownership of essential infrastructure, platform markets, or a single-employer region — rather than a desert island or an artificial community with no exit; the scenario itself should fit in one paragraph; and the mechanism must be verifiable against the theory's stated principles rather than stipulated (ending with "and then she was trapped" is cheating — the trap must be shown to close through transactions the theory blesses).

After presenting the scenario, do the following: (a) verify that each transaction really is a just transfer by the entitlement theory's lights, flagging any step where a critic might allege otherwise and showing why the allegation fails; (b) explain why the theory's verdict that the final state is just is supposed to be bearable; (c) construct Nozick's most likely response via the Lockean proviso, and diagnose precisely which reading of the proviso the response requires — the reading comparing the person's situation to how things would be if the holdings had remained unowned, versus the reading forbidding any worsening of her situation by the appropriation or transfer; and (d) assess whether leaning on the proviso that way costs the Chamberlain argument its generality, since the proviso begins to look like exactly the sort of end-state constraint on holdings that the argument was deployed against. Conclude with the strongest available counterattack for the entitlement theorist that does not simply declare the final distribution acceptable, and identify which feature of the case is doing the real dialectical work.
