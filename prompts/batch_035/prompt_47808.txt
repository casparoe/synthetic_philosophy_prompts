I do risk and scenario forecasting for a policy institute (economics background), and I've been asked to write up something like "how we should treat disagreement among our analysts" for an internal seminar. Before I write it I want someone to check whether my own package of views actually coheres, because I've never layed them out side by side. Roughly where I stand:

(1) I buy the Harsanyi doctrine: if two agents are rational and have the same information, they hold the same probabilities — differences in belief are traceable to differences in information, all the way back through the prior, which itself encodes earlier information. (2) Because of that, I think the common prior assumption in economic modeling is an idealization of a genuine rationality requirement, not just a convenient modeling choice — models that let priors differ freely are, in the ideal limit, modeling agents as less than fully rational. (3) In practice, when two of our analysts disagree and neither can point to information the other lacks, the right output for me is to average their probabilities — and empirically, averages of decent forecasters tend to beat the individual forecasters, which I take as support. (4) I find Dutch book arguments compelling: a coherent agent's credences are betting dispositions, and the agent should be willing to take either side of a bet at odds matching their probability, with any margin in their favor. (5) I accept the no-trade theorem: agents with common priors and common knowledge won't take purely speculative positions against each other — bets between them must come from something else, like hedging or liquidity needs, which is why I think most financial-market volume isn't really disagreement. (6) Aumann's agreement theorem is correct, but its practical upshot is only that common knowledge of posteriors is rare, so when two honest experts disagree publicly for decades, that's not evidence that either of them is irrational.

So: go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad. For each real tension, say what would have to give and roughly what it costs. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I genuinely don't have a favorite here — if you can make the whole thing hang together without revision, fine, but I'd rather know if I'm fooling myself. For context in case it matters: our shop aggregates around a dozen analysts, and the seminar piece is supposed to say something about wether our aggregation rules treat them as rational agents or just as noisy instruments, so I suspect your answer matters more for the piece than I first thought.
