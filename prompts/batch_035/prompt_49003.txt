I teach an intro philosophy course, and for the free will unit I'm ditching the standard paper this year and having students watch Spielberg's Minority Report (2002, loosely based on the Philip K. Dick story) and write about it instead. My problem: I'm not sure the film actually says anything clean about compatibilism vs incompatibilism, and I don't want to assign a question the movie only seems to answer. So help me think it through. Read the film through that debate properly. The premise is that three precogs foresee murders before they happen and the would-be murderers are locked up before acting -- which means every prediction is guaranteed to be self-defeating if the system works, and the predictors aren't reading off a fixed future so much as describing what would happen absent an intervention. Does the film know the difference between determinism (your action flows from prior causes) and fatalism (it happens whatever you do)? There's that early scene where Anderton and Witwer watch a ball rolling off a table toward the moment the predicted murder was supposed to occur, and catch it, and debate whether the catch changed anything or was always going to happen -- that seems to be the film's whole thesis statement in miniature, and I'd like someone to actually unpack it rather than hand-wave it.

The parts I most want a reading of: Anderton is predicted to murder a man he's never met, and his knowledge of the prediction is precisely what drives his flight and his choices -- his reasons for acting are shot through with the prophecy itself. Is that a compatibilist point (he acts freely in the sense that matters: responsive to reasons, doing it because he deliberated about it) or an incompatibilist fantasy of slipping the causal net? And the climax, where Agatha's vision has Burgess killing Anderton, and when it's put in front of him Burgess instead turns the gun on himself -- the prediction is falsified by its own disclosure. Is the film saying knowledge of what you're predicted to do is itself a new causal input, i.e., the future isn't written, or is it just stacking the deck with an implausible crisis moment where freedom conveniently shows up? I have a suspicion the film quietly needs compatibilism to be false for its plot but true for its happy ending, and I'd want your honest verdict on that.

A couple of extra asks. Tell me where the reading strains or breaks -- scenes the compatibilist story can't absorb, or places the film trades on confusion rather than dramatizing it, since I don't want to teach something misleading just because it makes a nice discussion. And at the end, say what the film gives the debate that the standard arguments (Frankfurt-style cases, consequence arguments, the standard compatibilist moves) don't get across on their own -- my students remember scenes, not intuitions, so if there's a scene I should anchor the discussion to, flag it. Theres no need to be exhaustive about the philosophy literature, this is for first-year students, but I do want it accurate where it touches the real positions.
