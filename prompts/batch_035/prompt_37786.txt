I want to practise attacking a certain kind of defence of introspective authority, and I need an opponent who will actually make me work for it. Please play a committed proponent of what I will call the self-warrant view, which I am stipulating rather than quoting from any literature: the view that introspective reports carry a default warrant that no other source about one's own mind can override wholesale, because the act of deliberately doubting them is itself a sequence of episodes one notices from the inside, so a standing policy of distrust is self-defeating in a way no perceptual doubt is. The character does hold a further, stronger claim, though: that this asymmetry puts introspection in a position of default authority over third-person psychology wherever the two conflict about the subject's own occurrent states. Constraints on your side: stay in character until I write 'stop'; reply in a single paragraph of no more than about 150 words so this feels like a sparring match rather than a lecture; concede small points when my arguments earn them, and genuinely concede them, but never give up the main thesis; do not cite sources or name researchers; and if you need a factual question answered about an experiment I mention, ask me for it in character rather than guessing. British English throughout, please.

Here is my opening move. The stock confabulation findings seem to me to pose a dilemma your view cannot escape. In the split-brain cases, patients fluently explain actions whose actual causes are in the disconnected hemisphere; in the choice-blindness experiments, subjects defend choices they never made; and in the older verbal-report literature, people confidently report reasons that demonstrably were not operative. Now, either the genuine introspective reports and the confabulated ones are distinguishable by something, or they are not. If they are not, your default warrant is a warrant extended equally to fabrications, which is no warrant at all. If they are, then tell me what the distinguishing feature is. If you say the genuine ones feel transparent from the inside, the confabulating subjects had precisely that feeling. If you point to any independently checkable criterion — consistency with behaviour, agreement with instruments — then the authority over one's own mind has passed from introspection to those checks, and the self-warrant view has quietly become the view that introspection is trustworthy exactly where third-person evidence says it is, which is the position I started from. So: what is the criterion, and does your main thesis survive it?
