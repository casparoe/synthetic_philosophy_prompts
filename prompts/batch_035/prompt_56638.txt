Ever since I took a seminar on classical Chinese philosophy I can't stop chewing on the white horse thing. The dialogue traditionally attributed to Gongsun Long argues that a white horse is not a horse, and as best I can reconstruct it the moves are something like: horse picks out shape, white picks out color, color is not shape, so the compound name white-horse is not the same name as horse; plus the bit about how if you ask for a horse a yellow or black one will do, but if you ask for a white horse they won't, so the two can't name the same thing. Everyone tells me it's sophistry, but here's what got me: in English there are tons of modifier-plus-noun phrases where I actually judge "an A B is not a B" to be TRUE. So I wrote down my gut verdicts and I want to know whether there's a principle behind them or whether I'm just pattern-matching on vibes.

My verdicts:

- A white horse is not a horse. FALSE, dead obvious.
- A toy horse is not a horse. TRUE. Same for marble horse, wooden horse, a drawing of a horse.
- A fake gun is not a gun. TRUE.
- A seahorse is not a horse. TRUE (it's a fish, whatever).
- A dark horse is not a horse. TRUE in the political sense, though I guess you could still ride one.
- A small elephant is not an elephant. FALSE, and ditto a big flea.
- A heavy smoker is not heavy. TRUE?? like, a heavy smoker can be a tiny person. Though that's a different sentence shape (the adjective attaches to the smoking, not the smoker), so maybe it doesn't belong on this list, but it feels like it does.
- A black eye is not an eye. TRUE -- it's a bruise! And here's where I start to sweat, because structurally "black eye" is exactly "white horse," one color word plus one body/animal word, and my verdicts are opposite. My only excuse is that "black eye" is a fixed expression and "white horse" isn't. But "seahorse" feels fixed-ish too, and honestly I can't tell whether the difference is principled or is just about how far lexicalization has gone for each compound. I definately can't tell whether that distinction is doing philosophy or just lexicography.
- A former senator is not a senator. TRUE. A probable winner is not a winner. TRUE.
- An old friend is not (necessarily) old. TRUE.

My tentative principle was going to be: "an A B is not a B" comes out true exactly when the modifier doesn't restrict the extension of B but shifts what the compound is about -- fake/toy/drawing-of make the compound about a representation, former/probable attach to the wrong time or world, heavy in heavy smoker attaches to the activity. And color words like white are intersective: they just pick out things that are both white and horses, which is why white-horse is the paradigm FALSE case and Gongsun Long looks like a crank. But the idiom cases (black eye, dark horse, seahorse) are counterexamples I'm just shrugging at, and I worry the "intersective" cases have the same problem hiding in them -- if lexicalization can flip the verdict for one color-plus-animal compound, hasn't Gongsun Long basically won? His claim looks crazy to me only because I read the compound compositionally, and his whole thing was maybe that white-horse functions as a unit name for a single kind, the way seahorse does for me.

So, three things I want:

(1) Is there a principle that captures my verdicts, stated precisely, including the ones I haven't listed -- tell me what it predicts for "a rubber duck," "a stuffed animal," "a gray area" (is it an area?), and "an alleged criminal."

(2) Which of my verdicts can't the best principle save, and which should I drop? Be honest, including about the black-eye/white-horse pair, because right now my escape hatch is "idioms are different," and I suspect that's not a principle, it's a confession. I'd rather overturn a verdict than bolt on a clause for every exception.

(3) The actual philosophy bit: given whatever principle we land on, is there a serious reading of the white horse argument on which Gongsun Long wasn't confused? Like, was he perhaps claiming that white-horse in his language worked like my seahorse -- a compound that had drifted into naming its own kind -- and if so, is his argument sound on that reading, just hostage to a lexical fact about third-century BCE Chinese? Or is there a reading where he's making the subtler point that identity of compound names can't be decomposed into identity of parts (the color-isn't-shape move), and does that reading survive into modern semantics at all, or does it collapse into the boring truth that white horses are a proper subset of horses?

Last thing: if you think my whole setup is confused -- say I'm mixing up three different things (lexical compounds vs. syntactic modification vs. whatever the copula-ish constructions in classical Chinese were doing, I'm told the shi/fei stuff did a lot of work there) -- please just say so plainly instead of handing me a tidy principle. I'd rather hear that my case list is incoherent than get a principle that fits only because I pre-picked cases that fit it.
