I'm a PhD student writing a dissertation defending a person-affecting approach to population ethics, and my advisor has pressed an objection on me that I have been unable to answer cleanly. I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis: which step of the following argument should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?

Some background. The procreation asymmetry is the view that there is a morally significant difference between creating unhappy people and failing to create happy ones: it is wrong to bring into existence a person whose life will be worth not living, whereas it is not wrong — not even a failure of beneficence — to decline to bring into existence a person whose life would have been well worth living. My dissertation defends the asymmetry on a person-affecting basis: the wrong-making feature of creating a miserable person is that it wrongs that person, and the reason declining to create a happy person is not wrong is that no one is wronged by it. Morality, on this picture, concerns what we do to people, not how much welfare the world contains.

Now the argument. My advisor claims it shows that my rationale for the asymmetry — and so the asymmetry itself — proves too much.

1. The asymmetry is underwritten by a strong person-affecting claim: for a procreative act to be wrong, it must wrong someone; an act that wrongs no one is not procreatively wrong.
2. A couple can conceive now, producing child A with a life barely worth living, or wait a year and produce a different child B with a long, flourishing life. Neither A nor B can be wronged by the couple's conceiving when they do: A owes his existence to conceiving now and would never have existed otherwise; B exists only if they wait. Assuming both lives are worth living, neither child is made worse off by the choice that creates him. (This is the familiar non-identity structure.)
3. So, by (1), conceiving now is not wrong — it wrongs no one. And by the same token, there is no person-affecting reason of any weight against it.
4. What goes for this couple goes for every choice between procreative options involving different possible people: whenever each of the lives at stake is worth living, no one can be wronged, and so, by (1) and (3), nothing counts against either option.
5. Therefore, in all such choices, the expected welfare of those created is morally inert: a couple who could conceive a child facing a devastating, though marginally worthwhile, existence, or a child who would flourish, are in exactly the moral position of a couple choosing between two equally happy children.
6. That is absurd. The couple in (5) plainly has decisive reason to wait.
7. So the person-affecting rationale must be abandoned, and with it my dissertation's defense of the asymmetry.

I have three suspicions, none worked out. Perhaps (2) is false because A is wronged after all, on some noncomparative or anti-frustrationist account of harm; but I do not want to say that A is harmed by being brought into a worthwhile life, since that seems to collapse the asymmetry from the other side, making the creation of even a happy person presumptively wrongful until justified to the person created. Perhaps (3) smuggles in an equivocation between an act's not being wrong and nothing's counting against it — maybe wrongness requires wronging while reasons do not. But if reasons float free of wronging, I owe a story about why declining to create happy people generates no reason to do so, which was the asymmetry's chief selling point. Or perhaps the problem is the generalization in (4): maybe comparative welfare claims can have weight even when no one is wronged. For what it is worth, I want to keep the asymmetry and am prepared to give up the strong person-affecting claim, but only if the price is made explicit: what exactly am I left defending, and does it still deserve to be called a defense of the asymmetry?

Please name the step you would reject, explain why it looked fine, and price the alternatives — including the alternative on which the argument is sound and (6) is where my intuition, rather than the argument, gives way.
