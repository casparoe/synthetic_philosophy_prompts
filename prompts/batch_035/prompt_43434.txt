I'm drafting a position piece for a workshop on deception in professional life — law, medicine, negotiation — and before I send it out I want an audit of whether the views I've committed to on paper actually fit together. Here is the package, roughly as it will appear:

1. Lying is asserting, in the assertoric mode and with the intent that one's audience believe it, something one believes false. There is no lying without assertion: implicature, strategic emphasis, selective silence, and true-but-engineered utterances are not lies.
2. The wrongness of lying is constituted by attempted doxastic manipulation — the liar aims to install a false belief, thereby usurping the hearer's epistemic agency. The wrong does not essentially attach to the act-type of asserting; it attaches to what the speaker is trying to do to the hearer's mind.
3. The knowledge norm of assertion is correct: one asserts properly only what one knows.
4. Misleading is sometimes permissible where lying would not be. When you mislead with true utterances, the hearer's false belief is partly the product of their own inference, so they remain, in a partial but morally significant sense, its author — and that difference in authorship can matter.
5. Doorstep case: when the person intent on violence asks me where their target is, I may answer "I've been out all afternoon" (true — the target arrived after I left, though I know perfectly well where they are now), but I may not assert a falsehood.
6. Negotiation case ("paltering"): a negotiator who secures a deal through deliberately chosen true statements that they know the counterpart will take the wrong way acts almost as wrongly as a liar. The authorship consideration in (4) does not exonerate them, because in negotiation the counterpart's reliance on cooperative interpretation is exactly what the palterer weaponizes.
7. Consent cases: lying to someone who has asked to be deceived — the surprise party, the "tell me the treatment is going well" — is not wrong, or barely.
8. A meta-commitment: the lying/misleading distinction must be normatively deep rather than a grammatical artifact, and it should be able to explain why professional codes of conduct draw the line roughly where they do — forbidding the statement of falsehoods while tolerating the failure to correct a counterpart's or client's false inferences.

I have two custom cases I use in the paper, and my verdicts on them are part of what's being audited. In the first, a landlord's agent is asked by a prospective tenant whether the building is quiet, and answers "the street is very lively," knowing the tenant will take this as praise of the location when the agent knows it means a nightclub across the road. I hold this is permissible, on the strength of (4). In the second, a physician who knows a diagnostic test was mishandled tells the patient "the results are consistent with what we expected," which is true of the mishandled results and predictably convinces the patient the test was sound. I hold this is impermissible, and closer to lying than the agent's utterance is, though it satisfies my definition of a non-lie in (1).

Go through the commitments and my two verdicts, and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and what each tension costs to resolve. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious, and identify the single revision that would clean up the most if I made it. Do not assume I have a favorite commitment I'm protecting — I genuinely don't know where the weak point is, and I suspect it may be somewhere I haven't looked, possibly in the interaction between (2) and (6) or between (4) and the physician case, but I may be wrong about that too. Finally, tell me what would change your verdict on the most severe tension — which argument or consideration, if it failed or succeeded, would lead you to a different ranking — and point to where in your own analysis you are least confident, so I know which parts of your audit to pressure-test before I rely on them.
