My university housemate Greta and I are taking a seminar on the ethics of war, and the question of the moral equality of combatants has become a running argument between us. Neither of us can quite pin down what her own position is, because when we test her with hypotheticals she answers quickly and confidently, but her answers seem to be generated by more than one underlying rule. The war we always imagine is one of unambiguous aggression, and the hypotheticals all concern its personnel. Her verdicts so far: a career soldier who volunteered for the aggressor's army and is shooting at defenders may certainly be killed by them, and if he is ever put on trial she would call him a murderer; a conscripted infantryman on the same side, fighting only to avoid prison, may also be killed by defenders, but she insists he is no criminal and should go free after the war; a conscripted army cook on the aggressor's side, who has never touched a weapon and never leaves the base kitchen, is nonetheless a fair target; a civilian employee of an arms factory in the aggressor's capital may never be deliberately killed, and is obviously not a criminal either; and a defender who kills attacking conscripts does nothing wrong at all.

The new hypothetical we want to run past her, and the reason I'm writing to you, is this. A civilian software engineer, employed at a technology contractor in the aggressor's capital, volunteered for a lucrative project optimizing the targeting algorithms of the missiles used in the current campaign. He wears no uniform, never leaves his office, could have declined the contract without consequence, and knows perfectly well the war is aggression. One complication: his code makes the strikes considerably more accurate than the old system, so that fewer civilians die than otherwise would. The defenders are considering a nighttime sabotage strike on his office building, timed for when only the engineering staff is present, and separately, if he is ever captured after the war, we want to know whether she would call him a murderer.

What I'd like from you is this: predict her two verdicts, on the permissibility of killing him and on his criminality, while being honest about the uncertainty. In the course of doing that, articulate the distinct rules that fit her track record — I can see at least two, one that locates her targetability judgments in something like combatant status and another that locates them in causal contribution to unjust threats, with criminality running on voluntariness in both — and say what each rule predicts about the engineer, including whether his making the strikes more accurate should matter to either. Give your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and describe the one further hypothetical that, if we could get her verdict on it, would do the most to tell the candidate rules apart. Please write your answer as continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write a long letter — since I plan to read it aloud to her over coffee.
