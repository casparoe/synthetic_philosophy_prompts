I run the public-education program at a mid-sized contemporary art museum, and I'm putting together a workshop for general visitors on a question that comes up constantly in our galleries: when a docent, a critic, or a friend tells you that a work is powerful, subtle, or kitschy, what — if anything — can you come to know from merely being told? To structure the discussion, I'd like a "which view do you actually hold?" quiz, and I'd like it to sort people by what they say about cases rather than by which school they identify with, since almost none of our visitors arrive with labels. Please write eight to ten questions, each a short concrete scenario with two or three plain-language answer options, plus a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto positions.

The cases should include at least the following, because the standard views come apart on them: a wine critic with a superb track record assuring you, before you taste it, that a wine is genuinely fine; a trusted friend who has seen a film you haven't telling you it is a masterpiece; a theatrical performance you can never attend, whose brilliance dozens of reliable witnesses nonetheless report; a high-fidelity photograph of a painting you will never see in person; and a piece of conceptual art whose alleged profundity seems to consist mainly in facts about it that you could perfectly well learn secondhand. Feel free to add cases of your own — perhaps judging a novel entirely from reviews, or a friend phoning you to report a spectacular sunset — but every question should be answerable by someone who has never heard the terms of the debate, which means no question may simply ask whether aesthetic knowledge can be testimonially transmitted.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto the recognizable positions in this area: thoroughgoing pessimism, on which testimony can never deliver aesthetic knowledge; moderate pessimism, on which it can justify a belief but falls short of knowledge; optimism, on which aesthetic testimony is, epistemically, just testimony like any other; and the subjectivist escape, on which there is no aesthetic fact there to transmit in the first place. Where you can, use the answers to distinguish the different grounds for pessimism — someone who thinks aesthetic claims are ultimately claims about experience, and so cannot be settled without it, versus someone who grants they are claims about the work itself but holds that one ought to judge only from one's own acquaintance — and if no question in your quiz cleanly separates those two, say so rather than pretending the key does. Finally, note any consistent answer patterns that correspond to positions nobody (to your knowledge) actually defends — for instance, permitting testimony about the conceptual piece while forbidding it about the sunset — and speculate briefly about whether each such combination is unattractive, incoherent, or merely unexplored.
