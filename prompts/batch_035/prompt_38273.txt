I'm grading midterms for a phil of mind course (third-year, topics course on emotion) and want a second pair of eyes on one answer before I commit to a grade. The question was:

"Explain the disagreement between (a) theories that analyze emotions as judgments, (b) theories that analyze them as perceptions, and (c) theories that analyze them as feelings. Give what you take to be the strongest consideration favoring one of the three, the strongest objection one of the other two could raise in reply, and say which position you think ends up standing."

Here's the student's answer, typos left as-is:

"The three theories basically disagree about how cognitive emotions are. Judgment theories, going back to the Stoics and defended more recently by Robert Solomon, say an emotion just is a judgment, e.g. anger is the judgment that you've been wronged. Solomon, following the Stoics, also thought that since these are judgments we make, they're under our control, and like the Stoics he concluded we should aim to eliminate emotions and reach apatheia. The obvious objection is that animals and infants clearly have emotions but can't make judgments, so either they don't have emotions or the theory is wrong.

Perceptual theories hold that emotions are perceptions. This is essentially William James's view: the emotion of fear just is the perception of your bodily changes -- racing heart, trembling, etc. Jesse Prinz later revived this kind of view. Feeling theories, on the other hand, say emotions are just feelings, inner sensations like the churning in your stomach. The big problem for the feeling theory is intentionality: a twinge isn't about anything, but fear is about the bear in front of you. The perceptual theory handles this nicely, because perceptions always have objects, so emotions must have objects too. That already gives the perception theory a clear edge over the feeling theory.

Overall I think the judgment theory survives best. The animal/infant objection can be answered by saying animals and infants make nonconceptual judgments, and once you allow that, the difference between judgment theories and perceptual theories is mostly verbal, since both are cognitive. Recalcitrant emotions, like being afraid of flying while judging it perfectly safe, are no problem either -- people just have inconsistent beliefs, and that's common enough."
