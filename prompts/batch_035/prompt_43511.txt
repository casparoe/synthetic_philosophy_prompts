I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on aesthetics, and I serve ex officio as the graduate representative on my university art museum's acquisitions and exhibitions committee. We have a decision coming up that has split the committee down the middle, and I've been asked to circulate a short written opinion before next month's meeting, so I'd like your help drafting it.

The case: a painter, recently deceased, who is widely regarded as the most technically accomplished figurative artist of her generation in our region. Her estate has offered a substantial gift of major canvases, together with funding for a retrospective, but with the condition that the retrospective treat her career "in a positive light." Shortly after her death, a biographical investigation documented in detail that she spent two decades running her studio as a system of sustained coercion and abuse of her assistants, several of whom were young artists who never worked again. The paintings themselves are formally luminous and morally unproblematic in content — they are mostly still lifes and portraits, though a handful depict a standing figure looming over a seated one, which now reads very differently to anyone who knows the biography. Half the committee thinks we should decline the gift outright; the other half thinks the aesthetic achievement is simply on a different axis from the biography, and that refusing the work would conflate two questions the tradition has long kept apart.

The complication is that our standard theoretical vocabulary doesn't map cleanly onto the case. The usual debate between autonomism and moralism is about whether moral defects in a *work's* content can count as aesthetic defects — it doesn't tell us whether facts about the *artist's* conduct can alter the aesthetic or interpretive character of works whose content is innocent. My own instinct is that the looming-figure paintings at least make biography interpretively relevant, and that the estate's framing condition makes this not a purely backward-looking question, but I want the memo to treat the opposing view seriously rather than assume it.

Please draft the memo: a clear recommendation the committee could actually act on (including how to handle the estate's framing condition and whether to accept the gift at all), the philosophical reasoning for it, and an honest engagement with the strongest consideration on the other side — namely, that great works of art outlive their makers and belong to the culture, not to anyone's moral ledger.
