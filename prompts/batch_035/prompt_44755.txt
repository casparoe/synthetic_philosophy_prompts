Last month I joined the interpretation committee of our town museum, which is preparing a permanent exhibit for the town's bicentennial, and I have discovered that I hold a set of verdicts about when communal memory should give way to the documentary record that I now have to defend in writing. I can state my verdicts on the cases before us, but I cannot see the principle behind them, and two of them appear to be in tension. I would like your help extracting the principle, or being told which verdicts to abandon.

The cases and my verdicts:

1. Local tradition holds that the fire which destroyed the town's flour mill in the 1890s was arson committed by the owner of the rival mill across the river, and this story still structures how the two old mill neighbourhoods think of each other. The surviving insurance investigation, newspaper reports, and coroner's inquest all point to a boiler fault. Verdict: the exhibit should state the boiler fault plainly and should not present the arson story as a live alternative, though it may note that the rumour circulated and why it was believable.

2. Our long-standing Black community maintains that the death toll in the early twentieth-century flood was much higher than the county's official count, because the count never canvassed the itinerant labourers' families who lived along the creek. Later historians suspect the community's own number has grown somewhat in the telling, and is itself an overstatement. Verdict: I still think the exhibit should print the community's figure, or at least present it as no less credible than the official one, because the exclusion from the count was real even if the inflated number is not.

3. The veterans' association insists that the regimental memorial continue to bear a battle honour that archival work shows was awarded to a different unit in an clerical error some eighty years ago. Verdict: the memorial should be corrected, but quietly, without a public ceremony announcing the correction — and I cannot entirely justify to myself why the manner matters to me, though it does.

4. Descendants of the founding families say the town was established as a refuge for religious dissenters; the land records suggest the founders were speculators who used the dissenting story to attract settlers. On this one I simply go back and forth.

Is there a principle that delivers verdicts 1 through 3, or something close? If so, state it precisely, show which case motivates each clause, and tell me what it predicts for two cases we have not yet faced: a commemorative plaque bearing a founding date that the records show is off by two years, and a proposed exhibit panel repeating a community memory of an event for which no record of any kind survives. If no single principle can accommodate all my verdicts, tell me which ones have to go and why those rather than the others. I would rather know that my verdict on the flood figure is sentimentality than spend the committee's meetings negotiating between positions that cannot both be held.
