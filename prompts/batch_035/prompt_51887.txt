I've been reading around Scheffler's afterlife conjecture — the thought that a lot of what we care about in our current lives quietly depends on assuming that humanity, or at least other people, will still be around after we die, so that a "collective afterlife" matters to us in ways we don't notice until we imagine it going away. What I want you to do is argue for a reading he doesn't really run with: that the collective afterlife doesn't have to consist of biological humans. Suppose humanity dies out but leaves behind successor minds — intelligent machines, or heavily engineered descendants, doesn't matter for the argument — that carry on our science, our art, our institutions, our projects in general. Argue that this kind of continuation is enough to secure the "afterlife" our values depend on, that the conjecture should be stated in terms of the continuation of valued projects rather than the survival of Homo sapiens as such.

Two things to take seriously as you build the case. First, the intuition that a world of machine successors finishing our projects is a hollow substitute rather than a genuine continuation — give that its strongest form rather than a strawman. Second, the possibility that once you divorce the afterlife from humanity, you've changed the conjecture so much that it stops being Scheffler's point at all — maybe what we actually rely on is the assumption that beings rather like us, with futures of their own that they care about, will go on, and no substitution can do that work.

And look, if you think the conjecture itself is shakier than I'm granting, or if the extension to nonhuman successors turns out to gut the idea rather than broaden it, just say so instead of politely playing along — I'd rather be corrected than agreeably misled.
