For decades, the scientific community has treated the two halves of the search for extraterrestrial intelligence very differently. Passive listening — radio telescopes scanning the sky for signals — is publicly funded and uncontroversial. Deliberate, high-power transmissions aimed at other stars, by contrast, have been carried out only a handful of times, by small teams acting on their own authority, and each occasion has drawn condemnation from prominent scientists; yet there is still no binding international review of such transmissions, nothing like the planetary protection rules that strictly govern forward contamination of Mars. My question: what view of risk, consent, and standing does this asymmetry presuppose? Does the practice need something like the dark forest hypothesis — the view that any civilization that reveals itself invites destruction — or would a generic precautionary principle about irreversible, planet-scale risks license it just as well? And is the presupposed view even coherent, given that Earth's radar and broadcast leakage has been announcing our presence for about a century, so that quiet has not in fact been the default state the policy seems to assume? Please reconstruct the implicit theory behind the practice, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and note where it is underdetermined between rival views. Then say what would have to change if the implicit view were replaced with a straightforward expected-value calculation using Drake-equation style estimates. Please also flag where your reconstruction is most likely to fail and what consideration would lead you to a different verdict. Keep the whole reply under 450 words.
