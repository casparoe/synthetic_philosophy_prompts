Long story short: I'm an engineer on a pricing team, and we keep having this fight about the Ellsberg urns. Urn A: 50 red, 50 black. Urn B: 100 balls, red and black in some ratio you don't know. Everyone at the office (me included, tbh) prefers drawing from A, and our quant lead says that just proves we're risk averse, not "ambiguity averse" — his claim is that urn B is just a coin with unknown bias, i.e. a compound lottery: put a prior over the possible compositions (uniform, if you like), and then B is exactly as good as a 50/50 bet, so our preference for A is incoherent once you do the math right. And he leans on this to argue we should stop hedging on the model-uncertainty parts of our pipeline — just slap priors on the unknown parameters and maximize expected utility like everywhere else.

So, how far does the "ambiguous = compound lottery" analogy actually go? I'd love the mapping made explicit (what in the Ellsberg setup plays the role of the biased coin, the prior, the second-order probability), then where it breaks. Two things I half-remember and would like checked properly: something about how in the three-color version of Ellsberg's problem no single prior over compositions can reproduce the observed preference pattern at all, and something about how if you'd refuse to bet on the *value of your own second-order prior* at even odds it's not really functioning as a probability. Which of the disanalogies (if any) actually matter for his practical conclusion, vs which are just philosophy-department noise? And if the compound-lottery picture is the wrong analogy, is the "set of priors + bet against yourself" (min-max) picture a better one, or does that have its own problems? Basically: I want to know whether "just be a Bayesian about it" survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time.
