A colleague of mine in cognitive science recently sent me a conference paper that opens by summarizing the debate over the norm of assertion, and her summary has been needling me ever since. She writes that the knowledge norm, associated with Timothy Williamson, holds that one may assert that p only if one knows that p; that since knowledge requires certainty, the norm demands certainty, making it far too strict to accommodate ordinary testimony or the practice of science; that the decisive consideration in favor of the knowledge norm is Moore's paradox, since the absurdity of asserting "p, but I don't believe p" is supposed to show that assertion is governed by a knowledge rule; and that the rival truth norm neatly avoids all this, since on that view it is permissible to assert p whenever p is in fact true, whether or not the speaker knows it, which better fits our tolerance of fallible but honest assertion. I am inclined to defend the knowledge norm, but I want a dispassionate audit rather than a lawyer's brief. Go through her summary claim by claim: identify what a defender of the knowledge norm would accept, where she has conflated distinct theses or overstated a modest one, and whether the errors matter to the comparison she draws between the knowledge and truth norms. In particular, I would like to know whether Moore's paradox supports what she says it supports, and whether her characterization of the truth norm is one anyone actually holds. Please close by telling me whether her implicit conclusion — that the truth norm is the more sensible position — survives the corrections, or whether the corrected dialectical picture is closer to a standoff.
