I'm a palliative care nurse practitioner who runs the orientation for residents rotating through our inpatient hospice service, and every few weeks one of them freezes at the same point: a dying patient's pain or agitation is out of control, the family is desperate, and the resident is scared that uptitrating the morphine or starting sedation is going to "kill the patient" — some of them have said flat out they don't want to be the person who gives a fatal dose. Our attendings each give different advice, so the residents get whiplash. One says intent is what matters and if you mean to relieve suffering you're fine; another says the doses we actually use don't hasten death, mostly; a third just tells them never give anything without a documented attending order. I want something more consistent and teachable: a written decision procedure the residents can run at 3am, built around the doctrine of double effect but honestly translated into clinical terms — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand off to the attending or an ethics consult because it's a values question rather than a clinical one.

The procedure needs to keep apart the distinctions the residents keep collapsing: intending a bad outcome vs merely foreseeing it, giving a drug that relieves symptoms and might shorten life vs giving something whose only possible effect is to end it (they ask about things like paralytics at the very end), and withdrawing something we started (ventilator, iv fluids) vs starting a new intervention. Also the residents can't tell the difference between sedation for refractory suffering and sedation for the family's peace of mind, so the procedure should catch that somewhere. Then run it on three cases: (1) refractory terminal agitation where the plan is continuous deep sedation and the family is on board; (2) a resident asked to push opioids for a patient who is actually comfortable but breathing noisily, where the family wants him "completely peaceful" — sedation for their sake, not his; (3) a planned extubation where death is expected within minutes and the resident has to give the pre-extubation opioids themselves — that last one is where I already know I won't like wherever the procedure lands, but I want to see it argued anyway rather than dodged.

One more thing: be honest that the intent/foresight distinction can't be settled by just asking the resident what they intended — if the procedure needs a proxy for intent, say what the proxy is and why it's better than self-report. And flag which steps are really judgment calls dressed up as checklist items, because the residents will trust it more if it admits its limits. It shouldn't be longer than what a resident could realistically read on a shift — aim for something we can print on two sides of one page, with the worked cases as an appendix.
