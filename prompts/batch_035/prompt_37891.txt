I teach an upper-level seminar in the philosophy of cognitive science — twelve students, one ninety-minute session — on the question of whether neural networks genuinely have representations: whether the features interpretability researchers claim to find in a model are really in the model, with causal powers of their own, or whether they are projections of the interpreter, useful fictions that organize prediction without corresponding to anything in the weights. Design a staged role-play in which the students run into this question before it has a name.

My own sketch, which you should feel free to override: half the students physically implement a small "network" — each is a node with a simple, private input-output rule written on an index card, wired into a fixed architecture that classifies drawn inputs — while the other half form an interpretability team with access only to inputs and designated readouts, never to the cards themselves. Whatever you design, preserve the two structural facts: that a system with determinate hidden internals exists, and that another group must learn it purely through behavioral access.

Constraints: no computers or code — paper, index cards, and a blackboard only. Points rather than money. At some point the interpretability team should be paid for predictions on held-out inputs, and at a later point for predictions about interventions — disconnecting a node, or forcing one to output a constant — because I want the moment where their interpretive story comes apart from a merely predictive summary to arrive as an event the room lives through, not as a claim the facilitator asserts. Include at least one round engineered to produce underdetermination: two different interpretive stories that both keep predicting correctly, so the class must confront what, if anything, makes one of them the representation the network actually has. And plant a scripted wrinkle that tempts the interpreters to ascribe a concept the implementers know is not there.

Then the debrief. Map specific moments in the session onto positions in the debate — the view that an interpretation is nothing over and above a predictive summary, the view that some features are causally real components of the mechanism, and any intermediate position you think earns its keep — and say which moments favor which side. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it. Finally, confront the deepest disanalogy head-on: in the classroom there is a ground truth — the implementers' index cards — whereas for a trained network the realist's entire claim is that something analogous exists without anyone having written it down. Decide for yourself whether that disanalogy fatally weakens the exercise or is itself the lesson. And in the debrief, take a side on whether the session should leave students closer to realism or to deflation; make the design choices definite and argue for them — do not hand me a menu of variants or a balanced both-sides ending and let me pick.
