My thesis is that the standard coherence arguments get you much less than people claim: they show, at most, that an agent shouldn't be exploitable by a shrewd bookie in a specific setup, but they don't get you all the way to expected utility maximization. My paper has three moves: first, Dutch book arguments only target credences that violate the probability axioms, so they say nothing about the utility side; second, the money pump story needs extra assumptions about sequential choice (that you can't just refuse to trade, that you'll notice the pattern, that the pump is finite) and once you make those assumptions explicit, the "coherence" they enforce is thinner than EU; and third, when you look at what's left after you strip the exploitability rhetoric away, what survives is basically just transitivity plus maybe some weak ordering conditions -- which I'm calling "thin coherence." I want to be upfront that I'm aware of Gustafsson's book defending money-pump arguments against the standard objections, and at least one of the economists there will have read Buchak's Risk and Rationality carefully, so I can't get away with the cartoon version of the dialectic where the pro-EU side just didn't notice the sequential-choice loopholes.
