I teach an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of probability, and I'm writing the take-home final. Please draft one essay question on the reference class problem, answerable in about 90 minutes, that cannot be passed by reciting that Reichenbach raised it and that Bostrom catalogued some responses.

The question should fix a concrete case up front, roughly this: an actuary must price a life-insurance policy for a specific 60-year-old — call him Mr. Jones, a non-smoking, somewhat overweight accountant with a family history of cardiac disease. Every candidate reference class (all 60-year-olds; male 60-year-olds; male non-smokers of that build with that family history; ...) gives a different relative frequency, and the obvious escape — always take the narrowest class containing the case — seems to end at the singleton, the class whose only member is Mr. Jones himself, where relative frequency has gone silent and we seem to have lost the probability rather than found it.

The question should then force three confrontations. First: is this a defect specific to frequency interpretations, or does it recur for any account that assigns single-case probabilities — and if a subjectivist says the actuary should just conditionalize on everything she knows about Mr. Jones, has she dissolved the problem or relocated it into the prior? Second: what exactly is wrong with the narrowing strategy — is it that the evidence runs out before the classes do, or something deeper about what a probability over a singleton could even be, and can a defender of narrowing specify a principled stopping point? Third, and this is where I want to separate the A answers from the B answers: does the same structure reappear when the "case" is oneself — as in anthropic and self-selection reasoning, where the question is which class of observers or trials your own situation should be treated as drawn from, and where the singleton problem seems to bite harder because the one thing you cannot do is observe yourself not existing? Students should have to say whether anthropic reference classes are the same problem wearing a costume or a genuinely new one.

Please also provide a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme of five must-hit points worth 20 marks total, with the marks for each point and a one-line note on how candidates typically fail it — in particular, name the specific move that separates an A answer from a B answer (my guess is it's whether they notice that the Bayesian relocation claim and the narrowing strategy are in tension, but you should judge). The model answer should take a position, not survey.
