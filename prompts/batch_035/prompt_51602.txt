As a journalist covering science and ideas, I keep running into one particular analogy offered as a response to the causal exclusion problem: the brain is the hardware, the mind is the software, and just as a bug in the code causes the crash without competing with the electronic transactions in the circuits, my decision to raise my arm can cause the raising without competing with the neural firings that also cause it. The analogy is meant to show that causation comes in non-rivalrous levels, and hence that Kim's exclusion argument — that wherever physical causes suffice, mental causes are either identical to them or idle — misses something about how levels work. Audit the analogy: give me the correspondence table (what plays the role of the program, the running process, the hardware, the crash), say where the mapping breaks, and flag which features of the computer case the analogy quietly imports — after all, the machine is a designed artifact, and its "software level" arguably exists only relative to the engineers and users who care how it is described. Then tell me whether the no-competition conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument the whole time, and what a defensible version of the response would have to look like if there is one.
