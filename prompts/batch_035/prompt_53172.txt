I teach a third-year seminar in democratic theory at a mid-sized university; sixteen students, one 90-minute session, and we have just finished a unit on the epistemic arguments for and against democracy — Condorcet-style aggregation on one side, voter ignorance and rational irrationality on the other. Next week I want to tackle futarchy: the proposal associated with Robin Hanson under which the electorate would vote only on a measurable national welfare metric, while the choice among policies would be delegated to prediction markets, with traders putting money on which policy will move the metric. Rather than lecture on it, I want to run an in-class game in which students first govern by voting and then by trading, so that they run into futarchy's distinctive problems from the inside before we name any of them. They should not hear the word futarchy until the debrief.

Please design the full game: rules, materials, a timeline that fits 90 minutes, and a payoff scheme using a classroom currency or course-credit tokens rather than money. Several things I want baked into the design. Students should hold private, individually weak signals about which of two fictional policies will perform better, so that the information-aggregation story is genuinely live and the contrast with majority voting is visible in the data the class itself generates. There should be a small faction whose payoff depends on which policy gets chosen, not on the accuracy of their trades, so the class meets the manipulation objection as participants rather than as readers. And the market should be conditional on the decision — positions pay out on the metric given which policy is adopted — because I want at least a few students to notice that the market is not predicting a fixed fact about the world but is partly causing the outcome it prices. A fourth wrinkle, if you can make it work cleanly: the class's welfare metric is only measurable in the distant future, so resolution has to happen on a proxy that I control, and I would like the students to discover the gap between the metric and the proxy through their own trading rather than through my announcing it.

Then the debrief: map each phase of the game onto the corresponding claim or objection in the actual futarchy debate — the epistemic-superiority claim for markets over votes, the manipulation objection and the standard reply that would-be manipulators are just a subsidy to honest traders, the self-referential character of decision-conditional markets, and the metric-selection problem (who votes on the value, and what stops that vote from being corrupted in the same way ordinary policy voting is said to be?). I also want the debrief to be honest about what the classroom version cannot reproduce — I assume real financial stakes, liquidity, and long time horizons are the big omissions — and which parts of futarchy's appeal will therefore have to be supplied by me rather than experienced by the students. Finally, since I have run classroom games before and they always break somewhere: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and what I should have ready as a contingency.
