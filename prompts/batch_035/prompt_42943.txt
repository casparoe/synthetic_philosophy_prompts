I run a small social choice reading group — mostly philosophy grad students who just worked through Arrow's theorem, plus two econ people, one of whom thinks the whole "rights in social choice" literature is philosophy cosplay and refuses to say the word liberty without doing air quotes. We've just finished Sen's 1970 impossibility of a Paretian liberal paper, and for the group's blog I want to make a quiz: "which resolution of the liberal paradox do you actually hold?" The premise is that everyone in the room will say they're fine just dropping Pareto or whatever, but once you hit them with the actual cases their intuctions scatter, and I want to catch that.

Format: eight to nine questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions. Use the classics and variants: the prude and the person who wants Lady Chatterley's Lover to be read, plus at least one case where the rights-holder barely cares about their own choice but cares a lot about everyone else's (the matching-pennies style one definately has to be in there — think two offices sharing a thermostat, or a neighborhood where everyone's deciding simultaneously whether to get vaccinated), and one symmetric case among strangers, like both neighbors would rather both throw loud parties than neither, but each most prefers partying while the other stays quiet. No question can just ask "do you accept minimal liberalism" — everything has to be a case a smart person could answer without ever having heard of Sen, and nobody should be able to tell from the wording which philosopher a question is funneling them toward.

Positions that need to show up as leaves: (1) the straight welfare economist, who drops liberty as an axiom and lets Pareto run everything; (2) drop Pareto, at least inside the protected sphere; (3) restrict the domain — kick the meddlesome preferences out of the formalism; (4) the Gibbard-style line that our preferences are all contaminated with nosiness anyway, so the whole setup is confused from the start (my hunch is this is really hard to seperate from 3 by cases alone — if so, just say so rather than pretending the quiz can tell them apart); (5) game-form rights, where your right is control over your move in the game and not over outcomes, with the social outcome being whatever the game form then delivers; and (6) Sen's own-ish stance of living with the impossibility — taking it to show that rights are about something non-consequentialist that this framework can't capture. If you think there's a well-known position I'm missing, add it as a leaf. And if some combination of answers lands nowhere, flag it and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored.

One more thing for the scoring key: please clearly seperate what's actually settled in the literature (the theorem itself, which axioms it really needs, what the formal result does and doesn't show) from what's genuinely contested among people who know this stuff — e.g. whether game-form rights actually solve the paradox or just restate it in different vocabulary, or whether leaf 4 is really a distinct position at all. I don't want the quiz smuggling in someone's contested take as if it were the consensus. Keep it to eight or nine questions, don't go over ten, and don't turn the scoring key into an essay.
