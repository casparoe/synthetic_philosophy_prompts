I'm a PhD student in epistemology, and this term I'm TA-ing a third-year course that spends two weeks on the norms of assertion. From past experience, students arrive with positions they've picked up by label rather than by verdict: they'll say they think assertion is governed by a knowledge norm, or that only truth matters, but their judgments about particular cases scatter across the logical space. So before the seminar, I want to give them a diagnostic quiz in the style of those internet sorting quizzes, but built entirely from cases: the point is to sort them by what they say about examples, not by which slogan they find congenial. Please write me eight to ten questions, each a short concrete scenario with two or three answer options phrased as verdicts about the scenario (never as endorsements of a labeled thesis), followed by a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the main positions — the knowledge norm (including any contextualist variant worth distinguishing), the truth norm, the belief or sincerity norm, and a justification-or-reasonable-belief norm.

The scenarios I'd like you to draw on should include, roughly: lottery propositions (someone asserting, before the draw, that their one-in-ten-million ticket will lose); selfless or self-less assertions (the classic teacher who asserts the evidence for evolution to her students while, because of her upbringing, sincerely failing to believe it — is that assertion proper or a violation of the rules of the game?); Moore-paradoxical assertions (someone asserting 'p, but I don't believe that p'); an assertion that is justified, true, and believed yet Gettiered; a confidently asserted falsehood the speaker had excellent evidence for; and behavioral evidence, like whether a challenged asserter should retract or merely qualify, and what counts as the appropriate response to the simple challenge 'How do you know?'. Feel free to add or replace scenarios where they do better diagnostic work, but each question should be one where the candidate norms genuinely come apart, and the options should be worded so a student can answer without being able to guess which theory they're voting for.

Two things I'd like the scoring key to be honest about. First, please flag any pair of positions the answer patterns cannot reliably separate — my suspicion is that a truth norm and a belief norm will issue identical verdicts on many of my scenarios unless the quiz is very carefully constructed, and I'd rather know that than paper over it. Second, please note which combinations of answers correspond to no defended position, and speculate briefly about whether that's because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. The quiz will go out to about sixty students with mixed backgrounds, so it needs to be runnable in ten minutes without any prior reading, and I'd like the key to tell me, for a student with a given answer profile, which follow-up case in seminar discussion is most likely to destabilize their position.
