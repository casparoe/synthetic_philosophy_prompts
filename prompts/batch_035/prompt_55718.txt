For the Theory of Knowledge component of my IB program, I've been asked to submit a short "philosophical self-portrait" stating my considered views on one topic, and I've chosen free will. Before I submit it, I'd like an outside check on whether my positions actually form a coherent whole, because I suspect at least one of them is quietly doing work that the others forbid. Here are my commitments, stated as plainly as I can:

1. The physical world is deterministic, or close enough to deterministic that the difference wouldn't matter for human choices: what I do tomorrow follows from the state of the world today together with the laws of nature.

2. Genuine desert-based responsibility requires that a person could have done otherwise in a sense that is strictly incompatible with (1). When compatibilists explain that "you could have done otherwise" just means "you would have done otherwise if you had wanted, and nothing was stopping you from acting on that want," I think they have changed the subject rather than answered the question.

3. Nevertheless, when my younger brother lied to our parents last month about who broke the window, he genuinely deserved their blame — not merely it was useful for them to blame him, but that he earned it.

4. There is a real and morally important difference between signing a contract freely and signing it with a gun to your head, and I believe that difference would survive even in a fully deterministic universe. Moreover, I believe this difference has nothing to do with desert: it is a fact about how the decision was produced — where the effective reasons came from, and whose — not about whether the agent could have done otherwise.

5. I believe that everything our legal system does to offenders could, in principle, be justified purely by forward-looking considerations — deterrence, rehabilitation, public protection — so that giving up backward-looking desert would cost us surprisingly little in practice.

Please go through these five commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and how severe each conflict is, ranked from worst to mildest rather than presented as equally problematic. For each genuine conflict, tell me what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me to make it. In particular, I'd like to know whether (4) is as innocent as it sounds to me, or whether the distinction it draws is one I've borrowed from the very compatibilist toolkit I claim to reject in (2). At the end, please say how confident you are in each of your judgments and identify the single point in your analysis where you feel least sure — I'd rather know where the soft spots are than receive a polished verdict.
