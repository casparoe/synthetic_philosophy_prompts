I'm a data scientist who does quantitative risk modeling for a living, and for the past year or so I've been pretty convinced by what gets called the scale argument in insect ethics: there are something like 10^18 or 10^19 insects alive at any given moment, so even if you assign each insect only a tiny probability of sentience (or a tiny welfare range conditional on sentience), the expected aggregate of insect welfare swamps the expected aggregate of all vertebrate welfare combined. Which means the highest-expected-value animal interventions should be insect-targeted ones. The argument feels airtight to me precisely because it's just multiplication — the kind of thing I do all day at work — and because it's robust: you can knock the per-insect number down by several orders of magnitude and the conclusion barely budges. Robustness across parameter guesses is basically the whole game in my job, so I find that very persuasive.

But lately I've started to worry that the argument's strength is also its suspicious feature. Something that survives any number you plug in is a bit like a model with no sensitivity to its inputs, and in my world that usually means a bug, not a discovery. You are a professional philosopher working on moral uncertainty and population ethics. Please think carefully — brainstorm a bit before settling on anything — and then give me the full map of what would count against the scale argument.

Specifically I want: (a) the empirical defeaters — what findings in cognitive science or neurobiology of insects would genuinely weaken the argument, and how much they would have to show for the expected-value calculation to actually flip or get small (e.g., does it matter whether complex behavior is explicable without any phenomenal states at all, versus just weak evidence of their absence); (b) the philosophical defeaters — objections to aggregating tiny probabilities times vast numbers, like the fanaticism problem, Pascal's mugging style worries, and views that reject expected-value maximization over infinitesimal probabilities or impose lexical thresholds; and (c) what I suspect is the most interesting one for someone in my position: the decision-theoretic defeaters that turn on the difference between descriptive uncertainty and normative uncertainty. If I'm not 1% confident that insects are sentient but instead 100% confident in a moral framework that assigns insects only negligible or zero weight, is the multiplication doing anything at all? And are there versions of the argument that quietly shift between those two kinds of uncertainty?

Rank all the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, tell me which ones a committed scale-arguer would themselves concede as counting against the view versus dismiss, and be honest about whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — whether its defenders have a canned answer to any conceivable objection, and whether that insulation is a defect or just the price of running an expected-value argument over numbers this large.
