I teach a graduate seminar on collective decision-making, and while preparing it I have been recording my own verdicts on a series of cases in which a group's majority judgements on some premises conflict with its majority judgement on a conclusion that those premises, by the group's own rule, entail. I have come to suspect that my verdicts do not fit any single principle, but I would like that suspicion tested rather than indulged. Please work through the cases below and tell me what principle, if any, captures them.

The cases:

1. The panel. A three-judge panel decides a civil claim under a settled rule: the defendant is liable if and only if the contract was valid and the defendant's breach caused the claimant's loss. Judge One finds the contract valid and the causation made out, and finds for the claimant. Judge Two finds the contract valid but the causation not made out, and finds for the defendant. Judge Three finds the contract invalid but the causation made out, and finds for the defendant. A majority therefore holds the contract valid, a majority holds the causation made out, and a majority finds for the defendant. My verdict: the panel, deciding as a panel, should find for the claimant. Its collective view is that both premises hold, the rule is fixed, and the recorded judgement should track the collective view of the premises.

2. The appointments committee. Five members, rule: appoint the candidate if and only if her research record is strong and her teaching demonstration was convincing. A majority (three to two) holds the research record strong; a (different) majority holds the teaching convincing; a majority, all things considered, opposes the appointment. My verdict: the committee's collective recommendation should be to appoint, even though a majority of its members privately oppose her.

3. The criminal panel. Structurally identical to case 1, but it is a criminal bench in a jurisdiction without juries, and conviction requires that the defendant performed the act and possessed the requisite intent. A majority finds the act proved; a (different) majority finds the intent; a majority would acquit, the members being unwilling to convict on a conjunction no single one of them endorses. My verdict: acquit. Here the verdict on the conclusion should follow the majority's view of the conclusion, not the conjunction of the premise majorities.

4. The parole board. Release requires that the risk of reoffending be low and that the tariff have been served. A majority holds each condition satisfied; a majority of the board, weighing everything, would not release. My verdict: I am genuinely torn here — it feels closer to case 3 than to case 1, but I could not defend that firmly.

5. The insincere judge. As in case 1, except that it later emerges that Judge Three voted against the contract's validity only to avoid a liability finding she thought too harsh; she sincerely believes the contract valid. My verdict: the panel should find for the claimant, and rather more comfortably than in case 1 — the premise dissent was not a judgement at all.

6. The discretionary rule. A club committee must admit an applicant if the applicant meets its criteria, but the criteria's mode of combination is the committee's own to settle, and it has never settled it. A majority finds the applicant promising; a majority finds her financially sound; a majority opposes admission. My verdict: there is no paradox here. The committee should first settle the rule, and the divergence dissolves in that choice. I do not think there is a fact of the matter about the committee's collective view that outruns the rule it chooses.

What I want from you:

A principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough to be applied mechanically to a new case. For each clause, say which of my cases motivates it. You will notice that cases 1 and 2 pull one way and case 3 the other; my own hunch is that the difference may be explained not by two aggregation principles but by something else — perhaps the asymmetry of the criminal burden of proof, or the fact that a liability finding can be decomposed into findings the panel is institutionally constituted to make whereas a conviction cannot — but I want your view, not mine rehearsed. Consider candidly the alternative that my verdicts in cases 3 and 4 are simply mistakes that a simpler principle would overturn; say which verdicts would have to go, and what it would cost to save every one of them instead.

Then apply your principle to three cases I have not listed: (a) a body whose rule is fixed by logic alone, with no statute or regulation behind it; (b) a case like case 1 except that the dissenting judge's premise-judgement rests on a demonstrable and admitted factual error about the document's date; and (c) a case like case 1 except that the premises are evaluative — an architectural jury awarding a prize for a design that is innovative and buildable — if your principle distinguishes factual from evaluative premises at all. Give your principle's verdict for each, and note where you are extrapolating beyond what my cases actually decide.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the single point you are least sure of — the clause or verdict you would expect a careful colleague to contest first. Please write in British English throughout.
