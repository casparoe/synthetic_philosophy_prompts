here's roughly where i've landed:

(1) the identity of indiscernibles strikes me as just obviously right at bottom -- if two things share every property then there's only one thing there. difference has to be grounded in something, it can't float free.

(2) i don't believe in "primitive thisness" or bare haecceities. when philosophers say two things could be distinct in a way that's not any qualitative difference at all, that sounds like mysticism to me. the thing i'd say in a code review is: show me the diff.

(3) Black's universe seems completely coherent to me when i imagine it. two spheres, no difference between them, still two. i've tried to picture it collapsing into one sphere and i can't.

(4) i think the standard "relational property" escapes are cheating. like "being two meters from sphere b" or "being distinct from b" -- those presuppose you already have two labeled things, so using them to ground the twoness is circular. cheap properties, don't count.

(5) also, doesn't actual physics already give us this? two electrons with all the same intrinsic properties, no problem, nobody thinks there's secretly only one electron. so the two-sphere world isn't even exotic.

and separately, my whole starting intuition here is from programming -- in code you can have two instances of the same immutable object, equal in every field, and they're still two because they live at different memory addresses. identity is just primitive in the implementation. part of me wants to say the universe is the same, and part of me suspects that's me confusing implementation details of my runtime for metaphysics.
