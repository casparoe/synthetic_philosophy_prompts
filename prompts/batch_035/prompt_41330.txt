I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about what it is for something to have a disposition, by listing cases, recording my gut verdicts on each, and seeing whether anything systematic comes out. The obvious starting hypothesis is the simple conditional analysis — a thing is fragile if and only if it would break if dropped, soluble iff it would dissolve if immersed, brave iff he would stand firm if danger appeared — but I suspect my verdicts don't all fit it. Case one: an ordinary wine glass that shatters when knocked off the table; clearly fragile, and the conditional holds. Case two: the same wine glass wrapped in three inches of bubble wrap, so it would survive being dropped; still fragile, surely — the wrapping blocks the breaking without changing anything about the glass. Case three: red wine gives me headaches, but I take an aspirin before dinner and so no headache arrives; I'd say I still have the disposition. Case four: a wire that carries a live charge, hooked to a device that would instantly drain the charge the moment anyone reached for it; the wire is still live, I think, even though touching it would give no shock. Case five: a dead wire hooked to the mirror-image device, which would deliver a charge the instant anyone touched it; there I want to say it is not live, though it would shock you. Case six: a soldier who is a thorough coward, but who has been fitted with an implant that floods him with valor-inducing chemicals whenever danger appears, so that he always stands his ground; not brave. Cases four through six all refute the conditional analysis, but my verdicts on them line up neatly: the disposition is present when its underlying basis is present and something merely intervenes to block the manifestation, and absent when the basis is absent and something intervenes to supply it at the last moment. The trouble is case seven, which I cannot square with case four: a soldier who is genuinely brave, but a sorcerer has it in for him and would drain his courage the instant danger appeared, so that he would always flee. My gut says he is not brave — and yet the wire in case four is live, and I cannot find any relevant difference between the two situations except that one disposition is a virtue and the other is an electric charge. And then there is case eight, on which I go back and forth: the wine glass, unwrapped, but watched by a sorcerer who would render it sturdy a split second before it struck the floor — fragile or not? I suspect that whichever way I settle case eight constrains what I should say in case seven, but I can't see how. Is there a principle — a single analysis of what it is to have a disposition — that captures all or nearly all of my verdicts, and if not, which of them should I give up? Please state the principle precisely, clause by clause, saying which case motivates each clause; tell me what it predicts about cases I haven't listed, in particular my friend who never snaps at anyone anymore but only because he is scrupulous about taking his medication — does he still count as irritable; and be honest about which of my verdicts, especially the pair I can't square, the principle cannot accommodate.
