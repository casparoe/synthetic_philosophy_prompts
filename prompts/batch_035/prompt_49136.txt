Last winter my grandmother asked me, in what turned out to be her final weeks, to read the memoirs she had assembled over forty years, and I found myself cultivating admiration for them — not because they were good, which mostly they were not, but because her peace depended on it, and she was acute enough to detect falseness. To my discomfort, some of the admiration stuck: she died three years ago, and rereading the manuscript last month I found I still feel it. I am now writing the central chapter of my master's thesis on the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem for fitting-attitude analyses of value, and I want to turn this into a properly constructed thought experiment. My thesis is that the familiar cases — being paid to admire, evidence that admiring will bring good luck — let the distinction between right- and wrong-kind reasons off too easily, because the wrong-kind reason is always prudential and the attitude always somehow tainted in retrospect. My suspicion is that the distinction gets harder to maintain when the wrong-kind reason is itself a moral one, so that the agent is all-things-considered obliged to acquire the attitude. That is what the case must be for, and it fixes what would count as cheating: a case that stipulates the memoirs are really admirable, or that the admiration is really covert pretending, fails.

So please construct the scenario to the following specifications. It should be realistic, one paragraph, and involve genuine admiration for a literary work of modest but real merit — the merits must be actual and stated (a certain courage in self-revelation, say), just clearly too weak to explain the strength of the attitude, so the case is not rigged. The wrong-kind reason (the dying author's wellbeing) must be causally necessary: include a control comparison, in which an identical manuscript by an anonymous stranger leaves the reader unmoved, so the case cannot be defused by claiming the admiration was secretly tracking merit all along. And the admiration must outlive the wrong-kind reason — the author dies, the agent still admires — so we can ask whether persistence launders an attitude acquired on the wrong kind of grounds into a fitting one.

After the scenario, walk through the verdicts. First, the simple fitting-attitude theorist who analyzes the admirable as that for which there is sufficient reason to admire: show exactly why the case forces her to distinguish kinds of reasons rather than kinds of attitude-objects, and where her simple formulation gives the wrong answer. Second, the theorist who accepts the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction: she says the memoirs are not admirable and that the surviving admiration is a misfire — and she owes an account of how an attitude the agent was morally required to acquire can be unfitting without the agent being criticizable for having it, which I take to be the real pressure point. Third, say which feature of the case is doing the work (I suspect the control), what happens if you drop the persistence clause, and how a defender of the distinction would attack the case — for instance by arguing that the moral reason is in fact a reason of the right kind for an attenuated attitude, or that the grounds of an attitude can change after acquisition. Close with an honest note on whether you think my four specifications are jointly satisfiable, or whether making the wrong-kind reason moral quietly forces one of the other constraints to weaken. Please do not rely on attributions of specific papers or examples; argue it through on the structure of the case.
