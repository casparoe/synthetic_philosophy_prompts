I'm a third-year med student on an emergency medicine rotation, and something about how my attendings make allocation decisions has been bugging me for weeks, so I want to work out what the practice actually presupposes philosophically. In the ordinary department, when things are scarce — one ICU bed, two patients who need it, say — nobody I've watched adds up dependents or future people affected on either side. The choice tends to be first-come, a waitlist, or even literally a lottery, and each patient is considered one at a time, as an individual with a claim. The number of lives at stake on each side just doesn't seem to enter. But the moment we drill a mass casualty scenario — bus crash, twenty-five patients, four available staff — the entire logic flips: we're taught greatest good for greatest number, tag the unsalvageable as expectant and move past them, treat the five walking wounded before the one guy crushed under the dashboard. Numbers go from counting for nothing (they only break ties) to counting for everything, and the switch happens at some fuzzy threshold that nobody ever names.

What does this flip presuppose about the moral significance of counting people? Does the everyday half presuppose something like the view (I think theres a philosopher named Taurek who argued this?) that numbers don't count in deciding whom to save — that each person gets an equal shot, maybe an actual coin flip, and that being five doesn't give a group a stronger claim to rescue than being one? And does the disaster half then presuppose the opposite, that lives or losses aggregate? Or is there one consistent underlying view that licenses both — something like "treat each person as an individual as long as you can, but once you can't, you're choosing a policy, and policies are utilitarian" — though that smells to me like it's smuggling in the conclusion, and I can't quite put my finger on why.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of what a claim to rescue is in each half of the practice, tell me which rival views each half quietly rules out, and be honest about where the whole thing is underdetermined — maybe alot of different theories would license what the ER does day to day. Then say whether the two halves are actually consistent or whether the switch is just an unacknowledged change of moral theory mid-stream, and sketch what bedside allocation would have to look like if we took the counting view seriously all the time, and what disaster triage would have to look like if we took the non-counting view seriously there too.
