I convene a monthly philosophy reading group of intelligent non-specialists — two engineers, a nurse, a retired history teacher — who relish hard ideas but have no training in epistemology. Next session runs ninety minutes, and I've committed to teaching them the regress problem and the three canonical responses to it: foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism. My fear is that they'll walk away treating the three positions as temperaments — the dogmatist, the relativist, and the crank — rather than as rival structural claims about what justification can be. I don't want you to explain the material to me; I want a plan for how I should build the explanation. Specifically:

1. **Sequence.** What has to come before what, and why. Does the distinction between inferential and non-inferential justification need to be on the table before the regress is even stated, or does the regress motivate that distinction better than any preamble could? Can the three responses be presented in any order, or must foundationalism come first as the view everyone already half-holds, with the other two introduced as escape routes? Should the distinction between linear and non-linear structures of justification come early or late?

2. **The carrying example.** It must work for all three positions, not just one. I've toyed with the chain of authorities cited in a legal opinion and a detective's case built on mutually corroborating witness statements, but you tell me what the example must have (a visible failure mode, presumably), what it must lack (any built-in tilt toward one of the three views), and which candidate — mine or a better one — should carry the whole session.

3. **The misconception map.** The errors I anticipate, roughly in order of arrival: that coherentism licenses any internally consistent story; that infinitism claims humans must actually entertain infinitely many beliefs rather than that an infinite chain could in principle justify; that foundationalism means you may simply assert whatever you like and stop talking; and, from the engineers, that the regress is exactly a circular dependency warning from a compiler, which it is and is not. Tell me where in the sequence each of these is best absorbed — and whether any should be allowed to surface first so the group feels the pull before it's defused.

4. **What to refuse.** What to openly decline at this level — externalism and reliabilism, the dispute over whether the regress concerns the origins of a belief or its justificatory status, whatever you judge most tempting and most derailing — and how to say no without seeming dismissive.

5. **The check.** One question I can put to the group in the last five minutes that would separate someone who grasped the structural issue from someone who merely acquired the vocabulary.

Please deliver the plan as a table with columns: Step; Minutes; What is introduced; Depends on (step number or prior idea); Misconception headed off (or a dash); Leader's move (question, example, or refusal). Beneath the table, give the closing check question and, in two or three sentences, why it discriminates real understanding from label-learning.
