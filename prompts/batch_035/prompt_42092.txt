I'm writing a piece (blog length, ~1500 words) on the dollar auction and escalation of committment, and before I send it round I want comments from someone who actually knows the decision theory. Notes for the draft are below — tell me whether the argument holds together, where it's weakest, and be blunt if you think the framing is off entirely.

The notes:

- Setup in one sentence for readers who don't know it: Shubik's dollar auction auctions a dollar bill to the highest bidder, except the second-highest bidder also pays their (losing) bid and gets nothing. Famously, people bid well past a dollar.
- Claim 1: the standard lesson ("look how irrational people are, sunk cost fallacy, etc.") is aimed at the wrong moment. Once you're the second-highest bidder, each individual bid is rational in a narrow sense — you either pay 90c for nothing or bid 95c and win a dollar, and so on up past a dollar. The irrationality worth talking about isn't the bidding past $1; it's that anyone bids at all in the first place given the trap is common knowledge. The auction is a machine for converting one small error (opening the bidding) into a big one, and the rationality of the later bids is just the rachet working as designed.
- Claim 2 (less sure about this one): it's tempting to say the later bids are rational "given you're already in this mess", but only in the same weak sense in which any sunk-cost reasoning is rational — the "given" is doing all the work. If you can see you'd rationally bid 105c for a dollar, you can also see that your opponent can, and so on back. I want to argue this creates an infinite regress that unravels backwards to the first bid, and that this is why framing the whole thing in terms of sunk costs misdescribes what the players actually get wrong. Maybe. This is the bit I most want pressure on.
- Third section: what this says about escalation of commitment in organisations (runaway IT projects, wars, etc.). Real escalations differ from the auction in at least one big way — the value of the "prize" isn't fixed but partly constructed by the escalation itself — and I suspect that difference means my "convert one small error into a big one" framing doesn't transfer cleanly. I have a hunch this is right but haven't worked it out.

What I'd like from you:

1. Does claim 1 hold up? Specifically, is the "one small error, big machine" framing compatible with the observation that at no stage is any single bid straightforwardly irrational?
2. Is the regress in claim 2 real, or have I smuggled in assumptions about common knowledge of rationality that the actual auction doesn't give you? My feeling is that if either player is even slightly irrational, or suspects the other might be, the unravelling stops — but then I'm not sure what lesson survives.
3. Does the fixed-prize vs constructed-prize distinction do the work I want in section three, or am I overreaching with a cute analogy?
4. Anything above that's just philosophically confused, or restates what everyone already says in slightly different words? I'd rather hear that now than after it's published.

Don't worry about references, I've got those handled. And please don't just be encouraging — if you think claim 1 quietly collapses into claim 2, or that the regress point is just backward induction in a trench coat, say so, and tell me what specific argument or consideration would change your verdict on it. If part of my reasoning is likely to fail quietly rather than obviously, flag that too.
