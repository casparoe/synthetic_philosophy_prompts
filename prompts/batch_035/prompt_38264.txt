Something's been bugging me since our seminar on future generations, and I can't find the way out of an argument my classmate Elena built. The setup: our city council is choosing between fixing a contaminated reservoir now, expensively, or doing a cheaper partial fix that leaves low-level toxins in the water for the next thirty years. Kids conceived after the decision will be different kids under the two policies -- the announcement itself shifts people's finances and stress levels, couples end up conceiving on different nights, a different sperm meets a different egg. So the people who grow up drinking the marginal water under the cheap option would simply never have existed under the expensive one. Their lives are decent -- clearly worth living, just with higher rates of illness and a few years shaved off at the end. Her argument: (1) You can only wrong someone by making things worse for that person than they would otherwise have been. (2) Nobody under the cheap policy is worse off than they would otherwise have been, since the alternative for each of them is never existing at all, and a life worth living beats no life. (3) So nobody is made worse off by the cheap policy. (4) A wrong needs a victim -- some person to whom the wrong was done -- and if nobody is worse off, nobody can be the victim. (5) Therefore the cheap policy wrongs nobody. (6) What's wrong simpliciter, wronged-nobody-or-not, isn't a category we have; morality is done by persons, to persons. (7) So the cheap policy isn't wrong, and the council members who vote for it do nothing wrong. That conclusion already smells, but then she pushed it further and this is where I really balk: the same reasoning shows we could adopt a policy knowing it will give everyone born after 2050 lives just barely above the worth-living threshold, full of preventable pain, and that would wrong nobody either, so long as they wouldn't have existed otherwise. At that point it seems like the argument proves there is no way at all to wrong future people through policy, since nearly any policy with long-run effects changes who gets conceived -- which would mean climate policy, depletion, the whole works, can't wrong anyone not yet born. So either the argument is sound and a huge chunk of what I thought I believed about obligations to the future is confused, or some step between (1) and (7) is rotten. I suspect it's the comparison in (2), that "worse off than they would otherwise have been" is doing something wierd, but I can't put my finger on exactly what, and I've also noticed that if I deny (4) I seem to be committed to wrongs with no victims floating around, which sounds like mysticism. If I absolutely had to pick I'd rather keep (1) and (4) and bite the bullet on (7), but I want to know what that actually costs me -- can I really tell the affected people, decades later, that they have no complaint? Don't give me a neutral survey of the positions with the choosing left to me; tell me which step you would reject, explain why that step looked so innocent in the first place, and defend your choice as the least costly one. And please, no headings or bullet points or bold text in your answer -- just continuous prose, like a long email to a friend.
