My flatmates and I agreed a cleaning rota back in autumn — nothing signed, just a chat in the kitchen — and when one of us quietly stopped doing her bit, the row we had turned oddly philosophical: had she broken a commitment to the flat as such, or just let each of us down individually? That's what sent me into the joint action literature, where I found what looks like the exact same fight, running since the early nineties. Margaret Gilbert says that when people do things together — her case is two people going for a walk — they form a joint commitment as a plural subject, and this creates genuine obligations: wander off mid-walk and you're blameworthy, no prior promise required. Michael Bratman says shared intention is just individual intentions that interlock, with sub-plans that mesh and each party knowing the other intends, and any obligations come from ordinary promises, not from the sharedness itself; Searle chimes in that the we-intention is primitive and lives in each head. Thirty-odd years on nobody has moved: Gilbert still leans on the walking-together intuitions, Bratman still points to spontaneous duets where nobody owes anybody anything. So my actual question: what kind of disagreement is this? My hunch, offered only as a hunch, is that they're answering different questions — Gilbert doing social ontology, Bratman doing a planning theory for individual agents — and colliding over cases. But it might be a verbal fight about what reduction means, or bedrock intuitions about test cases doing all the work. I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey, plus an honest word on whether anything — a distinction, a case both sides would accept, some treatment of the mutual-knowledge regress — could genuinely move it. Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion.
