Population ethics question, and I'm hoping you can untangle something I've been hand-waving for way too long. I'm a philosophy professor; I teach population ethics most springs, and for years I've been shutting down a certain line of student questioning with Jan Narveson's old line from the seventies — "We are in favor of making people happy, but neutral about making happy people" — the one Parfit later made famous. I picked it up in grad school, repeat it on autopilot, and have honestly never worked out what it actually commits me too.

Where it came to a head: last month at a colloquium, a grad student presenting on procreative ethics asked whether a couple who both carried a serious heritable condition did something wrong in conceiving, given that the child they'd predictably have would live a life that's worth living, but only barley — constant pain management, few of the goods the rest of us take for granted. A senior colleague waved the question off with the Narveson line: nobody is worse off than they otherwise would have been, there's no one to have been deprived, and morality isn't in the business of making happy people. Case closed, he seemed to think. The student looked unconvinced, and so was I, but I couldn't put my finger on where the move went wrong, or whether it did.

So here's the ask: take the slogan apart into the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, running from the trivial to the radical. I'd expect the sheer "there's no subject to receive the benefit" point to be in there somewhere, plus various person-affecting restrictions (I gather there's a narrow/wide distinction, if you find it useful), comparative versus absolute claims about wellbeing, whatever else you think is genuinely hiding in the sentence. For each reading, tell me: is it a platitude, is it substantive but defensible, is it flat-out false, and — this is what I actually care about — does it license my colleague's dismissal, or does it just sound like it does? Then don't leave me with a menu. Commit: say which reading you'd actually defend and why, and whether the colleague's move was legitimate or a sleight of hand. I want something I can send the grad student, so keep it in American English and reasonably tight — a bulleted list of the readings with verdicts under each would be perfect.
