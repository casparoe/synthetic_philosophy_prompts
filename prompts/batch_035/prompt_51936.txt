My brother-in-law plays poker for a living, thinks in odds all day, and he's walked me into something about Hume on miracles that I can't find my way out of. He got there by taking Hume's maxim about miracle reports literally and then turning it on all testimony. The argument, as clean as I can put it:

(1) Believe someone's report of an event only if the event, considered on its own before you heard the report, was more probable than the report being wrong — mistaken, exaggerating, or lying. (This is Hume's rule from the miracles section of the Enquiry, paraphrased: no testimony is enough to establish something vastly improbable unless the testimony being wrong would be even more improbable.)

(2) For any particular report by a particular person, there's at least, say, a one-in-a-hundred chance it's wrong in some respect — people misremember, embellish, misspeak. No individual report of a specific occurrence is anywhere near certain.

(3) Any event described specifically enough to be worth reporting had an antecedent probability way below one in a hundred. My coworker tells me she took the 4:15 bus, ran into her old roommate Petra, who told her about a job opening in Lisbon — out of all the days she could have had, that exact day had a prior of maybe one in millions. A friend describes the exact order the cards came out last night: one in 52 factorial. Even the news is like this: this particular quake, of this magnitude, at this hour, in that town.

(4) So by (1) we should suspend judgment on essentially all specific testimony — friends' stories, the paper, history, court witnesses.

(5) We don't, and we're right not to: testimony is basically our only window onto nearly everything that happens, and it mostly works.

(6) So Hume's rule, taken literally, destroys the very thing its supposed to be a rule about, and it has to go. And once it goes, his case against miracle reports goes with it — as far as my brother-in-law can tell, the whole case was the maxim, so the door is open again for evaluating miracle reports on ordinary grounds.

He likes that last part, because he thinks it reopens the door for miracles. I don't want that, but I also can't locate the flaw. Something about (3) feels like cheating — it compares a fully spelled-out event against a loosely specified error rate — but whenever I try to loosen the event description the whole thing just dissolves in a way I can't make precise. So: where's the trick? Which premise should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? To be clear, don't hand me a rescue of (5) just because sanity seems to demand it — if the argument genuinely goes through and we're entitled to believe almost no specific testimony, say that definately. Equally, if you think we've both mangled Hume — that his actual rule was never what (1) says it was — I want to hear that too, even though it makes us both look silly.
