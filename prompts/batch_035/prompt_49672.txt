Bit of an unusual ask, so let me give you the situation. I'm a novelist — literary fiction, mostly about ordinary people who go along with awful things — and this year im teaching an MFA seminar on writing perpetrators (we called it "the complicit protagonist," which in retrospect was a little precious). My students kept handing in cartoon villains, monsters with paperwork, so I built a history unit around the Browning/Goldhagen debate on who the Holocaust's low-level killers actually were and why they killed. The idea was that if my novelists understood that debate, their perpetrators would finally be people.

The unit went fine. The grading is the problem. So for this, act as a Holocaust historian who's taught this material for twenty years and graded a few thousand student essays. Think carefully, and before you grade anything, brainstorm two things: what a genuinely strong answer to this question must contain, and the standard misconceptions students fall into about this particular debate. Then grade against that, not against a vibe.

The exam question I gave:

"Both Christopher Browning (Ordinary Men, 1992) and Daniel Goldhagen (Hitler's Willing Executioners, 1996) built their books around the same unit: Reserve Police Battalion 101, roughly five hundred middle-aged, mostly working-class men from Hamburg, drafted into the German Order Police after being judged unfit for the army, who carried out mass shootings and deportations of Jews in occupied Poland in 1942–43. The two historians also accept the same core facts, including this one: at the Józefów massacre in July 1942, the battalion's commander, Major Wilhelm Trapp, told his men before the shooting began that any of them who did not feel up to it could step out, and only around a dozen of the roughly five hundred did. Both further agree that no member of the battalion was ever seriously punished for refusing to kill Jews. (a) Explain what Browning takes these facts to show about why the men killed, what Goldhagen takes them to show, and where exactly the disagreement lies. (b) State which interpretation you find more persuasive, and defend it against the strongest objection from the other side."

And the answer from student #7 (they're anonymous to me, numbers only):

"Browning and Goldhagen both wrote about Reserve Police Battalion 101, a unit of about five hundred order policemen, mostly middle-aged working-class men from Hamburg who'd been found unfit for army service, so on the basic facts they agree: these weren't SS fanatics, at least not at first. The famous scene is Józefów in July 1942, where the commander, Major Trapp, told the men that anyone who didn't feel up to it could step out, and only about a dozen did. Both historians also agree that nobody in the battalion was ever seriously punished for refusing to kill Jews.

Browning's reading is situational. The men killed out of obedience to authority and conformity: it was easier to shoot than to step forward alone in front of comrades, and once the shooting started, refusing meant pushing the work onto everyone else. He leans on the Milgram experiments, where ordinary people administered what they believed were dangerous electric shocks because a man in a lab coat told them to. On Browning's account the men weren't especially antisemitic by the standards of their time and place; they were ordinary, and that's the disturbing part.

Goldhagen's reading is the opposite: the men killed because they hated Jews. He argues they were antisemitic fanatics, unusual men even by the standards of the Germany they lived in, and that this willingness is what his title points to — willing, not coerced. This is what he calls eliminationist antisemitism, an ideology unique to Germany, though he concedes the rank and file weren't sadists in any clinical sense.

Here is where Browning's story runs into trouble. Browning himself admits no one was punished for refusing. But obedience only has force when there's a sanction behind it; if refusing carried no penalty, there was nothing to obey, and the men's participation was a free choice. That is much closer to Goldhagen's picture than Browning's, even though I don't buy the fanatics. In a way Browning wrote his book to refute Goldhagen, which is why the two are always taught together, but the free-choice point ends up on Goldhagen's side of the ledger. My verdict is still Browning, mostly because the historiography came down hard on Goldhagen — almost no historians accepted his account — and because situational explanations have held up everywhere else we've tested them. But the Józefów fact fits Goldhagen better than either of them admits."
