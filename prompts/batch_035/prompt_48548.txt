I've just spent the better part of an evening arguing with my oldest friend, and I want your help stress-testing the position I walked away holding.

The argument was about peer disagreement — what to do when you discover that someone is your epistemic peer on some question (equally intelligent, equally informed, equally attentive, with an equally good track record on questions of this kind) and yet they disagree with you. My friend took the steadfast view: she said that if she has examined the evidence and arguments as carefully as she can, the mere fact that a peer ended up elsewhere gives her no new information about the world — the disagreement only tells her that someone must be wrong, not who, and she has already done her best to figure that out from the inside. I ended up defending a conciliationist position, though not the crude "always split the difference" version: roughly, that learning of the disagreement does give her evidence, because the reliability of her own first-order reasoning is not something she can check from the inside, and the fact that a peer's careful reasoning pointed elsewhere is exactly the kind of signal about her own reasoning that she cannot generate on her own.

I'd like you to act as an expert epistemologist — the kind who has published on disagreement, but who is more interested in getting the dialectic right than in defending a school. Please begin with a brief brainstorm, laying out the space of possible defeaters for my position before you evaluate any of them, and then think carefully about each. Specifically, I want a map of what would count against my conciliationist view, addressed to me as someone who actually holds it:

- What internal problems does it face — for instance, that conciliationism's own standard tells me to lower my confidence in conciliationism when I learn that many epistemologists reject it, and whether that self-undermining is fatal, merely awkward, or actually a sign that the view is at least honest about itself?
- What my friend's strongest points were, stated as strongly as you can: that judging someone to be a peer in the first place seems to require exactly the first-order reasoning that conciliationism then discounts, so that the view risks double-counting the same evidence; and her challenge that adversarial practices we take to be knowledge-producing — courtrooms, peer review, chess engines evaluated by play against each other rather than by averaging their evaluations — all reward some form of reasoned steadfastness rather than mutual credence-splitting.
- Which consequences of my view are ones I'd have to swallow and might reasonably find unbearable — e.g., what it implies about long-running disagreements between genuine peers, or about whether I should ever believe anything that all my closest peers reject.
- What a conciliationist would herself concede as counting against the view, versus what she would dismiss — and where the line between those is drawn, given that a view can be defended either by principled qualification or by ad hoc insulation.

Finally, please rank the defeaters you identify from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and give me an honest assessment of whether my position has been qualified into something effectively unfalsifiable — and, if so, whether that is a vice for a normative view of this kind or just an inherent feature of normative epistemology. I'd rather find out now that my view was resting on a friendly reading of the evidence than keep winning arguments only because my friend lets me.
