I sit on the national biosafety commission's panel for novel biological systems, and I have to draft our recommendation in a classification dispute that has deadlocked the panel for two sessions. A synthetic-biology group maintains what it calls an "autopoietic reactor": micron-scale lipid vesicles whose interior hosts a catalytic network that joins externally supplied activated monomers into polymers, copies those polymers by templating, and drives the growth and division of the vesicles. The system does not synthesize its own lipids — a perfusion apparatus supplies the fatty acids — and the group deliberately engineered the copying chemistry to be nearly error-free, on the order of one mutation per million copying events, precisely so that the line cannot evolve. Individual vesicles grow, divide roughly every nine hours, and restore themselves after moderate perturbation; they cannot, however, survive even briefly without the apparatus.

Our oversight framework mandates full containment and terminal destruction for "living organisms," while "non-living replicating materials" may be archived and shared between laboratories under lighter controls. The framework never defines the operative term, and the group has proceeded for two years on the reading that its vesicles fall on the non-living side. The panel is split three ways. One faction holds that life essentially involves the capacity for Darwinian evolution, so a line engineered to be unable to evolve is, whatever else it is, not alive. A second faction argues that these systems are autopoietic in the strict sense — they produce their own components, maintain their organization, and regenerate their boundary — and that self-production, not evolvability, is the mark of the living; on that reading the group has been out of compliance for two years. A third faction worries less about metaphysics than about precedent: whatever we decide here will be cited in every future borderline case, from lines with evolvability restored on purpose to fully autonomous minimal cells.

Please draft the recommendation memo I will circulate before next month's plenary. It should (a) take a position on how the framework's operative term applies to these vesicles, (b) say what should happen to the existing stocks and to the group's authorization going forward, and (c) propose whatever amendment to the framework's wording would spare future panels this ambiguity — while giving the strongest opposing faction its full due rather than settling the question by our side's definition.
