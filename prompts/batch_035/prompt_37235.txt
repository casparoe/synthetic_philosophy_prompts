I spent the better part of my career in vision science, where one of the few clean distinctions available to us was that between a veridical percept and an illusion, and since retiring I have been reading steadily in metaethics and moral psychology. A distinction I now encounter constantly is that between a moral "preference" and a moral "bias," and the literature, as far as I can tell, treats it on the model my old field would use: a bias is to a preference as an illusion is to a veridical perception — a systematic error that the right sort of check can expose, much as the Müller-Lyer figure is exposed by reaching for a ruler. If that mapping holds, it suggests something I find rather attractive: that moral biases could be identified, and perhaps corrected, using morally neutral tests — inconsistency across structurally similar cases, sensitivity to morally irrelevant factors such as framing or the order in which cases are presented, discordance with the subject's other considered commitments — without anyone first taking a stand on the underlying moral questions. My reading group was skeptical of this; one member objected that the analogy quietly assumes there is such a thing as "veridical" moral perception for the biases to be deviations from, and that this is precisely what is in dispute.

I would therefore like a proper audit of the analogy. Please begin with an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the stimulus, the percept, the illusion, the measuring instrument, and the observer. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and kindly distinguish the disanalogies that matter for my intended use — the hope for morally neutral bias-detection — from those that do not. Conclude with a verdict: does the neutral-testing program survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If some better analogy is available for the preference/bias distinction, name it and say in a sentence what it gets right that the perceptual model gets wrong. Please keep the entire answer to 500 words or fewer; I would rather have a disciplined page than a sprawling essay.
