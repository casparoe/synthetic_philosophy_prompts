Please respond as a philosopher of economics, working in the tradition that runs from Samuelson's revealed preference through modern choice theory, but skeptical enough of that tradition to examine it rather than administer it. Think through the argument below step by step before answering, and be explicit about what each option costs.

My study group has an argument whose conclusion none of us wants, and we can't find the exit. It runs:

(1) Preference is defined by choice: an agent prefers X to Y iff, given the opportunity to take either, she takes X. This is what makes preference talk testable — it reports how a system behaves across situations, not some unverifiable inner state.
(2) Choice, so defined, requires no mentality: whatever a system does when alternatives are open to it counts as its choosing what it does.
(3) A sufficiently consistent pattern of choices is representable by a utility function, unique up to increasing transformation; so a system whose choices are consistent has a utility function, and it maximizes that function by definition.
(4) Many systems satisfy the consistency conditions without strain. A thermostat "prefers" the setpoint temperature to any other and "trades off" heating effort against deviation from it. A body in free fall "prefers" the trajectory of least action over nearby ones. A language model, by construction, "prefers" whatever token it emits to every token it doesn't.
(5) If the theory that is supposed to explain market behavior applies unchanged to thermostats, falling bodies, and language models, then it explains nothing: "utility" and "preference" mark no distinction that does any work.
(6) So the theory of utility, as used in economics, is either false or empty, and there is no substantive notion of utility at all.

I'd much rather keep (1) than accept (6) — I like the purge of unobservable mental states, and (6) seems to throw out the machinery along with the behaviorism. But I can't see which step is the guilty one: (2) looks like honesty, (3) looks like a standard representation theorem, (4) looks like just describing what the systems do, and (5) looks like a simple point about contentlessness. Tell me which premise to reject, why it looked innocent, what rejecting it costs, and whether there is a reading on which the theory keeps real content without smuggling unobservable preferences back in. If the argument turns on an equivocation, name it and say which steps trade on which sense.
