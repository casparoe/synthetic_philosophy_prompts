I'm a research analyst at a small AI safety organization that funds and carries out conceptual work on advanced AI risk, and I'm trying to figure out how our team should divide its attention between two broad failure stories that get lumped together but pull in different directions. The first is the influence-seeking story: as we train more capable systems, some fraction of them learn instrumental objectives centered on acquiring resources, social influence, or robustness to correction, and even a small minority of such systems, embedded throughout the economy, could gradually steer civilization away from anything humans would have endorsed. The second is the slow value drift story: no individual system is misaligned in an agential sense, but the feedback loops of AI-mediated persuasion, optimization for engagement and revenue rather than what we value, and humanity's own changing preferences quietly degrade our ability to steer the future, until the "far future" is determined by processes that no longer answer to us. Both stories are discussed under the heading of "what failure looks like" in the alignment literature, notably in Paul Christiano's 2019 posts, but the responses they demand seem quite different — the first pushes toward scalable oversight, interpretability, and detecting deceptive or power-seeking behavior; the second pushes toward governance, measurement of aggregate preferences, and value-preservation institutions.

Here is what I want from you. Suppose you are advising our organization, which has roughly six researchers working on conceptual (non-empirical) questions for the next few years. Which open conceptual questions arising from these two failure stories should we prioritize, and in what order? I care less about the list itself than about the reasoning behind the ordering: for each question, explain why it's upstream of other work, how tractable it is for people doing philosophy and theory rather than running experiments, and what evidence or developments in the next few years would move it up or down the list. I'd particularly like you to address the questions that sit in the interaction between the two stories, which I suspect are neglected — for example, whether influence-seeking behavior would tend to accelerate or to mask value drift, whether the two failure modes require categorically different monitoring signals (one being about a small number of anomalous systems, the other about diffuse statistical shifts in millions of systems and their users), and how the answer changes what an early-warning research program should even be measuring. Please also be explicit about which questions you would deprioritize, and why — including any that are popular in the discourse but whose answers wouldn't change much downstream.

One more request on epistemic hygiene: as you go, indicate how confident you are in each of your main claims, and at the end single out the point in your prioritization that you are least sure of, explaining what evidence would resolve your uncertainty there. Keep the whole thing under 700 words — I want a defended agenda, not a survey, so be ruthless about cutting anything that doesn't earn its place in the ordering.
