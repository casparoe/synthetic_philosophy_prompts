I coordinate a community mediation service. A large share of our caseload is disputes between adult children and their aging parents, or among siblings, over care decisions, money, the family house, and who is doing their share of the work. Our mediator training is entirely facilitative and autonomy-centered: we treat each party as an individual with interests, keep our own views out, and aim for a negotiated agreement the parties could all accept. A colleague trained in comparative philosophy argues that this model fits these cases badly, because it presumes the parties are separate contractors whose obligations must be built up from consent, when what is actually in dispute is what their roles — parent, child, eldest sibling, the one who stayed local — already require of them. She wants us to draw on Confucian role ethics: the view that persons are constituted by their relational roles rather than prior to them, that ethical judgment proceeds by asking what a good son, daughter, or parent does here rather than by applying impartial principles, and that harmony in the relationship is itself part of what a good outcome consists in, not just a means to one.

I want to take her seriously without turning mediation into moralizing, and without imposing a Confucian framework on families who don't share it — our clients come from every background and most just want the fight to stop. Please write a decision procedure a mediator could actually run across a case: the questions to ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure should conclude that the role-based conversation is not working and the mediator should fall back to standard interest-based mediation; and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. The procedure has to respect two hard constraints: sessions run ninety minutes, and our service's rules forbid mediators from recommending outcomes — so address directly whether that neutrality rule can survive a role-ethics approach, and if not, what the procedure should do about it. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) three siblings disagreeing about whether their mother, who has mild cognitive impairment and says she wants to die in her house, should move to assisted living; (2) a dispute in which the son who moved back home and provides all the care has been paying himself from his father's account, and the out-of-state siblings allege self-dealing; (3) a daughter who wants to be released from any obligation to help with her mother's care so she can take a job abroad. On the third case especially, I expect the procedure may yield an answer I won't like, since my own instincts are autonomy-first — show me what it gives and where it bends. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of cases where role-ethics framing will mislead or do harm, and the points at which the translator's judgment, not the procedure, is doing the real work.
