Two guys on my pickup soccer team got into it after practice about that news story where an autonomous forklift crushed a pallet stack at a warehouse and the worker standing nearby lost his foot. Marco said straight up nobody's to blame: the safety officer only signed off on the certification he was handed, the engineers built to spec, the spec was written by a committee that ran everything past the regulators, and the forklift's exact routing in that second was its own call — no single human made any decision that maps onto the harm. Making the company pay would just be punishing deep pockets, which he said isn't accountability, its a payout ritual.

Deshaun said that's not how responsibility for risk works — you don't need to control the precise moment of the harm to be on the hook, you're on the hook for unleashing something whose behavior you can't predict or correct. And if the whole setup is arranged so that no individual ever decides anything, that arrangement itself is the negligent act, and someone, somewhere, chose it.

Marco said by that logic every new technology was born negligent — early cars and planes killed people constantly, and we don't say the original manufacturers were morally guilty, we just tightened the rules as we learned. Deshaun answered that with cars there was always a who-to-ask: a driver, an owner, an inspector, and over time a paper trail for every crash. His point was that the problem isn't the harm rate, it's that with this thing there's no who-to-ask at all, and the family just gets a shrug, and a legal order that produces shrugs is doing something wrong.

Marco said needing a name to attach to it is about closure, and you can't conjure a culprit out of the need for closure — sometimes bad outcomes genuinely have no author.
