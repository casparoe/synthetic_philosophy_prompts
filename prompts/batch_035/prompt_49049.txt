I write a column on science and ideas, and a recent piece of mine argued that a single failed replication shouldn't by itself sink a well-confirmed theory. A statistically literate reader wrote back with an objection that has since hardened, over several exchanges, into an argument I can't find my way out of — and it appears to show that evidence doesn't support theories at all.

The argument as it now stands:

(1) An observation of an F that is G confirms the universal claim that all Fs are G — he calls this Nicod's criterion.
(2) Whatever confirms a hypothesis confirms anything logically equivalent to it — the equivalence condition.
(3) The claim that all ravens are black is logically equivalent to the claim that all non-black things are non-ravens.
(4) My white coffee mug is a non-black non-raven, so by (1) it confirms the second claim, and by (2) it confirms that all ravens are black.
(5) Nothing in the reasoning turns on ravens or on blackness: on this logic, every observation of anything confirms every universal hypothesis except observations of counterexamples, so my desk confirms that all ravens are pink as readily as it confirms anything an ornithologist has ever established.
(6) In the last exchange he tried to close off my escape routes with a box: suppose it was prepared so that it contains a black raven if all ravens are black, and a white dove otherwise. Opening it and finding the dove, I would rightly become less confident that all ravens are black — even though the dove is a non-black non-raven that by (1) and (4) should confirm that claim. So (1) cannot be right as stated either.
(7) His conclusion: confirmation is a myth. Observations never support universal hypotheses, they only refute them, and what we call well-confirmed theories are merely conjectures that have so far ducked the arrows.

Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want to keep the equivalence condition if I possibly can — my column's whole point was that the same evidence should bear on logically equivalent formulations of a hypothesis — but I also cannot write, in print, that a white mug confirms ornithology. And if the answer is that (1) holds only when we pretend to know nothing beyond the hypothesis itself, I'd want to understand how a notion of confirmation that requires pretended ignorance could be the one working scientists are actually using when they call a theory well-confirmed. Before I concede my reader's (7) and rewrite the column as an apology to Popper, tell me where the argument breaks.
