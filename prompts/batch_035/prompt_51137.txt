I'm an American who moved to Munich two years ago, and this spring, going through my paperwork with a colleague's help, I finally registered the line on my payslip I'd been half-ignoring: the Kirchensteuer, the church tax the German state collects on behalf of the churches — eight or nine percent of my income tax, a sum I've since learned is well over a thousand euros a year for me. I was raised Catholic, never got around to formally leaving, and so by German lights I'm a member and liable. Whether to file the declaration and leave the register is now a live, personal decision, and it has dragged me into a series of arguments — with a French colleague who thinks her country's laïcité is the only honest way to run a secular state, and with an English friend who can't understand why anyone thinks the Church of England's position is anything but charming. I've tried writing down where I actually stand, and I'd like an audit of whether the resulting package hangs together.

Here is what I believe. First, my baseline: the right arrangement between state and religion is something like the strict American separation — the state neither aids nor endorses nor obstructs religion, and I take that as a matter of political morality, not just of the First Amendment's text. Second, despite that, the German church-tax system strikes me as legitimate, maybe even admirable: it's opt-in through membership, exit is available to anyone willing to file a form, and the money funds hospitals, kindergartens, and social services that a secular state would otherwise have to provide. Third, I think France's 2004 ban on conspicuous religious symbols in public schools was a mistake — neutrality should bind the state, not teenage girls, and everyone knows which girls bear the cost. Fourth, I regard the Church of England's established status as benign: an anachronistic constitutional decoration, with the bishops sitting in the Lords, but not an injustice worth spending political capital to undo. Fifth, I oppose American voucher schemes that channel public money to religious schools, because the state shouldn't finance religious formation, least of all of children. Sixth, and seemingly in the other direction, I have no problem with faith-based organizations receiving public grants for food banks or homeless shelters on the same neutral criteria as secular nonprofits, so long as the money doesn't pay for proselytizing.

What I want from you is a consistency check, ordered by severity rather than a flat list. Which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and where the tension is real, what would have to give and what each candidate revision would cost me elsewhere in the set? In particular, I can't tell whether my sympathy for the German system and my tolerance of the English one just reveal that my American separationism is a local habit rather than a principle, or whether the three countries are actually run on one consistent idea that I haven't articulated. And I can't tell whether my line between vouchers for religious schools and grants for church-run shelters is a principled distinction about children and consent, or special pleading. Please be blunt about which commitment is doing the least work, and say whether whatever principle survives the audit would have me file the paperwork to leave or keep paying.
