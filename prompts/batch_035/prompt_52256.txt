I teach a graduate seminar on concept learning and machine cognition, and in the last session I defended a thesis I have since come to distrust; I want to know precisely where my argument fails. The discussion was prompted by Bongard problems — the puzzles with two panels of six boxes apiece, each box containing a small line drawing, where every box on the left shares some property that no box on the right has (figures large rather than small, outlines rather than filled shapes, and so on), and the task is to find the property. My argument ran as follows.

(1) To solve such a puzzle is to find some property that all six left boxes have and no right box has.
(2) For any given twelve boxes, there are always many such properties, not just one: alongside the intended rule, there are endless gerrymandered disjunctive properties — being a circle, or containing exactly one dot, or being the third box from the top — that happen to fit these twelve boxes.
(3) The examples therefore never determine which property is intended; the same twelve boxes are compatible with arbitrarily many rules, and the data alone cannot select among them.
(4) Yet solvers — human ones and trained classifiers alike — converge on the intended rule from these six boxes alone, and different solvers converge on the same rule.
(5) Convergence from data that underdetermine the answer must be explained by something the agents bring to the task: a preference among candidate properties that was already in place before the twelve boxes were drawn.
(6) So the capacity on display is not induction from examples at all: the right answer was effectively fixed beforehand, and the examples merely select among antecedently available candidates.
(7) Therefore there is no such thing as learning a concept from a few examples, in Bongard problems or anywhere else: either the concept is innate or pre-loaded, or, where it is genuinely acquired, it is acquired not from the examples but from whatever structure does the selecting.

Each step still looks presentable to me, and the conclusion is intolerable — it would make my own seminar title, Learning from Few Examples, a contradiction in terms. Where exactly does this go wrong? I am most attached to (3), since the gerrymandered properties really do fit the boxes, and I am prepared to sacrifice (6) or (7); but I do not want hand-waving about simplicity, I want a diagnosis. Note two places I suspect myself and cannot untangle: if the escape is that examples select rather than create, I need to hear how selection among prior candidates is supposed to count as learning rather than retrieval; and if the response is that the selecting structure was itself learned, I need to hear why that does not merely push the problem back a level rather than answering it. Please identify the weakest step or the most suspect transition, explain why it looked fine, and then price the alternatives honestly — say what each rival diagnosis has to give up and what explanatory work it still has to do. Close with a single line of the form Verdict: — stating which step or which transition between steps you reject, and in at most one clause, why.
