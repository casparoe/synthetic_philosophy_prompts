I'm taking a graduate seminar in epistemology this term, and the unit on bootstrapping and easy knowledge has left me less sure of my own reactions than when we started. Rather than trying to settle the literature, I've been cataloguing my gut verdicts on cases, and I'd like someone to tell me whether there's a coherent principle behind them or whether I'm just contradicting myself. Here are the cases. First, the classic gas-gauge bootstrapper: over the course of a long drive, a driver checks the gauge at each stop, each time reasonably believing the tank has gas partly on the gauge's say-so, and then at the end reasons that the gauge read correctly at every stop, so it is reliable. I'm confident she does not come to know the gauge is reliable that way. Second, the one-shot version: I glance at a thermometer once, and on that basis alone form both the belief that it is 72 degrees and the belief that this thermometer is accurate. Here I waver — it feels less bad than the driving case, but I can't say why, since one iteration is just the limit of many. Third, seeing a zebra in ordinary zoo conditions, I take myself to know it is not a cleverly disguised mule, without doing any further checking, so I affirm closure. Fourth, I also accept dogmatism about perception: I know the table is red by looking, without needing antecedent justification for believing my color vision is reliable, even though I have no way of checking my vision that doesn't go through my vision. Fifth, a weather forecaster who has been right about rain for thirty straight days, where I checked each prediction by looking out the window, now strikes me as someone I legitimately come to believe is reliable, and this feels different from the gauge even though the structure looks similar. Sixth, a newspaper reader who believes one report and immediately concludes that the paper is reliable strikes me as having no knowledge of the paper's reliability, even about that single instance.

The trouble is that my verdicts don't seem to compose. The obvious repair is that bootstrapping fails when the method is the only check on itself, since the forecaster case had independent checks and the gauge did not. But the perceptual case is also self-checking, and there I insist knowledge is possible — which, together with closure, seems to let me run the bootstrapping pattern on my own eyes, one reliable-seeming perceptual belief at a time, until I have derived that my vision is reliable from nothing but its own outputs. So either my confidence about the gas-gauge case is misplaced, or my dogmatism about perception is, or the single-check thermometer verdict has to go. A further wrinkle I keep circling: whether iteration is doing any real work. If one reading can't ground the reliability belief, it's obscure how thirty can, given that each is no better positioned than the first; but if one can, the driver's conclusion starts to look like ordinary enumerative induction and I lose my grip on why bootstrapping was supposed to be a problem.

What I'd like is this: a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough to be applied mechanically, with each clause motivated by a specific case above, so that I can see exactly which of my verdicts it captures. Then tell me what the principle says about cases I haven't mentioned — say, checking one's memory by remembering, or a navigation app whose turn-by-turn directions are the only evidence one ever has about where the streets are, or a spy who must judge a codebook's accuracy by decoding messages with the very codebook. Finally, be honest about which of my six verdicts your favored principle cannot accommodate, and say whether the right move is to complicate the principle to save the verdict or to overturn the verdict to save the simplicity. Please answer in American English, and assume I know the standard dialectic — you can name-drop the usual positions, but I want the reasoning, not a reading list.
