I'd like a specification for decision theories, written before I do any more reading in the updatelessness literature. The background that makes the specification non-obvious: an agent lives in time, acquires information, and by the moment of choice much of what mattered about its decision has already been settled by how other parties predicted it would decide. A theory that updates on everything and then takes the causally dominant action loses in cases where the prediction rewarded agents disposed to ignore that very information; a theory that refuses to update loses in the ordinary run of cases, where the new information is all that matters. Fix the purpose as follows: a theory good enough for a single agent to run over a lifetime, in an environment containing predictors, other agents running similar theories, and ordinary risks. Against that purpose, write the requirements list — what an adequate decision theory must deliver — with the motivating case behind each requirement. Cover at least the following kinds of requirement, sharpen or restate them as you see fit, and add any you think the list is missing:

1. Visible dominance. Where, at the moment of choice, one action outperforms the other in every situation compatible with what the agent then knows — the settled bet, the returned test result, the observed coin — the theory must take the better action. Case: a patient receives a result that makes one treatment better under every hypothesis still open to them; a theory that disregards this is unemployable.

2. Collecting wins banked in advance. Where a counterparty has already moved on a prediction of the agent's policy, the theory must do what the favorable prediction was a prediction of. Cases: the opaque-box version of Newcomb's problem, in which the large box was filled before the choice if and only if the predictor foresaw the agent taking one box; and the mugging in which a fair coin has already landed, the agent is told the outcome, and the theory must pay on the losing side just as it would collect on the winning side.

3. Parity of prediction, duplication, and policy choice. It should make no difference to the advice whether the counterparty formed its view of the agent by simulating the agent, by duplicating the agent, or by being the agent's own earlier self settling on a policy — the difference is not visible from inside the decision. Case: a prisoner's dilemma against a duplicate versus the same dilemma against a predictor that simulated the agent; a theory permitted to treat these differently has smuggled in a metaphysics the agent cannot act on.

4. No actionable regret. The theory's advice must not be visibly outperformed at the moment it is given: the agent should never stand at a choice point, able to see that a different action does better in every situation compatible with what it then knows, and be advised to take the worse one anyway. Case: the transparent version of Newcomb's problem, where the agent sees the large box full before choosing and can see that taking both boxes yields more than taking one.

5. Cross-time coherence. The sequence of recommendations the theory issues over a lifetime must be one the agent could have endorsed in advance about itself — either dynamically consistent, or explicitly indexed to the stage at which the agent decides, with the theory saying which.

6. Self-applicability. The reasoning by which a predictor foresees the agent's action must in principle be available to the agent about itself; a theory whose output the agent can only receive, never derive, cannot be deliberated with.

For each requirement, state whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp, and give the case for that classification. Then do the part I most want: determine whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. I expect the tension to fall somewhere between visible dominance and no actionable regret on the one side, and banked wins and parity on the other, but work it out rather than taking my word for it — and if a subset really is unsatisfiable, say so plainly and tell me which requirement you would keep and why. Finally, give a brief scorecard of the causal, evidential, and updateless families of decision theories against the list, one line each, and identify the single requirement that excludes the most theories.
