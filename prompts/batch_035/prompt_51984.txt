Longtime lurker in the rationalist sphere, and I've been chewing on something from all the embryo-selection threads (polygenic scores, "designer baby" discourse, etc.) that I can't quite shake. Fertility clinics do preimplantation genetic testing as a matter of course: make a batch of embryos, screen out the one carrying Tay-Sachs or Huntington's, implant a healthy one, and everyone treats this as obviously fine, almost praiseworthy. Meanwhile the same clinic will hand you a donor catalog where you pick sperm by the donor's height, education, and SAT scores, and nobody blinks. But the moment you suggest ranking your own embryos by those same traits, people reach for the e-word, and in several countries picking sex is banned outright even when you're doing the screening anyway. So one institution is fine with screening against disease, fine with shopping for a donor's smarts, and not fine with choosing your embryo's smarts or sex.

And there's a second thing that bugs me about the disease screening itself. When you pass over the Tay-Sachs embryo, the kid you eventually bring home is a different kid than the one you would have had -- it's not like you cured anyone. So who exactly got helped? My friends say "the parents, obviously," but that doesn't explain why the genetic counselor frames it as being done for the future child. I want to understand what the whole patchwork quietly takes for granted: is there some theory of harm vs. benefit that makes "screen out disease, don't select for advantage" a coherent line rather than an accident of history? A theory about which people exist and when? Something about the moral status of an embryo before transfer versus the child after? Or is the embryo/donor asymmetry just vibes?

You're a professional philosopher working on reproductive ethics. Please brainstorm a bit before answering and go slow rather than jumping to the standard talking points. What view(s) of parental obligation and the ethics of creating people does this arrangement actually presuppose, which rival views would it rule out, and where is it genuinely underdetermined -- that is, where could several different theories all license the same practice? I'd also like you to spell out which parts of the current practice would have to change if you thought parents were obligated to select for the best-off possible child rather than merely against the worst-off conditions, since I suspect that's where the real fault line is but I can't articulate why.
