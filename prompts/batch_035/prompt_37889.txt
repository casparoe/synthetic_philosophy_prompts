I'm in a philosophy of religion reading group that works through short papers, and last month we took up David Lewis's brief late essay "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" (Philosophical Papers, 1997; later collected in Papers in Ethics and Social Philosophy). As I read it, the paper's observation is this: a one-sided diet of mundane cases would convince us that we do not accept penal substitution at all. If a friend offers to serve your sentence or take your flogging, the authorities would find the offer senseless and decline it out of hand; the volunteered suffering of the innocent friend strikes us as no reason whatsoever to leave the offender unpunished. Yet Lewis notes that not everyone agrees even about the mundane cases, and that in other registers — the language of someone suffering or dying for another, of a death that was for us — many of us treat the volunteered suffering of the innocent as the deepest reason there could be to cancel the debt. His verdict is that most of us are of two minds, and that by the end both camps may turn out to agree that penal substitution sometimes makes sense, even if nobody can say how.

I've tried to turn his observation into an argument, and the result seems to prove far too much. I would like to know where it goes wrong.

(1) Justice forbids punishing anyone for an offense he did not commit. This is why the frame-up is the very type of injustice: the horror of the scapegoat is that the community purchases its peace with the suffering of a man who did not do the deed. Nearly everyone accepts (1), and I can't see how a legal order could function while rejecting it.

(2) Christ did not commit our offenses; that is the sinlessness the doctrine requires.

(3) On the theory of penal substitution, the suffering inflicted on Christ was punishment, imposed by a just judge, for our offenses rather than his own.

(4) So, running (1) through (3): either the transaction at the center of the theory was unjust, or it was not, strictly, punishment — and if the latter, the theory is misdescribed and is really a satisfaction theory or a vicarious-penalty theory in penal dress.

Now the part that bothers me. The standard escape, as far as I can tell, is to deny (1) in a qualified way: to say that justice permits punishing one who did not commit the offense, provided he consents, or provided he is in some relevant way not a stranger to the offender — united with him, answerable for him, his representative. But if that is the escape, then (5) by parity the frame-up becomes permissible whenever the framed man consents, or is a loyal member of the group that needs a culprit; the distinction between a substitute and a scapegoat reduces to a difference in paperwork. And (6) if instead we keep (1) and read it strictly, it seems to sweep out things we actually accept: the clerk who takes a check from an offender's brother for the offender's fine, the employer held answerable for what his employee did, the parent who steps in front of the child's consequence and is let to bear it. Either way the argument proves too much — in one direction it dissolves the doctrine into something else, and in the other it dismantles our reasons for condemning the scapegoat, which is exactly the judgment that made the doctrine intelligible as justice in the first place.

My unease is concentrated in the little word "for." Being punished "for" an offense sounds single, but I suspect it covers two things: the censure addressed to the deed — which looks untransferable, addressed as it is to the one who did it — and the bearing of a liability, which we do seem to let travel, from guarantors to employers to whoever stands answerable. Every time I try to split punishment along that line, though, one of Lewis's two minds reasserts itself: if I say the atonement transferred only the liability and left the censure pointing at the offenders, then the cross looks like the mundane case raised to the highest possible stakes — the offer of the innocent friend accepted at last — and I can't tell whether that is the theory vindicated or the theory abandoned.

Tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked fine going in, and what rejecting it costs. In particular: what does the rejection do to the mundane judgment that the friend's offer is no reason to remit; where does it leave the line between accepting a substitute and framing a scapegoat; and does Lewis's two-mindedness come out as our holding two concepts of punishment, or one unstable concept that the doctrine forces us to use in both ways at once? If it helps you price the options: I would most like to keep (1), since it is the best thing I know how to say against the scapegoat, and I am prepared to hear that keeping it costs me the doctrine.
