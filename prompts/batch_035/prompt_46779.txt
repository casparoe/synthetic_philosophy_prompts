I've been sorting my gut reactions to nudges and suspect there's a principle in there somewhere, but I can't state it. A cafeteria putting salad at eye level and chips on the bottom shelf: fine. Auto-enrolling employees in a pension with an easy opt-out: fine. Auto-enrolling them in a green energy tariff that costs more, with an opt-out that requires printing and mailing a letter: not fine. A doctor who tells me I have a ninety percent survival chance instead of a ten percent mortality risk, because she knows I'll cope better with the first framing: fine, somehow, even though it's the same fact steered through my known biases. An online store showing that only two items are left in stock when it actually has hundreds: not fine, even though the sentence is, strictly, true of the number they decided to stock. My boss pre-checking the box that donates one percent of my salary to the company foundation: not fine, and worse than the green tariff case even though the money is smaller and unchecking takes one click. My working guess is that the dividing line is who benefits — my laziness being steered toward my own good is fine, toward someone else's profit is not — but that doesn't explain why the doctor's framing feels fine while the store's stock ticker doesn't, since both steer me to the steering party's advantage. Is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures these verdicts, or near enough? I'd like each clause tied to the case that motivates it, a prediction for a case I haven't listed (say, a city that deliberately makes parking just inconvenient enough that people take the tram, or a supermarket that rearranges its aisles so the candy sits between the entrance and the bread — I go back and forth on that one), and an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle can't save and would have to overturn. Please write in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you'd write a long email to a thoughtful friend.
