Last night my friend and I had a long argument that started when he claimed, citing the statistical literature that goes back to Pinker's The Better Angels of Our Nature, that the world has obviously become far less violent than it used to be. I kept pushing back, not on the arithmetic but on what is actually being measured, and by the end we realized the disagreement was less about history than about the philosophical commitments built into the measurement practice itself. I'd like to understand those commitments better.

The practice I have in mind is the standard one in this literature: violence is tallied as violent deaths — battle deaths, homicides, genocides, sometimes executions — expressed as a rate per 100,000 people per year, then compared across centuries and aggregated to the world level. So my questions concern what that way of doing things presupposes.

First, the choice of unit. Counting deaths appears to presuppose that being killed is the paradigm of violence and everything else counts only insofar as it kills. Rape, assault, mutilation, displacement, and chronic fear fall out of the tally unless they happen to be lethal; so do institutional arrangements like slavery or systematic subordination, which can persist, or even be deliberately made less lethal, while the body count falls. Does the practice presuppose a thin, act-based conception of violence as discrete physical harm to bodies, and could it be made coherent with a thicker conception on which violence can be structural or distributed across a lifetime?

Second, the normalization. Per-capita rates make the deep past look far more violent than absolute tallies do, because population has grown enormously; a modern century can contain many more total murders and assaults than a medieval one while posting a much lower rate. Reporting rates seems to presuppose that what matters about violence is the risk faced by a randomly chosen individual rather than the total amount of it — which looks suspiciously like a commitment to something like an average rather than a total view of aggregate harm. Does the practice actually need that commitment, or is it compatible with a total view that would instead report absolute numbers, and is there any principled reason the literature settled on rates rather than both?

Third, the aggregation. Summing deaths across the whole world presupposes that a death here and a death there are commensurable and additive, and that harms to people centuries apart, in wholly different social worlds, can be placed on one scale and compared. Is that a substantive axiological assumption, and what would a measurement built on a non-additive or non-comparable view of harm — one that cared about relationships destroyed, or about the character of a social order rather than its body count — have to look like instead?

Finally, the meta-question my friend and I circled but never resolved: given that the answer to "is the world becoming less violent?" flips or blurs depending on these choices of unit, normalization, and aggregation, is there a fact of the matter about the trend at all, or only facts about particular measures? If the latter, I want to know which of the practice's commitments are doing real work and which are merely convenient, and what an honest headline claim about the decline of violence would have to be hedged to say.
