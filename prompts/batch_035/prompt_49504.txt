I teach a philosophy of science seminar that has just finished Lakatos's 1970 paper 'Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes' (in Lakatos and Musgrave, eds., Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge). For the final session I want a one-page handout: a decision tree that sorts a student from a fixed starting point — 'I accept that science is to be appraised at the level of research programmes, each with a hard core, a protective belt, and a track record' — down to a named position on the following question: what may a scientist rationally do when the programme she works in has been degenerating for a decade — anomalies absorbed by belt adjustments that predict nothing new, the last confirmed novel prediction long past, and a rival programme that both keeps predicting new facts and explains her programme's past successes?

Constraints on the tree. Every branch node must be a question the student can answer from cases rather than from labels. The nodes I expect to see, though you may restructure: whether a single stubborn anomaly ever by itself warrants abandoning a theory; whether an after-the-fact belt adjustment can count as progress if it is later independently confirmed as a novel prediction in its own right; whether appraisal should look at a programme's whole history or only its recent trajectory, i.e. whether an early brilliant run buys later patience; whether there is an objective, viewpoint-independent fact about which of two rival programmes is doing better, as opposed to shared values that guide but do not dictate; whether a scientist who knows her programme is degenerating may rationally stay in it — the comeback worry should enter here, e.g. through the case of a theory dismissed for decades that later revives; and whether the proper measure of progress is novel prediction or solved problems, which should split the Lakatosian from anyone who measures science by problem-solving capacity instead.

Leaves: naive falsificationism, Lakatos's own position in the 1970 paper, Kuhn's, Feyerabend's, and a problem-solving position in the neighborhood of Laudan's research traditions. Each leaf carries one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Two further requirements. First, flag honestly any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate — I suspect that Lakatos and Kuhn will collapse together once the polemics are stripped away, and if so I want to know which further question, if any, could prise them apart. Second, list the answer combinations the tree makes available that no one occupies, and for each say whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it has simply not been tried; do not paper over gaps by inventing distinctions.

Do not aim for neutrality at the cost of bluntness: I would rather the handout expose disagreement than smooth it.
