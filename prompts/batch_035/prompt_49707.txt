My partner and I have been arguing for months about how agents should handle threats they cannot answer at the time they're made, and I've decided the only way forward is to lay out my actual verdicts and see whether they amount to anything coherent. For background: I'm comfortable with the standard game-theoretic picture that being visibly the kind of agent who refuses to pay blackmail can make you not worth blackmailing in the first place, and I've read enough about commitments that only work because they're adopted in advance that none of that seems alien to me. The problem is that when I go through cases one by one, my instincts do not line up, and I can't tell whether there's a single principle underneath them or just a pile of biases. Here are the cases and my verdicts. First: an extortionist who has already spent real resources digging up something damaging on me threatens to release it unless I pay; I should refuse, and I should be the kind of agent who refuses. Second: a blackmailer whose threat is costless to carry out and whose entire business model is farming people who pay; still refuse, no hesitation. Third — and this is where it gets uncomfortable — I think surrogate goals are fine, maybe even smart: if I publicly and irrevocably adopt a policy of paying a small, capped amount to anyone who credibly threatens some cheap decoy (say, an embarrassing-but-harmless photo I've designated in advance), then would-be blackmailers waste their leverage on the decoy, and the real threats become less attractive to make. Paying the surrogate feels not just permitted but rational to me, even though by the lights of my first two verdicts, paying a real blackmailer anything at all is a failure of agency.

Fourth: I read about a hypothetical future institution that will, once it exists, simulate agents from our era and transfer money to the contemporary associates of those who donated to its precursor fund today; my verdict is that I shouldn't donate, though I confess I feel shakier about this than about the blackmail cases. Fifth: twenty years ago, someone set up an automated trust that pays me ten thousand dollars today if and only if I donate one thousand dollars to a specified charity; my verdict is that I should donate — it's free money for a good cause, and only a miser walks away. The fourth and fifth cases strike me as structurally much closer than my divergent verdicts suggest: in both, an agent that can no longer observe or causally affect me has set up a payment conditional on my present action, and my action is in some sense aimed at an agent in a different position in time. Yet I donate to the past agent's scheme and refuse the future one's. Sixth: suppose I adopt a surrogate goal and a blackmailer credibly threatens the decoy but then carries out the cheap threat anyway out of spite; my verdict is that I should not then escalate or retaliate beyond paying what the policy stipulated, even though my policy would deter better if it stipulated brutal post-hoc retaliation. Seventh: I feel that adopting surrogate goals is fine when I do it quietly, but something grates when I imagine a whole community of agents loudly announcing their decoys to each other — though when I try to say what changes, I can't.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough to make predictions. In particular: what exactly distinguishes paying a surrogate blackmailer from paying a real one, given that both are payments to agents who threaten me, made because I committed in advance to making them? Is the difference about the size and publicity of the commitment, about what the payment does to the incentives of the threatening population, or about something in the causal or evidential structure of the arrangement? And is my fourth-versus-fifth pair defensible — is there a real asymmetry between deals with the past and deals with the future, or should one of those verdicts go? Please tell me what your principle predicts for two cases I haven't mentioned: one where the surrogate payment is to a threat issued by an agent who demonstrably knows my policy and adopted its threat strategy after learning it, and one where the future institution from the fourth case credibly commits to making my life marginally worse rather than better if I don't donate. Finally, be blunt about which of my verdicts your favored principle cannot accommodate, because I suspect at least one of them is sentiment rather than decision theory, and I'd rather know which.
