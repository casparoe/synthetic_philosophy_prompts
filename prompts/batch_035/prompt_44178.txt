I teach a seminar on philosophy of history with an interdisciplinary mix of students — some theology, some political theory, a few physicists who take to any metaphor with geometry in it — and I've been building a unit around what may be the most load-bearing image in American public moral rhetoric: the arc. The lineage runs from the abolitionist minister Theodore Parker, who remarked in an 1853 sermon that, seen from a God's-eye distance, the arc of the moral universe is long but that it bends toward justice; through Martin Luther King, who made the line famous, most memorably to the marchers at Selma in 1965, in a register where the bending was clearly providential; to Barack Obama, who invoked it so often that his staff reportedly stitched it into the Oval Office rug, and who eventually added the gloss that the arc does not bend on its own — that people bend it. Along the way the metaphor has also been naturalized: Michael Shermer's book on the moral arc argues, if I recall his framing correctly, that science and reason, not providence, are the bending forces.

Here is what I want from you: an audit of the analogy itself, not a sermon on any side of it.

First, lay out the correspondence table. If history is an arc, what is the space it curves in? What plays the role of the trajectory — is history a path with a direction at each point, or a sequence of positions? What corresponds to the bending force in Parker's version (God), in King's (a God who works through movement politics), in Obama's emended version (the agents themselves), and in Shermer's (science and reason)? What, if anything, plays the role of the observer who can see that the curve is an arc at all — because an arc is only visible from a distance or from outside, and no participant in history occupies that standpoint?

Second, say where the mapping breaks, and sort the disanalogies by whether they matter for the uses the metaphor is actually put to. Some obvious candidates: an arc is a deterministic trajectory, but history has reversals — bronze age collapses, backsliding regimes, moral regress that lasted centuries; the long of the long arc does suspicious work, making the claim nearly unfalsifiable by stretching the payoff beyond any horizon at which it could be checked; a curve implies a smooth, continuous deformation, but moral progress looks punctuated, with long plateaus and sudden lurches; and the metaphor quietly imports a single metric along which everything bends, whereas the emancipations of one era have coexisted with new injustices not captured on that axis. But also tell me which disanalogies are idle — true but irrelevant to the conclusions people try to draw.

Third, and this is where my students get stuck, so be precise: examine the interaction between the metaphor's origins in providence and its current naturalistic uses. King's version had a bender distinct from the bent; Obama's emendation and Shermer's naturalization move the bending inside history. Does the metaphor survive that surgery? If what bends the arc is just us pushing, is the trajectory picture doing any work that a flat tug-of-war picture — no arc, no bend, only a rope and two teams — wouldn't do better? Or conversely, is there some residual content: a claim about accumulated institutions, ratchets, knowledge that doesn't get unlearned, that makes the curved geometry earn its keep even without a divine hand?

Finally, tell me whether the standard conclusions each camp draws survive the audit. The providential camp draws patience and confidence; the activist camp draws urgency (the arc won't bend on its own); the skeptics draw complacency, arguing the picture lulls people into waiting. Which of these, if any, does the metaphor actually support once the disanalogies are on the table? And if the arc fails, what is the better analogy for the relation between moral effort and historical outcome — a random walk with drift, a ratchet, an arms race, a canal that fills only when it rains — and what does the replacement get right that the arc got wrong? I would rather my students leave able to audit the replacement than able to recite the original.
