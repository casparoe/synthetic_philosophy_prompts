I help run a little reading group on the ethics of lying (mixed crowd, some phil grads, some not) and we just did the classic Kant case — the murderer at the door asking where your friend is hiding. Next and final session is on the lying/misisleading distinction: the idea that you can say only things that are strictly true and still do something that feels like lying, or vice versa say something false and arguably not be lying. I want to end with a poem that actually does the philosophical work instead of just describing it, because honestly our best discussions have been about exactly this grey zone where truthfulness and honesty come apart.

So: write a villanelle in the voice of the person at the door — the one being asked where their friend is — who has resolved never to utter a falsehood. Every single line must be capable of being literally true in its context, but the poem as a whole must leave the reader holding at least one false belief about where the friend is or what happened. The two refrains are where the real work happens: they come back verbatim, unaltered, but each return should do different work — the same words, true every time, but exploiting what the reader now expects from context so that by the last appearances they mislead harder than a plain lie would. The final couplet, where the refrains meet side by side, is the moment a careful reader should be able to look back and find no line they can actually accuse of being false — and feel the full discomfort of that.

Constraints: no line may be false on its face, no cheating with ambiguity so extreme that it's just noise, and it should be specific enough that someone rereading can reconstruct exactly which belief they were left with and exactly which line induced it. Someone who knows the pragmatic machinery (implicature, what's communicated vs what's asserted) should be able to point at the poem and say ah, THAT's the mechanism, and someone who's never heard of any of it should just feel the trick on the second read. Ten lines of "I will not lie to you" prose would be easy; make the form carry the argument. If it helps, my group argued last week about whether the true-but-misleading dodge is more or less disrespectful to the asker than a plain lie — feel free to let the speaker's self-satisfaction or unease bleed through.
