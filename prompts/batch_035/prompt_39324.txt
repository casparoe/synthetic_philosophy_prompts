Liberal democracies hold what look like three inconsistent attitudes toward secession. The UK granted Scotland a legally sanctioned independence referendum in 2014, decided by a bare simple majority, with a prior agreement to treat the result as binding. Canada, shaken when the 1995 Quebec referendum came within a fraction of a point of a Yes, responded with the Clarity Act, which demands a clear question and a clear majority while pointedly refusing to specify any numerical threshold. Spain goes further and denies that a regional referendum could authorize secession at all — the Catalan vote of 2017 was ruled unconstitutional and its organizers prosecuted. What theory of self-determination, if any, does this pattern of practice presuppose? Candidates: secession as a primary right exercisable by any cohesive people that wants it; secession as a remedial right triggered only by genuine grievance or oppression; or secession as something like a constitutional amendment, where the status quo deserves structural protection and a supermajority is the natural default. A related puzzle: rationalists have a lot of folklore about how irreversible, one-way-door decisions warrant higher evidential thresholds than reversible ones — does the Clarity Act's deliberate vagueness encode a coherent version of that thought, or is it just an unprincipled fudge designed to let the federal government move the goalposts? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say which of the three state practices it can actually accommodate and which it quietly rules out, and tell me what would have to change in any of these arrangements if you committed to one of the rival views — e.g., whether the remedial-right theorist should really be comfortable with Scotland's 2014 vote, or whether the primary-right theorist has any principled basis for demanding more than fifty percent plus one.
