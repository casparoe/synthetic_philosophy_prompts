I'm a retired immunologist -- 38 years of running assays and writing grants, and now that the lab is somebody else's problem I read philosophy at the kitchen table and bother my family with it. My brother-in-law Tom is a lapsed seminarian who thinks the doctrine of double effect is the best thing moral philosophy ever produced. My old labmate Priya is a utilitarian who thinks the whole doctrine is post-hoc rationalization of squeamishness dressed up in Latin. They've been having the same arguement at my table for two years: it always starts with the trolley (divert it onto one to save five -- fine; push the big man off the footbridge -- not fine), and it always ends with each of them deciding the other is dense. I'll admit upfront I find Priya more convincing, but Tom is sharper than people expect, and I've started to suspect a lot of their fight is about words.

Write the joint statement they should have produced if they'd done a proper adversarial collaboration -- the document both of them could actually sign. Call the sides the Tom camp (the intend/foresee distinction is doing real moral work) and the Priya camp (it's rationalized intuition). It needs:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign. At minimum, they should be able to agree on something about: the fact that most people judge diverting permissible and pushing impermissible, and that any theory owes some explanation of that asymmetry; what the doctrine owes in the way of an account of "intending" when the agent has to track the harm for the plan to work (the terror bomber who kills civilians to demoralize vs the strategic bomber bombing the same civilians' munitions factory as a side effect -- same deaths, different descriptions); and something about the status of intuitions themselves, since both camps need them as data but use them differently.

2. The disagreements, phrased so each camp would accept the wording of its own view. Include where exactly they part ways on: whether harm-as-a-means vs foreseen-side-effect is a real moral difference or a gerrymandered line; whether the loop case hurts the doctrine's usual explanation (that's the variant where the fat man's body would stop the trolley even if the five weren't tied to the track, so in outcome terms the five would die anyway and pushing him starts to look more like a necessary means); and whether a side effect that is foreseen with certainty is still meaningfully different from an intended harm.

3. The cruxes -- sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. I suspect one of them is whether there's a principled stopping point between redirecting a threat and using someone's body as a trolley-stopper, and another is whether the footbridge intuition is just an artifact of heuristics about close-range personal force, but they might disagree with me about that and that's fine.

4. For each crux, one concrete case or argument that both camps agree would bear on it, with each camp's prediction written down in advance -- the whole point is that the predictions are recorded before anyone gets to wiggle out.

5. If part of their dispute is verbal -- say, about what "intending" even amounts to, or whether they're both using "permissible" the same way -- say which part and why.

Format: numbered agreed claims first, then a disagreements table, then the cruxes with the recorded predictions. Describe any thought experiments fully in the document itself rather than naming papers -- I want it readable without a library card. American English please.

End with a single last line of the form "Bottom line: ..." giving, in one sentence, the crux you think would actually move one of the camps if it got settled.
