For a term paper in my political philosophy elective, I'm trying to figure out what Robert Nozick would say about some animal-welfare questions that either didn't exist or weren't on the horizon when he wrote Anarchy, State, and Utopia in 1974, and I'd like your help working out the prediction carefully rather than just grabbing a conclusion.

Here is the track record I'm working from, as my classmate and I have reconstructed it from the section where he discusses animals. First, he takes it as obvious that animals count for something: if snapping your fingers to the beat of some music would somehow cause ten thousand contented cows to die in great pain, it would not be perfectly all right to snap. Second, he says it's not all right to hunt animals merely for the fun of it. Third, he poses a case where you could get extra pleasure from swinging a bat, but doing so would smash a cow's skull as a side effect, and he says he can't find a principle that would allow eating animals for the extra pleasure of taste while forbidding the bat-swinging — the marginal gain in both cases being mere pleasure over the best harm-free alternative. Fourth, he notes he believes eating animals is not necessary for health in the United States, so the gain from eating them is confined to pleasures of the palate. And yet, despite all this, he never actually declares vegetarianism a duty — the section is framed as illuminating side constraints, and the strict inviolable constraints get attached to persons, who (he hints in a later chapter) are marked off by their capacity to shape a life through long-term plans, which is also why he thinks most people would refuse to plug into the experience machine, and why he thinks taxing a person's earnings from labor is on a par with forced labor.

The new cases my paper is about, all of which arose after Nozick's time: (1) cultured meat — suppose lab-grown meat becomes widely available, tasty, and affordable, so that eating conventionally raised animals is now clearly unnecessary for nutrition; (2) gene-edited livestock — proposals to engineer farmed animals with drastically reduced pain sensitivity, so that the usual suffering of factory farming is largely absent even though the animals are still killed; (3) octopus farming — intensive aquaculture of a solitary, curious, problem-solving invertebrate for food, with no cultured substitute available; and (4) intervening in nature to reduce wild animal suffering, for instance by vaccinating deer against a wasting disease.

What I want from you is this: predict Nozick's verdicts on these four cases. The reason I think this is genuinely open is that at least three different rules fit his earlier verdicts equally well — call them (a) the side-constraint rule, on which inviolability belongs only to beings capable of long-term projects, so animals get some weight but no rights against being killed when the alternative is worse for us; (b) the marginal-comparison rule suggested by the bat-swinging case, on which an animal's pain and death must be weighed against the marginal gain from harming rather than not harming it, so that where the gain is mere taste and an equal alternative exists, the harm is impermissible; and (c) the brutalization rule, on which our treatment of animals matters mainly through its spillover effects on how we treat persons, which Nozick himself seems skeptical of but which would explain why he never drew the vegetarian conclusion. Lay out what each rule predicts for each of the four cases, note where the rules diverge and where they agree, give me your best overall prediction for Nozick with your reasons for preferring it, and tell me what further case — one I could describe in the paper — would best distinguish between the rules, in whatever direction you favor. Since I'll be graded partly on intellectual honesty, please also state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and explicitly single out the point in your answer you're least sure about.
