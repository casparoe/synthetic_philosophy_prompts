I do observability for distributed systems -- traces, spans, structured logs, sampling, dynamic instrumentation, the whole "reconstruct what happened from telemetry after the fact" racket. Been at it like 10 years so assume I'm fluent. Lately I've been reading about the old Norman Malcolm vs Daniel Dennett fight over whether dreams are even experiences, and it weirdly feels like arguments I've had at work about logs. As far as I can tell the positions are roughly: Malcolm argued that dreams literally can't be experiences anyone has, because the only evidence there ever is, is the report given after waking -- the sleep itself leaves no independently checkable record, so the content gets fixed only at the moment of telling, and a claim that can't in principle be checked against anything isn't a claim about anything. Dennett came back with something like: fine, but that's just how consciousness works in general -- the dream content is composed and only becomes determinate when it's retrieved and reported, and waking cognition isn't fundamentally different.

Restate both positions in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can. Think: instrumentation that's off by default and can't be turned on retroactively, sampled vs unsampled telemetry, the difference between a span that recorded live and a timeline somebody reconstructed for the postmortem, whether a log line means the system "did" something or just that we now say it did.

Then I want the residue report, because that's the part I actually care about:

1. What translates cleanly? My instinct says Malcolm's verificationism is basically "if there is no way, even in principle, to distinguish a live-recorded event from a retro-composed one, you have no business asserting the live one happened" -- and I have that fight constantly, about whether an incident "really" happened the way the dashboards say. Am I right, or am I flattering myself?

2. What only translates if you bend it? E.g. we do have some instrumentation for dreams -- REM, sleep-lab awakenings, people muttering. Does that map to partial traces / coarse logs, and does it actually weaken Malcolm's point the way it would weaken a "no telemetry, no incident" argument?

3. What has no counterpart at all? The one I keep getting stuck on: the dreamer reports that the dream took what felt like hours, while the REM episode was ten minutes. In my world a reconstructed timeline can be wrong about duration but the system never *felt* like anything took longer than it did. Is "felt duration" something my vocabulary simply cannot express, or is that exactly the fudge Dennett is calling out?
