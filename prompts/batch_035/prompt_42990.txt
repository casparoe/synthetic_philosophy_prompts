A few friends and I keep a low-key blog tracking how governments handle apologies for historical wrongs, and we've been arguing about a case study we constructed around a hypothetical country (a fairly ordinary parliamentary democracy, invented for the exercise, so no real-world baggage). Here's its track record. It issued a full parliamentary apology for a forced sterilization program run from the 60s to the 80s, with a compensation fund, when a few thousand victims were still alive. It apologized without compensation for the wartime internment of an ethnic minority, by then with only a handful of living survivors. It refused any apology for a 19th-century colonial famine, on the grounds that the causal story was contested among historians and responsibility too diffuse. It issued a statement of "deep regret" but pointedly not an apology for colonial-era slavery, noting the trade predated the modern state and was largely run by chartered companies. It apologized with compensation for abuse in state-and-church-run boarding schools for an indigenous minority. And it declined to apologize for a 1970s mass eviction of a valley for a dam project, acknowledging the harm but saying the policy was lawful at the time, democratically adopted, and already followed by relocation payments.

The new case we're arguing about: in the 60s and 70s, the state's welfare agencies, cooperating with religious institutions, pressured unmarried mothers into surrendering their babies for adoption — coerced consent forms, months-long confinement in homes, threats about welfare payments. Several thousand mothers are alive today. At the time it was legal, broadly popular, and even some social workers of the era would still defend the practice in softer terms. The historiography is mostly settled on what happened, but some archives remain sealed, and a minority of historians argue the consent was less coerced than activists claim. There was a recent TV documentary that moved public opinion a lot, and the mothers' groups are asking for both an apology and compensation.

So: predict the verdict, assuming the country wants to stay consistent with its own precedents. Obviously there's more than one rule that fits the track record — e.g. "apologize when living victims were directly harmed by the state" vs. "apologize when the conduct is now seen as a rights violation rather than bad-but-lawful policy" vs. something more cynical about apologies tracking what's cheap or popular. Tell me which candidate rules fit the earlier cases, what each one predicts here, your best guess with reasoning, and what further hypothetical case would best distinguish between the rules (we want to design one for a follow-up post). Also say which precedent is the closest analog — my one friend insists it's the sterilization one, another says the eviction one, and honestly I can see both.
