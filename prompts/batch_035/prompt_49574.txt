I'm a PhD student in machine learning, and I've been made the de facto distribution-shift reviewer for a lab that ships trained models to external partners — hospital scheduling, retail demand forecasting, one agricultural application. The recurring question colleagues bring me is which of a model's learned regularities it is safe to carry forward into the future. Validation scores are high, but the validation set is drawn from the same period as the training data, and I've become convinced that this cannot by itself distinguish regularities that will keep holding from ones that merely fit everything observed so far — the situation Goodman's grue is meant to expose, where two hypotheses agree on all past data and diverge in prediction, with nothing in the fit deciding between them. What I want is a procedure I can actually run in an hour before sign-off: an ordered set of questions to ask about each salient feature or pattern the model relies on, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the answer terminates in a firm "don't deploy" or "deploy with monitoring," and where it correctly punts to a human because the remaining question is institutional risk tolerance rather than fact. Ground it in what philosophy of induction actually offers here — projectibility, entrenchment, the point that no purely formal feature of a hypothesis marks it as grue-like — but translate all of that into tests a grad student with no philosophy background can apply from the model card, the data documentation, and an hour with the feature importances. Then run it on three cases from my current queue: (1) a no-show model that leans heavily on appointment lead time; (2) a demand model trained on data spanning the pandemic, whose strongest feature is the online share of orders; (3) a triage-support model whose best feature is a stylistic pattern in referral notes — the procedure will have to tell me whether to keep it, and I admit I'd rather not hear the answer, since that feature is carrying most of the accuracy. Please commit to definite verdicts on all three rather than laying out options for me to weigh. And be explicit about where the procedure will misfire or run out: I know entrenchment-based solutions to Goodman's riddle are themselves contested, and I don't want a checklist that smuggles in a philosophy-of-science position while presenting itself as arithmetic.
