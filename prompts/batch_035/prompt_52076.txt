For the past two years I've run a small design studio, and I've come to suspect that something is wrong with how I've been thinking about its situation. Objectively, the signs haven't been good: revenue has been flat while expenses crept up, and two of our best clients left last fall. I prepare the financial summaries myself, so nothing was hidden from me — and yet a few months ago I quietly stopped opening the monthly accounting emails unless I absolutely had to. When people asked how the studio was doing, I'd say "in a rough patch, but fundamentally sound," and I want to stress that I meant it sincerely each time.

Last week a friend who worked with me years ago sat me down and said she thinks I've been lying to myself about the business. Her phrase bothered me, but it also rang true. The problem is that the more I reflect on it, the less I understand what could even be happening. To deceive someone, you presumably need to know the truth and steer them away from it. If I was doing that to myself — avoiding the numbers, curating what I said — then I must have known the studio was in trouble. But if I knew, how could I simultaneously have sincerely believed it was fundamentally sound? And if I didn't know, then whom exactly was I deceiving? It feels like I have to be both the con artist and the mark, which sounds incoherent.

I'd like help with three things. First, is my friend's description even coherent — can a person deceive themselves in the full-blooded sense, or is what happened to me better described as something else, like motivated avoidance that happened to produce a comfortable belief? Second, if I did in some sense engineer my own belief, does that make the belief different in kind from an honest mistake, in a way that matters for how much I should trust my judgment generally? Third, and most practically: now that I've flipped toward pessimism, how do I check whether this new view is any more accurate than the old one, given that I've now demonstrated my beliefs can be bent by what I want to be true?
