I'm applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need a two-page proposal in moral psychology. Topic: the moral worth of actions — what makes an act worthy, in something like Kant's sense, rather than merely right.

My half-formed idea: the debate has stalled on overdetermination cases. A nurse comforts a dying patient from compassion, but she would have done it anyway as a matter of professional duty; someone keeps a promise from fidelity, though fear of being caught would have secured compliance regardless. On one view, an act has moral worth only if the moral motive makes the difference — only if, without it, the right act would not have occurred. On another, moral worth requires only that the agent act from the moral motive, however causally redundant it is. I suspect the stalemate comes from both sides assuming a single notion of the right motive making the right difference. I want to split it in two: a causal role (the motive helps produce the act) and a constitutive role (the motive supplies the description the agent acts under — what she takes herself to be doing). My hunch is that moral worth tracks the constitutive role, so that the overdetermination cases do have moral worth when the moral motive, though causally redundant, is still what the action is of.

Please structure the proposal as follows. "The question" (half a page, stated precisely); "What existing positions leave open" (half a page — why each view fits some cases and distorts others; no citations, I will add those myself); "Method" (two-thirds of a page, including a rough month-by-month plan — I am imagining a mix of case-construction and working out the causal/constitutive distinction, but suggest something better if you see it); "Failure and scope" (one-third of a page — what would count as the project failing, and what is most likely to make it too big for a year). No bibliography.
