So I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, but I fell down the qualia rabbit hole a while back and have been slowly forming opinions, mostly by testing them on myself like I'd test a system. I read Dennett's Quining Qualia, with the two coffee tasters Chase and Sanborn who can't tell whether the coffee got worse, their taste buds changed, or just their standards drifted -- and I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about whether we can be wrong about our own experience. Here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I'd like to know if there's a principle that captures them, or whether I'm just contradicting myself.

My verdicts:

1. The Chase and Sanborn case itself. There IS a fact of the matter about whether Mr Chase's experience of Maxwell House coffee today is the same as his experience of it was six years ago. He can't access the fact, but it's not like the question has no answer. It feels to me like a variable that's been overwritten -- the value existed, it's just gone.

2. Pain. I'm basically infallible about whether I'm in pain right now. If I say I'm in pain, there's no gap between seeming to be in pain and being in pain -- the seeming just IS the being. If a machine logged my neurons and disagreed with my report, I'd trust my report on that one, at least about the being-in-pain part.

3. But here's where I start to wobble. I had my wisdom teeth out and got a course of strong painkillers, and my honest judgment afterwards was that the pain itself got less intense, not just that I coped with it better. But how would I know? That's exactly the kind of comparison-across-time that case 1 says I can't do. And yet my gut still insists: pain is different, I just know it got duller. So I hold 2 and 3 alongside 1 and they don't sit together.

4. The gradual inversion case -- suppose some process flipped my red and green experience overnight, very slowly over years, and adjusted all my memories to match. I want to say there's still a fact about what red looks like to me now versus what it looked like in 2010, even though no comparison I could make would ever reveal it. But then what is that fact even made of? What makes it true?

5. My working theory, as an engineer: introspection is like reading from a cache. It's perfectly reliable about the current state but it keeps no history, so any judgement that requires comparing now to then is a reconstruction, and reconstructions can be wrong without you knowing. Verdicts 1, 3 and 4 seem to fit this -- current pain (cache read, infallible), pain intensity over time (reconstruction, could be off), the coffee guy (reconstruction).

But I don't think my cache theory is actually a theory, it's a metaphor, and I can see it failing on a case I keep arguing with myself about: I've had a migraine aura maybe a dozen times, and I'd swear my current report about what it looks like is infallible, but I'd also swear my memory of the first one matches -- and at least one of those has to involve comparing across time, right? Also there's a deeper thing I can't shake: if the cache is the whole story, then Dennett seems right that qualia as philosophers define them just aren't doing any work -- the properties I'm infallible about are just whatever the report says, which makes 'infallible' cheap. But when I'm actually in pain, this all feels like sophistry.

So: is there a real principle here, or a small set of candidates? Please state it precisely, show me which of my five verdicts motivates which clause, tell me honestly which verdicts it can't save and would have to overturn, and tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed -- say, someone wearing tinted contact lenses for a year who takes them off and insists things looked completely normal the whole time, versus a friend who insists that's impossible. And if you think the whole thing bottoms out in whether 'experience' means two different things (the raw-seeming vs the judged-seeming), say that plainly, because I suspect that's the real reason this debate never moves.

Please answer in British English, lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning -- no preamble and no restating my question back at me.
