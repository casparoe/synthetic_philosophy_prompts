Title: The Coincidence That Isn't: Epiphenomenalism and Reports About Experience

Epiphenomenalism holds that physical processes in the brain cause conscious experiences, but that conscious experiences never cause anything in return. The experience of seeing red is like the shadow of a moving car: the car's motion produces the shadow's motion, and the shadow never pushes the car. On this view, when I say "I am having an experience of red," the physical causes of that utterance are entirely in the brain; the experience of red itself, as a conscious phenomenon, contributes nothing. This generates what is often called the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If my statements about my own consciousness are not caused by that consciousness, then the accuracy of those statements looks like an enormous coincidence. Worse, a hypothetical "zombie" — a being physically identical to me but with no experiences at all — would apparently write this very essay, including this sentence about the fact that it could not have written it. Philosophers such as David Chalmers have pressed this worry against epiphenomenalism, and Frank Jackson's knowledge argument is often placed nearby, though it is not the same point.

In this essay I argue that the paradox rests on an unstated and excessive demand: that a report must be caused by the very property it reports. I call this the direct-tracking assumption. Once it is rejected, the coincidence disappears.

Consider first an everyday case. A smoke detector in my kitchen goes off, and I say, "There is smoke in the kitchen." My report is caused by the detector, not by the smoke directly; the smoke itself played no direct role in producing my sentence. Yet no one thinks the truth of my report is a coincidence, because the detector is a reliable mechanism whose states are correlated with smoke. The lesson is that reports can be accurate without being caused by their objects, provided there is a reliable mediating mechanism.

Epiphenomenalists can say the same about the brain. The brain "registers" its own states the way the detector registers smoke, and my report about an experience of red is produced by the brain's registration of the neural correlate of that experience. The experience itself need not push anything, just as the smoke does not push anything, and the report is still not lucky.

A possible objection is that the smoke case differs in one respect: in the kitchen, the smoke did cause the detector to go off, so there is a causal chain from smoke to report after all. In the case of experience, the epiphenomenalist holds that there is no chain from the experience to the report, because the experience is causally idle by hypothesis. I concede that the smoke analogy is imperfect in this way, but I do not think the concession damages the argument, because my deeper point does not depend on it.

The deeper point is that the demand for the experience itself to appear in the causal story confuses levels of description. When I report a checkmate, my sentence is caused by chess pieces on a board, not by the abstract fact of checkmate; the checkmate is simply another description of the wood-and-positions. Likewise, the experience of red is, on reflection, another description of certain physical states, and the causal story told at the physical level is the same story told at the phenomenal level. Once this is seen, the paradox of phenomenal judgment dissolves: there was never a further fact about causation for the coincidence to be about.

I conclude that epiphenomenalism survives the paradox of phenomenal judgment. The appearance of coincidence arises only from the direct-tracking assumption, and once we see that reports can be accurate through reliable mechanisms, and that the phenomenal is a level of description rather than an extra pusher, the objection loses its force. The zombie would indeed write this essay, but that is no embarrassment, because there is nothing left over for the zombie to be missing.
