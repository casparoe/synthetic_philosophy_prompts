I'm a novelist starting a book set in the late decades of this century, in which civilization has done to suffering what it once did to smallpox: germline rewriting of the hedonic set-point across the population, with the whole apparatus of Pearce's abolitionist program behind it. The ghost in the room is Brave New World, and I need a careful reading of it rather than the shorthand everyone quotes at me. Read Huxley's World State through the lens of the modern abolitionist case: is the soma-soaked contentment of the castes an anticipation of that program, a caricature of it, or a distinct thing that keeps getting mistaken for it?

The crux is the late scene in which the Savage argues with Mustapha Mond: John demands the right to unhappiness, along with everything that supposedly comes with it — art, danger, family, old age — and Mond dryly concedes that the World State has traded all of that for comfort. The moral everyone takes away is that abolishing suffering means abolishing what makes us human. The abolitionist reply, as I understand it, is that Huxley ran two things together: the abolition of suffering and the flattening of motivation. On this view you could keep every gradient of desire, curiosity, and striving, and merely lift the baseline so that no state of the system feels bad. I want to know whether that reply actually defeats Huxley or merely re-describes the World State more charitably than the novel permits. One test: is the citizens' shallowness — Lenina's, especially — presented as a consequence of raised hedonic tone, or of the conditioning and caste engineering, which are in principle separable from it? And on the other side, is the Savage's beloved suffering — the penance at the lighthouse, the Shakespeare — actually the autonomous, meaningful kind, or does his final scene amount to the novel's quiet admission that it isn't?
