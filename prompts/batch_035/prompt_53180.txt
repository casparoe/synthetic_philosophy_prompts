My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about responsibility gaps for autonomous systems — cases where a system causes harm and no human seems appropriate to blame, because no one foresaw or controlled the outcome in the right way. She thinks the gaps are an artifact of sloppy institutional design and always close on inspection; I suspect they can be real but partial — open in one direction and shut in another. We stalemated on whether a case could exist in which backward-looking responsibility (blame) genuinely lapses while forward-looking responsibility (repair, prevention) remains clearly assigned. I want to pressure-test my side with a case built to order.

You are a professional philosopher working on responsibility and autonomous systems, used to constructing cases for teaching and for adversarial referees. Think carefully, and before committing, brainstorm two or three candidate settings and pick the one that satisfies all the constraints most naturally — then build it.

Construct a thought experiment to these specifications. The setting is a realistic workplace — a warehouse, hospital logistics, agricultural monitoring, your choice — and the autonomous system causes modest but serious harm (an injury, a significant financial loss), not a death. Crucially, the harm must be foreseeable in outline by at least one human involved: the standard unpredictability route to generating a gap is closed off. The gap must instead arise from many hands plus layered automation — enough people each making locally defensible, individually reasonable calls that no single person satisfies the conditions for blameworthiness, and such that the harm cannot be traced to any one decision that was unreasonable at the time. Yet, simultaneously, one identifiable party must plainly bear forward-looking responsibility for remediation and prevention, so that responsibility for repair is fully settled while responsibility for blame is not. The scenario itself should be one paragraph, and you must not rig the verdict by stipulating that no one is blameworthy — it has to fall out of the setup.

After the scenario: (a) show how each of three positions delivers its verdict — the denier, who holds that every such harm traces back to some human's blameworthy choice; the gap-realist, who holds the case exhibits a genuine blame-gap; and the collectivist, who locates responsibility in the firm or team as a collective agent rather than individuals; (b) identify which single feature of the case is doing the work; (c) say what happens to the gap if that feature is removed or weakened; and (d) give the strongest attack on the case from whichever position it embarrasses most, and your honest judgment of whether the attack succeeds.

Once the analysis is written, append a JSON object with the following fields: scenario_summary (a string); verdicts (an object mapping each of the three positions to a one-line verdict on the case); workhorse_feature (a string naming the load-bearing feature); removal_test (a string describing what changes when that feature is removed); strongest_objection (a string); and gap_stability (your judgment of whether the blame-gap survives the strongest objection: stable, fragile, or closes, plus one sentence of justification).
