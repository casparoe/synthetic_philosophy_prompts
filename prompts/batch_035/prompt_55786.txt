I'm putting together a problem set for a population ethics unit and I want one question that really isolates the nonidentity variable, because students keep assimilating it to ordinary harm. The assignment is to construct a matched pair of scenarios, and I'd like you to write it out as a fully worked answer would need to.

Both scenarios should be set in the same ordinary policy context — say, a regional health authority choosing between two long-running prenatal or public health programs, where it's plausible that shifting economic conditions, clinic schedules, and family planning across a decade changes the timing of conceptions and therefore which children come to exist. That mechanism has to be built into the setup, not stipulated in a footnote. In the first scenario, the choice leaves the same people existing but leaves each of them somewhat worse off (a few years of serious but non-life-threatening illness, say). In the second, the choice changes who exists, and every resulting person lives a life that is absolutely worse than the one lived by their counterpart in the first scenario — but still clearly worth living, and not worse than never existing at all. Keep population sizes and lifespans equal across the two scenarios, and make the total welfare loss in the second scenario at least as large as in the first, so that the numbers can't explain any difference in how bad the two policies feel; the only variable that differs is whether identity is preserved. One paragraph per scenario, realistic enough for a policy memo rather than a laboratory, no science-fiction devices.

After the scenarios, I need the demonstration. Show what a straightforward total utilitarian must say about which policy is worse, and why the arithmetic forces that verdict. Then show what a strict person-affecting view — on which an act is wrong only if it makes someone worse off than they would otherwise have been — must say about each scenario, including the uncomfortable conclusion that the second policy wrongs no one. State which feature of the pair is doing all the work, what happens to the rankings if you delete that feature (imagine the second scenario's beneficiaries turn out to be the same people after all), and finally give the best two moves a defender of the person-affecting view could make in response — for instance, widening the notion of harm so that someone can be wronged by being brought into existence with a worse-than-typical life even if not harmed relative to nonexistence — and say which of the two moves proves too much if accepted. The point of the exercise is that a case built to these specifications can't be answered by pattern-matching to the standard depletion example, so make sure the verdicts are derived from the structure rather than asserted.
