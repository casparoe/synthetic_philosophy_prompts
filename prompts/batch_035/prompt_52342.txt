In recent years, a growing number of restaurants, corporate cafeterias, and university dining halls have begun printing the greenhouse-gas footprint of menu items — grams or kilograms of CO2-equivalent per serving — and some mark dishes that come in under a threshold with a low-carbon badge. The practical effect, since beef dominates the carbon budget of a typical menu, is to steer diners from beef toward chicken and plant-based options. Many of the same institutions maintain animal-welfare policies on the side: cage-free eggs only, higher-welfare certified poultry, and so on.

Consider what the carbon label, as a piece of institutional design, takes for granted. First, it compresses the ethics of a food choice into one commensurable number. Second, it fixes the denominator: the moral accounting is done per serving, not per animal's life or per hectare of land. Third, the harms that enter the number are those mediated by climate; the figure says nothing about how many animals died for the serving or how they lived. And this matters, because the per-serving arithmetic runs the other way once animal lives are counted. A single cow supplies hundreds of servings, so a steak dinner is responsible for only a small fraction of one bovine death, while a chicken breast is most of a bird, so a chicken dinner is roughly one whole life. By the carbon metric, switching from beef to chicken is a large improvement; by a metric of animal lives or days of suffering, it is a large regression. The same menu change looks like progress or like a disaster depending on which implicit quantity the label is taken to measure.

There is a further wrinkle. Some animal advocates argue that even a pure farmed-animal suffering metric would leave the comparison unsettled, because cattle spend their lives on pasture while chicken feed comes from tilled cropland, where mice, birds, and invertebrates plausibly die in cultivation and harvest. If those deaths count, the beef-versus-chicken comparison is not merely uncertain but possibly reversed — and nobody knows even the sign of the effect.

My questions: What theory of value does carbon labeling of food actually presuppose — that animal suffering has no weight at all, that it has weight but climate is simply the institution's chosen instrument, or something about which harms are measurable and commensurable enough to display in front of diners? Is the labeling practice consistent with the same institution's welfare policies, or do the two presuppose incompatible things? Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely tolerates: which rival theories of value could each license it, and which could not? And finally, if the implicit metric were replaced with an estimate of total suffering per serving, farmed and wild animals included, what would have to change in the labels, the menus, and procurement — and would the resulting practice be more honest, or merely more precisely wrong?
