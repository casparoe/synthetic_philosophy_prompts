I'm prepping discussion material for a philosophy of biology seminar on function talk, and I want a thought experiment that isolates exactly one variable: whether a trait got there by selection or by accident. Everything else has to be held fixed. Here's the build order.

Construct two scenarios, each one tight paragraph, featuring two traits that are as close to identical as real biology allows — same mutation or same developmental outcome, same current causal profile, same current payoff for the organism — where one trait was fixed by natural or artificial selection and the other got fixed by pure drift. Realism constraints: no Swampman-style shortcuts, no organisms appearing fully formed, nothing that couldnt plausibly happen in an actual field population or a lab. The same mutation arising twice in two populations is fair game; so is a bottleneck or founder event washing the allele to fixation for reasons that had nothing to do with its effect. The drift case especially has to be a normal, boring population-genetics story — if you make it wierd, youve smuggled in a verdict.

Then, in both scenarios, make the trait break — disrupt it in each bearer, in the same way — because the interesting verdict is whether each theory calls that a malfunction.

After the scenarios, the walkthrough: what the selected-effect account says about each trait and each breakage, what a causal-role account (the Cummins kind, functions as contributions to a system's capacities) says, and where exactly the two diverge. The verdicts have to fall out of the accounts' own machinery, not be stipulated by the setup — the test for whether the case works is whether an etiological theorist could have predicted the outcome in advance, before you told her which trait was which.

Then the honest post-mortem. Tell me which feature of the case is doing the work — I half suspect it's nothing but the fixation process itself — and what happens to the pair if you erase the difference in history and let both traits have the same origin story. After that I want the strongest comeback for the etiological theorist who thinks the case is rigged against her. Give her at least two lines of attack — something like the Millikan-ish move where the function rides on the lineage/copying family rather than on the individual token, and the flat denial that a drift-fixed beneficial trait really is functionless — and then tell me whether either one actually saves her, or whether she just has to bite the bullet and accept that two otherwise indistinguishable organisms can differ over whether they've malfunctioned. If you think biting the bullet is fine, say why its not as bad as it sounds.

So you know what counts as cheating: the point of the case is to test whether selected-effect function is a genuinely historical property. If history is doing real work, this pair should make that visible. And if the two accounts end up agreeing anyway, thats a finding too, and I want to hear it rather than have you force a divergence.

Last thing, a flip: can you build the mirror-image case where causal-role is the theory that looks silly — the old over-generation worry, where suddenly every causal contribution to any capacity you happen to care about gets called a function — while selected-effect stays composed? Same realism rules, no sci-fi, and if you can't make the flip work realistically, just say so and explain whats blocking it instead of faking something.
