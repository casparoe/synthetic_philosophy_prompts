I am a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and my thesis concerns the status of probability in the Everett (many-worlds) interpretation of quantum mechanics. Next month our department hosts a visiting senior philosopher, one of the best-known contemporary defenders of Everett and, in particular, of the decision-theoretic resolution of the Born rule problem; I have been asked to moderate the Q&A following her colloquium. She is remarkably adept at turning away objections with the reply that, on her view, the Born rule is a theorem rather than an assumption, and I would like a battery of questions that makes that reply progressively harder to sustain. Please take the role of an experienced philosopher of physics who has moderated many research seminars, and think carefully before drafting: for each question, work out internally the strongest reply the guest is most likely to give, and build in a follow-up keyed precisely to that reply.

For context, since the questions should sit in the actual literature rather than in the popularisations: the underlying worry is the incoherence problem — a deterministic theory in whose dynamics every outcome occurs seems to leave no room for probabilistic assertions at all. Her preferred answer runs through the Deutsch–Wallace theorem: if an agent's preferences over acts in Everettian worlds obey a small set of rationality axioms (diachronic consistency, branching indifference, and their kin), then the agent is required to weight branches by their Hilbert-space measures, recovering the squared-amplitude rule as a theorem of decision theory. I want the questions to press where the literature presses. At minimum I would like one question on branch-counting, and whether the branching-indifference axiom rules it out by fiat rather than by argument; one on the alternative Sebens–Carroll route via self-locating uncertainty and the epistemic separability principle, pressing whether that principle is an innocent methodological assumption or a disguised statement of the very independence the derivation was supposed to deliver; one on the gap between decision-theoretic norms and outright probability claims, including the option of retreating to a mere caring measure in the spirit of Hilary Greaves, and what such a retreat would cost the Everettian dialectically; and one drawing on the analogy with the Sleeping Beauty problem, asking whether indifference over self-locating possibilities can simply be assumed for fission cases without importing a contested answer to Beauty. I confess that I cannot decide whether the self-locating route is genuinely independent of the decision-theoretic one or merely the same argument in costume, and I would be grateful if one question were designed to expose whichever is the case.

Please produce eight to ten questions in all, each preceded by a one-sentence rationale, ordered so that they escalate in difficulty. After the prose, provide a structured summary as a JSON array of objects with the following fields: "number" (an integer), "theme" (a short label), "presses" (the assumption or principle the question targets), "anticipated_reply" (one or two sentences), and "follow_up" (the follow-up question, verbatim). Please write in British English throughout, and keep everything intelligible on its face to an audience of graduate students, without presupposing access to any literature beyond what I have described here.
