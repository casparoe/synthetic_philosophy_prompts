I'm putting together a "what do you actually think about moral saints?" quiz for the students in my intro ethics class, loosely inspired by Susan Wolf's "Moral Saints" essay but NOT a summary of it — the quiz has to sort students by their reactions to cases, not by which label they like the sound of. Please write the whole thing in American English.

Here's what I want:

- Eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so a student could answer without knowing any of the philosophical terminology. No question can just be "do you think being maximally moral is a good way to live?" — that's the thing the quiz is supposed to uncover.
- The cases should cover at least these: someone who gives up all their hobbies and personal projects to do charity work; a person who is unfailingly kind but seems to have no personality, sense of humor, or life of their own; a saint whose personal life is going badly (lonely, no joys) but who does enormous good; a case where the same sacrifice is made joyfully by one person and felt as a crushing burden by another — does that change the verdict?; and a case that separates "morally maxed out" from "an admirable person all things considered" (e.g., someone who is merely decent morally but is a brilliant musician, a great friend, etc.).
- The quiz needs to sort students on TWO questions that the literature tends to run together: (a) is being a moral saint good for the person who is one, and (b) is it better from an impartial standpoint / would the world be better with more of them. A student might say yes to one and no to the other, so design the questions so that this comes out.
- The scoring key should map answer patterns onto positions something like: the maximizing utilitarian view (moral sainthood is an ideal, full stop); a Wolf-style view (sainthood is defective as an ideal of a good human life, whether or not it's impersonally optimal); a threshold view (there's a "good enough" level of morality, and beyond it more sacrifice isn't better, maybe even worse); a virtue-ethics flavored view where the human ideal includes non-moral excellences; and the "morality is undemanding, so saints are above and beyond the call but not thereby more admirable" view. Feel free to rename these, the labels just need to be apt.
- Also flag combinations of answers your quiz can't cleanly separate — e.g. if two of the positions only differ on some subtle point none of your cases actually tests, say so instead of pretending the key sorts it out. And if some combination of answers is logically possible but no real position occupies it, note that too and say whether you think that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Keep it at a level my 19-year-old students can handle — vivid cases, minimal jargon until the scoring key, where you can name names. Oh and please dont ask any question that requires having read the Wolf essay, most of them wont have. Thanks!!
