I teach applied ethics, and something I keep bumping into with my students that I can't quite articulate properly. In criminal law there's this whole apparatus built on the idea that guilt attaches to deeds, not to persons — character evidence is mostly inadmissible, prior bad acts only come in narrowly, and once you've served your sentence your "debt to society" is supposed to be paid. The informal sanctions of online life run on exactly the opposite principle. Somebody's tweets from 2012 get dug up and treated not as stale evidence about a deed but as a window into who they permanently are, and there's no mechanism at all for ending the sanction — no sentence length, no discharge, nothing. Even people who defend these sanctions tend to talk about what the person "revealed themselves to be," which is a claim about character, not about the act.

So my question: what theory of punishment or desert does that practice actually presuppose, if any? Is it that desert attaches to persons rather than deeds? But then why do the same people who defend cancellation usually also endorse rehabilitation and "people can change" when it comes to prisons — is that a flat contradiction, or can you hold both? Could the practice be made sense of on a purely expressive theory, where the sanction says something about a norm rather than about the person, without smuggling in a claim about fixed character? And I want you to be honest about where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — could a consequentialist and a retributivist both license it, in which case it presupposes much less than it looks like it does?

A second thing I want you to take seriously rather than waving off: does the temporal part matter philosophically, or is it just a practical difference caused by the internet never forgetting? The legal side, with statutes of limitations and the debt-paid idea, seems to presuppose that desert decays or gets extinguished with time and with suffering already endured. The online side presupposes nothing decays. Is one of these just confused, or do they rest on genuinely different views about whether blame tracks acts-in-time or persons-over-time? Relatedly, do the personal identity questions bite here — when the 22-year-old who posted the thing and the 38-year-old being fired over it disagree about everything, is that even the same subject of desert? I don't need you to go full metaphysics, but tell me whether that matters or is a lawyer's dodge.

And finally: suppose we wanted to rebuild informal social sanction on the legal picture — say, a norm that public condemnation "expires," or that apologizing and time-served actually discharges the sanction. What would have to change in the practice itself for that to be more than a pious wish? Is the obstacle in the technology (archives, screenshots), in the incentives of the people doing the sanctioning, or in the underlying theory of what the sanction is even for?
