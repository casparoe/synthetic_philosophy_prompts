I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar — fifteen students, one 90-minute session — and we are nearing the unit on illusionist and interface-style views of consciousness: the idea, running from Dennett's attack on the Cartesian theater through Tor Nørretranders's "user illusion" and Keith Frankish's illusionism, that what introspection presents to us is less a faithful window onto our own minds than a useful dashboard — a compressed, misleadingly rich-seeming interface whose primary job is guiding action, not reporting how things actually are downstream. My students find this view easy to state and almost impossible to feel: on paper it sounds like a claim about information processing, and they nod along without ever confronting the fact that their own introspective reports are exactly the kind of thing the view says are confabulated press releases. I want an in-class activity that produces that confrontation experientially rather than rhetorically.

Design a runnable activity built around staged demonstrations of introspective failure that the participants themselves generate and then mis-explain. You may draw on well-replicated paradigms — anchoring by arbitrary numbers, choice blindness (secretly swapping a chosen option and asking the person to justify "their" choice), post-hoc rationalization of decisions made under time pressure — but the justifications have to come from my students about their own real choices, and the class has to discover the manipulation rather than be told about it. I imagine something with roles (the subject, the auditors who bet on whether a stated reason is the actual cause, perhaps a "press secretary" who must defend a decision she didn't make), a point-based payoff scheme with no real money, and a structure where the participants' confidence in their introspective reports is pitted against the audit trail the facilitator has quietly kept. Please give full rules, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific components of the user-illusion picture: the distinction between misidentifying the causes of a decision and misrepresenting the character of an experience, the difference between an interface that is useful and one that is accurate, and why the illusionist says the sense of phenomenality survives being told it is an illusion.

Two further asks. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — students guessing the manipulation early, debaters digging in, anchoring effects too weak with a sample of fifteen — and how to handle each. Second, be blunt about which feature of the position the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in the debrief itself. My worry is that even a flawless run only shows introspection erring about causes, while the illusionist's radical claim concerns the apparent nature of experience itself, and a clever student will say the demonstration leaves phenomenal consciousness untouched. I would like your design to anticipate that objection rather than paper over it.
