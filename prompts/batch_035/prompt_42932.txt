I'm giving the opening remarks at a memorial concert for my aunt, and I could use help writing them. She taught violin at a conservatoire for thirty-five years and was a serious, unembarrassed Schopenhauerian — she'd tell anyone who'd listen that he was basically right about music being the odd one out among the arts, the only one that doesn't depict anything but somehow gets at the thing underneath all the depicting. The programme is hers, chosen before she died: the Bach Chaconne, some late Schubert, a bit of Beethoven she'd played at her own student's funeral years ago. The audience is about half family (my side thinks Schopenhauer is a kind of german sausage) and half her conservatoire colleagues, who know the philosophy cold and will notice if I get it wrong or water it down.

Here's my problem, and it's a real one. Everything a memorial wants to say is exactly what her philosophy forbids saying. "She lives on in this music," "she's speaking to us through the Chaconne" — for Schopenhauer that's all strictly false, since music on his account isn't the voice of any individual at all, it's not even a representation of anything in the world of appearances; it's a direct image of the will itself, the striving underneath everyone and no one. So the consolation-by-communication move is off the table for anyone who took her view seriously, and she did. But Schopenhauer also hands you something else: the thought that in really listening we get a brief holiday from willing, from the endless wanting that he thought suffering basically is. Maybe the honest consolation is that, not "she's still here."

So: please write me five minutes of remarks that work for both halves of the room, don't lecture the sausage half, and don't make the philosopher half wince. I'd like it to be genuinely true to her view rather than quietly smuggling the usual comfort back in — she would have caught that instantly and found it funny. If the Schopenhauer genuinely cannot console, I'd rather hear that said beautifully than be lied to. In British English please — she lived in Glasgow the last twenty years and would want it that way. And dont feel like you have to resolve everything; a bit of honest bleakness is fine, she was cheerful about it.
