Is there a name for this pattern? I read a fair amount of history of philosophy and something keeps bugging me. Every generation of philosophers seems to describe the previous generation as muddled in a very particular way: not wrong exactly, but confused about what question they were even asking, or doing something that "doesn't really count" by the new standards. The logical positivists wrote as if everyone before them was just doing unrigorous metaphysics. Then the ordinary language people wrote as if the positivists had misunderstood how words actually work. Now people write about mid-century analytic philosophy the same way — naive about language, oblivious to its own methods. And here's what gets me: each generation's story makes the field look like it's obviously progressing, because the past is always getting "cleaned up" in retrospect. But the same rhetorical move keeps being made, over and over, which makes me wonder whether the appearance of progress is partly an artifact of how the history gets retold each time.

Does this have an established name, either in the literature on philosophical progress or in the history-of-history world? I suspect somebody has noticed it. But I don't want just a label — I want to know how it differs from ordinary retrospective simplification, since some tidying of the past is surely legitimate, and what separates the load-bearing kind of tidying (the kind that manufactures the appearance of progress) from the harmless kind. Also curious whether the answer changes depending on whether the tidying generation turns out right or wrong about the thing they were tidying. And if nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and give me the boundary cases — cases where retelling the past this way would count as genuine progress rather than an illusion of it.
