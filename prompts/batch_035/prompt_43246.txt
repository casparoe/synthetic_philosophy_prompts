Next month I'm giving the pre-concert talk at a memorial performance of the Schubert C major String Quintet for my aunt, who died in the spring. She was a professional cellist for fifty years and, in her retirement, a genuinely obsessive reader of Schopenhauer — she had the Payne translation of The World as Will and Representation on her music stand, more annotated than her parts. She left written instructions about the evening, and they constrain me in an awkward way. She forbade the usual consolations: no suggestion that she is anywhere now, no claim that she "lives on in the music" in the literal sense she considered wishful, and — this is the hard one — no statement that the Quintet expresses Schubert's feelings, or hers, or ours. She thought the expressivist picture of music was a category mistake, and she caught me committing it once at a dinner party with some embarrassment to us both. What she asked for instead were remarks that treat the music the way Schopenhauer did: as the one art that doesn't copy appearances at all, but is a direct transcript of the will itself, with the four voices running parallel to the grades of nature — the bass as the inorganic, the middle voices as the plant and animal levels, and melody as the peak, the story of a single striving that meets obstacles, is delayed, attains, and is cut off; so that when the last note of the Quintet ends and the melody simply stops, the will that it mirrored is not diminished by it, and neither, she wrote, is she.

Please write the talk. It should be about five minutes spoken. The audience is mixed: on one side, the five players, two of them her former students, who will catch every Schopenhauerian turn of phrase and have opinions about it; on the other, family members who never read a page of him, some of whom are quietly furious that the instructions bar them from the comforts they wanted, and who need to leave the room feeling something other than lectured. So the metaphysics has to do real work rather than decorate: it should shape what I'm allowed to say, give the audience the one or two ideas they need in plain terms, and earn the consolation it offers — the claim being not that she is still present, but that the Quintet's last movement demonstrates, in Schopenhauer's sense, that an individual life's ending is internal to a larger willing that was never about that individual to begin with, and that this is the honest form of the comfort the others wanted. If you can, work in one moment for the players specifically — something keyed to how the two cellos pass the bass line between them, which she loved — and close with something I can say while they're still tuning, before the piece quietly takes over the job the speech was doing badly. Please don't use any direct quotation from Schopenhauer; paraphrase him in my aunt's plain register, as she always did.
