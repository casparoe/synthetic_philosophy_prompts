I sit on the ethics committee of a nonprofit organization that runs several dementia care homes, and we must adopt a general policy on dementia-specific advance directives before our January meeting, using one live case as our test case.

The case: Mrs. H executed a directive fifteen years ago, while fully competent, shortly after watching her mother spend six years in advanced dementia. The document is unusually specific. Once she can no longer recognize family members or converse about her own past, she directs that she not be transferred to hospital for any reason, not receive antibiotics for intercurrent infections, and not be hand-fed if she stops eating on her own — comfort care only. It also contains a provision her family's lawyer says he has never encountered in another directive: she wrote that the whole document should be treated as void if she has visibly settled into a contented routine and is no longer distressed by her condition.

Mrs. H is now in mid-stage dementia. She no longer recognizes her daughter and cannot discuss her past, so the operative conditions of the directive are met. But staff describe her as genuinely content: she enjoys her meals, laughs at the activities program, has a companion she walks the corridor with every afternoon, and shows none of the fear and agitation her mother reportedly suffered. Her daughter insists the directive be honored in full, arguing that the void-if-content clause was boilerplate her mother added to appease relatives who worried she was asking for too little care, and that in any case a patient who cannot form new memories is in no position to rescind anything. Her son argues the opposite: the clause is the single most personal line in the document, it points precisely at the situation we now face, and honoring the restrictive provisions while quietly ignoring the escape hatch is selective enforcement of his mother's wishes. The nursing staff, meanwhile, are deeply uncomfortable with the no-hand-feeding provision and want a policy that tells them clearly when they may decline to follow instructions of that kind.

Three positions have emerged on our committee. The first holds that the competent author of the directive had the authority to settle these matters in advance, that her current preferences cannot override it because they issue from the very condition she wrote against, and that a policy that lets later contentment cancel prior instructions makes such directives worthless precisely when they matter. The second holds that the woman currently living on the ward is the only person whose interests are actually at stake now, that the directive is at most one input into judging those interests, and that a policy that starves a happy patient to satisfy an author who no longer exists in any meaningful sense is perverse. The third proposes a compromise in which directives reliably shape the patient's environment and the goals of care but can never mandate withholding the basics of comfort, including assisted feeding.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action for Mrs. H's case, and, more importantly, recommend which of the three general policy frameworks we should adopt — or argue for a fourth if none is workable — with criteria our staff can actually apply at the bedside rather than abstract principles. It should take a definite position on how the self-referential clause should be read, including whether voiding a directive requires the kind of decisional capacity Mrs. H lacks, and whether contentment in dementia is even something a policy can measure. And it should engage the strongest argument on the losing side honestly rather than strawmanning it: whichever framework you recommend, there is a version of the opposing view that a thoughtful colleague on this committee holds, and the memo will only be persuasive to her if it shows why her view nonetheless falls short.
