Conservation law names its unit of protection at the level of the species: the Endangered Species Act, the IUCN Red List, and CITES all attach legal consequences to whether a population counts as a species, and in some cases to subunits below that (the Act's "distinct population segments," for instance). Yet biologists actively disagree about what makes a group a species at all — interbreeding, diagnosability, monophyly, ecological role — and taxonomic revisions routinely split one widespread species into several or lump several into one. Because vulnerability is assessed per named unit, a purely taxonomic decision, with not a single animal born or killed, can move a population from least concern to endangered overnight, changing what land can be developed and what trade is banned. What does this legal practice presuppose about the species concept? Does it require that species be real, mind-independent entities, and if so of which kind — or can it remain neutral among competing concepts, treating species boundaries as conventional and the choice of which populations to name as itself a policy decision? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely tolerates, and say what a legal regime would have to look like if it took seriously the possibility that there is no fact of the matter about species boundaries — for instance by protecting populations or lineages directly rather than the units taxonomists happen to name.
