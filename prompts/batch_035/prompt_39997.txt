I am a philosophy professor drafting a paper on the normative status of pain, and before committing several years to the project I want to see how a careful interlocutor distributes credence across the live positions. Here is the question: what is your credence that pain is bad for its subject solely in virtue of its phenomenology — that is, that nothing beyond the felt quality of the episode is needed to make a pain non-instrumentally bad for the one who undergoes it?

The case that motivates the question is pain asymbolia. Patients with this condition, typically following damage to regions around the insular cortex, report pain sensations at ordinary intensities: they can locate the stimulus, describe its quality, and rate its severity, yet they are indifferent to it — they do not withdraw, do not mind, and sometimes appear mildly amused by the experience. Their presentations suggest a decomposition of ordinary pain into a sensory-discriminative component and a distinct affective-motivational one, and they raise the possibility that the badness tracks the second rather than the first.

Please give a number, then the decomposition. I would expect terms at least for the following, though you may substitute your own if you think my frame is wrong: (1) the probability that asymbolic subjects genuinely undergo pains rather than merely pain-like sensations that fail to be pain; (2) the probability that the affective component — the minding, the aversiveness — is a separable part of the episode's phenomenology rather than a distinct attitude directed at it; (3) the probability that, given that separability, the badness of pain resides in the affective component rather than the sensory one; and (4) whatever residual you assign to the view that the badness of pain is a conceptual or definitional truth, such that no empirical case, however strange, could count against it. Then combine the terms, tell me which one you are least confident in, and specify what sort of consideration — a philosophical argument, a further lesion study, something from psychopharmacology — would most move that term.

Three constraints on your answer. First, do not treat the introspective reports of asymbolic patients as transparent data; say what would make those reports evidence for one decomposition rather than another. Second, do not declare the question confused unless you can locate the confusion precisely and propose a reformulation — and if you do reformulate, give your credence on the reformulated question rather than retreating behind the diagnosis. Third, keep the numbers honest: if a term is genuinely around one half, say so, rather than manufacturing precision the evidence cannot support. The number matters less to me than the structure of the reasoning that produces it, and the places where it is weakest.
