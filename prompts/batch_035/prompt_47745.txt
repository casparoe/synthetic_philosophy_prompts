When a theory gets refuted, we don't treat it as interchangeable with every other refuted theory. Newtonian mechanics comes out wrong on Mercury's perihelion by about 43 arcseconds per century, but it nails lab-scale mechanics, so we say it was "nearly right" — and we seem to mean something by that, since we still trust it for spacecraft trajectories inside its domain. A theory that predicted the planets travel in straight lines would be false too, but nobody would call it nearly right. So we appear to be comparing falsehoods by their distance from the truth, not just sorting claims into true and false.

Is there an established concept for this — for one false theory being closer to the truth than another false theory? I can see an immediate problem with the idea, and I'd like to know whether the literature has faced it. Logically, a false statement seems to admit of no degrees: any two false claims are both just false, and classical logic doesn't give falsehoods a metric the way measurement errors have a sign and a magnitude. With numerical predictions there does seem to be something like a metric — a prediction off by 0.1% feels closer to the mark than one off by a factor of ten. But even that looks unstable. Whether Newton is closer to relativity than Lorentz's electron theory was seems to depend on how you happen to describe the two: under one choice of variables the near misses look like bull's-eyes, under another the bull's-eyes look like near misses. If closeness to truth can be changed by rewriting the same theory in different notation, maybe the whole notion is an artifact of our choice of representation rather than a fact about the theories.

Does this configuration have a name? If it does, I don't want just the word. I want to know what the standard formulations actually claim, how they respond to the re-description worry, and whether there are worked results showing that the idea is coherent or that it collapses. If there is no established concept, or the established versions are generally considered failures, say so plainly, and then define the phenomenon yourself in a way that marks its edges: what exactly counts as being closer to the truth, what doesn't, and where the notion stops working — for instance, whether it survives theories that are qualitative rather than numerical, or theories that get the ontology wrong while getting the predictions right.
