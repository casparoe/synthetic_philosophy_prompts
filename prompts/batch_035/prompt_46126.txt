My book club read a popular work on behavioral economics and public policy, and afterward the friend who led the session summarized its thesis for me as follows: 'The central insight is that there is no such thing as a neutral environment. Every way of arranging choices — the order of items in a cafeteria line, the default on a pension form — already pushes people in some direction, so the question is never whether to influence people, only how. The authors' answer is libertarian paternalism: steer people toward the options they would have chosen if they were thinking clearly, but without forbidding anything or changing incentives, only by changing how the choices are presented. Because the person remains perfectly free to go the other way, this isn't paternalism in the old-fashioned sense at all. And because the steering aims only at the chooser's own good rather than at society's, it can't be manipulation. Some critics said that deliberately exploiting people's cognitive biases to influence them is manipulative regardless of intent, but the authors disposed of this: a nudge is legitimate if the person nudged would endorse it on reflection, and most nudges pass that test, so the manipulation worry has been answered.' I have two problems with this, and I'd appreciate a careful audit, since my training is in physics, not philosophy.

First, I want to know where the summary is faithful and where it isn't. It's presented as a report of a position, and some of it has the shape of a defender's wishful thinking — in particular the move from 'you remain free to choose otherwise' to 'this isn't paternalism,' which sounds to me like it makes the word 'paternalism' do no work at all, and I'd assumed the authors deliberately kept the word rather than apologized for it. Similarly, the claim that the steering aims at the chooser's own good 'rather than society's' may be true, but I don't see how it's supposed to connect to 'so it can't be manipulation' — that looks like a non sequitur rather than an argument, unless some premise has been dropped. And I'd like to know whether 'the options they would have chosen if they were thinking clearly' is actually the authors' criterion or the friend's gloss on it, because those two things come apart in ways that matter.

Second, there's an argumentative move I want examined on its own terms, because I find myself half-seduced by it. The impossibility-of-neutrality claim — that every arrangement of a choice environment pushes in some direction, so the only question is how to push — strikes me the way an old colleague's argument for a detector baseline once did: the fact that there's no privileged zero setting doesn't mean every nonzero setting is on a par, and it certainly doesn't mean that someone who deliberately turns the dial is doing the same thing as someone who leaves it where it drifted. My instinct, after a career spent worrying about what counts as a controlled experiment, is that 'there is no neutral default' conflates influences nobody chose with influences somebody designed, and that the whole case for the view may quietly depend on that conflation. But I also suspect a defender has a reply to this, and I'd like to hear what it is before I trust my instinct.

Please go claim by claim through the summary: which parts a defender of this research program would accept without discomfort, which are simplifications of that sort defenders tolerate, and which misrepresent the position or overstate how settled the debates are. Then tell me whether, once the summary is corrected, the impossibility-of-neutrality argument still supports the program — or whether, as I half suspect, it supports much less than my friend took it to.
