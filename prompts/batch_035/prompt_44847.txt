Okay so context: my friend and I had a three-hour argument last night about the paradox of deontology — the thing where deontologists insist rights can't be traded off, but then end up willing to let five people die rather than violate one person's rights, which looks self-undermining if the whole reason rights matter is that people matter. I kept hammering the standard line ("so youd rather have MORE of the bad thing you say is bad??") and she kept saying I was missing the point of what constraints are for. By the end neither of us had moved, but I walked away with a thought I want you to actually chase down.

Here's the thought. Every version of the paradox I've ever seen runs in one direction: the deontologist comes out looking cold or reckless, keeping their hands clean while a pile of bad outcomes stacks up, and the utilitarian gets to be the humane one. I want a case that runs the OTHER way. Build me a scenario where the deontologist's constraints end up protecting people better than the utilitarian's own math does — where the person with agent-relative constraints is, by the utilitarian's own lights over the long run, the one whose dispositions you'd want around, and it's the act-utilitarian's clean, reasonable-looking calculation that goes wrong.

Constraints, and I mean these:

- realistic setting — a workplace, a clinic, a housing office, whatever, no trolleys, no ticking bombs, no surgery-for-organs
- the scenario itself should be one paragraph
- no cheating: you can't get the reversal by making the utilitarian stupid or by fudging the numbers, and you can't just stipulate "and then things went badly." The utilitarian has to do the reasonable thing for genuinely utilitarian reasons, and the worse outcome has to fall out of the structure of the case, not out of a contrived coincidence
- same deal on the other side: the deontologist can't be following some obviously dumb rule that happens to work out — the constraint that saves the day has to be one a serious deontologist would actually endorse

After the scenario, walk me through it: what the act-utilitarian calculates and why it's the reasonable move, what the deontologist refuses and on exactly what grounds, and which structural feature of the case is doing the work in flipping the usual direction (my guess is it's something about iterated decisions, or information the actors cant actually get, but you tell me). Then tell me what happens if you remove that feature — does it collapse back into a standard paradox-of-deontology case where the deontologist looks bad again? And finish with the strongest version of "this case is rigged" that a smart utilitarian could run, plus whether the deontologist has a decent reply.

For what it's worth, here's what I think the case is for, so you know what counts as cheating: I suspect the paradox only stings if you demand that constraints justify themselves outcome-by-outcome, and if a case like this one works, maybe it shows they dont have to — that the right comparison is between whole agents with whole dispositions, not between single acts. If you think that framing is wrong, say so.
