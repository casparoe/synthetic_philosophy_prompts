The exam question was: Biologists have never agreed on a single definition of "species." Explain why, discussing at least two species concepts and the main problems each faces. Then say whether the disagreement is a substantive empirical dispute or merely a choice of convention, and defend your answer.

The student's answer:

"The main reason biologists can't agree is that evolution is gradual, so any line drawn between populations that are mid-speciation will be somewhat arbitrary. This isn't just our ignorance — the line often isn't there in nature to be found.

The oldest concept is the morphological one — group organisms by appearance — which is what Darwin used since genetics hadn't been invented yet. It's still what we mostly use for fossils. Its problems are convergent evolution and polymorphism: two very different-looking individuals can belong to the same species, and near-identical ones can belong to different species.

Mayr's biological species concept (1942) fixed this by defining a species as a group of actually interbreeding populations, reproductively isolated from other such groups. Since all members of a species must be exchanging genes, it works well for sexual organisms, and reproductive isolation gives an objective test rather than a judgment call. Its problems: it can't be applied to fossils, since we can't observe their mating behavior; it can't be applied to asexual organisms at all; and ring species like the herring gull complex break it, because neighboring populations interbreed while the two ends do not — so the ends are two species and also one species, a contradiction.

The phylogenetic species concept, which Mayr proposed later in his career, defines a species as the smallest diagnosable cluster of organisms that share a common ancestor. It works for asexuals and fossils, but it carves much finer than anyone wants — every slightly differentiated population gets called its own species, multiplying the number of recognized species enormously.

Since these concepts group the very same organisms differently, I conclude that species are not real lineages in nature at all, but conventional groupings we choose for convenience, like constellations. This also explains why the dispute has lasted so long: there is nothing in nature to discover, so the whole disagreement is purely a matter of choosing a convention."
