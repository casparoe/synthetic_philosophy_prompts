ok so me and my friend keep getting into this argument about why people resist change, and I want to settle it with data, sort of. I run a tiny blog (like 40 readers, mostly other students at my school) and I want to post a "what kind of traditionalist are you actually?" quiz.

Here's the thing I noticed: people give totally different reasons for keeping things the way they are, and they all sound identical until you push on them. Like, Burke (we read about him in history when we did the French Revolution) thinks long-standing institutions carry the accumulated wisdom of generations, so you defer to them basically by default. Chesterton's fence is different — it just says don't remove a condition you don't understand, go find out what it's for first, and once you know, you're allowed to remove it if the reason is bad. And then some people just like things the way they are and have no principle at all, and some people (Hayek-ish?) think society is too complicated for anyone to ever fully understand, so your caution should scale with how complicated the thing is.

So the quiz has to sort people by WHY they keep things, not by which label they like. Can you write me 8 questions, each one a concrete case with 2-3 answer options, plus a scoring key at the end mapping answer patterns onto these positions:

1. Burkean proper — prescription: keep the institution even once the original reason is known and dead. The wedge I care most about is a case like: a fence went up to keep sheep off a road, the sheep have been gone for a century, everyone knows this, and someone still says leave it alone. That answer is what seperates Burke from Chesterton, so the quiz MUST include some version of that case (doesn't have to be a literal fence).
2. Chesterton's fence only — cautious until the reason is known, then decide on the merits.
3. Spontaneous-order caution — you can never fully know, so the more complex the system, the more caution.
4. No principle, just liking the status quo.
5. Straight reformist — keeps things only when there's a known, still-valid reason.

Also: if the quiz can't cleanly seperate any two of these positions, flag that instead of pretending it can (I'm guessing 1 and 3 will bleed into each other?). And no question should just ask "do you respect tradition" — everyone would say yes to that and it tells you nothing. The questions should be cases where the positions actually come apart, stuff like: a rule where the original reason is known and obsolete vs. one where nobody remembers it; something old and bad vs. something new and bad; a rule that's 5 years old vs. one that's 500 years old (does how long it's been around matter, and to which position?); a ritual where even the participants have forgotten the point of it.

Keep the language simple enough that my classmates answer honestly instead of trying to look smart. And if someones answer pattern doesn't fit any position, tell me what to do with them — I bet there's alot of those people.
