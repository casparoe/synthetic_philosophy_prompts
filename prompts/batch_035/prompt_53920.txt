I teach a philosophy of science module for second-years — 16 students, one 90-minute seminar. We've just covered Popper and Kuhn, and next week's session is on the demarcation problem. Design an in-class game where the students run competing "research programmes" that I can actually run with paper, dice and a whiteboard — no software, nothing to print beyond what I can scribble during a coffee break.

The core mechanic I want: each team adopts a theory about a made-up world I invent for the session (something like a fake field of "comet herding" or made-up particle physics, so nobody can lean on real physics knowledge). Teams make risky predictions, I then reveal "observations" from a shuffled stack of cards I've prepared, and teams score points for boldness when they're right and lose points when they're wrong — so the incentive structure makes falsificationism feel like a live strategy rather than a doctrine they've been told about.

But bake in three complications: (1) each team gets a couple of "auxiliary hypothesis" cards they can play *after* a failed prediction to retroactively absorb the hit, at some cost to their score or their future boldness budget; (2) one team is secretly assigned an unfalsifiable theory that can never actually lose points no matter what happens, and another team gets a theory that makes razor-sharp falsifiable predictions but is junk — and the students shouldn't be told which is which; (3) at some point I want to rig the observation deck so that one bold, well-corroborated programme goes down in flames through pure bad luck, while a degenerating programme that's been burning auxiliary cards all session limps to victory.

Then give me a debrief script that maps specific moments from the game onto falsificationism proper — ad hoc rescue, the asymmetry between confirmation and refutation, what "risky prediction" is even measuring — and onto Lakatos's progressive vs degenerating programmes. Draw the analysis out of what the students themselves did rather than lecturing at them, and I'd like suggested questions that get the team with the unfalsifiable theory to articulate, in their own words, why never losing felt hollow.

Also be honest about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm guessing one clever student will refuse to play an auxiliary they know is ad hoc, someone will sulk about the rigged deck, and a team may just always make timid predictions and coast — and tell me which bit of the real demarcation debate the game structurally can't capture no matter how I run it.

Please write the whole thing in British English, and keep the tone casual — you're talking to a tired lecturer, not writing for a journal. And don't forget the timing breakdown for the 90 minutes, I always underestimate how long setup takes and end up rushing the debrief.
