Been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the Overcoming Bias days, and somewhere down the line I fell into a philosophy of science rabbit hole that I can't get out of: the Humeanism vs. anti-Humeanism fight about laws of nature. Quick map for context: the Humeans (Lewis's descendants) say the world is just a big mosaic of local particular facts, and the laws are nothing over and above the best summary of that mosaic — the best-system story where you trade off simplicity against strength. The anti-Humeans (Armstrong, Tooley, Dretske from the 70s, and Maudlin more recently) say laws govern: there's extra structure in the world, whether universals standing in necessitation relations or just primitive laws that push history forward, and the regularities we see are the *output* of that structure, not a raw material that gets compressed.

Here's my problem: this fight has run basically unchanged for 40+ years. Every move is standard issue and nobody budges. Anti-Humeans say a summary can't explain what it's a summary of — pointing out that all Fs are Gs doesn't explain why Fs are Gs, it just restates the mystery at lower resolution. Humeans reply that the governing-laws story is embarrassed by the old inference problem (the necessitation relation guarantees the regularity only if it's a law that the relation holds, which looks circular), and that positing invisible governors is ontology with no epistemology — what observation would ever detect them? Plus the "map is not the territory" line cuts both ways: the anti-Humean accuses the Humean of confusing the best compression of the territory with the territory itself, and honestly I don't know how to answer that.

I'll cop to having a horse in this race. Best-system analysis smells exactly like the stuff we say about Solomonoff induction and minimum description length, so my prior is that Humeanism is just "the mathematically honest version" and anti-Humeans are refusing to accept that explanation bottoms out in compression. But two things unsettle me. First, Maudlin's bit about what happens if the laws change partway through a world's history: the primitivist can just say the laws changed, while the best-system view seems forced to say the whole history always had one weird frankenstein system, which sounds wrong even to me. Second, the Humeans can't even agree among themselves — the variants keep multiplying, and Ned Hall's complaint that the view is really a loose package of choices always struck me as damning.

So here's what I actually want, and it's not a verdict: a diagnosis. Is this a substantive metaphysical disagreement, a verbal one about the word "law," or two camps answering different questions under one heading (something like: Humeans are doing epistemology of theory choice, anti-Humeans are doing metaphysics of explanation, and each side refuses to accept the other's project as the real one)? My working hypothesis is that it bottoms out in dueling modal intuitions — the whole dispute turns on whether a perfect mosaic-duplicate could differ in its laws, and neither side can point to an actual case — but I'd like that hypothesis stress-tested rather than nodded at. And whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would count as *progress* here. A distinction, a thought experiment both sides are forced to respect, some result about the formal structure of the two views? Or is this a case where the impasse is itself the finding, and the honest answer is that the disagreement has no empirical content and never will?
