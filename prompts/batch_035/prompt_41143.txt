I'm a third-year student in a philosophy of mathematics seminar, currently writing a term paper on whether the classical puzzles about actually infinite collections support finitism about the concrete world. Rather than hedge my way through the paper, I'd like to arrive at an explicit credence, and I want to see how someone else would build the number up and where its weak points are. Here is the proposition, stated as precisely as I can: it is metaphysically possible for there to exist a concrete, spatiotemporally located collection with actually (not potentially) infinitely many members — the sort of thing Hilbert's Hotel is meant to model.

Please give me your credence in that proposition, and decompose it into at least the following sub-questions, each with a rough probability and one line of justification:

1. That a scenario's formal consistency — say, its consistency with standard set theory — provides any evidence at all for its metaphysical possibility, as opposed to merely showing where our axioms are silent.
2. That the standard intuited absurdities of the hotel, such as that one can remove infinitely many guests (all those in even-numbered rooms) and be left with exactly as many guests as before, are genuine markers of impossibility rather than artifacts of intuitions formed for finite collections.
3. That modal intuition — our sense of what we can coherently conceive — is the right kind of evidence in this domain at all, given that it has led us astray elsewhere (e.g., with non-Euclidean geometry or continuous space).
4. That finitist arguments that target the actual infinite in the concrete world can succeed without also impugning the mathematical infinite that nearly everyone wants to keep.

There is one further wrinkle from our seminar that I'd like you to fold in as an additional term. We discussed a variant in which the hotel's manager tries to assign each arriving guest a room number by drawing it from a uniform lottery over the natural numbers — every room equally likely. It is provable that no such lottery exists: any countably additive probability distribution over the naturals must give some rooms higher probability than others, or none at all any positive probability. My question about this variant is whether the impossibility of the lottery tells against the possibility of the hotel itself (perhaps because a fully specified hotel ought to permit such random selection methods), or whether it is an independent fact about probability measures with no bearing on the metaphysics of the infinite collection. Please give a probability to the claim that this lottery consideration, properly understood, provides genuine evidence against the hotel's possibility, and explain how much it moves your overall number.

After the decomposition, combine the terms into your final credence, tell me which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or consideration would move that sub-question the most — for instance, whether a principled account of which intuitions to trust in infinitary contexts would change things more than any further formal result. I'd rather have honest, arguable numbers with visible weak points than a confident but unexplained figure.
