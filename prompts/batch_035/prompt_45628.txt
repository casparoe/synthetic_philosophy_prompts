My partner and I recently learned, through expanded carrier screening, that we are both carriers of the same recessive condition — serious, late-onset, and compatible with a good though shortened life. We now face a concrete choice: conceive naturally, accepting roughly a one-in-four chance that our child will express the condition, or pursue IVF with preimplantation genetic testing, which would give us a child free of the condition but, on anyone's account of embryonic development, a numerically different child. Reading around the issue, I ran into the nonidentity problem, and I have noticed that the standard presentations — Parfit's depletion case, the teenage mother, the risk-of-defect scenarios — do not cleanly settle our situation. Before we decide, I want to be clear about what I should be demanding of any answer to this problem, because the practical advice we receive will only be as good as the theory behind it.

Please write the desiderata: what an adequate treatment of the nonidentity problem must deliver, with the case or consideration motivating each requirement. I would particularly like you to address the following, though I do not want the list limited to them: which cases the treatment must get right (including, importantly, a probabilistic version like ours, where identity is fixed regardless of outcome and only the risk attaches to the choice — I suspect this behaves differently from the textbook cases, and a good theory must say whether it does); which distinctions it must respect — for instance, between being harmed and being wronged, between making a person worse off and making the world worse, and between the claim that an action was wrong and the claim that a particular person has a complaint against it; and whether the theory owes us a verdict that is action-guiding rather than merely classificatory, since some of us have a decision to make within the month.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for a theory that could guide a real decision like ours and which are, in your judgment, preferences of particular camps rather than genuine demands. Then tell me where the genuine tension lies: in particular, whether the person-affecting intuition — that morality is fundamentally about making people worse off — can be held alongside the conviction that identity-affecting choices (depletion, or conceiving when one could have avoided the risk) can be wrongs without being harms to any identifiable person. If some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly and say which requirement you would drop. Finally, in a few sentences each rather than a full scorecard, indicate how the familiar escape routes — harm-free wrongings, impersonal principles about the quality of lives, and views that deny identity is ever a moral obstacle in these cases — do against the list. Keep the weight on the requirements themselves; the camps and their signatures are the part I can look up, but only the specification will tell me whether the answer we eventually act on is any good.
