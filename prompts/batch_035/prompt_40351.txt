Please argue for a revisionist conclusion about Dutch book arguments: that they establish less than is typically claimed. The standard presentation says that an agent whose degrees of belief violate the probability axioms is irrational, because a bookie can construct a set of bets, each of which the agent regards as fair, that guarantees the agent a net loss. I'd like you to argue that the argument, so stated, shows only that such an agent is *exploitable in principle*, not that she is irrational, for at least these two reasons: (i) the exploitation requires a bookie who knows the agent's credences well enough to price each bet exactly at the odds the agent considers fair, and who is positioned to offer precisely the required package — conditions that essentially never obtain outside the thought experiment itself; and (ii) being vulnerable to a loss one would knowingly reject, were one aware of the trap, is not obviously a failure of rationality but rather a fact about one's informational disadvantages relative to an adversary. In developing the argument, please also say what you take the strongest response from a defender of the standard reading to be — particularly whether the agent's own willingness to accept each bet as fair does enough work on its own, without any actual bookie, to ground a charge of inconsistency. Throughout, indicate how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the point in your argument that you are least sure of.
