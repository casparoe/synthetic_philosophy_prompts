my reading group is wrapping up a semester on Borges and the final text was his essay "A New Refutation of Time," which broke my brain a little if i'm honest. I want to close our last session with a poem, and I want it to actually follow the essay's argument instead of just name-dropping it — a couple of people in the group have read the secondary literature and will notice if I get the logic out of order. The essay, roughly, does this: he takes the idealist move of Berkeley and Hume (if there's no matter and no self, just perceptions passing), and runs it twice — first to show that every mind lives in its own private sequence, so there can't be one shared river of time that we're all in together, and then to show that no moment can ever repeat, since even a second occurrence of the same event would already be a new moment. And then, in the postscript, he cheerfully undercuts the whole thing: he admits the refutation works as a trick of philosophy but that he can't actually live it, because time is the stuff he's made of — he can't step outside the river he *is*.

So here's what I'm asking for: a poem in exactly five numbered stanzas, no fixed meter or rhyme, where each stanza does exactly one job, in this order — stanza 1: dissolve the world into perceptions (the Berkeley/Hume move, no dreamer, only the dreaming); stanza 2: private times, each life its own sequence, no shared river; stanza 3: the unrepeatable moment — even if everything came back, the coming-back itself would be new, so circles turn out to be straight lines in disguise; stanza 4: the collapse, that if the refutation works it also dissolves the very Borges who wrote it, the man who wanted to be told he'd outlived himself; and stanza 5: the postscript's shrug, the admission that it's a beautiful artifice he cannot inhabit. Keep the argument straight even where the language goes dreamy — the dreaminess should ride on top of the logic, not replace it. Someone who knows the essay cold should be able to tell which stanza is doing which step, and someone who's never read it should still find it a good poem.
