I'm TA-ing a bargaining unit in an intermediate game theory course, and the professor wants to lead with "what should we demand of a bargaining solution?" before showing the students any of the actual solutions. So I need to write up a proper list of desiderata: what any adequate two-person bargaining solution has to deliver, which simple cases it has to get right, which axioms are load-bearing and which are just prejudices of one camp. The students already know Nash's solution will show up, and I know Kalai-Smorodinsky will be the second one presented as the alternative, but the list itself is supposed to be derived before either is named, so it can't just be reverse-engineered to crown a winner. Still, it has to be honest enough that the real tradeoffs show up.

The thing I keep running into, and what I'd most want the writeup to confront: some of the natural requirements seem to genuinely conflict, and I'd like that stated plainly rather than papered over. Like, on one hand there's the idea that deleting unchosen alternatives from the feasible set shouldn't change the outcome (if you'd never pick them anyway, why should their absence matter), but on the other hand there's monotonicity of some form -- if one player's best possible outcome gets better while everything else stays put, that player shouldn't end up worse off, which seems close to undeniable as a fairness condition. My understanding is you basically can't have both, and that the whole Nash vs. Kalai-Smorodinsky fight lives in that gap. So the writeup should say which subsets of the desiderata are jointly unsatisfiable, if any are, and make clear why -- that's the part the students will actually remember. Also worth covering: what role the disagreement point should play, whether solutions should be invariant to how we scale or shift each player's utility (and whether that invariance is a genuine insight or an artifact of taking utility functions too seriously), and symmetry, plus whether symmetry is doing anything once you allow asymmetric problems.

For seeding, give a couple of concrete toy cases the list has to handle -- simple ones a student can verify by hand, like two players splitting a surplus where the feasible set is a line segment, or a case where enlarging one player's utopia point changes who deserves more of the pie. Then, briefly at the end, it's fine to say how Nash and Kalai-Smorodinsky each do against the list -- the professor will do the full scorecard in lecture, so keep it short. Don't worry about citing papers, I have the references. And please don't just list axiom names and move on -- each requirement needs the case or intuition that motivates it, otherwise the students will learn the names and nothing else. Also flag which requirements you'd actually defend as non-negotiable versus which you'd drop under pressure, since I suspect half the standard axioms wouldn't survive a serious argument.
