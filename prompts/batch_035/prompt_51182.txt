I've been collecting my gut reactions to sunk-cost situations and I want to know if there's one principle behind them, because right now they look like a mess. Here are the cases with my verdicts:

Reading a book I'm not enjoying, 40 pages from the end, and finishing it anyway becuase I've already read 300: fine. Waiting another 25 minutes at a bus stop instead of just walking the 15 minutes it takes, on the grounds that I've already stood there 25 minutes: not fine. A friend staying in a PhD program he hates at year six because quitting would "waste" the six years: not fine, honestly seems crazy. A city finishing a half-built bridge that's way over budget, where the remaining cost is still less than the benefit of having the bridge: fine — but note it feels like nobody is honoring anything there, they're just comparing remaining costs to remaining benefits, so maybe the sunk cost isn't doing any work in that case. My uncle refusing to sell a money-losing shop because of the 30 years he's poured into it: not fine. And the one I keep flipping on: I'll drop a bad TV series three episodes in with zero guilt, but quitting a bad video game 40 hours in feels like a betrayal of some kind, even when the game is worse than the series was.

One more thing that might or might not be related: keeping a promise to a friend to help her move feels obviously fine, and citing the promise feels fine, but there's something that smells off about citing "I already promised" as the reason — like the reason is pointing backwards instead of forwards. Is that the same phenomenon, or am I mushing two things together? I have a half-formed suspicion that the answer here is going to be that honoring sunk costs is only rational when it happens to line up with a forward-looking comparison, and that all my "fine" cases are really forward-looking cases in disguise. If that's your conclusion, say it plainly — but then tell me what happens to my book case, since finishing those 40 pages is, looked at forward, a net loss for me, surely?

Give me exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro or conclusion. Across the five, cover: the principle itself, stated precisely; what it says about the case I keep flipping on; which of my verdicts, if any, it cant accommodate and has to overturn; and the last item should be you flagging the point you're least confident about, along with an honest statement of how confident you are in the whole thing.
