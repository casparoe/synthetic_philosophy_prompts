Longtime lurker here, our rationality meetup is doing a session on moral luck (Nagel/Williams, the classic drunk driver cases) and everyone has to post their position beforehand so the rest can tear into it live. Here's mine, and honestly I can't tell anymore whether it's a stable position or just vibes I've accumulated from a decade of reading this corner of the internet:

(1) I'm a consequentialist about how good an act was: it's fixed by what actually happened. The driver who drove drunk and killed a child performed a worse act than his twin who drove drunk and got lucky. No luck-free act appraisal for me, that way madness lies.

(2) Blameworthiness is different. The two drivers are equally blaimeworthy — nothing that happened after they decided, that they didn't control, can change how blameworthy they are. This one seems obvious to me, though plenty of things that seemed obvious to me in 2013 didn't survive.

(3) Decisions are judged ex ante. Whether a decision was rational depends on the expected utility at the time, full stop. Evaluating decisions by how they turned out is outcome bias, a documented bias, and basically my whole shtick is correcting for it in myself and calling it out in others. Poker-player view: you don't get credit for a bad call that happens to win.

(4) Praise and blame are tools. The practice of holding people responsible is justified entirely by its effects on behaviour — it's incentive design all the way down, and there's no extra fact about desert floating free of that which could constrain how we should distribute blame.

(5) I think the law is roughly right that attempted murder counts for less than completed murder, and that causing death by dangerous driving is punished way more than dangerous driving simpliciter. And I half-remember a law-and-econ argument I quite liked at the time: if you only prosecute the gamblers whose risk actually cashes out in harm, you can set the penalty very high with fewer prosecutions and thin enforcement, so you buy the same deterrence cheaper. Punish the unlucky few, but punish them hard.

(6) Regret is only appropriate when the decision was wrong given what I knew at the time. If I made the right call ex ante and it went badly, regret is a bug in me, not a reason.

So: audit time. Which of these are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, and for the real tensions what's the cheapest fix and what does the fix cost me elsewhere in the list? Rank by severity please rather than handing me a flat list of everything anyone could conceivably object to, and don't just tell me to reread Nagel — I want to know where *I'm* inconsistent, not where the literature is.
