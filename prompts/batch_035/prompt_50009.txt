I've been arguing with a colleague about Bostrom's simulation argument, and we've discovered we don't really disagree about the trilemma itself — we disagree about an auxiliary premise that gets almost no attention. I want your credence on a specific proposition, decomposed into the sub-questions it depends on.

The proposition: conditional on our civilization eventually reaching a stage where running large numbers of ancestor-simulations is technologically feasible, the majority of human-type minds in existence are simulated — i.e., that the third horn fails in the "many simulations" direction rather than the "extinction or loss of interest" direction.

Here is the premise we're stuck on. Everyone treats simulated minds as if they would be fully conscious, as we are. But suppose substrate-independence is only approximately true, and that the cheapest simulations behaviorally indistinguishable from humans — the kind a resource-constrained civilization would run at scale — are low-fidelity: detailed inner modeling only when a mind is being observed (by other sims or by the simulators), and coarse or absent modeling otherwise. If the overwhelming majority of simulated humanoids were like that, the population of minds capable of noticing their own existence might be dominated by originals — or it might not, depending on how the rendering works.

Decompose your credence in the proposition into at least these terms: (1) the probability that full substrate-independence holds, so that a high-fidelity simulation of a brain is conscious if the original is; (2) the probability that civilizations running simulations at scale would find full-fidelity minds worth the marginal cost, rather than running mostly low-fidelity ones; (3) the probability that a low-fidelity, render-on-demand mind is conscious at all; and (4) whether the anthropic update from "I am having this very thought" can distinguish you from a low-fidelity sim that happens, right now, to be rendered in enough detail to have this thought — that is, whether a mind rendered only at moments of reflection belongs in your reference class exactly when the question occurs to it. Give a number and a sentence of justification for each, then the combination, then tell me which term dominates your uncertainty and what argument or evidence would move that term most. My credence in the proposition is around 0.35; my colleague's is around 0.75 — tell me which of the sub-questions you think most likely explains the gap between us.

Two ground rules. First, skip the standard trilemma exposition; I know it. Second, please distinguish throughout between what is effectively settled among philosophers who work on anthropic reasoning and consciousness, and what is genuinely contested. In particular, don't present any one anthropic principle — self-indication, self-sampling, or something else — as the consensus view; as I understand it, the choice among them is precisely where the specialists disagree, and your final number should be honest about how much it swings on that unresolved choice.
