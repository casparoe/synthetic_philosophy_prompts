I'm taking a graduate seminar on Bayesian epistemology, and our professor has a standing bit he calls rulings on disagreement: he sketches a case in which two people hold different credences about the same question, and rules on whether each of them may rationally stand pat once the disagreement is out in the open, or whether at least one of them is required to move. His track record so far: two colleagues watch the same slow-motion replay of a photo finish and still disagree about which horse won — must move, one of them is misperceiving; two econometricians with the same minimum-wage studies on their desks disagree about the size of the employment effect — may stand pat; two radiographers with identical training read the same scan and come down differently — must move; two historians working from the same archive disagree about whether a statesman's protestations of peace were sincere — may stand pat; two forecasters who read the same polling averages give different probabilities for the election — may stand pat; two doctors considering the same test result disagree because they trust the lab's false-positive rate differently — may stand pat.

Next week he brings a new case, and I would like to walk in with a prediction. Two seismologists reviewing the same data on a fault zone spent a week comparing notes, went through each other's models line by line, and now agree completely about what the data would show under each hypothesis — the likelihoods, as they would put it, are settled, and each knows the other accepts them. But they came to the table with different starting credences: one has always regarded the region as quiet, the other as overdue, and each knows this about the other. They still disagree about the probability of a major earthquake within the decade, and both know that they disagree.

My working theory is that his rulings to date are consistent with more than one principle. On one of them, every disagreement he has allowed traces to differing information, or to differing readings of the same information, and would vanish once the information were fully pooled; on another, differences in starting points — in priors — may themselves carry a disagreement, and the allowed cases are simply the ones in which a prior difference was doing the work. If that is right, the two principles should come apart precisely on the new case, where the information has been pooled and shown to be common, but the priors have not. So I have three questions. First, what will he rule on the seismologists — may they stand pat, or must at least one of them move? Second, which principle is he really running, given that his rulings friendly to differing priors sit rather oddly beside his oft-repeated enthusiasm for the common prior assumption, the Harsanyi–Aumann idea that differences in rational credence ultimately trace to differences in information? And third, what further case — one he has not yet ruled on — would best settle which of the candidate principles he is actually applying?
