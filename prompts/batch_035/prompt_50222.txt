I run a student philosophy reading group at my uni — about 15 people, we meet for 90 minutes a week. A couple weeks ago we read Simone Weil's 1943 essay On the Abolition of All Political Parties, and honestly the discussion was a bit flat. Everyone kind of shrugged and said "sure, parties are bad, but she's obviously exaggerating" — nobody really felt why she insists this is a structural thing, that it's not about bad apples or dishonest people, it's that the party machine itself makes falsehood unavoidable for anyone inside it.

So I want an activity where they run into the mechanism instead of debating it on paper. Here's the shape I have in mind, but you fill in all the details:

The group has to make a real decision with actual stakes for them — something like how we pick next term's reading list, or how to split a small budget between guest speakers and books. Before anything else, I take a secret ballot on the issue, which I keep. Then I deal out party cards at random, splitting them into two parties whose affiliation has nothing to do with their real views. Each party has to arrive at a party line. Scoring rewards cohesion: the party that wins the final vote gets a big payout, but anyone who publicly concedes the other party made a good point loses their own party points. No quitting mid-game — your card is your card. Maybe a rule where the party line gets locked in early by whoever speaks first / loudest, and after that changing it costs something, so the early rando position hardens into doctrine. You figure out the exact mechanics, but the point is that everyone in that room has JUST read Weil, agrees with her, and is going to slide into party spirit anyway within half an hour — and I want the game set up so they can't help it.

For the debrief, I need you to map what happened onto her three arguments, and be concrete about which moments in the game correspond to which. To jog your memory: the party as a machine for producing collective passion — a feeling that feels like conviction but isn't any member's actual belief; the membership card as a kind of currency that only holds value while the party exists and grows, so members end up with a stake in the party's survival that has literally nothing to do with the issue at hand; and the pressure on members' minds — the one I most want them to feel, the bit where you know perfectly well the other side just said something true and you physically cannot say so out loud. That last one is where the private ballot comes back: in the debrief I want to lay the secret pre-game ballot next to the party lines people ended up defending, and let them see the gap. If you can think of a way for me to flag moments during the game where someone visibly knows better than what they're saying — a way to mark it without breaking the game — include that too.

Also please include: a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, materials list, what I as facilitator should NOT say while it's running, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce — I suspect the answer is her claim about founding, that even the initial reasons for forming a party are already corrupt, and a 90-minute game where I deal the cards at random probably can't show that part. Say so plainly if you agree, and tell me what I have to supply in the debrief to cover it.

Keep the tone casual if you write any facilitator scripts — this is a student group, not a seminar at the Sorbonne. Oh and dont make the stakes actual money, we're broke, points and bragging rights only.
