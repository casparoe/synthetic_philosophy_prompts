A friend of mine in the math department keeps shutting down my questions about weird gambling decisions with the line "if the expected value doesn't exist, there's no fact of the matter about what you should pay." Most recently this came up over a St. Petersburg-style game he called the Pasadena game (something like that): a fair coin is tossed until the first heads, and if the first heads lands on toss n, the payoff has magnitude 2^n/n dollars, but the sign alternates — with the version he wrote up, you pay the house when n is odd and the house pays you when n is even. So with probability 1/2 you're down $2, with probability 1/4 you're up $2, with probability 1/8 you're down $8/3, with probability 1/16 you're up $4, and so on, magnitudes growing almost like St. Petersburg but with the sign flipping every step. I asked whether it's okay to say the game is worth about 69 cents to whoever's side has the favorable parity, since adding the terms in the obvious order gives you the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ±ln 2 (call it 69 cents, sign depending on whether odd tosses make you pay or get paid). He said no — the expectation doesn't exist, end of discussion.

Here's what bugged me. For the ordinary St. Petersburg game he happily says "the expected value is infinite, so any finite ticket price is a bargin," but for this one he switches to "doesn't exist, so there's no answer at all." Those feel like two different registers of the same phrase, and I know just enough to suspect several distinct things "the expected value doesn't exist" could be claiming here: that the defining series diverges (it doesn't, in the natural ordering — it converges, just not absolutely); that it's only conditionally convergent, so by the rearrangement theorem you could order the outcomes to get any total you like, making the 69 cents an artifact of bookkeeping; that the partial sums oscillate around the limit, so no truncation of the game has a stable value; or something outright normative, like "this bet can't be assigned a fair price at all," which seems like an extra step way beyond the math.

Can you lay out the different precise claims "the expected value doesn't exist" could be making for a game like this — as a list I can bring back to him — and for each one say whether it's actually true here, whether it's trivial or substantive, and, crucially, whether it supports his move from "doesn't exist" to "no fact of the matter about what you should pay"? I'd especially like to know which reading, if any, would still license something like "at a 69-cent subsidy the game is a wash, so demanding 69 cents to play is fair," versus which readings genuinely leave you with no price being more rational than any other.
