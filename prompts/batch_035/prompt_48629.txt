I run a small grants program for a mid-sized city that funds what our enabling policy calls "community sport and physical activity organizations." That phrase has never been defined, and I have two assessors who screen about fifty applications a year in roughly twenty minutes each, with any denial appealable to a council committee that wants a written rationale. The disputes are constant and embarrassing: last cycle we had a youth chess club, an esports league, a dance-fitness studio, a pigeon-racing society, a marching band, and a parkour collective, and my two assessors split on nearly all of them. One of them applies an unspoken perspiration test (if nobody breaks a sweat, it isn't sport); the other an institutionalization test (if there's a league and a governing body, it's in). Neither test is in any document, so on appeal we lose as often as we win, and the assessors resent each other.

In trying to fix this I read some Wittgenstein, and his discussion of games and family resemblance looks almost suspiciously on point: the thought that there may be no single property shared by everything we call a sport, only overlapping similarities criss-crossing across cases — some competitive, some physical, some rule-governed, some institutionalized, some equipment-based — like a thread whose strength comes from many overlapping fibers rather than one strand running the whole length. But I also gather he insisted this doesn't mean the concept has no boundaries at all, just blurred ones, and that a boundary can be drawn for a particular purpose even where none was drawn before. So here is what I want: turn family resemblance into a decision procedure my assessors can actually run on an application. Give me the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure terminates with a no; how to handle paradigm cases, borderline cases, and cases engineered to look borderline; and where it must stop and hand the decision to the elected committee because what remains is a question of values rather than of classification. It has to be usable by people with no philosophy background, and each output has to include a rationale in plain language that will survive an appeal hearing.

Then run the procedure on three real cases from our docket: a longstanding regional table-tennis league, a youth chess club applying for the third time, and an esports league that wants funding to rent a computer lab at a community center. Include at least one outcome I won't like — I'll admit I want to fund the chess club because it serves kids in a low-income neighborhood, and I do not want to fund esports — and tell me honestly whether the procedure supports or overrides those preferences, and why that is the right behavior for a rule rather than a weakness in it. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do: where family resemblance gives my assessors no real guidance at all, and how I'll know when an applicant has gamed the checklist by mimicking the surface features of a paradigm case.
