A friend in my political philosophy reading group keeps reaching for the pie picture whenever we argue about inequality: society's wealth is a pie that arrives already baked, and the whole question of distributive justice is how to slice it — who gets which piece, whether pieces may be unequal, whether fairness means equal pieces. She uses it mainly to press a point against Nozick: if the pie is just there, then talking about how people came to hold their pieces — acquisition, transfer, rectification — is changing the subject, since the pieces are whatever they are and the only live question is the shape of the division. She thinks the Wilt Chamberlain story cuts her way too: even if a million people each voluntarily hand over a bit of their slice to watch him play basketball, we still end with a new arrangement of slices that has to be evaluated on its merits, and calling the process clean doesn't tell us whether the resulting pattern is acceptable. I'm fairly confident Nozick would say the pie picture is the thing he was denying rather than a neutral background to it, but I can't reconstruct the details well enough to hold my own in the group. Please audit the analogy. Set out what in Nozick's picture of holdings is supposed to correspond to which feature of the pie — the pre-baked whole, the slicing, the slicer, the equal-slice ideal — and then say where the correspondence breaks. I'm especially interested in which of those breaks actually matter for the argument my friend is using the analogy to run, as opposed to ones that are just loose talk: the pie being fixed in size while holdings get created and destroyed seems important, and so does the absence of anyone doing the slicing, since there is no central distributor in Nozick's story; but I can't tell whether those are the load-bearing disanalogies or whether they only matter if you already accept his conclusions. Then tell me which conclusions about distributive justice the pie picture carries that Nozick would resist, which of them he could actually grant, and whether my friend's use of it — to make the entitlement theory look like it's dodging the real question — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If you think there's a better analogy for what Nozick is claiming about holdings, sketch one. I half remember Nozick suggesting somewhere that things don't arrive like manna from heaven, already stamped or not stamped with entitlements, but I may be misremembering, so don't lean on that being his; the point stands or falls on its own either way. Please write continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — with the correspondence between the pie and the holdings given in full sentences, the way you'd write a long, careful letter.
