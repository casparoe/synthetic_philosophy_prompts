I help run a reading group on decision theory and rationality at my university, about 10-12 grad students and motivated undergrads, and we meet for 90 minutes a week. In a couple of sessions we'll be discussing diachronic rationality -- money pump arguments, naive vs sophisticated vs resolute choice, whether being exploitable over time actually shows anything about your preferences being irrational in the moment. The problem is that on paper these arguments feel like cheap parlor tricks: "suppose a cunning bookie offers you A then B then C and you end up back where you started minus three dollars." Everyone nods, nobody feels it. I want them to actually get pumped, in the room, with their own choices, before we ever say the words "money pump."

Design the activity. I'm imagining something with play money (a small real-money stake is fine too, like everyone gets $5 at the start and keeps whatever's left, but I'd rather not handle actual cash for the whole group -- you tell me if the real stakes are load-bearing or not). The hard part is the diachronic bit: I don't just want them to reveal intransitive preferences in a one-off questionnaire, I want them to make a sequence of choices over the session, maybe interleaved with other stuff so the trap unfolds across time, and to only notice at the end what happened to their money and how many times they paid to go around the circle. Some of the group will have seen the standard pairwise-choice setup (turkey vs ham vs roast beef and so on), so the naive version won't land. Maybe there's a way to get at the sophisticated/resolute distinction too -- like a phase where they can see the pump coming and have to decide how to bind themselves, or whether refusing a Pareto-dominant trade to stay safe really feels like irrationality or like self-protection. You have free rein on mechanics, but please commit to one concrete design rather than giving me three options to choose from -- pick the version you'd actually run, specify rules, materials, the payoff scheme, the timeline for a 90-minute session, and what you'd have me (as facilitator) say and not say at each stage. Then the debrief: map the moments in the game onto the philosophical distinctions, and tell me honestly what the activity structurally cannot reproduce about the real argument (e.g. the infinite pump, preferences stable over time, what have you) and that I'll have to supply in discussion.

One more thing: predict what will go wrong the first time I run it -- I'm guessing half the group will figure out the trick early and start playing to expose it rather than playing their actual preferences, or people's preferences just won't be intransitive enough to pump. Tell me whether your design handles that or whether it's a dealbreaker, and take a position on it rather than hedging: if the activity only works if participants stay confused, say so, and say why you'd still run it that way.
