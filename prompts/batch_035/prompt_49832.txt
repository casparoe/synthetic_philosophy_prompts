I'm on the allocation committee of my university's effective altruism club, and my friend Dana chairs it. Every term we give away the money from our fundraiser, and after four meetings I thought I had a read on how she decides, but our agenda for next week has two proposals I genuinely can't call. We covered importance-tractability-neglectedness in my ethics of philanthropy class, so I keep trying to force her votes into that mold, and I can't quite do it.

Her track record so far:

1. Funded corporate campaigns for cage-free eggs over topping up a clean-water charity. She said the poverty stuff is fine but the giant charities already have it covered, whereas the campaigns hit a ridiculous number of animals per dollar.
2. Rejected chipping in on existential-risk research, even though she said flat out it's probably the biggest deal in the room. Her two reasons were that we'd never know whether our few thousand dollars did anything, and that the tech billionaires are already dumping money into it.
3. Funded a small study on whether farmed insects feel pain over a well-evaluated deworming program. Basically her reasoning was that if insects do suffer, it's the largest number of sufferers on the planet, and nobody else on earth will pay for the study.
4. Rejected funding our city's homelessness outreach even though she agreed it obviously works and we'd see the results ourselves. Tiny number of people helped, and the city just passed a levy for it, so our money would be buying something that was happening anyway.

Here's what's on the agenda next week:

(a) A phase-1 trial for a rare disease affecting a couple thousand people worldwide. No pharma company or foundation touches it, and our grant would cover a real chunk of the trial, with a concrete paper at the end.
(b) A campaign to get an international body to ban farming of some newly-domesticated animal. Completely unfunded, and if it worked the scale would be enormous, but it needs a couple million and several years to have any chance, and our contribution would be a rounding error.

I can fit at least two readings onto all four of her past votes. One is that she's just neglectedness-first: fund whatever nobody else is touching, and everything else is a tiebreaker at most. The other is something like: she bets on huge-scale problems, but only when she's effectively the only buyer, so crowded causes collapse to zero no matter the scale. And maybe a third where it's not neglectedness but the counterfactual impact of our specific dollars that does the work — she funds things where the world is meaningfully different because we paid, which fits the insect study (we ARE the study's funding) and the x-risk rejection (our money just dissolves into the billionaire pool).

So: how do you think she votes on (a) and (b)? Which rules actually fit all four of her past decisions, what does each one predict for the two new proposals, and where do they come apart? Give me your best guess with reasons, and then tell me if our two cases really settle anything — and if not, what proposal we'd need to add to the agenda to tell the readings apart, because I want to know whether I understand how she decides before I open my mouth at the meeting.
