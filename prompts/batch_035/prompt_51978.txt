I teach intro philosophy of science at a big state school, and every year the unit on natural kinds goes sideways the same way: students nod along to water = H2O, and then it turns out half of them think biological species are kinds in exactly the way chemical elements are, a third think any useful category counts as a "kind" by definition, and one kid is a secret essentialist about chairs. So this semester, instead of lecturing, I want to hand them a sorting instrument and let it do the arguing.

What I want you to write is a decision tree — a "which view of natural kinds do you actually hold?" instrument. Some ground rules for how to build it.

First, take on the role of a professional metaphysician of science, someone who has actually had to teach the difference between realism and conventionalism about classification to skeptical 19-year-olds. And please think before writing: start with a short brainstorm paragraph that maps out the logical space of possible combinations of answers, and build the tree from that space. Only then locate the named positions inside it. Dont do it the other way around — I dont want a list of famous views with questions bolted on afterwards.

The tree itself: roughly 8-10 nodes, each node a question about a concrete case, with two or three answer options. Every question needs to be answerable by a student who has never heard the jargon — no node should just ask "do natural kinds exist?", that's the label, not the diagnostic. The leaves should be named positions, and at a minimum these need to appear somewhere: Mill-style classical essentialism, the Putnam/Kripke microstructuralist line about chemical kinds, Boyd's homeostatic property cluster view, Dupré's promiscuous pluralism, Kuhn-style conventionalism, and a social constructionist branch (Hacking's interactive kinds can live there, or get its own leaf if you think it deserves one).

These specific cases have to show up in the nodes — they're where the positions genuinely come apart, so I want the tree to turn on them:

1. Jade. One name covering two totally unrelated minerals, jadeite and nephrite. One kind or two? And does it matter that the gemologist and the geologist give different answers?
2. Ring species, the herring gull / lesser black-backed gull Larus case. Where does one species stop and its neighbor begin, and does that kind of vagueness count against there being a fact of the matter at all?
3. Heavy water versus ordinary water. Same kind at the level we drink it, different at the isotope level. Which level is the joint — and is there only one joint?
4. Viruses. Alive, not alive, or is the question itself confused, trading on two different senses of "kind"?
5. Chairs or weeds — pick one. An everyday category that's obviously useful and obviously purpose-relative. Does usefulness count as carving at a joint?
6. Money (or race, or temperature — your call). A social kind with real causal powers that exist only because we collectively believe in them. More real than deuterium, less real, or a different question entirely?

Then a scoring key mapping patterns of answers onto leaves.

Two more deliverables, and these are the part I actually care about most, so dont bury them:

- Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't genuinely seperate. I'm guessing conventionalism versus plain nominalism will be a problem, and maybe microstructuralism versus essentialism on the heavy water node, but you tell me — and for each flagged pair, say what additional case would be needed to split them, if any case could.
- Note any region of the logical space that ends up unoccupied, and give me your honest read on why nobody's there: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored?

One last thing: my students will try to reverse-engineer any quiz they can google their way through. So the questions should read like natural curiosity about cases ("would a jeweler and a mineralogist be having a real disagreement about jade?"), not like flashcards for name-that-philosopher. If a question is only answerable if you already know the literature, cut it and find a better one.
