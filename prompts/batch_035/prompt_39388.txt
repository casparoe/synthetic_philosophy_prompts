so i'm a novelist and i'm stuck on something i'd love you to actually think through rather than give me a survey of. my current book is about a biographer writing the life of a woman who founded a school in a small town — half the town thinks she was a saint, the other half thinks she was a brilliant manipulator, and the biographer narrator keeps flip-flopping depending on which neighbor she interviewed that week. writing her i've realized i'm unconsciously bouncing between two very old ways of teaching morals through a life, and i want a proper comparison of them:

the first is the plutarch way (lowercase, whatever): the life is full of small concrete stuff — how the person handled an insult, whether they were cheap with servants, what they did when they lost an election — and the whole point is that virtue is imitable. you read it and think "i could do that, or at least i could fail at it in a recognizable way." the anecdotes are mundane on purpose.

the second is the hagiography way, and here's what interests me: it seems to work in almost the opposite direction. the life is idealized, the ugly parts get sanded off or repurposed as pre-conversion material, and often there are miracles, which are precisely the part you cannot copy. you can imitate someone's patience; you cannot imitate raising the dead. so veneration replaces imitation.

my actual questions: (1) is moral learning from a life even possible when the subject is presented as inimitable, or does the hagiographic mode get its moral effect some other way — through awe, shame, aspiration-as-feeling rather than aspiration-as-plan? (2) is there a genuine middle mode, a way of writing a life that's exemplary without being inimitable, where the exemplar stays within the reader's reach, and if so what does it look like in practice — what kinds of episodes does it include and what does it leave out? (3) the anti-case: both traditions also use lives as warnings, so where does learning-from-a-bad-life fit, does it run on the same imitation machinery or a different one?

and then, because this is for a novel after all: do you have thoughts on how a fiction writer can exploit the contrast structurally? my instinct is that the narrator's unreliability could be built out of exactly this — every chapter she writes the woman either more plutarch (small, touchable, flawed, copyable) or more hagiography (remote, perfect, sealed), and the reader slowly realizes the choice of mode tells you more about the narrator than about the woman. does that seem conceptually sound to you or am i just decorating a simple bias with fancy genealogy? push back if so.

no reading lists or citations please, i just want the conceptual work done out loud. also feel free to tell me if the distinction i'm drawing (imitable vs inimitable exemplar) isn't actually the interesting cut here — maybe the real difference is somewhere else, like who the life is for, or what counts as an event worth recording. i'd genuinely like to know.
