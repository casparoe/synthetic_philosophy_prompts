I'm a retired geophysicist who got into philosophy a couple years after retiring — podcasts and popular books mostly, my wife says I've just swapped one obsession for another. A podcast I like did an episode on truth recently, and to argue that "there are truths no one could ever know," the host gave this account of Fitch's paradox of knowability:

"Back in 1963 a logician named Frederic Fitch proved something wild: if every truth is in principle knowable — no truth forever beyond our reach — then every truth is actually already known. It's a self-referential trick: you suppose some truth is unknown, show that it can't be knowable, and the whole edifice collapses. Since it's obvious that not every truth is known — nobody knows the exact number of atoms in the universe — Fitch had shown there must be unknowable truths. This was devastating for verificationism, the view that a statement's meaning, or at least its truth, consists in what we could do to check it. After Fitch that view was finished, and basically everyone accepted that reality outruns whatever we could ever verify."

My instinct from forty years in science is that when a one-paragraph summary of a theorem feels to tidy, something got flattened on the way — but I honestly can't tell what. Could you go through the host's account piece by piece and tell me what a philosopher of logic would nod along to, what they'd wince at, and what is just wrong? A few things specifically:

1. Is the statement of the result right, and is the proof really self-referential? I know enough logic to get suspicious whenever "self-reference" gets wheeled out to make a result sound deep.
2. Is verificationism really the view the result was aimed at, and did the result actually finish it off? I half remember that the people who held something like this — semantic anti-realists, I think they're called — had ways of responding, but I might be making that up.
3. And that last flourish, that reality outruns whatever we could ever verify: does it follow from anything correct in his account, or is that the host sliding his own realism in the back door?

I'd rather know what's actually established than win an argument at dinner, so don't spare my impressions.
