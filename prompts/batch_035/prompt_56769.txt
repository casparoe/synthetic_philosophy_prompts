I write about science and ideas for a living, and lately every third pitch that lands on my desk is about echo chambers. At a conference a few months back a researcher walked me through an argument (off the record, so I'll just give you the shape of it) and it's been bugging me ever since, because it seems to prove way too much.

(1) There's a difference worth marking between an epistemic bubble and an echo chamber. A bubble is just missing information: contrary voices have been kept out of your feed, but you'd engage with them if they showed up. An echo chamber is a structure that discredits them in advance, so that when they do show up, their testimony doesn't count as evidence at all. (2) Now look at any community that has epistemic standards. My editor won't run a claim from an anonymous tipster until it's cleared our own checks. A physics department won't update on a garage inventor's perpetual motion machine until it survives the community's own tests. (3) In both cases, testimony from outside is discounted by default until it passes gates the community itself controls. (4) So healthy newsrooms and healthy sciences are echo chambers too -- the gates are just wider than a cult's. Difference in degree, not in kind. (5) But then every community with any standards whatsoever is an echo chamber, and when my editor runs a piece telling readers to escape their echo chamber, the only advice he can honestly give them is to swap one echo chamber for another one they happen to like better.

I don't believe that conclusion -- but that's a hunch, not an answer. The slide from (2)/(3) into (4) feels like its doing something sneaky with the phrase "in advance", but I can't nail it down. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? And if no step is cleanly rejectable, tell me why the hunch is wrong instead.
