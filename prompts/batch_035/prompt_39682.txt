So here's something that came out of my course this semester (it's one of those joint econ-ethics seminars on "markets and their limits," basically half price theory, half moral philosophy) and I can't stop chewing on it. We did the standard unit on price controls, and the professor drew the same diagram twice: minimum wage one day, rent control the next — identical downward-sloping demand, identical upward-sloping supply, identical deadweight-loss triangle, identical story about the shortage and the non-price rationing that follows. And I went in assuming economists treat these two policies as basically the same animal. Then I actually looked at surveys of the profession, and it's the opposite of what I expected: economists are split roughly down the middle on the minimum wage — this has been true ever since that famous mid-90s study comparing fast-food employment in New Jersey and Pennsylvania after NJ raised its minimum, and even more so now that the monopsony revival has made labor-market wage-setting power a respectable thing to believe in — but on rent control they're close to unanimous, like 80-90% saying it reduces the quantity and quality of housing. Rent control is apparently THE textbook example of a policy economists of all stripes line up against. So the two policies get the same diagram in week 3 and totally different verdicts in week 10, and I can't make the asymmetry make sense.

The obvious candidate is monopsony: employers in labor markets have wage-setting power, landlords... don't? But that seems backwards. The cities with the strictest rent control are exactly the cities where housing supply is famously inelastic and a handful of big landlords and homeowner-nimby coalitions control the terms — that looks like market power on the seller side, which should make the price-control diagram LESS apt for housing, not more. And it can't be that the harms are just obviously bigger for rent control, because everyone agrees the apartments-never-built are real losses and everyone also agrees the teenagers-never-hired are real losses, and both sets of victims are invisible in the data in the same way — the person priced out of San Francisco never shows up as a San Francisco renter, the person not hired never shows up in the employment stats. There's also this complication our TA raised: most modern "second generation" rent control isn't even a hard cap, it's more like vacancy decontrol and eviction protections, which is arguably not a price ceiling at all, so maybe the profession is united against a policy that mostly doesn't exist anymore and split over one that does? And on the ethics side it's just as weird — the Nozick-inflected people in my seminar say any price control is a form of coercion because it blocks trades both parties want, but a bunch of them then turn around and support the minimum wage, and the egalitarians who defend rent control because of what it does for sitting tenants seem totally unmoved by the exact same argument for incumbent workers. It's like the two policies attract completely different moral instincts even though the distributional logic — incumbent beneficiaries versus unseen outsiders who'd like to enter — is structurally identical.

So my question is: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is the split on the minimum wage and the consensus on rent control tracking an actual empirical difference (elasticities, market structure, how measurable the effects are), or is it sociological — whose interests are organized, which side of each debate the profession's class intuitions land on, the fact that opposing rent control let economists feel anti-landlord in a way opposing the minimum wage never let them feel anti-worker? My own half-baked theory is that it's about which invisible losers we find it easy to model: economists have gotten very good at narrating the apartment-never-built but the teenager-never-hired only recently got a monopsony story that makes it respectable. Diagnose it for me — tell me whether the debate has genuinely stalled or is actually moving, whether my asymmetry observation survives scrutiny or partially dissolves once you look at what the two policies actually are in 2024, and what would even count as settling it: better natural experiments, a theory of whose losses count, or nothing, because this was never really an empirical dispute to begin with. Don't just survey both sides — I want a diagnosis with reasons, and be honest if the answer is "your premise is confused."
