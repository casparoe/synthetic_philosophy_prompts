I've spent the last few years thinking about wild animal suffering entirely on my own — no philosophy or biology training, just books, essays, and long arguments with people in online animal-welfare forums. A local animal-advocacy group I've been attending has given me a twenty-minute slot at their monthly meetup to present an argument I've worked out, and I want to walk in prepared for the question period rather than be mauled by it.

The argument, briefly, so the objections can target it: when we ask whether a stretch of habitat is good or bad for animals, we should be counting individual animals and what their lives are like, not species counts, not ecosystems, not "balance" or biodiversity as such. In most intact habitat, the overwhelming majority of individual animals are small, fast-breeding creatures — insects, fish larvae, small rodents — whose lives are very short and typically end in predation, starvation, freezing, or disease. If nearly every individual born dies before anything we would recognize as a decent stretch of life is possible, then the average welfare of a wild population can plausibly be negative, and the total is dominated by enormous numbers of brief, bad lives. From there it seems to follow — at least in principle — that some habitat destruction can be a net reduction in suffering, by shutting down a breeding cycle that exists only to produce creatures that mostly die badly, the way a bad year for a pond means far fewer mayflies hatching to live for a day. I am not arguing that we should bulldoze anything. I am arguing that "preserving habitat" is not automatically a way of helping animals, and that animal advocates treat it as one because they've absorbed a picture of nature as harmonious that comes from conservation, not from caring about individuals.

The room: roughly thirty members of a group that's been running for years — mostly vegans who run corporate chicken-welfare campaigns, shelter volunteering, and the occasional protest. A few have read the standard philosophical case against factory farming and can argue a straightforward utilitarian line when prompted. One is a retired ecology lecturer who volunteers with a land trust and thinks fluently in terms of niches, food webs, and natural balance. They are decent people who have never encountered this argument, and I expect the first reaction to be horror rather than curiosity.

What I need is a prep sheet for the question period. List the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise — not the ones an audience of philosophers or ecologists would raise — ranked by how much damage each does if I handle it badly. Write each objection the way someone from this room would actually put it, using their vocabulary of cruelty, sentience, and what we owe animals. Under each, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes in plain, non-technical words, and be blunt about where that reply is thin. Tell me which objections I should concede outright instead of fighting, and where I should reframe rather than answer. Include the one question I'm privately hoping nobody asks, along with the best answer I have. Throughout, take definite positions on which parts of my argument can bear weight and which are overreach — don't lay out the options and leave the choosing to me, and don't soften the verdicts to spare my feelings.
