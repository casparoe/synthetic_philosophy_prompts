I'm a software engineer, been doing the on-call rotation and incident command stuff for a few years now, so I've written my share of blameless postmortems. The ritual, in case it matters: after an outage we do a writeup that sticks strictly to how the failure happened — which alert fired late, which flag got flipped, which race condition won — and there's a hard norm against saying anyone screwed up. The stated reasoning is that nobody comes to work intending to break prod, everyone made reasonable calls with the info they had, and if we punish people, next time they'll hide stuff and the postmortems turn into fiction. Fine, I mostly buy that. But here's what's bugging me: the same org, in the same quarter, gave a guy a fat spot bonus for heroically restoring service at 3am after an outage that his own config push caused, while another engineer got quietly managed out because her canary rollout — which cleared the same review process his did — happened to take out payments during peak. On-call bonuses, promo packets, who gets credit for "ownership," who gets trusted with the scary migrations: everything around the postmortem tracks outcomes, and outcomes in this game are basically luck — the same broken deploy is a nothingburger at 4am Sunday and a Sev-1 at 9am Monday.

So what does the blameless postmortem actually presuppose about responsibility? Is it commited to the view that blame tracks what you did rather than what happened, or is it just an information-gathering hack that would survive just fine even if you thought outcomes do affect blameworthiness — and if so, is it strange that we never say that part out loud? And does the surrounding reward system presuppose the opposite of what the postmortem presupposes, or is there some coherent picture that holds both together without it being pure hypocrisy?

Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro or conclusion. Each of the first four should be a candidate commitment that the practice rests on, with a quick verdict on whether it genuinely requires that view or is compatible with several rival ones, or else a place where the pieces clash. Make the fifth point about what would actually have to change in how we run incidents and reviews if the real presupposition turned out to be the outcome-tracking one rather than the blameless one.
