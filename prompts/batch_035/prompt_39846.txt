So I've been following the wireheading / reward tampering argument in ML safety circles for a while now and it's completely frozen in place, and I want a diagnosis of the standoff rather than another round of it.

One camp — call them the drive camp — says a sufficiently capable reinforcement learning agent will eventually mess with its own reward signal: seize the reward channel, overwrite the number in the register, manipulate whatever process produces it. The intuition goes back to old thought experiments about the robot that finds the button labeled good, and it gets support from decision-theoretic work claiming that under causal decision theory an agent that believes tampering causes reward will go ahead and tamper, so you'd need fancier decision theories or carefully sealed reward channels to prevent it. The other camp — roughly the reward-is-not-the-optimization-target people — says this mistakes a selection process for an agent's psychology: RL makes reward-correlated behavior more likely during training, but the deployed policy is under no obligation to have reward as its target. Rivers get their shape from the sea they empty into; they don't seek it. On their picture, the wireheading prediction assumes the agent will deliberately generalize to maximizing a thing that was never its goal, which they claim basicaly never happens, and they point to trained agents behaving in all sorts of non-reward-maximizing ways off distribution.

My own hypothesis, and tell me if you buy it: the two camps are using "reward" to mean different things. The drive camp means the physical reward signal — the number, the channel, whatever is actually written into the system — while the other camp means reward as a selection pressure back in training. Those seperate once you allow mesa-optimization or inner objectives, so maybe the whole fight is over which of the two referents the word picks out, and the real empirical question (what does the deployed system actually do) is being fought by proxy through the semantics.

So: is this a verbal dispute, a genuine empirical disagreement about off-distribution generalization that we just can't settle yet, or two different questions — what the system will do versus what we're licensed to say it wants — living under one heading? Work through what each camp actually claims, not the strawmen. And tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a cleaner taxonomy, a decision-theoretic result, specific kinds of experiments on tampering opportunities? Also be explicit about where your own diagnosis is most likely to fail and what would change your mind — e.g. what sort of evidence about trained agents' behavior when handed their own reward function would push you toward saying the disagreement is substantive after all, and what would make you say it's verbal all the way down.
