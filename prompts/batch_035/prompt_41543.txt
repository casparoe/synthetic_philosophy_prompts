I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, writing a dissertation chapter on the ethics of truthfulness, and I recently came across a popular book on applied ethics that closes its chapter on lying with this summary of Augustine: "Augustine held that every lie is a sin, and since all sins are equal in God's eyes, every lie is equally wrong — the polite social falsehood is as culpable as perjury. His reasoning was simply that Scripture forbids lying, so the doctrine is an article of faith rather than an argument. And when you press it with the obvious case — lying to the murderer at the door to save his intended victim — it collapses, which is why no secular philosopher now defends anything like it." I suspect this is too quick, but I don't know Augustine's texts on lying well enough to say exactly where. Could you audit it for me: which claims would a scholar of Augustine accept, which are conflations or overstatements, and which are simply false? In particular, I'd like to know whether the "equally wrong" step is something Augustine actually held, whether his case against lying really rested only on scriptural authority, how he himself handled the life-saving lie if he addressed it at all, and whether the book's dismissive conclusion — that the position is of no interest to a secular ethicist — follows from the parts of the summary that are actually true.
