I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background at all, and this came out of an argument with my roommate about my job. At work we log basically everything users do — every click, session length, rough location, device fingerprint. No human ever reads these logs; they feed models that tweak recommendations and ranking. When I asked whether that was creepy, the answer I got (and gave my roommate) was that it's fine because no person ever looks at the data, only machines do. I tried to turn that into a proper argument to win the debate, and now the argument has kind of turned on me:

(1) A privacy violation happens only when somebody actually comes to know something about you that you wanted kept to yourself. Before a mind has the information there's at best a potential violation — like a letter that's been written but never read.
(2) Automated collection and processing doesn't put the information in any mind. It sits in storage and gets fed to models.
(3) So automated collection and processing, on its own, never violates privacy. A violation would only occur at the moment a human looks.
(4) In practice, human review of stored data is rare and targeted — an investigation, a content moderation queue, debugging an incident.
(5) So mass data collection as it exists today is mostly fine, and the right fix for whatever problems remain is to restrict human access to the data, not to restrict the collection itself.

But now the same logic says: if my landlord hid a camera in my bedroom and stored the feed on an encrypted drive he never once opened, and the drive got shredded when he died, then my privacy was never violated at any point — the "violation" would need a viewer that never came. That's got to be wrong; I'd say the violation happened the moment the camera went in, or at least at capture, not at some hypothetical viewing that never occured. Yet I can't find which step to give up. (1) still sounds right to me (a file no one will ever read really does feel like a tree falling in an empty forest), and (3) follows from (1) and (2) pretty cleanly, so if I keep the steps I like I seem stuck with the camera conclusion. My roommate also says that even in our system, the models' outputs get used to act on people — different prices, different feeds — so the company in effect "knows" things through the model even with zero human reading. I'm not sure if that breaks step (2) or is just a separate issue from the argument proper.

Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. If the answer ends up being "restrict the collection itself," I'll admit I have an interest in that not being the answer, so push back if you think I'm rationalizing. And please be clear about what's actually settled among people who study privacy versus what's genuinely contested — e.g. if the framing where privacy is about control over access rather than about somebody knowing something is itself one camp's view and not a consensus, say so rather than presenting it as the standard position. I'd rather know where the live disagreements are than get a tidy answer that hides them.
