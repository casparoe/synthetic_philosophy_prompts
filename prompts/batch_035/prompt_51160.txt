I write a series of courtroom novels set in an invented republic between the wars, each built around a single hard case decided by my recurring heroine, Justice Maren Brandt. I am at work on a fifth novel and have discovered that I no longer know my own character, so I would be grateful for a disciplined reading of her record before I commit her to the bench again.

Her rulings so far, in order of publication. In the first novel she upheld a wartime statute, worded with perfect clarity and promulgated by a competent military government, that extinguished the compensation claims of families of executed so-called traitors; her opinion held that her revulsion at the content was beside the point, since her office was to apply the law, not to grade the legislature. In the second she voided an emergency decree against currency hoarding, but strictly on formal grounds: the issuing minister lacked authority, the decree was unpublished when first enforced, operated retroactively, and defined its central term so loosely that the mint's own director could not say whether she was guilty. In the third — the book readers still argue over — she struck down a statute stripping a pacifist sect of the right to hold property. That law had been rammed through in a single sitting after the opposition was ejected from the chamber, which would have sufficed; yet her opinion went further and insisted that no obedience could be owed to a statute of that character whatever its pedigree. In the fourth she dismissed the appeal of a tax resister who claimed his conscience stood above the revenue code, holding that sincerity of moral conviction is no answer to a clear law.

Now the fifth novel. The republic, fully democratic again, has enacted — after two years of open debate, a published commission of inquiry, full participation by the opposition, prospective effect, and wording a draughtsman could set in amber — a statute providing that persons thrice convicted of habitual petty criminality shall be surgically sterilized. A young man convicted for the third time of stealing coal stands before Brandt for sentencing, and the statute directs her to commit him to the surgeon.

I have considered resolving the book with her resignation, but that would be an author's rescue rather than the character's own logic, and I would rather know what her record actually commits her to. As far as I can see, at least two principles fit her four rulings equally well: one on which the sect statute in the third novel fell because of what it was, and another on which it fell because of how it was made, with its monstrousness being so much rhetoric. These come apart cleanly on my new case, which is immaculate in process and monstrous in content. So: predict her ruling, set out the candidate principles that are jointly consistent with her track record, say which you find most probable and why, and tell me what weight each gives to her conduct in the fourth novel, since dismissing the conscience appeal seemed at the time like the most positivist thing she ever did. Finally, since two of the plausible principles may agree on how she should treat the sterilization statute, describe a further case — one I could hold in reserve for a sixth novel — that would separate those principles, so that I can decide which of them I have really been writing all along.
