You are a philosopher of history with a strong background in environmental history, and I want you to think carefully and systematically before writing anything: begin by brainstorming the possible readings of the key terms, settle on definitions you can defend, and only then produce your analysis.

The episode is the eruption of Tambora in April 1815 and the so-called "year without a summer" that followed. The relevant facts, which you may treat as given: Tambora, on Sumbawa in Indonesia, was the most powerful volcanic eruption of the historical era; the sulfate aerosols it injected into the stratosphere lowered global temperatures in 1816-1817; New England saw snow in June and repeated killing frosts that summer; Western and Central Europe suffered cold, wet harvests on top of the economic exhaustion that followed the Napoleonic Wars, with grain shortages, food riots, and a major typhus epidemic among the destitute; the gloomy weather at Lake Geneva in the summer of 1816 is famously credited with keeping Mary Shelley and her companions indoors long enough to incubate Frankenstein; and a popular-culture claim you sometimes encounter holds that the resulting oat shortage, by making horses scarce, spurred Karl Drais to invent his walking machine in 1817, an ancestor of the bicycle. Note also that the eruption is sometimes mentioned, more speculatively still, in connection with the onset of the 1817 cholera pandemic in Bengal, via disruption of the monsoon.

The lens is the idea, familiar from environmental and disease history, that non-human entities -- germs, climate fluctuations, volcanoes -- should be treated as "historical actors." Tambora is a favorite exhibit for this view: a mountain that rearranged harvests, migration, epidemics, and possibly the history of technology and literature.

Your task is to analyze this episode through that lens, running in both directions.

First, construct the analysis as its proponents would: what exactly would it mean for the volcano to be an "actor" here? Be disciplined about the mapping. What are the volcano's "deeds" and where do they end? Distinguish carefully between the eruption itself, its climatic effects, and the human consequences, and say at which joints the "agency" metaphor is doing real work and at which it is merely decorative. In particular, note that the worst suffering occurred in a Europe already destabilized by war, poor harvest management, and rigid grain markets, and in a New England whose agriculture was organized around fragile monocropping and marginal land. What does the counterfactual look like -- how much of the 1816-1819 crisis does the eruption explain on its own, and how much only in combination with vulnerabilities that were themselves human products?

Second, stress-test the framing. An "actor" with no intentions, no responsiveness to what others do, and no possibility of being persuaded or deterred seems to lack everything we ordinarily build the concept of agency around; does calling Tambora an actor illuminate anything that "cause" or "trigger" would not, or does it smuggle in a misleading picture? Consider the moral exculpation risk: if the famine becomes "something a volcano did," what happens to the accountability of governments and grain speculators? And consider the historiographical temptation in the other direction: the eruption makes a wonderfully clean narrative, and clean narratives attract credulous claims -- evaluate the epistemic status of the Drais bicycle story and the cholera speculation as test cases for how this style of history disciplines itself. Where the evidence for a Tambora connection is thin or contested, say so plainly rather than letting narrative momentum carry the claim.

Third, run the analysis back the other way: what does the episode show about the "non-human actors" program itself? Does Tambora confirm it, constitute an anomaly for it, or reveal that the program was never really about cases like this -- a sudden exogenous shock -- as opposed to the slower, feedback-laden cases (endemic disease, gradual climate shifts) where human choices and non-human dynamics are genuinely entangled? Draw the contrast explicitly: is a volcano more like a pathogen in how it "acts" through history, or less, and why?

Structure your answer around these three tasks, be explicit about where the actor framing is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and close with a considered verdict: is "Tambora as historical actor" a piece of environmental determinism in respectable dress, or a legitimate and clarifying historiographical move? Argue for your verdict rather than merely announcing it.
