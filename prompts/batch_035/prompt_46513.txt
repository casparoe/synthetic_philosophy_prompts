After forty years as a behavioral physiologist, I have retired into philosophy of mind and free will, and one case from my own field keeps snagging on the literature. The female digger wasp of the genus Sphex paralyzes a cricket with a sting, drags it to her burrow, leaves it at the threshold, enters alone to inspect the chamber, and only then emerges to pull the cricket in. Jean-Henri Fabre reported in the nineteenth century what every subsequent experimenter has confirmed: if the cricket is moved even a few centimeters while she is inside, she emerges, retrieves it, drags it back to the threshold — and re-enters to inspect the burrow again. She will run this loop many times over, with no sign of learning or fatigue, and yet she is no simple reflex machine: she navigates home from long distances, circumvents obstacles, and delivers her stings to the cricket's nerve centers with surgical economy. The case has become the standard emblem of sphexishness — the term was coined for it by Douglas Hofstadter in the early 1980s — and it is usually cited as a two-line refutation of the idea that flexible-seeming behavior implies free agency. That citation, I have come to think, is too quick.

Please analyze this episode through the lens of the two families of theory that dominate the contemporary free will literature on which I have been lecturing myself: hierarchical accounts of agency in the Frankfurt style, in which freedom turns on the alignment between an agent's desires and her higher-order attitudes toward them, and reasons-responsiveness accounts in the manner of Fischer and Ravizza, in which it turns on whether action issues from mechanisms that are appropriately sensitive to an agent's reasons. I want the mapping done seriously, as a case analysis rather than a gesture. What, on each account, would count as a reason for the wasp, and what evidence do we have about which features of her situation she registers at all? Which of her behaviors do the frameworks classify, and where are they simply silent — was either theory built to say anything about a creature with no language, no deliberative speech, and no evidence of attitudes toward her own attitudes? I have a suspicion I would like tested rather than indulged: that both frameworks were designed for reflective human agents, so that they return a trivially correct verdict (the wasp is unfree, and so what) while missing what the case genuinely teaches, which is that freedom is not a single capacity that an agent has or lacks but a matter of the shape and distribution of her sensitivities — of what perturbs her behavior and what leaves it untouched. The wasp is exquisitely sensitive to the cricket's position and wholly insensitive to the futility of her own re-inspection; a reflective human is the reverse in the second respect, but presumably not in every respect. If that suspicion is wrong, tell me why. Then run the analysis in the other direction, as I am told a good case analysis should: does the episode confirm these theories, expose an anomaly they never anticipated, or suggest that freedom-as-flexibility was never really about behavioral flexibility at all? And if you think some framework does better — one that grades an agent's freedom by the profile of what it responds to and what it does not, rather than by possession of one reflective capacity — sketch what such a framework would look like, what it would imply about degrees of freedom across species, and what it would cost us, philosophically, to adopt it. I would rather have an honest accounting of where the theories go quiet than an enthusiastic verdict in either direction.
