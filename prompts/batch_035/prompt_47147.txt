I'm finishing up a position paper for a seminar on collapse narratives (the syllabus ran from the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean through the Terminal Classic Maya to Rapa Nui), and before I submit it I want a consistency audit of the package of claims I've ended up defending, because I suspect at least one of them is quietly eating the others. Here is the set. (a) The term "collapse" is doing normative work it shouldn't: in most canonical cases what we observe is reorganization, and the most visible discontinuities are in elite cultural production — monument construction, palatial administration, writing — rather than in the lives of ordinary people, who mostly keep farming. (b) Tainter's account of collapse as a response to diminishing marginal returns on complexity is basically the right general mechanism, and it makes collapse an economizing adaptation rather than a failure. (c) The Terminal Classic Maya is nonetheless a genuine collapse and not just reorganization: the southern lowlands lose on the order of ninety percent of their population over a couple of centuries, kingship as an institution disappears, and the long-count commemorative tradition ends — that is not the same phenomenon as a shift in elite fashion. (d) Rapa Nui, by contrast, is a bad poster child for ecocide-driven collapse: following Hunt and Lipo, I take the worst demographic and ecological damage to have followed European contact and the slave raids and disease that came with it, so the standard self-inflicted-deforestation story is misleading. (e) On the strength of all this, I conclude that contemporary worries about civilizational collapse are overblown: no complex society has ever permanently vanished, "collapsed" societies reorganize and persist, and popular apocalypticism about our own future is mostly the same narrative bias that misreads the archaeological record.
