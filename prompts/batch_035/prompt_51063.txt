I should tell you up front that I have no philosophy training whatsoever — I run a bookkeeping practice and came to this puzzle through a friend who reads widely, so I may be phrasing things in an odd way, and I would rather you took the question seriously than corrected my terminology.

The puzzle is Kavka's toxin one: a billionaire offers you a million pounds at noon tomorrow if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink a vial of toxin at noon tomorrow — a toxin that will make you ill for a week but do no lasting harm. The money is paid before you drink, and the billionaire's decision depends only on your intention tonight, not on whether you actually drink. My friend and I have been arguing about it for months. He says a rational person can simply form the intention, collect the money at noon, and then quietly let the intention lapse before drinking, since by then there is no further benefit to drinking and a week of illness to avoid. I keep feeling this misses something: once you know at midnight that you will let the intention lapse at noon, it's not clear you had the intention at midnight at all, and yet I also can't see any principled line between an intention you might later abandon for good reason and one you already plan to abandon. To sharpen our disagreement, we invented a variant: suppose you may, before midnight, sign an irrevocable bond forfeiting two million pounds unless you drink — my friend accepts this device gets you the money but insists it is somehow cheating, that paying your way to compliance is not the same as intending, and I don't fully understand what distinction he is drawing.

I want you to give me your honest credence, as a number, that a fully rational person who has not used any external commitment device can form the intention at midnight in a way that genuinely satisfies the billionaire's condition — not merely fake it, and not merely hope the plan to drink survives until noon — and, more importantly, show me the reasoning behind the number. Please set the answer out as a numbered list of exactly five items and nothing else: no introduction, no conclusion, no summary at the end. Items one through four should each take one sub-question the credence depends on — I would suggest something like whether intention is under direct voluntary control at all, whether an intention you already expect to abandon for good reason counts as an intention, whether the causal structure (the money being already paid and untouchable) matters or is a red herring, and where, if anywhere, the irrevocable-bond variant really differs from plain intending — give each a rough probability and a sentence or two of justification. Item five should give your combined credence, say which of the four sub-questions the number is most sensitive to, and tell me which of us — the one who thinks you can simply intend, or the one who thinks there's a principled block — that final credence puts you closer to. Please answer in British English.
