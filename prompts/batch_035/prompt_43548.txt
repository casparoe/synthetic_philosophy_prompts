I'm a third-year PhD student, and in a few weeks I'm presenting at a small interdisciplinary workshop on causation in the special sciences. My paper defends a version of the view that there is, strictly speaking, no such thing as causation by omission — no absence ever does anything, nothing in the world answers to "the failure to water caused the death." But I go a step further than the usual line: I argue true omission claims aren't just loose talk either; they're compressed claims about which ordinary, positive causal chains actually occurred, evaluated against a contrast class fixed by a norm (a role, an expectation, a regulation). So "the nurse's failure to check the drip caused the collapse" is elliptical for something like: within the range of cases the nurse's role requires, the actual positive chain (no check occurred, the infusion kept going, the collapse followed) is a difference-maker for the collapse, while "the failure" itself is not a relatum of any causal relation. The norm does no causal work — it only selects which counterfactuals are relevant.

The audience is tough, kind of deliberately assembled: about half experimenal philosophers who've run studies showing ordinary judgments about omissions track norms (they'll treat my view as just confirming their data and then ask why I need the metaphysics on top of it), two hard-line process theorists who think absences are nonstarters and will be annoyed I concede them anything at all, and one legal scholar who works on tort liability for negligent omissions and has heard a hundred philsophers tell her that her cases "dissolve on analysis" — she will not be charmed by that move again. No interventionists in the room, which is a blessing, though the legal scholar knows that literature well enough to use it as a club if it helps her.

List the objections I'll actually get from this crowd, roughly in the order they'll arrive and ordered by how much damage each could do, phrased the way each type of person would actually phrase it. For each one give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, flag where the reply is honestly soft, and mark the question I'm hoping nobody asks. And if there's something I should just concede in the talk itself so it doesn't look like a retreat when it comes up later, tell me that too.
