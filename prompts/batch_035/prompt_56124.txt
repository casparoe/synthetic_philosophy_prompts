I'm grading a mid-term for an upper-level philosophy of language course and would like a second opinion on one of the answers before I commit to a mark. The exam question read:

"Consider the sentence 'It is raining, but I don't believe that it is raining.' Explain (i) why the sentence is not self-contradictory — that is, how someone could be in a situation it accurately describes; (ii) why it nevertheless cannot be sincerely and competently asserted, in ordinary circumstances; and (iii) how the 'omissive' version above differs, if at all, from the 'commissive' version 'It is raining, but I believe that it is not raining.' Finally, say which diagnosis of the sentence's absurdity you find most defensible: a pragmatic account on which asserting it violates a norm governing assertion, or an account on which the sentence, though consistent, is in some stronger sense incoherent. Defend your choice."

The student's answer, transcribed in full:

"Moore's sentence is not a contradiction in the logical sense, because both conjuncts can be true at once. Someone staring at a window rather than the sky, or someone self-deceived about their own attitudes, could be in exactly the situation the sentence describes: it is raining, and they do not believe that it is. So the sentence is consistent and could even be true — a distracted forecaster could satisfy it. However, it cannot be sincerely asserted, because asserting that it is raining expresses belief that it is raining, while the second conjunct states that the speaker lacks that belief. In asserting the whole sentence, the speaker both expresses belief that p and denies holding that belief, so the sentence is self-contradictory once we take into account what assertion involves: after pragmatic enrichment it is a contradiction. As for part (iii), the commissive version, 'It is raining, but I believe that it is not raining,' is really just the same sentence in different words, since not believing that it is raining and believing that it is not raining amount to the same thing, which is why both versions are equally absurd. Moore's own diagnosis was pragmatic: he held, following Grice, that asserting something one does not believe violates the cooperative principle, and the absurdity we feel is simply our recognition of that violation. The pragmatic diagnosis is therefore the best one, and it also explains why the sentence sounds fine when embedded: nobody thinks 'Either it is raining and I don't believe it, or the picnic is cancelled' is incoherent."
