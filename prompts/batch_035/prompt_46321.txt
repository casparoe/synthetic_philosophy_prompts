Write a villanelle on Pascal's mugging, and let the form carry the argument rather than merely decorate it.

The scene is the classic one: a shabby figure stops you on the street and asks for a small sum — a few dollars, your wallet at most. When you hesitate, he explains that he is no ordinary thief. He claims access to vast resources, perhaps another dimension or a simulation, and says that if you pay him, he will return an astronomical payoff — a number of happy lives, or units of utility, so large it cannot be spoken aloud, only gestured at with towers of exponents. The philosophical problem, of course, is that expected utility seems to side with him: whatever minuscule credence you assign his story, a sufficiently large claimed payoff swamps it, so refusing looks irrational, and yet paying feels like an obvious mistake.

Make the two refrains do the structural work. One refrain should be the mugger's demand, fixed and unembellished every time it returns — the same ask each time it recurs, just as the argument's skeleton never changes no matter how large the numbers get. The other refrain should be the passerby's answer, also fixed. The escalation — the exponent towers, the increasingly vertiginous claims — belongs only in the middle lines of the tercets, never in the refrains, so that the poem's invariant frame and its swelling middle enact the very asymmetry at issue: the form of the mugging never grows, only the payload. Somewhere in the middle stanzas, introduce a second mugger who makes the same kind of claim with a slightly larger number, and let the reader see that the first mugger's promised payoff is contested before it could ever be collected — rival muggers each demanding the same five dollars being one of the standard worries for the naive expected-utility reasoner, and the villanelle's compulsive returning machinery should make their proliferation feel inevitable rather than contrived.

For the closing couplet, where the refrains finally meet: resist the urge to let either one win. Let them arrive side by side, the demand and the refusal, and leave it to the multiplication that never quite gets performed to hang unresolved between them — the honest state of the debate is that nobody has a clean answer to why the arithmetic fails, only the strong conviction that it must. You may, if you can manage it without breaking the form, let one middle stanza glance backward at the argument's ancestor, the wager about belief in God, where the same structure of infinite-or-enormous payoff overwhelming any finite credence first appeared — but as a shadow or an echo, not a lecture.

The standard for success: someone who knows Pascal's mugging should be able to see exactly which line of the argument each formal feature is doing, and should find the poem's treatment of the escalation-invariant argument and the rival-mugger problem accurate rather than name-dropped. Someone who has never heard of the problem should still come away with an unsettling little poem about a street-corner shakedown in which the ask stays small and the promises get larger than language.
