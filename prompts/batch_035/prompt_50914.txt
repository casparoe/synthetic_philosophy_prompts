I have read rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, and a slogan that used to surface occasionally in consciousness threads now shows up everywhere: "consciousness is a user illusion." Its pedigree seems respectable enough — Tor Nørretranders built a whole popular book around the phrase, The User Illusion: Cutting Consciousness Down to Size, and readers of Dennett have been circulating variants for thirty years.

Last month I watched it do real work. In a thread about what criteria should guide animal welfare policy, someone raised, in essentially Nagel's form, the question of what it is like to be a fish — whether there is anything it is like for the animal itself, or only nociception and withdrawal reflexes with nobody home. A well-regarded commenter dismissed the question as confused: consciousness is the brain's user interface, a desktop hiding the machine code; there is no inner light to ask about, and wondering what a fish experiences is a category error on par with asking what the trash icon feels. Several people replied with approval, the thread moved on, and nobody explained what, exactly, we had all just accepted.

I want that explanation, or at least the raw material for one. Please take the one-liner apart into the distinct precise theses it could be expressing and give each a verdict. I can see at least these candidates, and I suspect there are more: (1) the trivial physicalist claim that conscious experience is something the brain does rather than a ghostly add-on; (2) the bandwidth claim in the spirit of the Norretranders book, that the contents of consciousness are a heavily edited, action-relevant digest of vastly more parallel processing; (3) the claim that the self who seems to face the interface is itself an item represented within it — a constructed user rather than a real one; (4) the claim that phenomenal qualities are interface conventions, so that asking what red is really like is like asking what the recycling bin really holds; and (5) the fully eliminativist claim that felt interiority itself is illusory, with no further fact about "something it is like" beyond functional organization.

For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, substantive but well supported, substantive and contested, or plainly false — and, crucially, whether it would license the dismissal from the thread even if true. My suspicion is that the dismissive move needs something like (5); that (1) through (3) leave the fish question exactly as open as it was; and that (4) quietly equivocates between an epistemic reading (the interface hides the mechanisms of experience) and an ontological one (the interface's qualities correspond to nothing at all), which are very different claims. But I have watched this slogan deployed in at least four other contexts — an argument that anesthesia cannot be morally different from dreamless sleep, a claim that reports of meditative selflessness are naïve, a dismissal of machine-sentience worries as confused, and an assertion that anhedonia research rests on a conceptual mistake — so I want the full menu before the next thread.

Please present the readings as a structured list with verdicts, flag wherever the ordinary-language word "illusion" is doing illegitimate work (an illusion, unlike a user interface, is normally something that fails to occur), and close by saying which reading the thread's commenter actually needed in order to close the question, and what it costs to hold that reading rather than the weaker ones.
