My colleague serves on our city's autonomous-systems incident panel. Whenever one of the municipality's automated systems causes serious harm, the panel issues a short public opinion on who, if anyone, bears moral responsibility for it — deliberately kept apart from legal liability and compensation, which other bodies handle. I have read all four opinions it has issued, and two new complaints come before it next month. I want to predict the rulings, but I have noticed that more than one principle would generate exactly the four opinions on record, and I suspect the new complaints fall where those principles part ways. Your job is to help me see the structure.

The four opinions so far:

1. The tram case. An autonomous tram's vision system failed to register track workers in nonstandard high-visibility gear; the remote supervisor on duty had the live feed and an emergency stop, but hesitated, and a worker lost a leg. The transit authority's own safety office had twice warned about the classifier's weakness in exactly the conditions that night. The panel held the supervisor morally responsible and the authority culpable as well for disregarding its own warnings.

2. The rookery case. A delivery drone swarm rerouted around a wind shift and crossed a protected nesting island at night; the live map marked the reserve, the depot operator watched the new track and judged the detour within tolerance, declining to ground the fleet; and an engineer had earlier set the airspace-restriction penalty to near zero, skipping the review that should have caught it. The swarm did exactly what it was specified to do. The panel held both the operator and the operator's employer responsible.

3. The ferry case. In a storm, the uncrewed freight ferry's collision-avoidance system, certified after an unusually thorough review, made a rudder decision following from an interaction of a drifting buoy and a cross-current that no one had modeled; regulation kept humans out of the loop for the whole crossing. The berth was destroyed and a dockworker badly injured — the most serious harm in any case the panel has heard. It found no one morally responsible: a genuine responsibility gap, it said, recommending the certification regime be changed. The engineer who signed off was explicitly cleared.

4. The sweeper case. An autonomous street sweeper working an empty boulevard at night struck and injured a man asleep in a bike lane, through a plain sensor-fusion malfunction; but the fleet had been approved to run unsupervised overnight after diligent trials, and the failure mode was not one anyone could reasonably have anticipated. Again the panel found a gap: no one morally responsible.

The two new complaints:

A. The shuttle case. A driverless shuttle misread a man walking with crutches as street furniture. The vendor's trials had covered wheelchairs, walkers, and strollers, and the city's procurement review was unusually careful; nothing in the record suggests anyone was careless. But an attendant was riding aboard with a working override pendant, watching the road, with about three seconds of clear warning, and brakes that stop the shuttle well within that time.

B. The floodgate case. The storm controller was configured — after documented internal debate, with the reasoning written down — to keep the freight rail line dry during storms at the cost of occasionally flooding a low-lying meadow, the residual risk to life judged negligible because the meadow was expected to be empty at night. This storm, several people camped there were injured, one seriously; the camp had appeared only days before, after the downtown shelter closed. The controller runs autonomously and the water moves within minutes; by design, no human is in the loop anywhere.

Please tell me: what will the panel say about each complaint — who, if anyone, is morally responsible? Lay out the candidate principles that fit the four published opinions equally well, and what each one predicts for A and for B; note in particular that the earlier blamed cases involved both a human who could intervene at the time and an earlier careless decision, while the gap cases involved neither, so the record never had to choose. Say which prediction you would bet on for each complaint and why, and describe the further case that would most cleanly separate the candidate principles — including whether the two rulings, once issued, could still be made consistent under a single principle, or would force the panel to admit it had been running two.
