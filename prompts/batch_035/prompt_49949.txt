The precautionary principle keeps showing up in what I read, and I honestly can't tell whether the debate is deep or whether everyone is just using words I never needed. So here's what I want: take the main positions in the PP debate -- the weak version where uncertainty doesn't excuse inaction, the stronger versions people associate with European environmental regulation, and the critics who say the principle is incoherent or ends up forbidding everything including doing nothing -- and restate them in the vocabulary I actually used for a living: null and alternative hypotheses, type I versus type II error, statistical power, no-observed-adverse-effect-levels, the ten-fold uncertainty factors you stack for interspecies extrapolation and human variability, linear no-threshold assumptions, weight-of-evidence review, and expected loss versus minimax regret when you can't put a distribution on the harm.

Then give me the residue report, since that's what I actually care about. Which parts of the philosophical debate translate cleanly onto things we argued about anyway (e.g., how conservative to be with alpha when the power calculation is impossible because nobody knows the shape of the dose-response curve, or whether a default assumption of a threshold is itself a value judgment)? I'm especially hung up on irreversibility -- the philosophers treat it as load-bearing, and in my world "irreversible" was a statement about kinetics and half-lives, not something that changed the decision rule itself. So is the residue there something the statistical framework can't say, or something the philosophers never made precise enough to say in any framework?
