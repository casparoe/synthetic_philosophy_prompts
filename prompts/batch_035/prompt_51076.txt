I am writing a novel set in a mid-sized provincial city, and I would be grateful for your judgment on a question that has stalled my third act. My protagonist is a well-loved local fixer who gets things done for his neighbors: he persuades a clerk to expedite a housing permit, arranges a hospital bed for the widow's son, and in exchange takes no money, only gestures — crates of oranges, an introduction, a favor remembered for later. A second character, a young official trained abroad and sent to modernize the municipal administration, insists on calling him corrupt, and my current draft deliberately leaves it to the reader to decide who is right. My editor says this is a cop-out and wants me to commit. So: is the fixer corrupt, is the reformer mistaking ordinary reciprocity for bribery, or is the question itself badly posed? Does it matter to the ethics of the case that the gifts arrive after the deed, in a currency of gratitude and standing obligation rather than cash negotiated in advance, and would your answer change if the same favors were done for strangers? Please give exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, in American English.
