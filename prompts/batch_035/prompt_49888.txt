So: I'm on my township's environmental advisory board (long story, literally nobody else wanted the job), and next month we vote on whether to endorse taking about 200 acres of marginal cropland out of production and restoring it to wetland and prairie. Separately, there's a proposal to pave over a scrubby field next door for a distribution center, and my brother-in-law, who's been reading about wild animal suffering, keeps insisting at family dinners that the paving is "actually the good one" because fewer animals will exist, and most of the ones who would have existed would have had lives not worth living. And I've realized I don't have a clean answer to him, because of this pile of views I've accumulated. Here it is:

(1) I'm an anti-speciesist about suffering — a vole's pain counts the same as a dog's, and I've never seen a species boundary that does any actual moral work. (2) I lean toward most wild animal lives being net negative, mostly because of the r-selected species: the overwhelming majority of individuals die within days of being born, and the survivors face starvation, freezing, or being eaten while conscious. (3) I donate to a land trust, felt physically sick watching a farmer clear the last hedgerow off a field this fall, and think intact ecosystems and biodiversity genuinely matter — although when I try to cash that out in terms I'd accept, all I can produce is "humans enjoy and are enriched by nature," which seems way too thin to explain how strongly I feel it. (4) I think our ecological predictions are terrible — trophic cascades, mesopredator release, the whole mess — and I distrust anyone confident about what a land-use change will do to animal populations or welfare. (5) Related, I hold something like a rule against endorsing irreversible actions whose entire case rests on one highly contested empirical estimate with giant error bars. (6) But I also believe there is no neutral position — the wetland gets restored or it doesn't, the scrub field gets paved or it doesn't, and "waiting for better evidence" is itself a choice with welfare consequences, so I can't pretend it's a null move.

Now for the audit I'm asking you to do: go through these commitments pair by pair and tell me which ones are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and what the cheapest fix for each real one would be, ordered by severity rather than all equally bad. A few things I specifically can't sort out: my brother-in-law claims that (4) means I can't even be confident in (2), and I can't tell if that's right or a trick; (1) plus (2) seem to hand him his conclusion about the parking lot for free, and I want to know exactly where the flaw is, if there is one; and (5) versus (6) keeps me up at night because together they seem to say I'm both required to take a position and forbidden from taking one. I genuinely don't know which of these I'd give up — (3) feels like it's just who I am, but (1) and (2) feel like conclusions I actually reasoned my way to, and I keep going back and forth on wether (3) is real or sentimentality I should have outgrown. If one single revision would clean up most of the mess, say which one.
