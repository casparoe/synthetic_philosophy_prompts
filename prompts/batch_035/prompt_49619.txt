I heard a longform podcast episode, adapted from a popular-science article, that explained moral foundations theory to me roughly as follows: "Psychologist Jonathan Haidt and his colleagues discovered that human morality is built on five innate modules in the brain — harm, fairness, loyalty, authority, and purity — each corresponding to a distinct, evolved neural circuit. Liberals are people in whom only the harm and fairness modules are active; conservatives light up all five. Because the conservative brain engages more of the moral machinery, the researchers concluded that conservatism is the psychologically more complete moral outlook, and they urged liberals to widen their moral palette, which later studies showed people can do simply by reading more widely. In the piece, fairness is defined as a concern for equality."

The episode closed by drawing a policy lesson: since liberals operate on only two foundations, their moral judgments rest on a truncated emotional palette, and progressive policy positions are therefore systematically less morally grounded than conservative ones.

I came away both interested and uneasy. Something about the leap from "conservatives draw on more foundations" to "conservatism is the more complete moral outlook" bothered me, and I also have a vague memory that the theory is meant to be descriptive psychology rather than an argument for who is right. But the episode sounded authoritative, and I don't know the literature well enough to pinpoint where it goes wrong — or even whether it does.
