You are a political philosopher specializing in Mill and in the philosophy of law, practiced at testing principles against hard cases rather than reciting the textbook verdict about them. Think carefully about what follows, work through the mapping on your own before committing to conclusions, and give me only the finished analysis.

The episode: in 1977 the National Socialist Party of America, a small neo-Nazi group, announced a march in Skokie, Illinois — a village with a large Jewish population that included, by contemporary estimates, several thousand Holocaust survivors. The village responded with ordinances requiring march permits and large insurance bonds and prohibiting the dissemination of materials promoting hatred on grounds of race or religion. The ACLU took the Nazis' case pro bono and lost a substantial share of its membership for doing so; the courts, including the Seventh Circuit, struck the ordinances down on First Amendment grounds. In the event the march never took place in Skokie — the group ultimately rallied in Chicago instead — but not before months of litigation and news coverage that many residents reportedly experienced as the most distressing part of the whole affair.

Analyze this episode as a case study in Mill's harm principle, on the understanding that the principle licenses coercive interference only with conduct that harms others, where harm is something more than offense. I want a disciplined mapping, not a peroration:

1. Fix the mapping. Who is the actor whose liberty is at stake; what exactly is the conduct (the physical march, the messages carried, the publicity surrounding it); who are the parties at risk; and what are the candidate harms, stated as precisely as you can.

2. Run the harm test twice, under two accounts of harm that philosophers of law actually use. (a) A descriptive, counterfactual account: an event harms you only if it leaves you worse off than you would otherwise have been — which forces you to say what the baseline is and worse off in respect of what. (b) An interest-based account: harm is a setback to interests that merit protection, whether or not there is a clean counterfactual comparison. State what verdict the principle issues on each account, and be explicit about where each account strains.

3. Confront a wrinkle the standard treatment skips. Whatever distress the threatened march caused was mediated by the survivors' learning of it: had it occurred unobserved and unreported, no one would have been distressed. So the operative causal path runs through a channel of belief and publicity, not through the physical conduct — and on the evidence of the episode, the most acute suffering came during the litigation and coverage, before any march was to occur. Work out what this does to the principle. If the harm is produced by the audience's knowledge of the conduct rather than by the conduct itself, does the principle license regulating the publicity — the coverage, the permit process — rather than the march? And is that a principled application of the harm principle or an abuse of it?

4. Say where the harm principle is silent. Distinguish it, as the literature does, from Feinberg's offense principle, and say honestly whether the offense principle — not the harm principle — was doing the villagers' real work, and what follows if so.

5. Run the analysis in the other direction. What does the episode show about the harm principle: a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated? In particular, address whether the episode reveals that harm was never a purely descriptive notion, so that the principle's content is fixed entirely by whichever account of harm we feed it — and if so, whether the principle is doing any independent work in a case like this.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate the question.
