I'm a graduate student putting together a position paper on distributive justice for a seminar on equality, and before I submit it I'd like a rigorous consistency audit of the package of views I've committed to on luck egalitarianism. Please take on the role of a political philosopher who specializes in egalitarian theories of justice, think carefully and systematically before answering, and don't soften the diagnosis to be polite — I need to know where the real problems are. Here is my position set. (1) The core of justice in distribution is that inequalities traceable to brute luck — circumstances beyond anyone's control — must be neutralized, while inequalities arising from informed, voluntary choices are not unjust; people should bear the costs of the gambles they knowingly take. (2) I follow Dworkin on the currency: what people are owed is a share of resources, not welfare, so hypothetical insurance against brute luck is the right mechanism, and someone who simply develops expensive tastes has no additional claim. (3) I am firmly committed to an unconditional social minimum — housing, food, basic income — that is never withdrawn no matter how badly someone chooses, including the person who gambles away everything and refuses to work. (4) I don't believe anyone deserves returns to natural talent, but I'd allow the talented to keep extra rewards when doing so generates incentives that improve everyone's situation, including the worst off. (5) On cases: the outcome gap between two equally hardworking students, one whose family could afford years of test prep and tutoring and one whose family couldn't, strikes me as unjust; and the climber injured free soloing, who fully understood and freely accepted the risk, seems to me to have a weaker claim on public compensation than someone injured in an unavoidable accident — though I can't quite pin down why "weaker" rather than "none," given (3). (6) I also believe that people's willingness to take risks — their discount rates, their gambling dispositions, even their capacity for long-term planning — is itself substantially shaped by childhood environment and temperament, all of which is brute luck; and yet I still want to use the choice/luck distinction to draw real policy lines, like treating being laid off differently from quitting, or compensating accident victims differently from voluntary risk-takers.

Please go through this set and tell me which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension and how badly, which tensions are merely apparent and dissolve on closer inspection, and what each genuine tension would cost to resolve in terms of what I'd have to give up. I'd like you to rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally serious, and for the most severe one, tell me what the least costly revision would be — and what that revision would then force me to concede elsewhere in the set. Finally, if there's a single revision that would clean up the most conflicts at once, identify it, but be honest if no such unifying fix exists and I instead face a genuine choice between rescuing different parts of my view. Please answer in American English, and structure the response as an audit with clearly numbered findings rather than a general essay.
