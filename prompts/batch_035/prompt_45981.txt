I've been working through Thomson's lamp — switched on at the half-minute mark, off at the three-quarter-minute mark, on again at the seven-eighths mark, and so on, with the question of whether it's on or off at the one-minute mark — and I've come to think the standard hand-wringing about it is misplaced. I'm fine with the part people usually object to first: the switching intervals form a convergent geometric series, so the whole infinite sequence of flips fits inside a single minute, and I take it the lamp has some definite state when the minute is up. My worry is about the philosophers' next move, the claim that the state is *indeterminate*. After each flip the lamp's state traces the sequence 1, 0, 1, 0, ..., which is exactly the sequence of partial sums of Grandi's series 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + ... Now, that series has no limit in the ordinary sense, but mathematics has perfectly respectable machinery for assigning it a value anyway: Cesàro summation gives 1/2, and Abel summation agrees. Physicists do this sort of thing routinely — renormalization assigns finite values to divergent expressions and yields empirically confirmed predictions — so it can't be illegitimate in principle. So why isn't the right answer simply that the lamp is "half on" at the one-minute mark, and why do philosophers treat the indeterminacy as a deep problem rather than as a failure to apply the available mathematics? One request: if the move I'm making trades on an equivocation — say, between assigning a value to a series for analytic convenience and describing a physical state — or if the setup smuggles in some other confusion about how summability methods work, tell me that plainly. I'd rather have the error exposed than be granted a "sense in which" the lamp is half on.
