I'm the medical director of a small crisis stabilization unit, and my intake team (mostly residents plus a couple of experienced nurses) triages between ten and fifteen new presentations a day, often at 2am with a worried family or a cop standing in the room, and whatever we decide has to get signed off by the on-call attending within the hour. Recently I reread Szasz — The Myth of Mental Illness, plus his later writing on coercion and psychiatric law — and I've been going back and forth on it. What sticks with me aren't the slogans but his actual distinctions: literal disease, meaning a pathological process in the body you can detect through signs and tests independent of what the person says or does, versus the metaphorical "disease" language psychiatry borrows; "illness" as a social role that legitimates exemptions and care, which is partly why families, courts, and schools keep wanting the label attached; most of what we see being what he called problems in living; and treatment as a contract between consenting adults rather than something done to someone. I'm about half convinced — the other half of me thinks that following him faithfully would one day get a patient killed — and I don't want my junior staff becoming idealogues in either direction.

So what I want is a procedure the intake clinician can actually run in the first fifteen minutes: the questions to ask, in order (about the person and their history, the physical exam and labs, and crucially about who is asking for what), what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "this is medicine, work it up as literal disease," where it lands on "this is a problem in living — offer help, don't impose it," and where it explicitly refuses to decide because the question is one of values, custody, or law rather than facts, and says so out loud. Please build in the branch that mattered to Szasz himself, the general paresis lesson: when a "mental" presentation turns out to have a bodily cause, it stops being psychiatry's metaphor and becomes literal medicine — so the procedure needs to say what makes an organic cause plausible enough to route someone to the full medical workup, without sending every sad or frightened person off for a CT.

Then run it on three cases. One: a 34-year-old who calls the unit herself, on her own initiative, asking for help with panic attacks that are wrecking her job. Two: parents arrive with their 22-year-old son, who has stopped bathing, says he hears his dead grandfather, insists he's fine, and wants to leave; they want him "treated" and are asking whether we can hold him. Three: a 58-year-old brought in by police after a week of escalating paranoia and mild confusion, no psychiatric history at all. For each, tell me what the procedure yields, and flag it if the answer is one I won't like. And be honest with me at the end: which steps are real tests, which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and what the whole thing cannot do. My guess is it can't weigh dangerousness and can't settle any question about holding people against their will — I want you to say plainly where those live, if not inside the procedure.
