For several years I have been trying to settle, by consulting my own reactions to concrete cases, whether compatriots have special claims on one another that they do not have on strangers abroad, and the verdicts I keep arriving at refuse to line up into a principle. I would like to know whether there is one that captures them, or close to it; what it implies for cases I have not considered; and, if it cannot save all my verdicts, which of them I should abandon.

The verdicts, roughly as I find them:

1. I think it is legitimate for my state to tax me to fund medical care and unemployment support for poor compatriots, even though the same money, sent abroad, would help people in far greater need far more effectively. The state may give compatriots priority in the distribution of benefits.
2. Yet I do not think a private employer may hire a less qualified compatriot over a better-qualified foreign applicant. Nationality may not figure in private hiring the way I just allowed it to figure in public spending, and I cannot see why the state's permission in the one case does not carry over to the firm in the other.
3. I do not think an emergency service may take nationality into account in rescues. If two swimmers are equally far out and equally in danger, the lifeboat goes to whoever is nearest, and a crew that passed over a drowning foreign fisherman in order to rescue a citizen at equal cost would be doing something close to monstrous.
4. But I accept, with very little unease, that a government may evacuate its own citizens first from a disaster zone when it has seats for only some of those present — that is, may do as an evacuator what a lifeboat crew may not do as a rescuer. I can feel the two cases pulling apart, but I do not know what separates them.
5. On the personal side: I think I may give to a local shelter rather than a more cost-effective charity abroad, and may favor my family and friends over strangers anywhere. But when the favoring rests on shared nationality alone — a businessman, with no personal ties to any of them, funding scholarships only for compatriots while rejecting equally needy foreign applicants — my reaction turns sharply negative, even though this is roughly what I let the state do in (1), and I cannot articulate why the individual's act strikes me as worse.
6. Whatever the basis of the claim, it cannot be contribution. I think a compatriot who has evaded taxes all his life retains the full claim, a newborn citizen has it immediately, and a foreign resident who has worked and paid taxes for thirty years without naturalizing has most of it — perhaps all. The case I keep going back and forth on is what the last sliver consists in: what, exactly, does the thirty-year resident gain at the citizenship ceremony, if anything?

So my question is this. Is there a single principle — something about shared institutions, or the state acting as an agent of a cooperative scheme its members author, or the difference between what an agent may do in its own name and what it may do in ours — that accommodates the state's priority in (1) and (4) while forbidding the employer's discrimination in (2) and the lifeboat's selectivity in (3)? If so, please state it precisely, clause by clause, and show which verdict motivates each clause. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and why it is those rather than the others that should go. Finally, I would like to know what the principle predicts for cases I have not listed: whether a prosperous region may secede and take its taxes with it; whether foreign aid is merely permissible for states or in some way owed; and whether a state may restrict immigration to protect compatriots' wages when the cost to the excluded foreigners is very large.
