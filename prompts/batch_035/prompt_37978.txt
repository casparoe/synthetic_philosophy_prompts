I spent thirty-nine years as an experimental radio astronomer before retiring, and my late arrival at philosophy has mostly consisted of applying old professional habits to new material. The chief of these habits is a refusal to accept a disagreement until it has been written down in a form in which something could refute it. My retirement reading group has now spent a year failing to meet that standard on Douglas Hofstadter's two decision experiments, the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery, and I would like you to produce the document that our two most committed disputants — one a convert to Hofstadter's superrationality, one an unbudging defender of textbook causal decision theory — ought to have produced after a serious adversarial collaboration.

So that no one needs the original columns at hand, here are the setups as I want them treated, with any departures from Hofstadter's exact apparatus deliberate:

1. The Platonia-style dilemma. Twenty participants are publicly named. Each may, by a stated deadline, either send the sponsor a single telegram or send nothing. If exactly one telegram arrives, its sender receives $1,000,000; if none arrives, or if two or more arrive, no one receives anything. Telegrams are free. Participants cannot communicate with one another beforehand.
2. The Luring Lottery, simplified. Anyone may submit any number of free entries by postcard. The $1,000,000 prize is divided equally among all entries received; if no entries are received, the prize goes unclaimed. There is no cap on entries, so one entrant flooding the mailbox drives every other entry's value toward zero.

Draft the joint statement, roughly 900 to 1200 words, in four parts:

A. A numbered section of agreed claims — about the formal structure of the two games, about what each side concedes the other has shown, and, importantly, about what superrationality actually prescribes in the Platonia-style dilemma, where agents reasoning identically will either all send (nothing to anyone) or all abstain (nothing to anyone). My two disputants do not even agree on whether superrationality issues any verdict there; the document should settle that among the agreed claims if they can honestly do so, or flag it as a live issue.

B. A disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated once in wording the superrationalist would accept for her own view and once in wording the causal theorist would accept for his, with no strawmen on either side.

C. A cruxes section. At minimum the document should address: whether the correlation between my choice and others' choices must be causal in order to bear on what I rationally ought to do; how similar two agents must be before the superrationalist's mirroring premise applies (bit-identical twins, two readers of the same technical column, or merely two humans); and whether superrationality is a norm for idealized agents, an empirical prediction about real ones, or a policy recommendation for us confused ones. For each crux, include a concrete test, argument, or case both parties would accept as bearing on it, with each party's prediction recorded in advance. Two tests of my own devising must be worked in: the twin-corridor experiment, in which the Luring Lottery is run simultaneously on a population recruited from readers of a single technical column and on a population drawn at random; and the open-ledger variant, in which the sponsor publishes, after each round, every entry together with the entrant's written justification, and entrants know in advance that this will happen. I want to know whether cooperation premised on mirrored reasoning survives the moment the mirrors can be inspected.

D. A closing note identifying which parts of the residual dispute, if any, are merely verbal.

The document must end with a single machine-readable line of the form \residual{...}, containing, in twenty-five words or fewer, the shortest formulation of the disagreement that both parties would still refuse to sign.
