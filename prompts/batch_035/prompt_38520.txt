I've been arguing with a friend about truth, and I'd like your help figuring out whether an argument I built against him actually works. He read somewhere that truth isn't a deep feature of the world — that saying "it is true that the kettle is on" contributes nothing beyond saying the kettle is on, and that the word's only real job is letting us endorse a whole batch of statements at once ("everything the witness said is true") without repeating them one by one. He calls this the deflationary view of truth and he's very taken with it. Last week I tried to argue him out of it and came up with the following, which has been bothering me ever since, because if it works it seems to prove far too much: (1) Belief has a point: beliefs can be correct or incorrect, and being correct is what having a belief is for in the first place. (2) The deflationist says truth is not a property that all true statements share — there is nothing in common between the kettle being on and 7 being prime except that each of them is the case; "truth" just lets us talk about all of them at once. (3) If there is no shared thing that my beliefs are supposed to match, then belief isn't aimed at anything; it is just a state I'm in, which happens to fit the world or not. (4) A state that isn't aimed at anything can't be corrected, since correcting something requires a standard to correct it by. (5) But we correct beliefs all the time, and we know perfectly well what we mean by that. (6) So there is a shared thing after all, and truth is a real property, not a mere manner of speaking.

My friend said fine, and then turned the argument around on me in two ways I can't quite shake off. First, he said that if my reasoning is good, then by the same logic there is no such thing as winning a chess game, since winning has no nature beyond the many different ways a game can end — no single state of the board that all wins have in common — and yet chess is obviously winnable and losable, and pointing at a finished game and saying who won is perfectly meaningful. Second, he said my argument proves too much in the other direction as well: on my reasoning the deflationist couldn't even hold the beliefs he needs in order to state his own theory, since step (3) applies to every belief whatsoever, not just to some special subclass. That last point feels like a cheap shot to me, but I can't articulate why.

So here is what I want from you. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher who works on truth and take this seriously as an argument, not as a beginner's confusion. Think it through carefully — perhaps sketch the candidate weak points first before committing to one — and then tell me exactly which premise or inference to give up, why that premise looked so plausible when I first wrote it down, and what rejecting it costs. I have the sense that something slips between (3) and (4), but every time I try to pin it down, (3) starts sounding undeniable and (4) starts sounding like plain common sense. If I have to choose, I'd most like to keep (5), and I'm rather fond of (1) as well. Also please tell me honestly whether my friend's chess analogy and his "can't even hold a belief" jab are fair extensions of my argument, or whether they miss what the argument was really about.
