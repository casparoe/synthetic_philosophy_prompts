You are a professional philosopher working in action theory and bioethics, with deep familiarity with the doing/allowing and killing/letting-die literature — Foot, Rachels, Quinn, Kamm, Bennett, and their successors. I want you to build a thought experiment to specifications, and then demonstrate that it meets them. Think carefully before writing: begin with a short brainstorm of candidate background facts that could earn the classifications I demand, then construct the cases, then show the machinery working.

The assignment: a matched pair of ICU cases, engineered so that, under the standard philosophical analysis of the killing/letting-die distinction:

(1) In the first case, the agent performs a definite positive bodily movement that is the proximate trigger of the patient's death — and yet the case is correctly classified as *letting die*, not killing.

(2) In the second case, the agent performs no bodily movement at all during the entire relevant window — and yet the case is correctly classified as *killing*, not letting die.

The purpose of the pair is to test whether the killing/letting-die line, as it is actually used in medical ethics, tracks morally significant structure — defaults and baselines, initiation versus interruption, special duties, reliance, use of the patient's condition as a means — rather than the crude act/omission line. That purpose fixes what counts as cheating: a construction that gets its verdicts only by tendentious narration ("the nurse murdered him by doing nothing") fails, as does one that smuggles the killing into an earlier act of the agent and then calls the later inaction itself the killing without argument. Each classification must be earned from the standard analytic machinery and shown to follow.

Constraints: both cases take place in the same intensive care unit and involve the same kind of agent, a nurse; only ordinary equipment and drugs, no futuristic devices and no fantastic villains; each case is one paragraph. Across the pair, hold fixed the patient's diagnosis, prognosis, and stated wishes, and the nurse's ultimate aim. To keep you honest, you may not reach for the familiar moves: no ventilator-switch-off, no Rachels-style bathtub pair, no trolley. If your best construction must lean on a borderline device — for instance a positive act whose content is an omission, such as signing or declining a prompt, or a lethal process the agent set up earlier — say so explicitly and assess whether it still counts.

After presenting the pair: (a) walk through the classification argument case by case, showing which background fact earns each verdict; (b) identify the single feature doing the most work in each case, and state what happens to the classification when that feature is flipped; (c) give the strongest resistance a defender of the moral significance of the killing/letting-die distinction would mount against your pair; and (d) take a definite position, with reasons, on the final question: in this mirror-image pair, is the killing-by-inaction morally worse than the letting-die-by-doing, or are they on a par — and what does your answer suggest the distinction is really tracking?

Commit throughout. Where a step in your construction or analysis is contestable, flag it briefly — but still choose. Don't lay out the options and leave the verdicts to me.
