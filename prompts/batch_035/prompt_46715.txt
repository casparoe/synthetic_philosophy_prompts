I lead a Sunday-morning discussion group at my local community center — a dozen or so regulars, mostly retired professionals (an engineer, two nurses, a former high-school history teacher), all sharp readers of popular science but with no philosophy behind them. Next month I want to walk them through the idea, associated with Daniel Dennett, of a free-floating rationale: a real reason why something is built or behaves as it is, which nobody designed, nobody formulated, and nobody has to grasp in order for it to do its explanatory work. The fact I most want them to leave with is that the rationale for a killdeer's broken-wing act — luring a predator away from the vulnerable nest — was there and doing its job for millions of years before any human articulated it, and no bird ever needed to know it. I'm not asking you to write the explanation itself; I'm asking for the plan for delivering it in about fifty minutes with a flip chart.

Work out the order the ideas have to come in. My instinct is that before I can even utter the phrase without half the room hearing "Mother Nature's secret intentions," they need some distinction firmly in place — between a reason that is represented in somebody's mind and a reason that explains a pattern regardless — but I'm honestly unsure whether that comes before or after they accept that a mindless process can produce things that are for something. So lay out the dependency structure and settle that for me. Then tell me which single example should carry the whole session, and what it must have and must lack: is the killdeer too theatrical, and should I steer clear of the eye-for-seeing type of example precisely because it's the one thing several of them half-remember from school and will half-recite? Map the misconceptions as they'll actually arise, in the order they'll arise — I'm betting on "so the bird does know why, deep down," "so this is just Mother Nature under another name," and "so nothing has a real reason unless a person states it" — and tell me at exactly which step in the sequence each one should be preempted rather than repaired after it has formed. Give me one closing question whose answers will tell me who has actually got it; I've been toying with asking whether the Grand Canyon has a free-floating rationale for existing, but I suspect that one separates the confident from the correct rather than the understanding from the confused, so propose something better or fix it. Finally, name what I should openly decline to get into at this level and say so to the group — I assume the question of whether free-floating rationales could ground normativity, make something actually good or right, is beyond this format, but perhaps there is something subtler I should also refuse.
