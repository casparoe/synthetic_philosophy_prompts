I write novels for a living, and somewhere along the way I became a true believer in Bernard Williams's view that all reasons for action are internal reasons. So: saying you have a reason to do something is true only if it hooks up to your subjective motivational set — your existing desires, commitments, dispositions — through some chain of correct practical reasoning. If nothing in you could get you there even in principle, then you simply have no reason, however much the rest of us might want to insist you do. This is genuinely load-bearing for my job: it's why I can't just have a charachter "realise" she owes her sister an apology, I have to build the motivational bridge first or the scene reads fake. I've even staked a plot on the strong version — a man who survives an illness that rewires what he cares about, and the question of whether the new him was given reasons or just given desires that impersonate them.

So, what would count against the view? Not the lazy "but morality!" stuff — I want the real defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Cases it has to be able to describe: the fully informed amoralist who hears every arguement and shrugs; the person whose motivational set has been emptied by despair or padded out by manipulation; epistemic reasons, if internalism about practical reasons says anything about them at all. And the structural worries: whether the argument against external reasons is circular, and whether "correct deliberation" smuggles normativity back in through the side door. Which of these would internalists themselves concede as damage, and which would they wave off?

One more thing: please keep what's settled apart from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this — e.g. how to read Williams's own argument is fought over, so don't hand me one reading as if it were consensus. And British English throughout, if you don't mind.
