In my consciousness reading group we have been going through global workspace theory and Block's distinction between access and phenomenal consciousness, and one member keeps deploying Dennett's line that consciousness is "fame in the brain" — cerebral celebrity — as if it were a knockdown argument. Most recently she used it to dismiss the whole overflow debate: if consciousness just is wide cerebral celebrity, then asking whether the Sperling-type phenomenology of a briefly flashed array outruns what we can report is like asking whether someone can be famous before anyone has heard of them, and the question dissolves. I have the sense that the slogan is doing several different jobs at once and only some of them earn her that dismissal, but I can't sort out which. Could you lay out, as a list I can bring to the next meeting, the distinct precise theses the fame metaphor could be expressing — from the near-trivial (conscious contents are, as a matter of fact, the widely available ones) through substantive functionalist claims (global availability is what consciousness is, or is what explains everything we care about in consciousness) to the radical eliminativist reading (there is no phenomenal residue beyond accessibility for anything to overflow) — including any readings that turn on features of the fame analogy itself, such as that fame is a matter of degree, that it is constituted by actual influence over time rather than a momentary state, or that there is no sharp fact about when fame begins. For each reading, say whether it is defensible, whether it is merely terminological, and, crucially, whether it would actually support dismissing the overflow question as confused — since my worry is that the readings on which the slogan is clearly true are exactly the ones too weak to dismiss anything, and the ones strong enough to dismiss it owe us an argument rather than a metaphor.
