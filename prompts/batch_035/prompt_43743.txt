I'm a journalist who writes long-form features on science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the argument — most closely associated with David Pearce's hedonistic imperative — that we should abolish suffering throughout the living world by re-engineering the reward circuitry of sentient creatures, starting with our own. Whenever this idea surfaces in public discussion, someone reaches for Huxley's Brave New World within a paragraph or two, as though the novel were a settled refutation: a society that has abolished suffering and, with it, everything that makes life worth living. My editor wants me to take that reflex seriously rather than wave it away, so that is what I'm asking you to do: read Brave New World through the lens of the hedonistic imperative and tell me whether the novel actually refutes Pearce's project, or whether the two are largely talking past each other.

Please be rigorous in both directions. On one side, show where Huxley's World State is a poor stand-in for anything Pearce proposes: soma is a chemical sledgehammer that flattens affect rather than recalibrating it, whereas Pearce's scheme is supposed to preserve information-sensitivity to the world — motivational gradients without the raw negative valence — and the castes are engineered for contentment within confined roles in a planned economy, not for open-ended flourishing. The novel's own society hasn't even abolished suffering: Bernard Marx is resentful and alienated, Helmholtz Watson is restless, and both are quietly miserable. On the other side, don't let Pearce's defenders off too easily. The confrontation between Mustapha Mond and John the Savage in the final chapters is the strongest form of the objection, because Mond's defense of the regime rests on a tradeoff Pearce denies exists: society chose happiness over truth, beauty, and high art, since those feed on suffering and instability, and John's insistence that he wants the full human package — including misery — strikes most readers as noble rather than deranged. If it does, that suggests a psychological fact the abolitionist has to confront: we appear wired to valorize suffering and its byproducts. Is that wiring a constraint to respect, or just one more bug to fix, and does the novel give us resources to decide?

For my piece I need three things. First, the specific scenes and characters I should point readers to, with an account of what each one shows or fails to show about the argument. Second, an honest account of where the novel resists being used this way — where Huxley is satirizing consumerism, mass production, and totalitarian social control rather than paradise engineering as such, so that invoking him against Pearce is close to a category error; it's worth considering whether Huxley's own later utopian novel, Island, tells against reading him as an enemy of engineered happiness at all. Third, and most important for my conclusion: what does Brave New World add to this debate that the philosophical arguments alone cannot — and if the honest verdict is that it is a brilliant attack on a different target, say so plainly, while if it stages a genuine objection Pearce has never fully answered, say that instead. Keep it accessible to educated general readers, glossing any technical vocabulary, and give me argument and scene analysis rather than a bibliography.
