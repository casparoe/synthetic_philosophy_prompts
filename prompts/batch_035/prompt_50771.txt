I am a senior at a high school that offers a semester-long elective in philosophy, and this term our teacher has asked me to serve as a peer grader for the written homework. The current unit is on rational choice, and this week's assignment covered Douglas Hofstadter's idea of superrationality, which he introduced in his Scientific American column and later collected in his book Metamagical Themas. Every student answered the same three-part question. Before I commit to a grade and write my comments on one classmate's response, I would like a second opinion. Please read the question and the answer below, assign a letter grade with a percentage, and give me the point-by-point comments that justify it: where the answer earns credit, where it loses credit, and precisely why. If you think the question itself is ambiguous in a way that affects how the answer should be scored, please flag that as well. Finally, since our teacher insists that a careful grader knows the weak spots in their own grading, please say how confident you are in each of your judgments and single out the one point you are least sure about.

The homework question was:

"This week we discussed Hofstadter's notion of superrationality. Answer all three parts.

(i) In your own words, explain what superrationality is supposed to add to ordinary game-theoretic rationality in the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, and why two superrational players would cooperate with each other. For reference, use the standard payoffs: mutual cooperation gives each player 3, mutual defection gives each 1, unilateral defection gives the defector 5 and the cooperator 0.

(ii) A superrational player S is now paired with a player R who is an ordinary payoff maximizer: whatever S does, R will defect, and S knows this about R. Should S cooperate in this game? Explain your reasoning.

(iii) Finally, S plays against another superrational player, T, but the payoff matrix is no longer symmetric. If both cooperate, S gets 3 and T gets 4. If S cooperates while T defects, S gets 0 and T gets 6. If S defects while T cooperates, S gets 5 and T gets 0. If both defect, each gets 1. Both players know the full matrix, and each knows the other is superrational. Does superrational reasoning still support cooperation here, or does the payoff asymmetry undermine it? Explain."

The classmate's answer, which I must grade, reads as follows:

"(i) Ordinary rationality says to defect, because defecting earns you more no matter what the other player does. Hofstadter's superrationality says this analysis misses something when the two players are reasoning in exactly the same way about exactly the same situation. If you and your counterpart are symmetric reasoners, you will necessarily arrive at the same decision — it is impossible for one superrational player to cooperate while the other defects — so the real choice open to you is between the outcome where both cooperate and the outcome where both defect. Since both players prefer mutual cooperation (3 each) to mutual defection (1 each), the superrational player cooperates. My cooperation is what brings my counterpart's cooperation into existence: in deciding to cooperate, I am at the same time causing the other player, who reasons exactly as I do, to cooperate as well. That is the extra element superrationality adds. It turns what looked like a one-move causal decision into something more like a joint decision, as if a single mind were controlling both hands.

(ii) S should cooperate. Superrationality is not a strategy one switches on and off depending on the opponent; it is a fixed feature of what kind of reasoner you are. If S defected against R, S would be abandoning the very reasoning that justified cooperating in part (i), which would make S inconsistent. Cooperating against R does cost S something, but consistency is the whole point of the view: a superrational player is someone who cooperates because that is what reasoners of that kind do, not because cooperation happens to pay off in a particular pairing.

(iii) The asymmetry does not undermine the reasoning, because what superrationality requires is that the two players reason identically, not that they be rewarded identically. The numbers differ, but both players can see that their deliberations run in lockstep, so their choices will still match: either both cooperate or both defect. Both prefer mutual cooperation (3 and 4) to mutual defection (1 and 1), so both cooperate. What would genuinely break superrationality is if one of the players stopped being superrational, not if the payoffs happened to be uneven."
