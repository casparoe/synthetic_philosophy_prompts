I need some kind of verdict on a sentence that is being used, around me, as an all-purpose counsel of acceptance. The sentence is the last line of Camus's essay The Myth of Sisyphus (published 1942): "One must imagine Sisyphus happy." I should say up front that I have read the essay, some years ago, and remember its shape: it opens by declaring that judging whether life is worth living is the fundamental problem of philosophy and suicide the one serious subject; it argues that the universe's silence meets our demand for meaning in a confrontation it calls the absurd; it derives from lucidity about that confrontation a triple discipline of revolt, freedom, and passion; and it closes by retelling the myth — Sisyphus condemned to roll his boulder uphill forever, the boulder always rolling back down — with the observation (just before the famous line) that the struggle itself suffices to fill a man's heart, and then with the sentence in question. What I cannot do is turn that sentence into the different claims it might be making, and that is what I need from you.

The immediate occasion: I recently complained, over coffee, to a colleague about the tedium of my work — a job that is repetitive, largely pointless by my own lights, and unlikely to change. He answered with the line, and glossed it as follows: the point is that happiness is available inside meaningless toil; you don't need the work to be meaningful or even to change anything; you need to adjust your attitude toward it. The tone was consoling but also a little closing: as though the sentence had settled whether I should stay. I have since seen it used the same way in career-advice pieces, in a graduation speech, and tattooed on the forearm of someone at my gym. But I have also seen it cited, in discussions of the essay, with visible puzzlement — as if the line were a puzzle about how we could be asked to imagine happy a man being tortured forever, or as if the imperative were doing something strange for an essay that had been arguing, not prescribing daydreams.

So: what precise claims could that one sentence be making? I want the readings laid out as a menu, each stated as a distinct thesis, and each with a verdict — trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive and dubious, or false — together with a note on whether the people who repeat it probably mean that reading. For each, please also say whether it would actually support my colleague's use: the counsel that one should stay in meaningless toil and adjust one's attitude, since happiness is available there. A few distinctions I suspect the menu will need, though I leave the carving to you:

1. The grammatical oddity first. The sentence is an imperative about an act of imagination — it tells us what we must imagine — not an indicative report about what Sisyphus is. So one family of readings will concern the reader's interpretive act rather than Sisyphus's condition: perhaps a rule about how to read the myth (I recall the essay suggesting that fate becomes tragic only in the rare moments when it is conscious, which would make the imagination of Sisyphus's consciousness, or the refusal of it, load-bearing for whether the story is a tragedy at all). Does that reading make the line a claim about fiction and about us, and does it then support anything at all about my job?

2. Another family will concern Sisyphus himself: that he is, in fact, happy — some claim about his actual psychology, his scorn for the gods, the thought that his contempt makes his fate look conquered. Is that reading an assertion we could only accept on the essay's own terms, and what exactly would make it true rather than stipulated?

3. A third family will generalize: that we are in Sisyphus's position, and that happiness of a certain defiant kind is available in lucid, unillusioned persistence. What is that happiness supposed to consist in, is it the same thing as contentment, and — this is my real worry — does the quietist reading my colleague gave it (settle in, adjust your attitude) actually conflict with the essay's revolt, or is it a legitimate descendant of it?

Please end by telling me which reading the consoling use genuinely needs, and whether, on your verdicts, that reading survives. Set it out as a list I can bring to my reading group; we meet on Thursday, and I would rather arrive with a disambiguation than with a tattoo.
