My flatmate and I spent most of Sunday evening arguing about something that happened while I was away, and I would like a considered adjudication of it, because the argument has left me unsure not only about who owes whom what, but about whether either of us was reasoning soundly at all.

The facts: I was away for three weeks caring for a relative. I left my dog, who has a heart condition requiring a daily pill, with my flatmate, Sam. I asked him directly, he agreed, and I posted the schedule on the fridge. A neighbour, Jo, had separately offered to help out if anything came up while I was gone, and Sam knew she had. Midway through the second week, Sam realized he had forgotten the pills for several days running. Rather than tell me — he says he didn't want to worry me at a difficult time — he simply resumed and said nothing. The dog was visibly worse by my return, spent two nights at the vet, and the bill was large. The vet could not say whether the dog would have declined anyway, since the condition is progressive, only that the lapse can't have helped.

Sam accepts that he forgot but denies everything beyond that. His position, which he pressed for two hours, is: (1) forgetting is not doing; he didn't do anything to the dog, the condition did it, and the vet's own uncertainty proves as much; (2) if his not giving a pill counts as a cause of the decline, then so do Jo's not checking in, mine for leaving at all, and in principle every person in the city who didn't drive over with a pill — an endless list, which he takes as a reductio of the whole way of thinking; and (3) at worst he botched a favour he was doing me, and the remedy for a botched favour is an apology, not a share of the vet bill — which is what I told him I thought he owed.

My position, which I found much harder to defend than I expected, is that Sam's forgetting doesn't stand in the same row as Jo's not checking in or a stranger's not driving over: he took the task on, I organized the trip around his having done so, and there came a moment when telling me would have set things right and he chose silence instead. But when he demanded to know what makes his not-acting a cause of the harm rather than just a broken promise, I found I couldn't give an answer that didn't slide from "he's to blame" into "he did it," and I could feel the whole argument turning on that slide.

So I would like a definite verdict, not a neutral survey of positions, on three questions. First, is Sam right that if his not giving the pill caused the decline, the causes must then multiply without end — and if he is wrong, what principled line separates his not-acting from Jo's and from the strangers'? Second, do the two distinctions I was groping toward — that he had taken the task on, and that his later silence is what kept me from setting things right — do any causal work in this case, or only moral work? Third, given the vet's honest uncertainty about whether the dog would have declined regardless, is it even coherent to hold Sam responsible for a portion of the bill, and if so, on what basis should we think about dividing it?

Since I intend to raise this with Sam one more time, please also tell me plainly where your own reasoning is most likely to fail, and what — some further fact about the case, or some argument Sam could still give — would move you to the opposite verdict.
