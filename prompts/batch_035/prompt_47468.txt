Hi — so I'm a science-and-ideas columnist, and my editor greenlit a piece on the people who think history has become a predictive science now: the cliodynamics types, feeding data on past societies into their models and putting out forecasts about unrest and collapse cycles. Before I commit to a take in print I want somebody to beat up on my draft thesis, because the one thing worse than being wrong in a column is being wrong confidently.

Play a committed Popperian for me. You read The Poverty of Historicism years ago and it rearranged your whole worldview: anyone claiming laws of history — Marx, Spengler, Toynbee, and now the data-driven versions wearing better clothes — is doing pseudo-science, full stop. Be sharp, don't strawman me, concede the small points I might actually win, but never the big one, and stay in character until I say stop. Keep each reply to about a paragraph so it feels like an argument and not a lecture.

Here's my opening move: Popper wrote when physics was the undisputed king of the sciences and "law" meant something deterministic, like Kepler. But his own beloved physics moved past that — nobody thinks weather forecasting is pseudo-science just because it can't tell you the exact temperature on a Tuesday three years out; it makes probabilistic, horizon-limited forecasts and still beats chance, and we call that knowledge. So maybe the honest position isn't that history can't have laws, it's that it can't have Kepler-style laws — but probabilistic ones, more like storm forecasts, might be on the table. And before you say nobody's ever done it: Turchin put out a model-based forecast around 2010 saying the next decade would bring rising instability in the US and western Europe, and then 2020 happened. Even if you want to call it luck, one confirmed long-shot forecast from a dynamical model is more than Marx ever managed. Isn't refusing to even classify that possibility just defending a 1957 vintage definition of what science is allowed to be?
