So heres the deal: I'm a retired protein crystallographer (40-odd years, mostly membrane proteins, don't ask) and since retiring I've gotten properly into philosophy for the first time. Reading popper, kuhn, some lakatos, even Weinberg's "Dreams of a Final Theory" which I somehow missed when it came out. I've written up some thoughts as a draft essay and I want comments before I send it off to a philosophy-of-science mailing list my son talked me into joining. Be brutal.

Title: "Forty years at the beamline and not one philosopher in the lab"

Draft:

"When I finally retired I had time to read the philosophy of science I'd always pretended to know. And my honest conclusion after four decades of actually doing science is that philosophy of science had nothing to do with any of it. Not once in my career did anyone I worked with change what they did on Monday morning because of something a philosopher wrote. You learn the method the way you learn baking: apprenticeship. Which controls to run, which datasets smell off, who to trust as a referee. No book of epistemology teaches you that.

Popper gets taught as how science works, but falsificationism describes almost none of the science I saw. A good theory survives anomalies — Newton did, continental drift did, half the models I cared about did — and if we'd abandoned ship at the first contrary data point nothing would ever get solved. I gather the philosophers even have a name for this, the Duhem–Quine thing, which makes you wonder why the pop account still gets taught to first years.

The big guns are with me here. Hawking opened The Grand Design by declaring that philosophy is dead. Weinberg wrote a whole chapter called Against Philosophy where his verdict was basically that philosophy has had consequences for physics, but they were bad ones — as I remember it he blamed positivist scruples about unobservables for slowing down quantum field theory and even chaos theory (need to double check that). And then there's the famous line about philosophy of science being as useful to scientists as ornithology is to birds, which everybody pins on Feynman even though I've never seen an actual source for it, which maybe tells you something about how carefully this debate gets conducted.

Where I wobble: quantum foundations. Once a field runs out of experiments, its questions turn philosophical, and some of the best work on the interpretation of quantum mechanics is being done by card-carrying philosophers publishing in physics journals. So maybe the right thesis isn't 'philosophy is useless to science' but 'philosophy is what the frontiers of science become when the data dries up.' That feels a bit too neat though. Like something you'd embroider on a pillow."

Questions for you:

1. Where is the argument weakest? I want the strongest objection someone on that mailing list would raise, not a polite one.
2. Is the ornithology quip actually Feynman's, and is my memory of what Weinberg blamed in that chapter right? If I've got the history wrong somewhere, I'd rather hear it from you than from the list.
3. Is my Popper paragraph fair, or am I beating a dead horse? It occured to me that philosophers themselves gave up on naive falsificationism decades ago, in which case I'm arguing with people who already conceded.
4. Does the quantum foundations bit at the end rescue the essay or is it just me hedging because I secretly like the philosophers I've been reading?

Also just tell me straight whether this is interesting or just a retired guy venting. I can take it.
