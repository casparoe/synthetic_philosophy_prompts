A friend of mine — a systems engineer who has spent a decade on the edges of the animal-welfare world and argues the way an engineer argues, treating every food as a spreadsheet of deaths per calorie and suffering per calorie — has built up a stable track record of verdicts about what he will and won't eat, and I want to predict his rulings on some new cases before I put them to him, partly for a running bet and partly because I genuinely cannot tell which rule he is running. His verdicts so far: (1) he eats beef but not chicken, on the ground that a single cow spreads one death across hundreds of thousands of calories while a broiler covers only a few thousand, and that a broiler's life is worse than any life a beef animal has; (2) he eats dairy and backyard eggs but not commercial eggs, because of what the layer industry does to male chicks at hatch; (3) he eats grains, vegetables, and lentils without hesitation, though he knows cultivation kills rodents, snakes, and insects — including in the feed grown for the cattle he eats — and says the field deaths are a tragedy but not his kills the way slaughter is; (4) he eats honey, has eaten roasted crickets at a conference, and has said that if he ever gives up beef it will be for oysters and mussels rather than plants, since whatever happens to a bivalve is below the line; (5) he has said he'd have no objection in principle to venison from a deer that would otherwise starve in a hard winter, though he has never actually been offered any. The new cases: (a) a farm near him now sells beef from cattle that graze unplowed rangeland year-round and receive no purchased feed, so that no crop cultivation sits anywhere in the supply chain — is that, for him, the most ethical food there is, or does it change nothing? (b) the state wildlife agency culls deer each winter to prevent starvation and gives the venison away free to anyone who will take it — will he take it? (c) a startup is selling protein flour made from black soldier fly larvae raised on food waste — will he treat it as a moral breakthrough or as an arithmetic atrocity? I am less interested in the three predictions than in what sits underneath them: the candidate rules that fit his track record (deaths per calorie above some sentience line, intended killing versus collateral harm, suffering versus death as the currency, a capacity threshold that exempts invertebrates), where those rules agree and where they diverge on my cases, your best guess on each with your reasons, and the single further question I could put to him over dinner that would most cleanly expose which rule he is actually running. As you go, please keep straight what is settled among people who have worked the numbers — the order-of-magnitude arithmetic of deaths per calorie, the way feed conversion stacks crop deaths behind every animal product — and what is genuinely contested even among informed people, such as the relative moral weights of different species, whether death or suffering is the currency, and whether collateral harms may be discounted against intended ones; do not present the contested half as though it were the settled half. Please answer in American English.
