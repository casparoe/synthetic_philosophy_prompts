One part of most legal systems treats vagueness as a fatal defect: courts will strike down a criminal statute as void for vagueness when its terms fail to give people of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is prohibited and fail to constrain enforcement officials with determinate standards. Another part treats vagueness as an ordinary resource: the same systems routinely uphold statutes built on predicates like reasonable, due care, cruel, or obscene, and legislatures cure vagueness worries by stipulating sharp thresholds — a fixed blood-alcohol limit, a fixed age of majority — whose arbitrariness at the margins is openly admitted. What account of vagueness does this bundle of practices presuppose? Consider in particular whether the fair-notice requirement fits epistemicism, on which vague terms have sharp boundaries that we simply cannot know, so that a vague criminal statute prohibits a determinate but unknowable range of conduct; or supervaluationism, on which borderline applications lack truth value, so that a vague prohibition fails to bind at all in penumbral cases; or whether the practice is compatible with several theories of vagueness and needs none in particular. Reconstruct the implicit view, say whether the three parts of the practice presuppose something consistent, identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and explain what would have to change in adjudication or legislative drafting if the presupposed view were replaced by its rival.
