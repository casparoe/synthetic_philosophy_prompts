I am a machine learning engineer who works on offline reinforcement learning and model-based planning. I am fluent in counterfactual reasoning, off-policy evaluation, do-calculus, distribution shift, and the distinction between a simulator and a fitted model, and I know enough philosophy of religion to follow the technical vocabulary. Restate Molinism in the terms I actually use, and then tell me what does not survive the translation.

The view to translate, as I understand it: Molinism, associated with Luis de Molina, holds that God's knowledge includes middle knowledge — knowledge of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom: true conditional claims about what any free creature would freely do in any circumstance in which God might place it. These conditionals are contingent, are not settled by God's will, and are prior to his creative decree. God surveys the space of possible worlds fixed by these conditionals, selects one to actualize, and thereby exercises meticulous providence over the outcome without determining any creature's choices. The perennial worry is the grounding objection: what makes a claim about what someone would freely do in a circumstance that never obtains true, given that nobody ever acts in that circumstance?

Do the translation systematically. God becomes a planner with a world-model; middle knowledge becomes a family of counterfactual queries of the form: what would this agent do if placed in this state, on branches where no training trajectory ever reached that state; the decree becomes the choice of an environment design and initial conditions; meticulous providence becomes closed-loop planning through the counterfactual branches; the grounding objection becomes the question of where the labels for off-distribution branches could come from. Also translate the main rivals and objections: the simple-foreknowledge view (God merely observes the actual trajectory in advance and does not plan through unrealized alternatives); the Boethian or Thomist move (God is atemporal, so his knowledge is not counterfactual at all but something like a read-out of the actual run from outside time); and the complaint that middle knowledge is either covertly deterministic, because the conditionals fix what the creature will do, or vacuous, because nothing grounds them.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the debate map cleanly onto things my field has names for — off-policy evaluation, extrapolation error, the impossibility of evaluating a policy under conditions you never sampled, identifiability, model misspecification? Which parts translate only with distortion, and say precisely what gets distorted? Is there a residue with no expression at all in my vocabulary — for instance, the claim that the counterfactuals of freedom are true prior to and independently of God's choice, and would remain true for worlds God never actualizes? If so, does that residue indicate something my vocabulary is missing, such as a notion of truth for unperformed interventions that is not estimation from data, or does it indicate that the theological disagreement is less substantive than it looks? Be scrupulous about the direction in which the translation could mislead: do not smuggle in the assumption that the world-model is fitted to observed data if that assumption is doing work the Molinist would reject, and flag it explicitly where it is the real crux.

Please write your answer in British English, in a relatively formal register.
