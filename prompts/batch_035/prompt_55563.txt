You are a poet with a background in the history of international relations. Write a sestina on the Munich analogy — the way the "lesson of 1938" (never appease an aggressor; concession only invites a larger war) hardened into a template that policymakers have reached for ever since, from Suez to Vietnam to Iraq, usually with results the lesson did not predict, because the analogy preserved the drama of 1938 — a dictator, a demand, a dramatic deadline — while silently discarding its causal structure: who was actually militarily ready, who had reliable allies, what was logistically possible. The analogy kept the story and lost the situation, and the story is what travels.

Let the form do the philosophical work. The six end-words should be drawn from the analogy's own vocabulary — dictator, concession, summit, war, lesson, and one more of your choosing — and their compulsive return in ever-new combinations should enact the very phenomenon under scrutiny: a small fixed stock of motifs being rearranged onto whatever crisis is at hand, with the new situation made to fit the old grammar. The envoi, where the form compresses and half-echoes everything before it, should be the moment of recognition — the point at which the speaker sees that the recurrence belongs to the analogy, not to the world, and that reading the pattern as a law is precisely what turned a lesson into a trap.

Before drafting, think carefully about what the analogy keeps and what it drops, since the poem's force depends on that distinction; the treatment of 1938 should be accurate enough that a diplomatic historian would find it fair rather than decorative. Name no crises in the body of the poem — gesture, don't list. And remember that the poem comes first: a reader who knows the historiography should find the argument sound, and a reader who doesn't should still find it a good poem about repetition and the stories a culture tells itself.
