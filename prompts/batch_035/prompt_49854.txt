I direct a mid-sized annual literary prize: around 180 submitted novels, five judges, three months of reading, a public shortlist of six, a single winner, and a press release we have to defend in interviews. Our current method is horse-trading. Every year produces the same fights: two judges adore a formally daring novel the other three find cold and hollow; a sentimental crowd-pleaser splits the panel between those moved by it and those who consider it manipulative; and there is always one immaculately crafted book whose moral vision half the panel finds repellent. We resolve these by exhaustion and compromise, not by any principle, and our shortlists read accordingly.

I have become convinced that the most promising theory for our situation is Hume's, in his 1757 essay on the standard of taste: beauty judgments are not arbitrary, because the joint verdict of qualified judges — those with practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, and delicacy — tracks real features of works, and the verdicts of the qualified eventually converge on the great works even where the unqualified diverge. But an essay addressed to eighteenth-century connoisseurs is not something a working panel can run. Please translate it into a procedure we can actually operate, with the following components.

1. Judge selection. We must choose the five before we have seen any of their verdicts on these books. Specify the observable proxies you would substitute for practice, comparison, and freedom from prejudice — breadth of reading, a track record of public reviewing, demonstrated willingness to reverse an earlier judgment, financial and personal conflicts — and be explicit about where each substitution is weakest. Also address the circularity worry directly: if we select judges whose taste we already approve of, the standard has collapsed into our prior preferences, and Hume has given us nothing independent. Say whether the procedure can answer this or only contain it.

2. Deliberation protocol. Hume concedes that even the best judges disagree, and that their disagreement is widest about lesser works. Specify what this licenses in our setting: whether judges must articulate reasons before scoring, whether scores precede or follow discussion, how a judge registers a considered dissent on the shortlist, and at what point the protocol must stop and declare an issue a values question that a procedure cannot settle.

3. The morally difficult cases. Hume appears to hold that a work's moral character affects its aesthetic standing, which our panel cannot agree on. Give the procedure a rule for the two directions: works of high craft with a vision the panel finds repellent, and works of clumsy craft with an admirable heart. The rule should be usable by judges who have never read a word of aesthetics.

4. The distortions. Say plainly which parts of Hume's theory you had to drop or bend to make the procedure usable at all, and what each distortion costs us — where the simplified procedure will misfire, and which distinctions simply cannot survive into a checklist without becoming vacuous.

Then run the procedure on three cases drawn from our recent years. (a) A formally brilliant novel that one judge described as a machine for impressing critics: technically immaculate, emotionally inert, without a single scene anyone would reread. (b) A sprawling sentimental novel containing four superb chapters and a bloated remainder, beloved by librarians and booksellers. (c) A novel of merely adequate prose whose final movement one judge called the most moving thing she had read in a decade, while two others dismissed it as engineered tearjerking. Show the procedure's output at each stage for these three, and ensure that at least one comes out in a way I will not like — identify which, and explain why the procedure nonetheless says it.

Finally, state the limits: what questions about our prize does this procedure silently refuse to answer, and where must the director simply decide?
