Ok so I'm a software engineer, zero formal philosophy training, but I've gotten weirdly obsessed with a question that keeps coming up in arguments at work: who actually deserves a vote on a thing? Like, our team ships an recommender system that ends up being used in a country none of us live in, and it arguably shifts an election there. Did those people somehow "deserve" a say over it? Nobody can give me a principled answer, everyone just vibes it.

I've since gathered there's an actual debate about this (the boundary problem, who belongs in the demos), and the two positions I keep circling between are: (a) everyone whose interests are significantly affected by a decision should get a say in it, versus (b) only people who are subject to the decision — bound by it, coerced by it, living under the rules it creates — get a say. I want to actually stress-test both.

So: write a dialogue between two smart, good-faith proponents of these views. Set it somewhere concrete rather than in the abstract — maybe two engineers on a train back from a public hearing about a planned datacentre that will suck water from an aquifer shared with the next county over, and the hearing only let residents of the host city vote on the referendum. Have the all-affected person push the point that the neighbouring county's farmers bear the cost so they get a say, and the all-subjected person push back that literally everyone is "affected" by everything if you trace the chains far enough (supply chains, climate, markets), so the affected-principle needs some cutoff, and any cutoff looks arbitrary — which is just the boundary problem one level up. Let the subjected person also get pressed hard: why is being bound by a law the magic threshold when a drone strike or a currency decision affects people who arent subject to your laws at all? And at some point have them notice that "who should vote" maybe isn't one question — maybe the demos for a water referendum and the demos for a national election shouldn't have the same boundary at all, and see whether they think that's a cop-out or the actual resolution.

Important bits:

- Both characters should be genuinely competent — no strawmen, and they should actually respond to each other's specific points, not just take turns monologuing. If one of them lands a punch, the other should visibly have to adjust.
- Don't have them name-drop philosophers or cite papers. I want it to feel like two sharp people working it out from scratch, not a literature review.
- Somewhere in there, get in the future people problem (people not born yet are affected by basically everything) and at least one scenario involving non-residents or cross-border effects, since that's what kicked off my interest.
- It can end unresolved, that's fine — honestly that would be more honest — but it should end with both of them clearer about what the real crux is.

Please write the whole thing in British English (so "neighbouring", "programme" where relevant, etc.). Length: something like a proper short piece, long enough for the argument to develop, not just a skirmish. Cheers!
