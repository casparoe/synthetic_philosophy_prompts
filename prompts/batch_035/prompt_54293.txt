You are a professional philosopher with a specialty in ancient ethics, and I'd like you to think carefully and methodically about a prediction problem, starting with some brainstorming before committing to an answer. I'm taking an introductory ethics course in which we've just finished the part of Plato's Republic where Glaucon revives the story of the ring of Gyges and argues that anyone who could act unjustly without fear of detection would do so, and that people are just only reluctantly, for the sake of reputation and from fear of punishment. My friends and I have been testing this on each other. One friend has declared herself firmly on Glaucon's side — she says the challenge is basically right and that all apparent justice is just restraint imposed by consequences — and we've started recording her concrete verdicts, because in practice they turn out to be far more discriminating than her professed theory.

Her track record so far, with every case stipulated to involve guaranteed impunity (no detection, no retaliation, and no one ever finding out): a typical person would quietly stop reporting cash income to the tax authority; would walk out of the store with an item the self-checkout failed to ring up; would take a second free sample when the sign says one per customer; would submit a padded expense report to a large corporation. On the other side: a typical person would not cheat on a devoted spouse even with guaranteed impunity; would not read a best friend's private journal even if purely out of curiosity and guaranteed never to be discovered; and would not sell the family piano after promising a now-deceased parent never to sell it, even though no one alive would ever learn of the sale. Notice that her strong Glauconist thesis — everyone defects under immunity — is already contradicted by her own verdicts, so whatever rule she is actually running must be something more selective.

Tomorrow we're putting a new case to her: a typical person who discovers they can take credit for a junior colleague's idea in a way that is undetectable and that causes the colleague to be passed over for a promotion she would otherwise have received — an identifiable victim with a face, but also something the person could rationalize to themselves as ordinary office competition, and no explicit promise or vow is being broken. Please predict her verdict; lay out the candidate rules that could fit her track record (for instance: defection against anonymous or diffuse victims but never against identifiable persons one has a relationship with; defection wherever the act can be rationalized as not really wrong versus abstention wherever the act would damage one's self-image as a decent person; abstention wherever an explicit commitment is at stake); explain what each rule predicts about the new case; and give your best guess, with your reasons for preferring it. Finally, tell me what further hypothetical, decided either way, would most cleanly separate the leading candidate rules, and say whether any recognizable version of Glaucon's challenge survives her own pattern of verdicts, or whether her track record already concedes that some restraint is internalized rather than imposed from outside.
