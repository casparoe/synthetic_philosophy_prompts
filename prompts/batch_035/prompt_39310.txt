I've been going down a rabbit hole on the Judy Benjamin problem and I feel like I half get it and half don't. Can you walk me through what the actual issue is, at gut level? Here's the version I've been playing with (slightly simplified from van Fraassen's original):

A soldier gets dropped in a swampy region split into two equal halves, Red territory and Blue territory, and she's equally likely to have landed in either. Red territory is divided into three equally sized districts — HQ district, 2nd battalion district, 3rd battalion district — so on landing her credences are 1/2 for Blue and 1/6 for each of the Red districts. She radios command, and the officer says: "if you're in Red territory, you're in the 2nd or 3rd battalion district, not the HQ one," and then goes off the air. That's all she learns.

So she's learned a conditional, and the textbook move is to treat its content as the corresponding material conditional — just "not (Red AND HQ district)" — rule out that one cell, renormalize. That leaves her at 3/5 for Blue and 1/5 for each of the two Red battalions. Here's what I can't get my head arond: why did learning something that only mattered *conditional on being in Red* make her more confident she's in Blue? The tip seems to say nothing about the Red/Blue split at all. It feels like it should leave the Red/Blue line alone (1/2 still?), or at least that's my gut, and I'd like to know where that intuition comes from and whether it's trustworthy.

What I want is intuition, not formalism-first. Why exactly does ruling out the one conjunctive cell pump probability into Blue — is it just renormalization spreading the excluded mass everywhere, is it that conditioning always rewards cells the evidence doesn't touch? And is there a principled alternative update that keeps the antecedent's probability fixed? I've heard there's a thing called imaging that supposedly does exactly this, and that treating conditionals as material conditionals gets tangled up with old triviality results about whether an if-then statement can have a probability of its own, but I've never seen either explained in a way I could actually feel. So: what maps to what, why does the standard update do the thing it does, and does imaging actually solve it or is it a hack with its own problems?

And the part I most want an honest answer on: is the puzzle maybe just underdetermined as stated? Does the right update depend on *how* the officer came to know the conditional — if he literally checked whether she's in HQ and said no, that feels like one update; if he's just reciting a map fact about where Red's HQ sits, that feels like another. Can ordinary Bayesian machinery even represent that difference, or is this a genuine hole in the framework?
