I recently inherited a modest sum and am deciding between a low-cost index fund and paying for active management. A cousin who trades professionally sent me the following summary to argue that active investing is pointless and that market prices are basically meaningless:

"If markets are informationally efficient, prices already reflect everything that is known. There is even a mathematical result, the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem, showing that rational traders can never profit from private information: the moment they attempt to trade on it, the attempt itself reveals the information to the other side, and the expected profit evaporates. So all actual trading must be either irrational or driven by non-informational needs like liquidity and hedging, and the enormous daily volume of the stock market is therefore evidence that prices are driven by noise rather than knowledge. It follows that prices cannot be trusted as a measure of what anything is really worth."

I suspect the summary is partly right and partly wrong, but I don't know enough to say where. Please go through it claim by claim: which parts a careful financial economist would accept, which are subtle misstatements of what the theorem actually establishes, and whether the final conclusion — that prices are not a trustworthy guide to value — follows from the accurate parts. Where a claim is technically true about the theorem but misleading as guidance for someone in my situation, please say so and explain the difference.
