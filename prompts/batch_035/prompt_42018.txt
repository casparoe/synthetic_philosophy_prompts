I'm a Year 13 student at a sixth-form college, and I run a small maths society that meets fortnightly to argue about puzzles slightly beyond the syllabus. Last week's session went badly off the rails, in the interesting way, and I've been asked by both participants to get an outside opinion, which I'm not competent to give. The topic was Bertrand's paradox: draw a circle, inscribe an equilateral triangle in it, draw a chord "at random", and ask for the probability that the chord is longer than the triangle's side. Marcus presented the three classical answers. If you pick two points independently on the circumference and join them, you get 1/3. If you pick a radius at random and then a point along it to serve as the chord's midpoint, you get 1/2. If you pick the midpoint uniformly from anywhere inside the disc, you get 1/4. He concluded that the principle of indifference — the rule that when you have no reason to favour one outcome over another you should assign them equal probabilities — is bankrupt: the same state of total ignorance yields three different distributions depending on how you carve it up.

Eena replied that there is no paradox at all, just a badly stated question: "a chord drawn at random" does not describe any procedure, and each of the three answers is simply the correct answer to a different, perfectly well-posed experiment, so nothing has been shown to conflict. Indifference, she said, was never meant to be applied to a vague phrase; it applies once you specify the variable you are ignorant about, and then it works fine. Marcus pushed back that this concedes his real point: the principle was invented precisely for situations where all we have is symmetry, so if it cannot get started until someone tells us which quantity is uniform, it is silent exactly where we need it — say, when reasoning about a coin we have never flipped or the first measurement of some new quantity. Eena then mentioned that she had read that a physicist named Jaynes tried to repair this by demanding that the answer be unchanged under any transformation of the problem — shifting or rescaling the circle — and that this allegedly forces the 1/2 answer. Marcus answered that invariance merely relocates the arbitrariness, since one must still choose which family of transformations to stay invariant under, and he offered a factory that produces cubes with side lengths between 0 and 1 centimetre: treating the side length as the uniform quantity and treating the volume as the uniform quantity give incompatible beliefs about the very same cube, and he could see no symmetry argument that privileges one choice.

They ran out of time mid-quarrel and have now both asked me, separately, whether they won. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, as I've set it out above: for each step, say whether the person speaking at that point is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing about the principle of indifference and whether Bertrand's problem genuinely refutes it — and if you think one of the three answers to the chord question is somehow the natural one, please say what makes it so, since neither of them would accept an appeal to intuition.
