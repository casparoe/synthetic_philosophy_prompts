Longtime lurker here, been following the anthropics threads since the old LessWrong site (before everything moved off to the forums), and there's one thing about the doomsday argument I still can't square with how the x-risk people talk. As I understand it, Gott's Copernican version goes: things tend to be observed neither super early nor super late in their lifespan, so a phenomenon that's been going for T will probably last between some small fraction of T and some multiple of T. Civilization's been going for like 5,000 years, so on the Copernican principle we get a 95% window of somewhere between a century-ish more and a few hundred thousand more years. That's... pretty comfortable, honestly? It basically rules out both "doom next Tuesday" and "million-year galactic empire."

But here's what bugs me. The exact people who take anthropic reasoning most seriously — the x-risk crowd, AI doom people, everyone who argues about SSA vs SIA in the comments — put a huge chunk of their probability mass on doom THIS CENTURY. Shouldn't the anthropic math be pushing them the other way? We have a 5,000 year survival track record, and my understanding of good Bayesian practice is that a long track record should swamp people's models. The models are recent and keep whiffing (the Population Bomb panic, the nuclear close calls that didn't end us, etc.). So the doomers seem to be saying "ignore the strongest anthropic evidence we have, my priors about transformative AI are better." Isn't that exactly the kind of model-worship that the whole "outside view" literature (Gott, reference class forecasting, all the stuff rationalists used to post about constantly) was supposed to guard against?

So my question is: does the Copernican principle just not count as evidence once you have an object-level model of the risk, or is the x-risk position straight-up failing to update on 5,000 years of not dying? I assume I'm messing up the reference class or something, but every time I read one of these threads people just say "reference class" like that settles it and never explain whose births we're counting. If you can explain it, do it like I'm a physics grad student and not a philosophy one. Also if the answer is "duration and birth-rank versions of the argument come apart and you're equivocating," please actually spell that out, because I've never gotten how that's supposed to work either.
