I do risk analysis at a small think tank that gets contracted by a government office to write medium-term forecasts — energy, pandemics, great-power conflict, that sort of thing. Nothing classified, just structured judgment, and we deliver maybe six reports a year. Increasingly my colleagues' drafts are full of reasoning that I've come to realize is self-locating belief dressed up as ordinary updating, and nobody here has a rule for when it's allowed. Examples that have actually come up in drafts: (a) someone lowered our estimate of great-power war because humanity has survived 70 years with nuclear weapons, so the annual risk must be lower than the modelers fear — survivorship treated as evidence of safety; (b) a paragraph that dragged our forecast of civilizational lifespan way down by appealing to a version of the doomsday argument, on the grounds that we find ourselves early in the human sequence; (c) a pandemic chapter that reasoned roughly 'given that I'm observing this outbreak at all, it's more likely to be a big one', which is basically the logic that makes the Sleeping Beauty problem disagree with itself depending on whether you count experiments or awakenings.

So here's the ask: turn whatever the philosophy of anthropic and self-locating reasoning has actually settled — plus whatever it hasn't — into a procedure I can run on a draft paragraph in fifteen minutes. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a 'strike this inference', and where it has to hand the call back to a human because the question is one of values or of choosing between frameworks rather than of facts. I want the distinctions that must survive simplification to survive: plain evidence vs. indexical evidence, whether the observer was guaranteed to be around to make the observation (the reason survival data can be treacherous — you only hear from the worlds that got to write the report), whether the sampling setup is more like one experiment or many awakenings, and the fork where a draft has to either flag its assumption or drop the inference entirely. And be honest about which steps of my so-called procedure are really judgment calls in a costume.

One constraint: our director signs off on every report, and she has zero patience for jargon, so the procedure can't require whoever runs it to know what any of the abbreviations stand for — it has to be self-contained, a checklist a smart person with no philosophy background can follow.

Then run it on three cases. First, the nuclear-survival inference in (a). Second, the doomsday-argument paragraph in (b) — and include one where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, because I suspect it's that our near-miss anecdotes can't straightforwardly lower our war estimates, and I'd rather find that out now than in review. Third, the outbreak-observation reasoning in (c). Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure can't do at all — where this whole body of theory is silent and the honest move is a footnote saying so, not a paragraph pretending otherwise.
