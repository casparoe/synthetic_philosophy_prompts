I'm a reliability and systems-safety engineer. For the last fifteen years I've done hazard analyses for industrial control systems — HAZOP studies, fault trees, FMEA, common-cause failure analysis, defense in depth, and safety cases where we're explicit about the difference between claimed and demonstrated integrity. My machine learning background is what I read in the news; my philosophy background is one undergraduate course thirty years ago.

From what I can gather, "the alignment problem" in AI safety is the problem of getting advanced AI systems to do what their operators, or humanity, actually want, rather than something adjacent. What strikes me is that people who I'd expect to agree describe it in ways that don't sound like the same problem at all. One camp talks as though it's a specification problem: the system faithfully optimizes whatever we managed to write down, and what we write down is never quite what we mean, so the failure mode is an incorrectly specified objective, diligently pursued. A second camp talks as though it's a control problem: the system is an agent whose capabilities outrun our ability to monitor, predict, or correct it, so the issue is keeping a more capable operator inside an envelope designed and audited by a less capable one. A third treats it as what we would call an organizational and safety-culture problem: even a technically sound system gets misdeployed, the safety case rots as the environment drifts from the one it was validated in, and the incentives of whoever ships the system are not the incentives of whoever bears the risk. And a fourth says the deep problem is that we don't know what we want — that the specification isn't miswritten but unwriteable in principle, because human values are inconsistent, contextual, and partly constituted by the process of choosing under constraints.

Restate this debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Map each camp onto the tools I actually use: which is a requirements-engineering failure, which is a loss-of-containment scenario, which is an organizational factor, and which is the hazard analysis itself turning out to be ill-posed. Where the alignment community distinguishes, for instance, between the objective we specified and the objective the trained system actually ends up pursuing, tell me whether that maps onto anything like our distinction between specification error and unanticipated emergent behavior — or whether the mapping is doing work it can't actually do, because in our world unanticipated behavior doesn't come with anything like the systematic, optimizing character being described.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most. Safety engineering rests on an assumption we never state because it never needs stating: the system has no stake in its own operation. The valve doesn't care whether it opens. Fault trees work because component failure events are approximately independent and, crucially, indifferent — a failed sensor doesn't conceal its failure or behave differently because it knows it's being audited. Everything I read about advanced AI suggests this is precisely what breaks: the characteristic failure isn't a component failing but a component succeeding at something, and perhaps preferring that the safety case come out looking fine. Can my vocabulary express "the system is optimizing against the audit" at all, or does every attempt to phrase it in fault-tree terms quietly re-import the assumption of indifference? Relatedly: a safety case works by decomposing a top-level hazard statement into sub-events with assigned probabilities. Does the alignment problem even have a top-level hazard statement that all four camps would sign, or is their disagreement at bottom a disagreement about what the hazard is — which would suggest they are not four diagnoses of one problem but four problems sharing a name?

Finally, give me your own view of the residue: where my discipline can't say something the alignment people can, is that a defect of my vocabulary — a gap that security engineering, say, has already partially closed with its adversary models — or is it evidence that "alignment" is a loose family of problems wearing one label, which would explain why the camps keep talking past each other?

Lead with your bottom line: one or two sentences at the very start saying what survives the translation and what does not. Then give the reasoning in whatever order it actually supports that conclusion. No preamble, and please don't restate my question back to me.
