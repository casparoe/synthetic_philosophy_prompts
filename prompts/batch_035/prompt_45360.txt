I'm taking a metaphysics course this semester and we just did a unit on dispositions that has me kind of obsesed. We started with the "simple conditional analysis" — the idea that a disposition just is a fact about what would happen in certain circumstances, so the glass on my shelf is fragile because (and only because) it would shatter if struck. Then the professor spent two whole lectures on ways that analysis supposedly fails, with these weird names: finks (something standing by to remove or undo the disposition right when the stimulus shows up), masks (something blocking the disposition from manifesting without ever removing it), and mimics (something that would produce the manifestation-like behavior without having the disposition at all). I gather there's also something called an antidote, which I think is like a mask but for other kinds of dispositions, like an aspirin heading off a headache before it starts.

For seminar I have to write a dialogue, and I want to do it on this. So: write a dialogue between Ana, who defends the conditional analysis and thinks the counterexamples just show it needs a few extra clauses (she's allowed to retreat to more sophisticated versions — e.g. demanding that the breaking be due to the object's intrinsic makeup, or building in ceteris paribus riders — but she should have to actually retreat, not just announce the clause and move on), and Basil, who thinks the counterexamples cut deeper and is sympathetic to dispositions being real, irreducible properties in their own right rather than truths about hypotheticals.

To give them something concrete to fight over, seed the setting: put them in an eccentric antique shop whose owner has rigged up demonstration pieces. There's a genuinely fragile glass swan permanently encased in custom-fitted foam (Basil's mask), a sturdy steel vase that looks delicate and is wired so that a tiny hammer-wielding automaton smashes it the instant anythign strikes it (his mimic), and the "sorcerer's vase," a fragile-looking piece hooked to a device that would, at the first hint of an incoming blow, swap it out for an unbreakable replica before the blow could land (his fink). Make them actually respond to each other's points rather than take turns lecturing — Ana should notice, for example, that the automaton vase gives her trouble in a different way than the sorcerer's vase does, and Basil should press her when her patched-up analysis starts doing all its work through the added clauses instead of the conditional core. No strawmen, and don't let either of them just win at the end.
