Write a dialogue between two evolutionary biologists — one a staunch defender of the gene's-eye view of selection, the other a committed proponent of multilevel selection — who have been asked to co-author a joint review of the following experimental system.

In a laboratory population of yeast, some cells secrete an enzyme that breaks down sucrose outside the cell, so that the digested sugar diffuses freely and can be absorbed by any neighboring cell. Cells that secrete the enzyme grow more slowly than cells that do not, since secretion is metabolically costly. When rare, non-secretors ("cheaters") outcompete secretors within any local clump; but clumps dominated by cheaters exploit the sugar so inefficiently that they leave far fewer descendant colonies than clumps with many secretors. The experimenters can quantify both effects: within-clump selection favors cheaters, between-clump selection favors secretors, and which force dominates depends on how clumps are formed and dispersed.

The dialogue should meet the following requirements. Both characters should be equally competent, and neither should be a strawman; each should concede what is genuinely awkward for their own view. The gene-selectionist should press the claim that describing the case in terms of group selection is at best an alternative bookkeeping of one and the same causal process, and that since the only things that reliably persist across generations are genes, the ultimate beneficiary of the secretors' costly behavior has to be identified at that level. The multilevel selectionist should respond that the causal statistics of the experiment — how variance is partitioned within and between clumps, and how the mode of clump formation and dispersal reshuffles that partition — are themselves doing real explanatory work, and should accuse the gene-selectionist of committing something like an averaging fallacy whenever the fate of the trait is assessed only at the level of the well-mixed population. At some point the two should disagree sharply about whether it makes sense to ask what the secreted enzyme is *for*, given that the answer seems to depend on which level of the population structure is treated as the character-bearing unit. The dialogue should end not with a resolution but with the two of them drafting a single sentence, for their joint review, stating what the yeast experiment demonstrates — arguing over every word of that sentence.
