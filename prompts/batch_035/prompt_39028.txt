Third-year grad student in behavioral ecology here, and I keep getting into it with my labmate about what actually guarantees honesty in a signal. She's a Grafen-style strategic handicap person -- the cost itself keeps the signal honest, costlier display = more credible. My gut verdicts on cases keep not lining up with that, so I want to know what principle, if any, my verdicts actually add up to. Here they are: peacock's tail -- honest signal of quality, and yes it's the cost doing the work. Gazelle stotting -- same, honest, cost paid right in front of the predator. But poison dart frogs: the bright coloration is basically free to wear once you're toxic, and I still think it's a perfectly honest signal -- because the cost of FAKING it (being a non-toxic frog in bright colors) gets you eaten, so honesty comes from punishment of cheaters, not from the producer paying anything. Hoverflies that mimic wasp stripes: cheap and dishonest, fine, and I'd predict it only works because mimics stay rare. Nestling begging: the chick pays a real metabolic cost begging, there's genuine conflict of interest between chick and parent, but my gut says the handicap story is wrong there -- the cost feels like a side effect, not the thing guaranteeing honesty. Rented Lamborghini for the instagram: the display is expensive but I say it's not an honest signal of wealth, because the cost is refundable-ish and anyone can rent it -- so maybe what matters is that the cost be sunk and non-transferable, not just large. And then the one I flip back and forth on every week: counter-signaling. The old-money guy in the frayed jacket signals his wealth more credibly than the new-money guy with the watch, and his display costs nothing, which seems to break the whole cost story, unless the shabby jacket is only cheap to wear BECAUSE everyone knows the other guy paid. My tentative guess is the real condition is something like differential cost -- cheaper for the high-quality signaler than the low-quality one, counting punishment of fakes as a cost paid later -- but I don't trust myself to state it cleanly. So: is there one principle that captures all these verdicts, and if not, which ones would you overturn and why? Tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't given you: song complexity in birds where the song partly reflects developmental stress early in life (is that a handicap or just a record?), and someone putting #opentowork on LinkedIn. Also be straight with me about where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what would change your mind -- like, if someone could show a stable honest signaling system where faking is costless for everyone, that should kill the cost-based principle, so tell me whether anything in my cases comes close to being that counterexample, and don't just hand me back my own hunches dressed up.
