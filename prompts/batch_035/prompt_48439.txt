i keep going back and forth on this with a friend and want an actual analysis, not vibes. Around 1900 electric cars were a serious rival to gasoline ones — by some counts a quarter to a third of cars on American roads were electric, they were quiet and clean, no hand-cranking to break your wrist, and they were marketed to women and city drivers for exactly those reasons. Then gasoline won, obviously, and the standard story is path dependence: once enough gas cars were out there, gas stations got built, once stations existed gas cars were better to own, mechanics learned gas engines, R&D money followed, and the electric alternative got strangled in its crib even though (the story goes) it was arguably the better technology. But the counter-story seems strong too: gas cars were cheaper, gasoline was absurdly cheap, early batteries gave terrible range, and by 1912ish the electric starter had removed the one huge advantage electrics had. On that view they just lost on the merits for the tech of the era, and there was no lock-in at all, just a choice that would've un-happened whenever conditions changed.

So what I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions. First the mapping: who or what are even the relevant "players" when we say lock-in here (car makers? buyers? the people financing gas stations?), what exactly is the network externality supposed to be, what counts as one technology being "better" — and better for whom, over what timescale, since a drivetrain that's better for the climate in 2020 was pure cost to the buyer in 1910. Then tell me where the lock-in story genuinely explains what happened, where it collapses into a just-so story ("gasoline won, therefore lock-in, therefore gasoline must've been worse"), and what parts of the episode path dependence is just silent on.

The part I most want your take on is the EV revival of the last fifteen years. Is the comeback evidence there was never deep lock-in (the underlying economics just took a century to reassert itself), or evidence the lock-in was real and enormous (it took ~100 years, battery breakthroughs, climate subsidies, and a maniac CEO to dislodge it), or a sign that "lock-in" is covering two different things — a contingent choice that persists only while its supporting conditions persist, versus a true lock-in where the choice actively reproduces its own conditions? If gasoline really did reproduce its conditions, name the specific loops you have in mind (fueling infrastructure, repair networks, engineering human capital, range expectations baked into what people think a car is), and say which of those loops the EV revival has actually broken and which are still intact.
