I'm a cognitive science PhD student who has ended up in a reading group with several people from philosophy of science, and I keep bumping into an argumentative move there that I can't put a standard name to. The pattern: someone establishes a purely mathematical fact about a formal model — that exact inference in an arbitrary Bayesian network is NP-hard, say, or that no comparison-based sort can do better than on the order of n log n comparisons in the worst case — and then, with no further empirical premise, draws a substantive conclusion about what actual physical systems can do. The brain can't literally be doing exact inference, because that would require solving an intractable problem, so it must be approximating; any proposed device that seemed to crack hard instances at scale would be presumed mistaken or fraudulent, as though NP-hardness were a conservation law rather than a statement about abstract machines. What interests me is the missing bridge: the proof quantifies over algorithms in a formal model of computation, the conclusion is about neurons or lab equipment, and the only thing connecting them seems to be the prestige of complexity theory itself.

I've noticed the move runs in both directions, which makes me think there is a real phenomenon here rather than one author's idiosyncrasy. Downward, worst-case bounds get treated as constraints on what organisms and engineers can achieve — even though nearly everyone agrees that the instances nature actually serves up tend to be far easier than the adversarial ones the bounds quantify over, and there's a whole line of work on why realistic inputs behave so much better than the worst case. Upward, that very easiness then gets promoted into something that supposedly demands explanation, as if the fact that the physical world doesn't confront us with hard instances is a contingent, almost anthropically suspicious feature of our universe rather than a triviality about which problems we happened to evolve to care about.

The nearest things I've been able to find are the extended Church-Turing thesis, which sounds close but is normally stated as a conjecture about physics from which complexity conclusions flow, whereas this move runs the other way, from a mathematical result to a claim about what physics permits; the logical omniscience problem in formal epistemology, which concerns idealized agents rather than machines; and ordinary engineering talk about feasibility, which is different because it is explicitly about our contingent resources. Is there an established name for this reification of model-relative resource bounds into what look like physical constraints, and more broadly for the practice of treating complexity classes as features of the universe rather than of our descriptions? If some corner of the philosophy-of-science or philosophy-of-computation literature has already carved this, tell me how it carves it and how the carving handles the two directions I described; if not, I'd like a coined term with a definition and fixed boundaries — in particular, where exactly the phenomenon stops being this and becomes either an ordinary auxiliary premise about typical-case difficulty or a genuinely physical limit like Landauer's principle. I won't be satisfied with just being told it's related to the Church-Turing thesis.
