I have been trying to work out, by my own reactions to particular cases, what I actually believe about the claim that religion makes people more moral, and I would like help extracting the principle behind my verdicts — or learning which verdicts I should abandon.

The cases and my verdicts. A devout woman volunteers every week at a soup kitchen and says, without embarrassment, that she does it because she takes herself to be commanded to love her neighbor: she is a good person, and her religion is part of what makes her good. A man who tithes exactly ten percent of his income and gives nothing beyond it, because he regards the tithe as discharging his obligation in full, seems to me made somewhat worse by his religion — it has installed a ceiling on his generosity. A believer who refrains from shoplifting solely because he thinks an all-seeing God will punish him does not seem more moral than the atheist who refrains because shoplifting is wrong, even though their behavior is identical. A congregation with low crime, high charity, and warm internal trust, which nonetheless shuns members who leave the faith, is a case where religion seems to make people better toward insiders and worse toward outsiders at the same time. A believer who admits that if he lost his faith tomorrow he would probably start cheating on his wife still seems, as things stand, a more dependable husband than he otherwise would be — the belief is doing real moral work, even if the work is fragile. And a secular society that sustains high trust and low corruption without any shared creed suggests to me that religion is at most one route to the goods it delivers, though I concede I could not begin to say whether the route is the easiest one.

Two of these verdicts strike me as difficult to hold together. If the reward-and-punishment believer is not more moral for being deterred by God, then it is hard to see how the fragile-faithful husband is made better by his belief; if he is made better, then deterrence seems to count for something after all. So my questions are these. Is there a single coherent notion of being "more moral" — whether in terms of behavior, motivation, or stable dispositions — that fits all six verdicts? If not, which is the better repaired position: a principle that keeps every verdict but must distinguish motivations in some complicated way, or a simpler principle that overturns some verdicts? And I would like your view on the case I keep going back and forth on, which I have not listed above: a person who does dramatic good works for the explicit purpose of earning an eternal reward, in a religious tradition that promises exactly that — more moral than the same person would have been without the promise, morally the same, or perhaps moral in one sense and not another?
