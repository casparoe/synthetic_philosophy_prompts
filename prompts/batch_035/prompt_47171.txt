Last night a friend and I spent close to four hours arguing about Grice, and we ended up in the odd position of each claiming his name for our own side. It began with dinner-table small talk and ended well past midnight. Her position was that when a speaker implicates something, the substantive fact is a fact about the hearer: an inference drawn against conventions of usage, in which the speaker's intentions play at most a supporting role. I held that implicature is part of what the speaker meant, secured by the reflexive intentions Grice built his analysis of speaker meaning on, so that the theory of implicature is an extension of the theory of meaning rather than a theory of audience psychology. As we talked we surfaced two further cruxes underneath that one: whether the Cooperative Principle does any genuine explanatory work, or whether implicature can be derived from bare rational coordination (or, on her version, from convention) with the principle discarded as ornament; and what to make of so-called conventional implicatures — whether a word like "but" carries encoded content at all, or is merely a worn-down inference. Neither of us moved an inch, and we concluded that the disagreement runs deeper than either of us can locate introspectively. I would like to settle it empirically, on my readers.

I write a small blog for language-curious non-specialists. Please design a diagnostic quiz, of the sort I could post there, that sorts people by where they actually stand on the nature of implicature and speaker meaning. Requirements:

1. Eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete case with two or three answer options. The options should be phrased as verdicts about the case, never as endorsements of a school; no question may ask which theory the reader likes, and no technical vocabulary may appear in the question text. Someone who has never heard the word "implicature" must be able to answer every question.

2. The questions should separate positions along the three fault lines from our argument: whether an implicature is a fact about what the speaker meant, a fact about what a hearer could reasonably work out, or a fact about linguistic convention; whether cooperativity is explanatorily essential or dispensable, including in cases of irony, flouting, and open non-cooperation; and whether conventional implicature is genuine encoded meaning or standardized inference.

3. Among the cases, include (or devise close kin of) the following: a planted-evidence scenario where the planter intends the finder to recognize the evidence as planted yet not to act on it; a case where someone says "some" and the question is what, exactly, cancellation succeeds or fails in showing; a hearer who confidently reads in something the speaker never intended, where the question is whether an implicature occurred at all; someone speaking to a tape recorder or diary with no audience; an ironist who means the opposite of her sentence while expecting that opposite to be recognized; and at least one case hinging on a conventional-implicature word such as "but" or "even."

4. Then provide a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves that must all appear: Grice's own intention-based view; the neo-Gricean picture of generalized implicatures associated with Horn and Levinson; relevance theory as developed by Sperber and Wilson; Bach's standardization treatment of conventional implicatures; Lewis's convention-first view of meaning; and the skeptical, hearer-side view, associated with Wayne Davis, that implicature is not speaker meaning at all.

5. Finally, note any combination of answers the questions cannot reach, and briefly diagnose it — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. And flag honestly any pair of positions the instrument cannot genuinely separate, rather than pretending the scoring key draws a line it cannot. I would rather publish a quiz that admits two leaves collapse into one than one that silently decides our argument for us.
