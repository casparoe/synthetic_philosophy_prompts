I host a podcast that interviews the interlocutors of Plato's dialogues rather than their authors — the characters who are usually treated as furniture. Next week's guest is Glaucon from the Republic, appearing in his capacity as the man who reopened a case Thrasymachus had already conceded, and who insisted that justice be praised for its own sake with every reputational consequence stripped away. Please draft nine or ten questions that press on the actual structure of his challenge rather than inviting him to restate it. Each question should come with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give.

I want at least one question on each of the following:

1. The status of his advocacy: he presents the case for injustice as one he does not himself endorse, offering it only so that Socrates will defend justice properly. Ask him whether that stance is coherent, or whether a person who can articulate the strongest case for injustice while disclaiming belief in it is exactly the person whose belief should worry us.

2. His threefold division of goods into those valued for their own sake, those valued for their consequences, and those valued for both, into which he files justice in the second-or-third category over most people's objections. Press him on whether the taxonomy can accommodate relational goods — things whose value is partly constituted by being valued by or for others — and on whether such goods make his demand to evaluate justice in isolation from reputation a demand of the wrong category, like asking whether a promise is valuable to a hermit.

3. The assumption, built into the ring of Gyges story, that detection by others is the only obstacle to profitable injustice. Confront him with the thought that self-opacity cuts the other way: an unjust man invisible even to himself, rationalizing each betrayal as it happens, may be unable to sustain the long-horizon discipline and cooperation with his own future plans that his picture of the perfectly unjust man requires — so that the ring, which removes the external check, only makes the internal bookkeeping worse. His likely reply is that a genuinely strong-willed wrongdoer can compartmentalize; the follow-up should ask what keeps a compartmentalized agent's commitments stable over time when they are invisible to everyone, including himself.

4. Whether the Myth of Er answers him on his own terms at all, since a cosmic audit of souls, complete with tenfold penalties, looks precisely like the reputation-and-consequence machinery he bracketed. If what finally persuades him is the myth rather than the argument in Book IX that the tyrannical man is the most wretched of men, then his challenge has quietly been declined rather than met. A follow-up for whichever half he retreats to.

5. Whether the Book IX argument even targets him. The tyrant is depicted as ruled by lawless appetite, but Glaucon's own stated loves — victory in battle, civic honor, prominence in the city — belong to the honor-loving part of the soul, which the Republic ranks above appetite. Ask him whether he escaped the refutation on a technicality: Socrates may have shown the glutton and the tyrant miserable without showing the ambitious and self-disciplined wrongdoer anything of the kind.

6. What he makes of the detail, in the myth, that the soul of Odysseus, remembering his labors, deliberately chooses the life of a private man who minds his own affairs. Ask him plainly whether he would make the same choice if he drew his lot in that line, and what it says about his challenge if the man who had every worldly prize tells him the prize was not worth the remembering.

Please keep the questions adversarial but fair: he should be treated as an opponent with a specific, articulable position, not as scenery in someone else's dialogue. Avoid generic biographical questions about Socrates or about his brother, and avoid anything that requires him to comment on Plato rather than defend his own case.
