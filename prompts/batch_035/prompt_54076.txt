I work in the testing office of a large university system, and I've been trying to work out what picture of human capacities our accommodation policies quietly commit us to. The practice, concretely: we administer proctored aptitude and placement exams under fixed conditions — timed, silent, closed-book. A score is then read as a measure of a stable capacity: a student's mathematical reasoning, say, not their performance on one particular Saturday morning. Students with documented disabilities — anxiety disorders, ADHD, slow processing speed — are granted accommodations, most often extended time or a separate quiet room. The rationale we give is that the condition interferes with the display of a capacity the student genuinely has; the accommodation is meant to remove the interference, not to add ability. Meanwhile, we grant nothing to students who test poorly because of a lousy schooling history, nothing to students who are simply exhausted that day, and there is perennial institutional hand-wringing about whether coaching and test preparation change what is being measured or only reveal it.

I've since read enough philosophy of mind to put names to my unease. These capacities sound like dispositions, with their possession conditions spelled out conditionally: if a student were placed in test-like circumstances C, they would display performance M. And I gather conditional analyses of dispositions run into trouble with finks and masks — factors that respectively remove the disposition just when it is about to manifest, or block the manifestation while leaving the disposition and its causal basis intact. What I would like is a reconstruction of the theory of capacities our practice presupposes. Specifically: in granting accommodations but doing nothing about bad schooling, does the practice presuppose that anxiety masks a capacity while educational deprivation constitutes its absence — and is that a defensible line to draw, given that from the test's observational standpoint the two cases look identical (low score, traceable cause, no way to see the counterfactual directly)? Does the practice presuppose a privileged, fine-grained individuation of capacities on which processing speed is not part of the capacity being tested, so that extending time unmasks rather than changes the target? Extended time worries me in particular: unlike a quiet room, which arguably just neutralizes an interfering factor, it alters the trigger conditions themselves — part of C is being under time pressure — which suggests we are no longer testing the same disposition at all. And behind all this sits the deeper question the practice must have an answer to: whether a capacity is individuated by its intrinsic causal basis or by its conditional role. That question decides whether a student whose anxiety is chronic and internal has the reasoning-without-time-pressure capacity in any robust sense, or whether we are preserving a flattering fiction.

Please also tell me: which parts of the practice strictly presuppose which views of dispositions, and which parts are philosophically underdetermined, compatible with several theories at once? And what would have to change in the policy if the presupposed view were replaced — for instance, if we adopted a view on which anxiety is part of the capacity's basis rather than a mask over it, or a view on which dispositions are individuated by their full trigger-and-manifestation conditions, so that reasoning under time pressure and reasoning with generous time are simply different dispositions with no fact of the matter about a shared core?

I'd like you to be explicit about how confident you are in each of the claims you make, and to single out the reconstruction you are least sure of, with a note on what evidence or argument would settle it.
