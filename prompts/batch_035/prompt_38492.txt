So I've been reading about pain asymbolia -- people who, after certain brain injuries, say the pain is all still there (they can tell you exactly where it is and how intense), but they genuinely dont mind it. They'll keep a hand in ice water, they don't flinch at threats, they're not afraid of being hurt. And it's gotten me trying to work out what I actually believe about when pain is bad for you, case by case, going with my gut. My verdicts:

- Torturing an ordinary person: bad for them, obviously, and it's the pain doing the badness-work.
- Torturing someone with pain asymbolia: still wrong, but I don't think the pain itself is bad for them. The wrongness comes from the humiliation and the violation. If there's no damage and they really don't mind, the badness-to-them of the pain is basically zero.
- The classic morphine patient who says the pain is still there but doesn't bother them: I keep feeling like something bad is still happening to them. Not as bad as before, but not nothing.
- Chronic pain patients who say they've genuinely made peace with it -- same pain, same intensity, they've just stopped fighting it: I think it's no longer bad for them. Which is awkward, because on paper this looks exactly like the morphine case.
- The masochist: the pain is bad for them, but they're right to want it. Bad, yet all-things-considered welcome, like a brutal workout that costs you something you're glad to pay. And I can't explain why this combination feels fine there.
- A jammed finger in the middle of a game, versus a mild toothache the night before a flight: the finger hurts way more but is way less bad. So the badness isn't just proportional to intensity.

My tentative principle, which I'm not sure I believe: pain is bad for you iff hating it, wanting it gone, is the fitting response. That handles the asymbolia case and the made-peace chronic patient, but it makes the masochist's pain not bad, which I think is wrong, and I genuinely can't tell what it says about the morphine patient. Also possibly relevant: my physio friend says chronic pain questionnaires ask about intensity and bothersomeness as separate questions, and patients' answers come apart, which at least suggests the bothering is somehow separately trackable from the feeling.

What I want from you:

First, please be careful to separate what's settled from what's actually contested among people who know this stuff. Like, which parts of the asymbolia picture are just the clinical facts versus what's a philosophical interpretation layered on top -- and whether pain and suffering come apart at all is itself a contested question, so don't hand me one side as if it were consensus. If the best principle is defended by a particular camp, say so.

Then the main job: is there a principle, or maybe two or three candidates, that captures my verdicts? State each one precisely, and for each clause tell me which of my cases is forcing it in. Then run the principle on cases I haven't listed -- a soldier in the field who notices a wound's pain but is unmoved until the adrenaline wears off; whether relieving the morphine patient's bothersomeness while leaving the feeling is a reason for them; someone whose made-peace chronic pain starts spreading to new areas. And be honest about which of my verdicts the principle can't save: I suspect the morphine one and the made-peace one can't both survive, and I want to know which is cheaper to drop and why.

Format, please, with rough lengths:
