As a journalist who has spent the better part of two decades writing about science and ideas for a general readership, I'm at work on a long-form retrospective essay taking stock of the humanitarian interventions I covered over my career — the Kosovo air campaign in 1999, which I reported from the Macedonian border, the run-up to the Iraq war in 2003, and the Libya operation of 2011 — and before I file the piece I want a rigorous audit of whether my considered positions actually cohere. Here is the set of commitments I would defend in print: (1) the Kosovo intervention was justified even though it proceeded without Security Council authorization, and the later independent commission's verdict that it was technically unlawful yet legitimate strikes me as roughly right; (2) the 2003 invasion of Iraq was unjustified, and the absence of Security Council authorization is an essential part of the case against it that I have repeatedly made; (3) my underlying principle is a responsibility-to-protect view — when a state turns its machinery of violence on its own population at scale, sovereignty forfeits its protective force, and outside powers may legitimately use force to stop the killing; (4) Security Council authorization cannot be a strict requirement for justified intervention, because the veto makes the Council hostage to the parochial interests of its permanent members; (5) the Libya intervention was justified at the outset under the 2011 resolution authorizing all necessary measures to protect civilians, though I believe the mandate was betrayed when civilian protection slid into regime change; (6) for an intervention to be legitimate rather than a euphemism for ordinary power politics, it must be non-selective in principle — it cannot turn on whether the interveners have oil interests or a nearby base — even though I also accept that states will only ever intervene where their interests align, and that we cannot reasonably demand otherwise of them; and (7) force can stop atrocities but cannot build liberal institutions, so democracy promotion cannot be the aim of a humanitarian intervention, and an intervention that leaves a country worse off than non-intervention would have is unjustified even if it stopped the killing.

I have defended each of these at one time or another, often in the same column, and my editor has begun to make pointed noises about consistency. The trouble is that I cannot see where the genuine problems lie, and I do not want to find out from the letters page. So treat the seven commitments as a fixed set and audit them as a whole: which pairs are in real tension, which only look like it, and what each tension costs to resolve.

Please structure your reply in the following sections, in this order: "Summary" (no more than 120 words, stating up front whether the set is jointly tenable); "Apparent tensions" (those that dissolve on inspection, with an explanation of why); "Genuine tensions, ranked" (ordered from most to least severe, each with the specific commitments involved and what exactly would have to give); "The cheapest revision" (the single change that would clean up the most, together with what I would lose by making it, given that some of these positions are ones I have publicly staked my credibility on); and finally "What my essay can still claim" (roughly 150 words, a candid statement of what a defensible position on humanitarian intervention looks like after the audit).
