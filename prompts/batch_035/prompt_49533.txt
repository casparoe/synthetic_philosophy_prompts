Having read LessWrong and Overcoming Bias since the late 2000s, I've noticed that the St. Petersburg paradox tends to get settled in the comments within about five replies, usually by somebody announcing that the problem is trivially dissolved because (pick one) the casino has a finite bankroll, utility is concave in dollars, or you should be using the Kelly criterion anyway. Everyone upvotes, and the thread moves on. I've come to suspect this confidence is misplaced, and I'd like an essay that takes the problem seriously rather than reaching for the first available deflation.

Set up the game properly first: a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads; if the first heads appears on toss n, you're paid 2^n dollars, so the expectation is 1/2 + 1 + 2 + 4 + ... and diverges. Yet almost nobody would pay even a hundred dollars to play. Then weigh the standard resolutions carefully: truncation at the counterparty's actual wealth, logarithmic or otherwise bounded utility, risk aversion, the Kelly/time-average perspective (a la the ergodicity economics crowd), and the move of simply rejecting unbounded utilities altogether. Explain what each resolution actually buys you and what it costs, since several of them quietly change the theory of rational choice rather than merely fixing one weird case.

Then I want you to grapple with two wrinkles that the blog threads rarely address. First, the resale puzzle: suppose you already own a ticket to the game, the coin hasn't been flipped, and a broker offers to buy the ticket off you for a flat fee before play begins. Symmetry considerations suggest your fair selling price and your fair buying price should coincide, yet almost everyone's gut says to sell for a few hundred dollars while refusing to buy at the same price. What, if anything, does this asymmetry reveal about whether our intuitions or expected value theory is the thing that's broken? Second, the pooled-wealth version: much of the standard resolution leans on the diminishing marginal utility of money, but imagine you're playing not for yourself but as the discretionary allocator for an enormous altruistic fund whose utility in dollars is effectively linear, with the counterparty backed by an economy vastly larger than the fund. If the concavity story was carrying the weight, the paradox seems to come roaring back here. Should the rationalist altruist bite the bullet and pay essentially any finite stake to play such a game, in the same spirit that some are willing to bite fanaticism-style bullets about tiny probabilities of astronomical payoffs?

Reach a definite verdict: is the St. Petersburg game a genuine defect in the decision theory that rationalists actually endorse, or does some fully worked-out position dissolve it, and if so, which one and at what cost? A conditional verdict is acceptable, but don't end on a shrug.
