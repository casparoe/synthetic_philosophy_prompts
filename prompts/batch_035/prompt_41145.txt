I'm on the board of a mid-sized land trust — we do habitat restoration, wetlands, grassland easements, that kind of thing. A big decision is coming up at next month's meeting and I want a decision memo written beforehand that I can circulate.

The situation: a donor family wants to fund a 30-year restoration of a drained valley bottom back to wetland, several thousand acres, beavers, fish, waterfowl, the works. It would be the biggest project in our history. One of our newer board members, who's deep in the effective altruism world, is arguing we should decline it. His claim: more habitat means more wild animals, and if the average wild animal life is bad — starvation, disease, parasitism, most offspring dying before maturity, and wetlands are especially brutal about this — then restoring habitat is basically manufacturing suffering at scale. He's not being lazy about it either, he brought population estimates. Most of the old gaurd on the board are horrified and say that's just not how anyone thinks about conservation, but nobody has actually written down why it's wrong, and I don't want us to vote on vibes.

Draft the memo the board will actually vote on. It needs to recommend a concrete course of action — fund it, decline it, fund with conditions, whatever you think holds up — and I want it to do a few specific things:

- Keep the empirical question (are wild animal lives net negative on average, and how confident can anyone honestly be about that) separate from the moral ones (does aggregate welfare even constrain what we do, vs biodiversity or ecological integrity as goods in their own right, donor intent, what we owe the community). Don't let one get smuggled in as the answer to the other.
- Take the EA guy's strongest version seriously rather than treating it as an intrusion on the mission. If our whole organization implicitly assumes flourishing wild nature is a good thing, the memo should own that and either defend it or qualify it.
- Give us a decision procedure for this whole class of case, not just this project — what evidence would change our answer, and who we'd be if we acted differently depending on how the numbers came out. If the honest answer is that the empirical question is so uncertain both sides are betting, say that and explain how to bet.

One more thing: keep it readable. Our board includes a retired dairy farmer and a dermatologist, so no wall of philosophy jargon, but don't dumb it down either — they can smell a dodge.
