For the final session of my philosophy of science course we have just finished the unit on what probability claims are actually about, and I would like to close it with a poem rather than a recap slide. Please write a sestina — six sestets and a closing three-line envoi, following the traditional permutation of end-words — about a single throw of an ordinary die, a throw that happens once and will never be repeated. Choose six end-words that are plain and physical, one or two syllables, and able to shift their sense as they recur: a die has a face, and so does a clock, and so does an opponent. Across the six stanzas the poem should pass through the three classic answers to what the chance of that throw consists in, without naming any interpretation, any philosopher, or any school: let the opening pair of stanzas build the world of the tally and the long run (somewhere here, let the poem admit that the run is never observed to its limit); let the middle pair concern whatever it is in the die and the wrist and the felt that disposes the outcome before it exists; let the final pair belong to the person who must bet now, on incomplete knowledge, with no run to consult. The envoi should address the die after it has come to rest, when the question of what the probability really was still has three honest answers and no arbiter. The recurring end-words, arriving each time in their fixed order yet meaning differently, should carry part of the argument. Aim for a regular meter if you can, but the ideas must be exact: someone who has taken the course should be able to tag each pair of stanzas with the right interpretation, and someone who hasn't should still be able to enjoy the poem.
