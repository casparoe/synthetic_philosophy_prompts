I write literary fiction, and I am at work on a novel that has, in the drafting, developed a philosophical spine I only partly understand. The chapter as sketched: my protagonist winters alone as caretaker of a decommissioned weather station on an island, keeping to a routine so unbroken — the same chores, the same weather, the same few hours of grey light — that her waking mornings are indistinguishable from within. On the Sunday before the chapter opens, one event of consequence is settled for good: her sister's transplant either takes or fails, and she will not learn which until the supply pilot tells her; if it failed, a second procedure adds a further day of waiting, and so a further indistinguishable morning, before the news arrives. A philosopher friend who read the draft informs me, with what I fear was a smirk, that I have written Sleeping Beauty in a cardigan, and that if I intend to exploit the structure I had better first understand it.

I am not a philosopher, and I refuse to write the scene by accident. I want it constructed deliberately, to the following specifications:

1. The chapter itself should be one paragraph of ordinary prose, readable by someone who has never heard of the problem. Within it, my protagonist must face a decision — it need not be money; any irreversible act she must take before the pilot's knock will do — where what it is rational to do depends on how confident she should be that this particular morning is the earlier one.

2. No memory-altering device of any kind. Only ordinary sleep separates the mornings. Their indistinguishability must be earned: the sameness of her days must be depicted convincingly enough that a reader accepts she genuinely cannot tell which morning she is in, rather than my simply declaring that she cannot.

3. She must have no records, timestamps, or counting tricks available — but their unavailability must not feel like authorial cheating; it has to fall out of her situation naturally.

4. Every worldly fact must be fixed and known to her, so that all of her residual uncertainty concerns where she is in the sequence of mornings rather than how the world stands. This is the point of the whole exercise, and if the scene smuggles in ordinary empirical uncertainty instead, it fails.

5. The decision must be set up so that a halfer and a thirder give different answers to what she ought to do. The case must generate the disagreement from the theories rather than stipulate it, and the stakes should be ordinary ones a reader will accept as worth agonizing over.

After the paragraph of prose, walk me through the machinery as one demonstrates a design: state her credence under each party's reasoning, show which act each prescribes, tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed (what if she is given a calendar? what if the outcome is not settled until after she acts?), and how a defender of whichever side comes out looking irrational would attack the case. I am especially interested in whether the mundane setting alters the dialectic: a halfer might argue that rational betting behavior need not track credence in self-locating cases, or that a wager made once but possibly settled twice must be assessed differently — does the ordinary, un-drugged version of the setup leave that defense open, or close it? Close with an honest note on whether the cardigan distorts anything: whether the novelistic setting quietly breaks the analogy with the original problem, and, if so, whether I should mind.

Use the party names — halfers, thirders — and do not trouble yourself with citations; I want the reasoning, not the bibliography.
