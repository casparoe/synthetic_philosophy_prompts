ok so bit of a rabbit hole story: I stumbled on Gödel's ontological argument last week (the modal logic one, not the incompleteness stuff) and I've been going down the wikipedia + SEP spiral ever since. The thing I found out that really got me is that the formal side is basically settled at this point — people formalized the whole proof in higher-order theorem provers and it checks out, valid. But two nasty things also came out of that work: Sobel showed back in the 80s that Gödel's original axioms entail modal collapse, i.e. every truth is necessarily true, no contingencies anywhere, which is obviously too much. And weirder, the automated provers later found an actual inconsistency in Gödel's original 1970 axiomatization, one that Dana Scott had silently fixed in the version he circulated, so the version most people knew for decades wasn't even Gödel's. There are patched versions now (Anderson's emendation is the famous one for blocking modal collapse) and they keep the conclusion, so the whole fight has moved to one question: are the axioms any good?

That's what I want from you, and I want it quantified. The proof runs on roughly five axioms plus some definitions, and the crux is the notion of a "positive" property. In plain terms, roughly: (1) if a property is positive, any property it entails is positive; (2) positive-ness is necessary, i.e. if a property is positive it's positive in every possible world; (3) the negation of a positive property is not positive (so no property and its negation are both positive); (4) necessary existence is a positive property; (5) plus something like: if being God-like (having all positive properties) is possible, then it's necessary — though actually that one is supposed to follow from the others, that's the magic of S5. Anyway: please rate each of these axioms on a 0-10 scale for plausibility, where 0 means "I'd reject it out of hand" and 10 means "I'd accept it without hesitation," and give each a sentence on why it lands where it does. Then tell me which single axiom you'd jettison first if you had to, and whether Anderson's fix makes the overall package more or less believable to you — my intuition is that patching a system to avoid a disaster it entaild is a bit like fixing a prophecy after the fact, but maybe that's unfair. Also curious if you think the whole "positive property" primitive is even intelligible, or whether it's just the Cartesian perfection list wearing a tuxedo. For context, I'm a grad student in logic, an agnostic, and honestly more interested in the structure of the thing than in God, so don't soften anything for me.
