I chair the safety review for a small company that builds autonomous warehouse robots, and next month we have to decide whether to certify a system whose primary safeguard is an impact penalty: the planner is charged, at each step, for how much it is forecast to change the world relative to a baseline in which it does nothing, measured over a chosen set of state variables. I was inclined to approve it until a colleague on the review board handed me the following argument, which she says generalizes from a worry she has seen in the AI safety literature, and I cannot find a clean way out of it:

(1) Calling a change a "side effect worth avoiding" is already a value judgment: knocking over a shelf is a side effect only because someone disvalues knocked-over shelves, and whether the robot's pushing air molecules around counts as an effect or as noise is likewise settled by what we care about.

(2) So any impact measure is a value measure: which changes get counted, and how heavily, is fixed by the same considerations that fix what has value.

(3) If the measure captured value fully, it would simply be a utility function, and if we had a correct utility function we would have no need of a side-effect penalty at all -- we would just tell the robot to maximize it.

(4) If instead the measure captures value only partially -- restricted to some basket of "impacts" and a chosen baseline -- then whatever did the restricting (the choice of baseline, of measured variables, of discount rates) was itself a set of value judgments that the system now enforces without anyone having audited them as ethics.

(5) Therefore an impact-penalized system either smuggles in a complete, unexamined value theory or enforces an arbitrary fragment of one; either way it is not the neutral, theory-light safeguard it is advertised to be.

(6) Therefore we should not certify the system on this basis, and the broader program of "low-impact AI" as a stand-in for safe AI is confused in principle.

The argument strikes me as proving too much. If it worked, it would seem to condemn the tort system, environmental impact statements, and the physician's maxim to do no harm: every one of these defines a class of harms and a baseline of "business as usual" without first possessing a complete theory of value, and we do not conclude that they are all circular or all covert dictatorships of whoever wrote the form. But I cannot locate the precise step where my colleague's argument outruns that parallel -- whether it is in the slide from "value-laden somewhere" in (2) to something much stronger in (4), or in (3)'s assumption that the only alternatives are a complete utility function and a hidden fragment of one, or somewhere in what "neutral" is supposed to mean in (5).

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? For what it is worth, I would like to keep (1) and (2), since denying that impact measures encode any values at all seems to me false and complacent; and I would like the conclusion (6) to be false, since I do not think the right lesson is that "first, do no harm" is incoherent as a design principle. But I want to know exactly what I am paying, in the currency of what my approved system would then be committed to, for whichever premise I give up.
