I'm a backend engineer, and our team has this rule that no bugfix gets merged unless the PR includes a test that failed on the old code and passes on the new code. We're pretty religious about it -- a fix with a test that passed before and after doesn't count, even if the change looks obviously correct. I've been reading a bit about Popper and the demarcation problem for fun, and it got me wondering: does our rule basically presuppose falsificationism? Like the idea that you haven't learned anything about the code until you've actually seen a prediction it makes come out wrong, and that reproducability of the failure is what seperates a real bug report from a hallucinated one?

But there's a wrinkle that bugs me: the same team happily merges big refactors with zero behavioral tests added, and nobody demands a "could have failed" test for those. So is the practice actually committed to some implicit philosophy of science, or does it just rest on engineering pragmatics -- regressions are expensive, the CI is cheap? And if a rival view took over, say one where statistical evidence from logs and error rates matters more than a single decisive counterexample, what parts of the merge policy would actually have to change? I'm also curious where the analogy breaks down: a program isn't a theory about nature, the "experimenter" wrote the thing being tested, so maybe falsifying code tells us nothing about demarcating science from non-science in the first place. Would love your take on what the rule really presupposes, if anything.
