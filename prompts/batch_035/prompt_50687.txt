I'm a second-year PhD student in economics, and this semester I find myself TA-ing two courses that pull in opposite directions, which is what prompts my question. In the morning section of intermediate game theory, the professor has students solve games by iterated deletion of dominated strategies, including cases where a pure strategy is dominated only by a mixture of two others, and the resulting single surviving outcome is presented as the rational solution — students lose points for stopping after one round of deletion. In the afternoon, I'm grading for a market design course in which the same department's auction theorist insists on dominant-strategy incentive compatibility precisely because he does not trust bidders to reason carefully about each other's rationality or information; he designs mechanisms so that no one ever needs to hold beliefs about anyone else at all. I told students in the morning that any rational player must avoid a strategy dominated by a mixed strategy, that everyone knows this, that everyone knows everyone knows it, and so on; in the afternoon I told them that relying on such chains of reasoning in a high-stakes environment would be malpractice. The juxtaposition is starting to bother me.

So here is what I want: an analysis of what the textbook practice of solving games by iterated dominance in mixed strategies actually presupposes, if anything. Does it require full common knowledge of rationality in the formal sense, or is something weaker sufficient — and if so, exactly how many levels of "I know that she knows"? Does it presuppose a thin, quasi-circular notion of rationality (never play a dominated strategy) or the full apparatus of expected utility maximization given beliefs, and does the answer change when the domination is by a mixed strategy rather than a pure one, since the mixture seems to presuppose that players are comfortable randomizing and attaching probabilities to their own actions? Then assess whether the presupposition is even coherent as a story about how finite human beings in an actual auction room could come to have it. And finally, tell me whether my department's two practices are genuinely in tension: is it flatly inconsistent to teach iterated dominance as solving a game while designing mechanisms for agents assumed incapable of the reasoning it requires, or is the situation better described as the discipline quietly using "rationality" to mean different things in different rooms, with each usage locally fine? In working this out, please distinguish what the practice of iterated deletion strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, identify where it is underdetermined (several different epistemic stories might all license the same deletion sequence), and say what would have to change in either the teaching or the design practice if we replaced the common-knowledge presupposition with a graded notion along the lines of common p-belief. Please write in American English, and don't worry about citations — I can find the formal references myself; what I want is the reconstruction of the implicit theory.
