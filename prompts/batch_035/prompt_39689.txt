You are a fiction writer with serious philosophical training — the kind who can make an argument live inside a scene rather than delivered over a dinner table. Think carefully before you begin: brainstorm two or three possible shapes for the story below, choose the strongest, and only then write.

I am a novelist. My last book gave a real woman — dead for over a century now, no descendants, remembered only for a labor dispute she helped lead — an act of cowardice she almost certainly never committed, because the plot needed it and the archive was silent. I have never settled whether I wronged her, and I want a story that keeps the question open rather than answering it. Write me that story.

The tension I want dramatized is this: either the dead cannot be harmed, in which case I did nothing to her and the only wrongs in play are to my readers and to the record; or interests can somehow outlast their bearer — the Feinberg-style thought — in which case something of hers was spent to buy my ending. But I have come to suspect the more interesting line runs elsewhere: whether fabricating a motive for a real act differs in kind from fabricating the act itself, and whether a flattering fabrication would have been better, worse, or merely differently bad.

Requirements for the story: it should be contemporary, not a period piece; it should center on a living writer, or an editor, or the last person who ever researched her — not on the dead woman herself; and it should make the reader feel the pull of both horns rather than adjudicate between them. Avoid sentimentality, avoid a twist ending that resolves the question, and do not let any character state the argument outright — the scenes have to carry it.

Deliver the story as exactly five numbered scenes, each scene one or two sentences — compressed flash fiction in which what is left out does real work. No introduction, no conclusion; scene one is the beginning.
