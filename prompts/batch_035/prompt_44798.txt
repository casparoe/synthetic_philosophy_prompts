I teach an upper-level seminar called Truth and Paradox, and I'm designing the take-home final. My students are well drilled on the standard menu — Tarski's hierarchy, Kripke-style fixed points with three-valued gaps, and the one-paragraph versions of revision-theoretic and contextualist approaches — so a question that merely asks them to "explain and evaluate" the main responses to the Liar gets back essentially the same survey-shaped essay from everyone, including the students who understand almost nothing. I want to force a real confrontation with the structure of the problem instead. Here is the situation I'd like the question built around, taken from an actual office-hours exchange this term.

A student — call her Dana — argued that the Liar is harmless because truth is a property of propositions rather than sentences, and that the Liar sentence, though grammatical, simply fails to express a proposition: on her view the words are in order but the semantic work never gets done, so there is nothing for truth to attach to in the first place. When I pressed her with the obvious revenge — a sentence that says of itself that it does not express a true proposition — she replied that this sentence also fails to express one, and that her view licenses a principled division of sentences into those that express propositions and those that don't. When I then asked what settles, for arbitrary sentence-like strings, which side of the division they fall on, and whether the boundary itself can be made to lie about itself — by, say, a sentence that declares it is on the wrong side of the boundary or false-if-on-either — she said the boundary is fixed by "semantic dependency structure," though she couldn't say much more.

Please write one essay question, answerable in ninety minutes, that puts Dana's position at the center: the question should require students to (a) reconstruct the propositions-first move charitably, (b) say precisely why the revenge sentence is supposed to be harder for her than the original Liar was, (c) evaluate whether her division of sentences is a genuine stopping point or merely relocates the paradox one level up, and (d) say what the exchange teaches about the standing choice between hierarchical, gap, and revision-theoretic approaches — in particular, whether her position inherits the standard objection to gap theories (that they can't explain why we can't just say of the Liar that it fails to be true) or avoids it. The question must not be answerable by reciting the course menu; a student who has memorized the labels but never thought about where the paradox regenerates should score poorly. Along with the question, provide a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme out of 25 marks, built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how students typically get each wrong. Make sure one of the must-hit points concerns the difference between denying that the Liar is true and asserting something about it, since that is the trap most of my students fall into. Please end your entire reply with a single line beginning "Crux:" that states, in at most fifteen words, the specific move that separates a top answer from a merely competent one.
