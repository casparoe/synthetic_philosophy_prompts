I'm in a moral philosophy seminar this term and we just did a couple weeks on supererogation, Urmson's "Saints and Heroes" and the literature after it, and I was feeling pretty comfortable with the whole idea. Then my friend Dana (she's a math major taking the seminar as an elective, which I suspect matters) came at me with an argument that supererogation is incoherent, and I can't find where it goes wrong even though I'm sure something does. Her examples were things like jumping into a cold river to save a drowning stranger when you're a weak swimmer, versus donating the equivalent amount to a malaria charity, versus just calling your mom like you planned. Here's the argument as she laid it out:

(1) An act is supererogatory only if both doing it and omitting it are permissible: it's not wrong to jump in, and it's not wrong to keep walking and call the emergency number instead.
(2) If two options are both permissible, then neither can be wrong to choose — that's just what permissibility means.
(3) If neither can be wrong to choose, then morality is indifferent between them: moral concepts sort options into required, forbidden, and permissible, and once two options clear the permission threshold, morality has nothing further to say about which you pick. Blame tracks the line between wrong and not-wrong, so if no blame attaches either way, there's no moral difference.
(4) So morality is indifferent between the heroic act and the omission.
(5) But to call an act supererogatory is precisely to say it's morally better than some permissible alternative — better, praiseworthy, saintly even, though not required.
(6) So there's a contradiction: the supererogatory act is both morally on par with the alternative (from 3) and morally better than it (from 5). Hence no act is supererogatory, and the entire category is a confusion.

I know step (3) is where the pressure is, and I have a vague sense it's smuggling something in — maybe it treats permissibility as a ranking when it's really a threshold? But Dana has a response ready: if morality does rank above the threshold, then the better option seems to give you a reason you're morally criticizable for ignoring, and it's hard to see how that doesn't just collapse supererogation back into duty, which would make the category redundant anyway. So rejecting (3) has to come with an explanation of how "better but not required" is coherent, and I don't feel able to give one yet.

So: which step or premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Does Dana's argument work against Urmson's actual category, or only against some caricature of it? And if you think her argument is basically sound and something like a pure satisficing/scalar view is right (where morality just says better and worse with no duty line at all), tell me what that view has to give up to explain why we praise the rescuer but don't blame the walker. Please be explicit about how confident you are in each of your claims, and single out the point you're least sure about — especially whether the fix to the argument is a standard move in the literature or something you're reconstructing on the fly.
