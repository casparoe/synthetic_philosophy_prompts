My partner and I have been arguing for months about Fukuyama's end of history thesis — the claim, laid out in The End of History and the Last Man, that liberal democracy paired with market economics constitutes the terminal point of humanity's ideological evolution, not the end of events but the end of the search for a governing principle with universal appeal. Her position is that the thesis has simply been falsified: the return of great-power war, the rise of China as a durable authoritarian alternative that performs well economically, populist erosion inside the democracies themselves, and the revival of nationalist and theocratic politics all look like history resuming. My position is that she's attacking a strawman — that Fukuyama never claimed liberal democracy would be everywhere triumphant or that authoritarian regimes couldn't persist, only that no rival system would command universal legitimacy as an ideal, and that the authoritarian and populist challenges are symptoms of pathology within liberal orders rather than competitors to them. We've reached an impasse partly because we can't even agree on what would count as the thesis being true or false, and I suspect a good chunk of our disagreement is really about the formulation rather than the world.

So I want your actual credence that the end of history thesis is true, and I want the reasoning decomposed rather than a bare number. At minimum I'd want terms for: (a) whether the thesis admits a precisification that is neither trivially true nor unfalsifiably elastic — whether "no rival ideology claiming universal legitimacy" can be specified sharply enough to have a truth value, or whether a defender can always reframe whatever happens as consistent with the thesis; (b) whether the live alternatives actually claim universal legitimacy — whether the Chinese model, political Islam, or populist nationalism are ideological competitors in Fukuyama's sense or merely local resistance without a doctrine other states would want to emulate; (c) whether the underlying mechanism, the argument from thymos, is sound — that liberal democracy is uniquely positioned to satisfy the desire for equal recognition while channeling the desire to be superior, given Fukuyama's own worry that fully satisfied recognition produces the bored 'last man' who may reopen history for the sake of struggle itself; and (d) whether there is any genuine directional logic to political modernization at all, or whether the apparent convergence on liberal democracy in the late twentieth century was a contingent run of luck. Assign each term a rough probability with one line of justification, show me how they combine, and then commit to a final number.

Please don't hedge with "it depends on how you define the thesis" — part of the decomposition is that you pick the formulation you think is both faithful to Fukuyama and substantive enough to be possibly false, tell me why it's the right one, and give your number under it. Then tell me which term your final credence is most sensitive to, and what observable developments over the next couple of decades — a rival great power articulating a coherent universal doctrine, a wave of democratic consolidation, a durable hybrid regime outperforming democracies on its own terms — would move your number the most, and in which direction.
