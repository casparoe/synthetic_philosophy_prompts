I teach an upper-level seminar at the intersection of philosophy of biology and philosophy of mind — 15 students, one 90-minute session, in a room with movable tables and a whiteboard. The class has just worked through the distinction between cranes and skyhooks in explanation: cranes build the complex out of the simpler in installments, each stage resting on what has already been erected, while skyhooks lower the finished structure down from above in one piece. We have also discussed the difference between patient reductionism, which is happy to pass through intermediate structure, and greedy reductionism, which tries to collapse a phenomenon straight to its physical base in a single derivation — getting something true in principle that is nonetheless empty as an explanation.

Design an in-class game in which students run into both distinctions themselves, before either is named. My provisional idea, which you may improve on or replace: a tower-building game in which teams explain cards drawn from a deck spanning several levels — basic chemistry, single-cell behaviors such as bacterial quorum sensing, evolved organs, animal signaling, and human institutions like money or courts, plus a few deliberately exotic cards — and a team may explain a card only by citing (a) cards already accepted lower on the board, or (b) a small fixed set of physical primitives distributed at the start. Each team also holds two kinds of special moves: a skyhook, which explains any card in one stroke at a cost, and a shortcut, which attempts a single derivation from the primitive set straight to a top-level card, accepted or rejected by a judge. I want the rules arranged so that, in an audit round, skyhooks turn out to be unfalsifiable — they would have explained whatever card had been drawn — and so that shortcuts, though every step is true, get rejected because they omit the intermediate structure that does the real explanatory work. If you see a mechanic that produces these effects more reliably, propose it; the requirement is only that participants hit greedy reductionism and skyhook-thinking through play, not lecture.

Please provide: full rules and a materials list (I can print and cut cards in advance), a scoring scheme that requires no real money, a minute-by-minute timeline for the session, and a debrief that maps specific moments in play onto the two distinctions. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be frank about which feature of the real dispute the game cannot reproduce — in particular, the historical contingency of actual crane-building, and the fact that in the biosphere no one printed the deck in advance — so that I know what I must supply myself in the debrief.
