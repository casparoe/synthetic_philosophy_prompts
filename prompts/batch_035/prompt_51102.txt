Last autumn I wrote a long feature on the remapping of flood zones, and the closer I looked at the language regulators use — the so-called 1-in-100-year flood, which is officially a 1 percent chance in any given year — the less I understood what the number was claiming to be about. When I put this to a statistician I was interviewing, he said the confusion wasn't mine; the numbers really don't mean anything, and he sketched an argument I've since transcribed and rechecked. I can't find the flaw, but I'm confident there is one, because the conclusion would take out far too much.

His argument runs:

(1) A probability claim earns its place in empirical science only if it is testable — only if some possible observation would count for or against it.
(2) Probability claims are tested, when they are tested at all, by observed frequencies in a series of trials.
(3) A series of trials exists only where events of the same kind recur; a single, unrepeatable event generates no frequency.
(4) So a probability attached to a single event can be tested only by locating that event in a reference class of similar events and checking the class frequency.
(5) For the frequency to be relevant rather than merely available, the class members must resemble the event at issue in every causally pertinent respect.
(6) Events resembling a given reactor in every causally pertinent respect include at most the reactor's own operating history, so the reference class degenerates to the event itself, whose observed frequency is simply 0 or 1.
(7) Any broader class — all light-water reactors, all coastal rivers — drops pertinent similarities, so its frequency is the frequency of a different kind of event, not a test of the claim being made.
(8) Therefore no probability attached to a single, unrepeatable event has empirical content: not the reactor's failure odds, not tomorrow's 30 percent chance of rain, not the odds of one particular electron's spin measurement coming out as it does.

When I objected that weather forecasts of one-off events are checked all the time — a forecaster who says 30 percent twenty times should be right about six times — he said that tests the forecaster's calibration, not the individual forecast, and that calibration is exactly a reference class whose members are not similar in every pertinent respect, so by his own (7) it doesn't touch the single claim. That reply struck me as too quick, but I can't say why.

Here is what I want. First, tell me which step is the weak one, or if the argument is valid, which premise is cheapest to surrender — and be concrete about what each rejection costs, including the one you'd urge on me. Second, walk the diagnosis through the three standard interpretations of probability. It seems to me the frequentist, the propensity theorist, and the subjectivist must fail at different steps — the frequentist at (5) or (6), perhaps, the propensity theorist somewhere around (2), the subjectivist perhaps at (1) — but I want to know whether that's right, and what each is paying. Third, and this is the part I most want to get right in a follow-up piece: does the argument fail in the same place for the reactor, the rain forecast, and the quantum measurement, or do these cases genuinely come apart? If a single-event probability can be scientifically respectable in meteorology but not in reactor safety, or the reverse, I need to understand what makes the difference. For my part I'd rather keep (1) than nearly anything else — a science journalist who gives up on testability has given up on the job — but I want to know if I'm paying more than I think.
