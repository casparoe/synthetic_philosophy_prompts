I'd like a specification, before I go further into the literature, of what an adequate account of idealization in scientific modeling has to deliver. The occasion is a running dispute in my department over Friedman's 1953 methodological essay in economics — the position usually labeled the F-twip, or F-twist, by its critics: the idea that the unrealisticness of a theory's assumptions is simply irrelevant, and that only the accuracy of its predictions should count. I don't want to argue that essay head-on yet; I want the standard of adequacy first, so I can see what any account of idealization — Friedman's included — must get right. Please write the list of requirements, with the case or consideration behind each, and tell me which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps.

The cases I want the list built around, roughly in order of how much they worry me:

1. The benign cases. Point-mass frictionless planes, ideal gases, infinitely deep heat reservoirs. As the idealization is relaxed, the model's answers converge to the right ones. An adequate account must classify these as harmless and explain why — presumably something about stability under perturbation.

2. Cases where relaxing the assumption makes the model worse, at least locally. Models of traffic flow that treat drivers as identical particles yield clean macroscopic laws (shockwaves, phase transitions in flow density); a model of a national economy with a single representative agent can get aggregate behavior right where a heterogeneous model drowns in its own degrees of freedom. The account can't just say idealization is always temporary scaffolding to be removed, unless it can say something about when removal is a regression.

3. Cases where the unrealistic assumption is constitutive of the result. Deterministic evolutionary models with infinite populations: in finite populations, noise can flip which states are stable, so the qualitative results sometimes do not survive. Here the assumption isn't a simplification of anything; the exact model has no exact counterpart. Any adequate account must handle these, and I suspect this is where most accounts break.

4. The levitation-type objection. On the pure F-twist, a theory built on the assumption that agents levitate would be vindicated if its predictions came out right. This strikes nearly everyone as absurd, but the account needs to locate precisely what the levitation case has that the point-particle case lacks — presumably some connection between the assumption and the mechanism doing the explanatory work, not merely predictive success. If your account can't rule out levitation, it isn't an account of idealization; it's an account of lucky prediction.

5. The multiplicity of jobs a single false assumption can do. Some assumptions are negligibility claims (relax it and little changes), some are domain claims (it holds only within a stated range), some are heuristic (false, but needed to set up the mathematics at all). The account should respect this distinction, or else explain why it collapses.

6. Asymmetry between prediction and explanation. A model can predict well while explaining nothing, if its idealizations sever the link between the result and the machinery that produces it. If your account is purely predictive in its standard of legitimacy, say so plainly and defend it; otherwise it must say what idealization does to explanation.

Then the part I most want to see: identify any pair or subset of the requirements that are jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, whether the demand that the account give prospective guidance (telling the modeler in advance which assumptions are safe) conflicts with fidelity to actual practice, in which modelers typically learn which idealizations are harmless only by relaxing them and finding out. If some subset can't be jointly met, say which requirement you would drop and why. Finally, a brief scorecard: how do three contenders fare against the list — the F-twist itself, the Galilean picture on which idealization is a deliberate act of isolation to be progressively undone by concretization, and the view that models are useful fictions evaluated only by their output. Keep the scoring short; the weight should be on the criteria. And tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates — I suspect it's the constitutive-idealization one, but I'd like to know if you disagree.
