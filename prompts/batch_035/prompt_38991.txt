My friends and I have been playing Nomic for about six years — the game Peter Suber invented in 1982, in which the moves are mostly changes to the rules themselves, so the whole thing is essentially a running argument about its own constitution. Early on we established a standing referee to settle interpretive disputes; the office rotates, but our friend Dana has held it longest, takes it seriously, and keeps a notebook of her precedents. A new dispute is about to go to her, and before it does I would like to work out what she will say.

Her precedents, as best I can reconstruct them:

1. The self-locking rule. We had adopted a rule that no rule whose number ends in a zero could be amended. It was itself numbered 240. When someone tried to amend it, Dana ruled that it applied to itself and was therefore unamendable, even though the players who wrote it insisted they had meant to protect the old rules, not the protector itself. The text was the text.

2. The self-raising award. A proposal raised the points awarded for an adopted proposal from ten to twenty, and its author claimed the higher award for that very proposal. Dana gave him the twenty: points are scored at the moment a proposal resolves, by then the new rule was in force, and nothing exempts an author from the benefit of his own rule.

3. The bootstrapped rule. A player declared a certain text to be binding on everyone, arguing that no rule forbade the declaration and that rules are simply texts we treat as binding. Dana refused to recognize it: a rule exists only by passing through the rule-changing procedure, and no text can confer authority on itself.

4. The threshold trick. A proposal would have lowered the vote needed to adopt amendments from two-thirds to a bare majority; it itself received only a bare majority, and its author argued that it had thereby adopted itself under its own new threshold. Dana ruled that it had failed: whether a proposal is adopted is decided by the requirements in force at the moment the votes are counted, and a proposal's content cannot reach back to validate its own adoption.

5. The two-game winner. A drafting slip left our win rule saying that reaching 100 points wins the game and all subsequent games. A player hit 100, won, and on that basis claimed to have won the next game in advance. Dana refused: the extra words had no effect, and a fresh game starts fresh, whatever the old rule literally said.

Now the new case. A player has submitted a proposal through the ordinary procedure and under the normal threshold, reading in full: upon adoption of this rule, the player who drafted it is deemed to have won the current game. It will come to a vote next session. If it is adopted, does the drafter win the game on the spot?

Can you predict Dana's ruling? More precisely: what rule or rules is she actually running across those five precedents — to me they seem jointly consistent with more than one principle, and I am not sure which one is doing the work. Rulings 2 and 5 seem to pull in opposite directions here, but I cannot tell which one this case more closely resembles, or whether the common ground in 3 and 4 settles anything once a proposal has cleared the ordinary procedure. Give me your best guess with your reasons, say what principle she would be adopting with each possible answer, and tell me what further case we could put to her, on some later occasion, that would best tell the candidate principles apart.
