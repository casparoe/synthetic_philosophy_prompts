You are a senior decision theorist — someone who has spent a career on expected utility theory and its rivals, and who has no patience for hand-waving. I have been asked to circulate a one-page statement of my views on risk and rationality for a workshop, and before sending it I would like a consistency audit from you. Here is the package I have landed on:

1. Decision theory is normative all the way down: it says what you ought to do, and an agent who violates it has made a mistake, not merely exhibited a taste.
2. The right framework is risk-weighted expected utility: an act is evaluated by ranking its outcomes from best to worst and aggregating them with weights determined by a risk function, so that, for a risk-averse agent, the worse outcomes carry more weight than their probabilities alone would assign. My reason for abandoning standard expected utility theory is that its Independence axiom is too strong, and my own preferences in the Allais case show it: in the classic setup I would take the certain million over the gamble in the first pair, and in the second pair I would take the option with the higher chance of the larger prize — and I think both choices are rational.
3. Within that framework, the risk function is a free parameter on a par with the utility function: there is no uniquely rational degree of risk aversion, just as there is no uniquely rational utility function. Different rational agents may weight risk differently, and none of them is mistaken.
4. Some things remain non-negotiable. First, if one option is better than another in every state of the world, you must take the better one. Second, rationality forbids ending up, through a sequence of choices you could see coming, with less in every state than some plan you could have adopted at the outset — an agent who can be led into that has defective preferences and has been exploited, whatever the theory says about each choice taken on its own.
5. Impartial ethics is different. When I act for others rather than for myself — a public official allocating a scarce vaccine between two groups, say — risk attitude drops out entirely: the right act maximizes expected total welfare exactly, not as an approximation that would be justified if similar choices were repeated many times. It is not merely unadvisable but wrong for such an official to be risk-averse about the worse outcome in the way I am entitled to be when I buy insurance for myself. And I accept the ex ante Pareto principle: if every affected person, acting on her own rational preferences, would prefer one policy to another before the uncertainty resolves, then that policy is at least as good for society.

I honestly cannot tell which of these, if any, pull against each other. The set feels stable to me, which is exactly when I mistrust it. So before I answer, please work through the commitments pairwise, in whatever depth you need — but I want only the final audit, not the working. Decide which tensions are real and which dissolve on inspection; rank the real ones by severity; and for each, tell me what would have to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the package. Do not flag everything equally: a list of five tensions all billed as severe tells me nothing. The whole audit must come in under 700 words.
