Every other month I run a little discussion group at work — six or seven coworkers, none of them philosophers, one 90-minute session on some ethics-ish topic. Next up is standing to blame and hypocrisy, and what I want from you is a plan for how I should run the explanation, not the explanation itself. The core thing I want them to walk out with is the distinction between whether someone's blame is on target and whether they're well positioned to deliver it — the hypocrite can be right about the wrongness but lack the standing to blame, and "do as I say, not as I do" doesn't settle it either way. I'm torn on the carrying example: the obvious one is the parent who smokes telling their kid not to smoke, but I worry it actually undercuts the point, because the parent seems to have a special license there ("I know because I did it"). Maybe a better carrier is the coworker who quietly takes credit for other people's work and then gives a big speech about team ethics? You decide — and tell me what the example needs to have and to lack. Misconceptions I'm bracing for: that hypocrisy shows the blame is wrong (conflating standing with truth), that you have to be blameless yourself before you can blame anyone for anything, and — this one is subtle and I half beleive it myself — that the only cure for hypocritical blame is to stop blaming rather than to stop the hypocrisy. Where in the sequence should each of those get headed off, and in what order do the pieces need to come so nothing lands before its time? Also tell me where to slot the question of wether standing to blame is even a real moral phenomenon or just a rude way of pointing back at the blamer, and what one question I can ask at the end to check it landed. Skip the academic literature entirely, they'll glaze over. Lead with the bottom line — your call on the right sequence and carrier — in your first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question.
