I'm a handling editor at a mid-tier interdisciplinary journal — we publish a lot of complex-systems, physics-adjacent biology, origin-of-life type stuff — and I swear half the manuscripts I triage lean on the word "emergent" in one of two dodgy ways. Either it's a placeholder for "we don't know how this works yet," or it's quietly doing metaphysical work the authors never defend, like claiming the phenomenon is in principle not derivable from the micro level when all they've actually shown is that nobody currently has the derivation. And I get the opposite move too: reviewers dismiss whole lines of work as "not fundamental" because in principle it should reduce to chemistry or physics, so supposedly it isn't a real level of explanation at all.

I want a procedure I can actually run when triaging a manuscript. Constraints: about twenty minutes per paper, it's just me, no committee, and I'm a scientist not a philosopher, so it has to be in terms I can apply without knowing the literature. Ground it in what philosophy of science actually says about reduction and emergence — the difference between reduction as successful derivation (thermodynamics out of statistical mechanics, once you fix things like thermodynamic limits and coarse-graining) versus reduction as a promissory note, and weak emergence (you can in principle compute your way up from the micro level, it's just intractable or you need the higher-level description to see anything) versus strong emergence (the micro facts plus the laws don't fix the macro facts, period). The procedure should give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and which outcomes mean the emergence talk is harmless shorthand, which mean I should flag for reviewers that the authors owe an argument, and which mean it's actually a substantive claim that deserves attention rather than suspicion.

Then run it on three manuscripts I'm plausibly going to recieve: (1) an origin-of-life paper arguing that life is emergent, so we shouldn't expect to reconstruct it step by step from prebiotic chemistry and should fund the search for general principles instead; (2) a paper on flocking or superconductivity that uses "emergent" roughly the way physicists do and might be deploying it perfectly legitimately; and (3) a consciousness-adjacent paper that appeals to strong emergence to get around the explanatory gap. Include at least one case where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like, and tell me honestly where the twenty-minute version runs out — where the difference between "intractable" and "impossible in principle" just isn't decidable by an editor, and I'm making a judgment call dressed up as a step in the flowchart. Also be upfront about what the procedure can't do at all.
