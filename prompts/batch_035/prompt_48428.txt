I've been asked to run a seventy-five-minute session on vagueness for our company's trust-and-safety policy team — the people who write and apply the rules governing which user posts get removed, restricted, or left up. The session's focus is the dispute between epistemicism and supervaluationism: whether there is, in principle, a fact of the matter as to whether a borderline post violates a policy, or whether borderline statements of the form "this post is harassing" are neither true nor false. The team is analytically sharp, experienced, and deeply skeptical of philosophy as an academic indulgence; they will arrive convinced that the issue is just imprecise drafting, and that the remedy is always to write a better definition. Don't explain the debate to me; plan how I should teach it. I want to know the order in which the pieces have to arrive and why — what depends on what, and in particular whether the sorites-style reasoning they'll need has to come before either theory is named, or the theories will just sound like stipulations. I want one carrying example built from their own working material — perhaps a continuum of posts running from clearly fine to clearly impermissible — and I want you to tell me what that example must have and what it must lack (for instance, it can't be resolvable by any plausible rewrite of the policy, or the whole lesson collapses). I want the misconception map: I'm guessing they'll conflate vagueness with ambiguity, take the epistemicist to be claiming that more training data could reveal where the line is, and read the supervaluationist as saying that borderline claims are somehow both true and false — but tell me what I've missed, and exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be headed off rather than left to fester until the Q&A. I also want one question I can pose at the end that would reveal whether they've understood the dispute itself, as opposed to merely having taken a side in it. Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — I suspect higher-order vagueness, but I'd like your judgment on whether it can be entirely skipped or whether some gesture at it is load-bearing — and how I should frame that refusal so it reads as intellectual honesty rather than evasion.
