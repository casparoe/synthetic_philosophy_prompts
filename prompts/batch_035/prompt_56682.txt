I keep hearing the 1975 Asilomar conference held up as the precautionary principle's finest hour, and I want a careful analysis of whether that's actually true, because something about it nags at me. Here's the episode as I understand it, so we're working from the same facts: in 1974 Paul Berg and a group of leading researchers called for a voluntary worldwide moratorium on recombinant DNA experiments (Berg had been spooked, partly by Robert Pollack, about the last step of putting SV40 tumor-virus DNA into E. coli), then in February 1975 about 140 biologists, along with some lawyers and physicians, met at Asilomar in California and drew up voluntary guidelines that matched containment levels to estimated risk, which the NIH then formalized in 1976. What everyone feared — lab strains picking up dangerous genes and escaping, workers getting cancer, that kind of thing — never materialized, and by the early 80s most people judged the guidelines overcautious, so they got progressively loosened. Meanwhile the risks that actually became contentious later, like deliberate release of engineered organisms into the environment, were basically not on the agenda at Asilomar at all, and part of what the guidelines achieved looks pretty pragmatic in hidsight: preempting messier public regulation (there were soon city council hearings in Cambridge, Massachusetts about Harvard's labs) and keeping the science in the scientists' own hands.

So here's what I'm asking for: analyze this episode through the lens of the precautionary principle, running in both directions.
