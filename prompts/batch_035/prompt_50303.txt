so heres a thing i keep running into and can't get unstuck. i'm a clinical psych PhD student who's spent the last couple years reading philosophy of psychiatry, and there's one argument that never resolves no matter how many times i sit through it. one side thinks psychiatric classification is basically in the business of carving nature at its joints: disorders track underlying dysfunctions, and our categories are (ideally) natural kinds or something close enough — that's the neo-Kraepelinian operating assumption behind the DSM, and it's what Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis tries to make precise, the idea being that a disorder is a psychological mechanism failing to do what it was selected to do. the other side is the Hacking crowd: human kinds are interactive, classifying people changes them — the people under the label adjust their self-understanding, doctors adjust in response, the category migrates, and round it goes — so asking whether the DSM categories are valid in the chemical-element sense is asking the wrong question. everyone in my orbit knows all the moves by now, and literally nobody has conceded anything in years, which is what's bugging me. i don't want a survey of who said what, i want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. my working hypothesis, presented as a hypothesis: the two sides are answering different questions under one heading. the naturalists are answering an epistemic question — does the category track real causal structure, will it hold still across contexts — and the Hacking people are answering a diachronic/practical one — what happens to people who live under a label, and can a classification ever stay still once the classified know about it. the fight only feels like one debate because both get run under the word validity. but i can also tell a second story where it's a genuine substantive disagreement about a hidden empirical question nobody can test: how big the feedback loops actually are. if looping effects are marginal noise around a stable underlying condition, the naturalists win; if the loops swallow the category, the constructionists win; and since nobody can measure loop size, the empirical core stays untouched and the whole thing proceeds on vibes. and then there's a nagging third option, that the question is ill-posed because natural kind was never a fixed enough notion for either side to be contradicting the other. so: which of these is right (or is it a fourth thing?), argue for it from what each side actually claims, and tell me what would count as progress here — some distinction, a longitudinal study design, a formal model of the feedback, a change of question — or whether no progress should be expected, and why. one more thing that i keep turning over and want your read on: Hacking himself more or less said goodbye to the making-up-people topic decades before he died and kept coming back to say goodbye again. is that a clue that the program stalled for structural reasons, or just biography? sorry for the length, this has been bugging me thru a whole qual exam.
