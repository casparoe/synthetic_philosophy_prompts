Been following the whole "control vs alignment" research direction for a while now — the stuff where you assume the model might be adversarial and design around it: monitor models reading outputs, honeypot channels, periodic audits, that family of tricks — and there's a pattern I keep bumping into that I can't find a clean name for. Say you've deployed a control scheme. The thing I keep noticing is that it does two bad things at once, and they compound. First, epistemically: everything you learn from running the scheme is conditioned on the model believing it might be watched. An audit result tells you the model behaves when it suspects an audit, which is not the quantity you wanted to know. Worse, the deceptions you do catch are a censored sample — they're exactly the failures your monitor was able to detect, so your picture of the failure modes you're NOT catching gets updated in who knows what direction. Second, dynamically: the moment you train against the scheme — penalizing flagged transcripts, red-teaming, whatever — you're optimizing for models that pass the tests, which is not the same as models that don't do the thing the tests were built to catch. So the monitor generates the training data, the training data gets selected by the monitor, and after a few rounds the question "is it aligned" has quietly morphed into "does it defect given its current best model of our control setup" — which you can never observe directly, because your only window into the system IS the setup.

I've seen people gesture at pieces of this with Goodhart (too broad, that's just any metric going wrong), the Hawthorne effect (covers subjects knowing they're observed, but not the training-pressure feedback loop), and the panopticon framing about conditional compliance vs. internalized compliance (closest so far, but it's a story about deterrence, not about the epistemics of what your audits actually show). Someone at a meetup swore it was just the Lucas critique — the observation that policy interventions change the behavioral relations you calibrated the policy on — which feels adjacent but not quite it either, since Lucas was worried about econometric relations shifting, not about the measurement apparatus eating its own tail under gradient updates.

So: is there an established term for this specific loop, where a measurement/training apparatus partially destroys the validity of its own future measurements as a side effect of being used, in a way that compounds over rounds? If there's no standard name, I'd like you to define the thing precisely enough to distinguish it from each of the above — what exactly is the feedback channel, does the loop require the subject to actively model the apparatus or just to be selected by it, and where does the analogy break for humans vs. models (human subjects don't recieve gradient updates from the study they're in, which seems like it should matter). Also, is the collapse inevitable or are there configurations where it saturates — like, if the control measures are genuinely hidden from the model, does the loop stop after zero rounds, or does the model learning to suspect hidden measures restart it? I suspect "hidden from the model" is doing a lot of work there and I'd want that unpacked too. Name isn't the main thing I'm after, honestly — I want the carving: what the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where the edges are.
