I'm setting the philosophy of science section of our PhD comprehensive exam and want one essay question on structural realism that a candidate could answer in 90 minutes and that cannot be passed by reciting the standard Worrall-on-Poincaré narrative. Base the question on the following scenario, which you should state compactly in the question itself: classical electromagnetism admits an exact duality under which electric and magnetic quantities are interchanged, so there are two empirically equivalent formulations that appear to disagree about what the fundamental quantities are. In the scenario, one character — an epistemic structural realist — argues that duality supports her view, since what is preserved across the two formulations, namely the shared mathematical structure, is all we are ever entitled to believe in. A second character — a scientific antirealist — replies that if structure exhausts the theory's content, the structural realist cannot even say that the two formulations describe the same world rather than merely fitting the same evidence, because 'same structure' is a claim that outruns the observational consequences both characters accept. The question should require candidates to adjudicate this exchange, and in doing so to address three specific pressure points: whether the ontic structural realist's claim that there is nothing more to being an entity than occupying a position in a structure helps with the duality case or generates a worse problem about individuating entities; where exactly Newman's objection to structuralism bites, and whether it is a problem for the duality case specifically or only for the Ramsey-sentence reconstruction; and whether duality gives the structural realist a distinctive response to underdetermination or merely restates it at the level of structure. Also require: a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth 5 marks each (total 25), with a one-line note on the most common way candidates botch each point — e.g., treating Newman's objection as just another form of the underdetermination argument.
