I'm a 3rd year PhD student in political economy, and every fall I run a session for the incoming MA students on the "why are some nations rich and others poor" debate. Last year I did it as a lecture and everyone nodded along and then wrote term papers saying "it's probably a mix of institutions and geography and culture," which is the worst possible outcome, so this year I want to force actual commitments. Please write me a decision tree that starts from something everyone in the room already accepts — that income gaps between countries are enormous and have persisted for a long time — and ends at named positions.

Some constraints on how I want it built:

- Build the logical space FIRST, from the questions, and only then locate the actual positions in it. Don't start from the list of famous books and backfill the tree around them.
- Every branch node has to be a question someone can answer without knowing which position it leads to. Prefer concrete contrasts over abstract commitments — e.g., what they make of the fact that Korea is now two countries with a giant income gap, or that the Americas seem to have had a reversal of fortune (places that were relatively rich and densely settled around 1500 are relatively poorer today), or that landlocked tropical countries dominate the bottom of the rankings. Don't feel bound to those exact cases, pick your own if better, but that's the genre I want.
- The tree must include at least these leaves: the strong institutions view (inclusive vs extractive, with critical junctures like colonization doing the causal work), deep-geography determinism (climate, disease burden, domesticable species, continental axis), culture as an independent variable, and the causal-inference-skeptic position — the one that says the whole debate runs on regressions with ~150 country data points and centuries of reverse causation baked in, so "why are nations rich" may just not be an answerable question at the nation-state level. And no, the skeptic can't be a throwaway leaf — my advisor's whole thing is that identification skeptics get taught as a footnote when the actual literature is more methodologically pessimistic than the textbook story, so if anything that branch should be the most developed part of the tree.
- Include the interaction/multiplicative positions too: geography selecting for institutions, culture as mediator, etc. These aren't cop-outs, they're real positions, and half the field holds one. Wherever a combination of branch answers ends up unoccupied (say, pure culture with no geography channel and no worries about identification), say whether nobody sits there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
- One fork somewhere should be about granularity: whether nation-states are even the right unit, or whether the action is at the region / frontier / within-country level. That one tends to get skipped in teaching and I want students to hit it before they reach any leaf.
- Flag honestly which pairs of leaves the tree can't seperate. My suspicion is that "institutions as the deep cause" and "geography working through institutions" are going to be observationally equivalent on most of the diagnostic cases, and I'd rather students see that than pretend the tree is sharper than it is.

Aim for maybe 8-12 branch nodes and no more than 10 leaves. Audience is bright MA students with one semester of econometrics — they know what an instrument is, but they havent read any of the primary literature. One last thing: I don't care about author names or book titles in the leaves. Phrases like "the reversal-of-fortune institutions view" are better than citations anyway, since I want them thinking about the structure of the argument rather than who said it. If you want to add a short scoring-key style summary at the end ("if you answered mostly the second option at the first three forks..."), go for it, but the tree itself is the main deliverable.
