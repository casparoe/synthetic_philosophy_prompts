Two friends of mine have been arguing about immigration for literally years and it never goes anywhere. One is basically an open-borders guy — he thinks freedom of movement belongs in the same tier as freedom of speech, and that a state turning away peaceful would-be migrants at gunpoint is a rights violation, full stop. The other defends the right to exclude: political communities get to control their membership as part of self-determination, sort of like clubs (or at least like collectives with projects worth protecting), and that control isn't automatically an injustice even if it has bad effects. Neither of them is a hack, they both know the standard literature territory.

Write the joint statement they should have produced after a serious, good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want:

1. A numbered section of claims both could actually sign — about things like the sheer size of the global economic gains from freer movement, the moral weight of the coercion involved in border enforcement, the difference between excluding newcomers and deporting residents, and the fact that some migration restrictions (e.g. screening for violent criminals) don't divide them at all. Don't pad this with platitudes, only stuff they'd really agree on.

2. Their exact remaining disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording — i.e. no strawmen, and no wording that smuggles in a win for one side.

3. The cruxes underneath it all. Two I suspect matter: (a) is freedom of movement a basic liberty that can only be limited for very weighty reasons, or more like an important interest that can be fairly outweighed by a community's other claims? and (b) does the right to exclude require justification to the excluded outsiders themselves, or only to the community's own members? For each crux, give at least one argument, case, or piece of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. For example: how does each of them expect the moral picture to change under a bundle like "you may come, but you pay a hefty entry fee, can't vote for 15 years, and can't access most welfare for a decade" — does that dissolve the objection or reveal it was never about fiscal effects? And would each side predict such a scheme is politically stable, or that it collapses into either full openness or creeping exclusion?

4. If any part of their fight turns out to be verbal — say, about what "a right to exclude" even means once you seperate blanket exclusion from regulating the terms of entry — flag which part and why.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table (columns like: disagreement, side A's accepted phrasing, side B's accepted phrasing, underlying crux, what would bear on it, predictions). Tone can be casual but the wording of the disagreement section has to be careful — that's the whole point. This is for our reading group, not a journal, so don't bloat it with citations either, just make the thinking sharp.
