I'm a third year cog sci PhD student and my labmate and I have been arguing in circles about something I've kinda done to myself. Quick background: I'm diagnosed with ADHD and take prescribed stimulants, and nobody ever treats that as cheating. But over the summer I also bought one of those consumer brain stimulation headsets (the tDCS ones marketed for focus) that I now use a few times a week while studying, because the meds alone don't really rescue my afternoons. My labmate thinks these two things are categorically different: the pill treats a condition, the headset is me trying to be better than well, and she says the fact that I can't see the difference is exactly why the distinction matters. The thing that keeps tripping me up is that on the attention tests they gave me at diagnosis, even WITH medication I score around the 30th percentile — i.e., worse than the average person who's never been near a clinic. So if the headset bumps me from below-typical up to typical, is it in the same moral category as my prescription, or the same category as an athlete doping? And while we were arguing my roommate chimed in that she takes a beta blocker before every job talk because her hands shake, she has no diagnosis at all, and literally nobody in the department thinks that's weird — including my labmate, who defended it as "just managing nerves."

So here's what I actually want from you: is there a principled way to draw the line my labmate is drawing, between fixing something broken and upgrading yourself, and if so which side of it am I on with the headset? Does the line run through statistics (anything that moves you toward the population average is treatment) or does it have to be something more evaluative, like what counts as a genuinely human life going okay? And does the fact that we're all competing for the same handful of academic jobs change the analysis — like, if half the department is quietly doing something similar, is my headset different than if I'm the only one, the way doping is different in a sport where nobody else does it? My labmate's strongest point, I think, is that if we let "I felt below my potential" justify interventions, then the concept of a medical need eventually swallows everything, since everyone is below their potential at something.

Honestly a big part of why I'm asking is that I want to know whether I should feel guilty. Not "is it technically allowed" — I checked and it's not against anything — but whether my labmate's moral intuition is picking up on something real that I'm rationalizing away, or whether her line, once you try to actually draw it, falls apart in exactly the cases we described (my below-average baseline, my roommate's shaking hands, the fact that she gives one a pass and not the other). If you think the line does survive, tell me where it comes down on all three of us. And if you think it doesn't survive, tell me what, if anything, replaces it — because "there's no distinction so anything goes" feels like it proves too much, and I don't think either of us actually beleives it.
