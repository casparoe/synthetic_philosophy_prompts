I'm taking a philosophy of science course that has an ML unit bolted on, and I keep coming back to the argument about whether neural networks "really" have representations, which as far as I can tell has been stuck for years. On one side, the mechanistic interpretability people treat discovered features as genuine things in the network — they find a feature with sparse autoencoders, clamp it on or off, the model's behaviour changes in the predicted direction, case closed. On the other side there are the anti-realist types who say the features are artifacts of whichever method you used to find them, that the network is just matrix multiplications with no commitments of its own, and that carving it up into "concepts" is us projecting onto the weights. And a third crowd hovers around saying the question is just confused. Nobody budges — the interp people keep publishing feature atlases, the skeptics keep saying "so what". My working hypothesis is that it's partly a verbal fight about the word "real" smuggled in via "representation", but there seems to be an empirical core hiding in there too, like whether the same features get recovered under different methods, architectures, and training runs, or whether polysemanticity and superposition mean the "true" units of the network aren't neuron-aligned features at all. So: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two different questions — one scientific, one metaphysical — being run under a single heading? Diagnose it based on what each side actually says, and then tell me what would genuinely count as progress given your diagnosis, whether that's some kind of experimental result, a sharper way of posing the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected here. Please write the answer in British English.
