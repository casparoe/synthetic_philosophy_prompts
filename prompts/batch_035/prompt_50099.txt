I've loved Dead Poets Society since I first saw it as a teenager, and over the past few months I've been reading Whitehead's essays on education on my own — no background in philosophy, I just picked up The Aims of Education after someone mentioned it to me. Something about the pairing has been nagging at me, and I'd like help working it out.

Whitehead's most famous complaint in that book is about inert ideas: knowledge that is received into the mind without being used, tested, or thrown into fresh combinations. Watching the film again with that in mind, Welton Academy looks like a walking illustration of the diagnosis — the boys can recite the textbook's introduction ranking poems on a graph of greatness, and Keating has them tear those pages out. So far, so congenial. But Whitehead also argues that education proceeds in a rhythm of three recurring stages — romance, precision, and generalization — and that romance, the stage of first wonder and half-knowledge, must give way to exact discipline, which then opens back out into wide, connected understanding. Nobody is meant to stay in romance.

Here is my puzzle. Keating is a magnificent teacher of the romance stage: the courtyard scene where the boys find their own walks, standing on desks to see differently, the cave. But the film never shows him teaching the precision stage — the actual craft of scansion, the drudgery of meter, which on Whitehead's account is what comes next after you catch fire. Neil catches fire as an actor, but he has no disciplined road from the fire to the craft, and when his father closes off the one institution that could have provided it, everything collapses into tragedy. Is the film quietly showing what happens when romance is never allowed to ripen into precision — a deeper critique of Welton than audiences usually take from it? Or does the film simply romanticize romance, in a way Whitehead himself would have found sentimental?

I'd like a reading that takes both the film and Whitehead's position seriously. Where does his framework genuinely illuminate particular scenes — I'm especially curious about Todd Anderson, the shy boy who can't write a poem and is made to improvise one aloud in front of the class: is that romance, precision, or something his cycle has no slot for? And where does the film resist the framework rather than confirm it? I also want to know if there is something the film understands about education that Whitehead misses — perhaps the role of the group of boys egging each other on, which doesn't look like any stage in his rhythm at all. Please don't flatten the film into an illustration of the theory; if the fit is loose in places, I'd rather hear that than have it papered over.
