Ancient LW lurker here, finally de-lurking over a Berkeley question. After years of only knowing the caricature (esse est percipi, trees are ideas, kick a rock), I actually sat down and read the Three Dialogues, and I was doing fine with the sensible-qualities material — the argument that intense heat is a pain, and a pain can't exist unperceived, I can see how that's supposed to work, whatever I think of it. But then Philonous makes a move that people apparently call the master argument, and I genuinely can't tell whether it's clever or a con. As I read it: Hylas tries to imagine a tree standing in some remote, solitary place with nobody around, and Philonous pounces — all you're doing is conceiving the idea of a tree in a solitary place; the conceiving itself is a mental act, so the object of your thought never actually exists unperceived. At most, Philonous says, you imagine or suppose that you don't perceive it, but that supposing is itself a form of perceiving. Hylas concedes surprisingly fast, and honestly I would have caved too, because something about it feels like it works while also feeling like a trick, and I can't put my finger on where the trick is.

So, two things I'd like help with. First, what exactly is the slide here — is it an equivocation between the act of conceiving and the content conceived, i.e., the opponent claims you can conceive-of-a-thing-existing-in-a-world-without-perceivers, and Berkeley answers a different claim, that the conceiving can't occur unperceived, which nobody denied? Or is there a more charitable reading where it does real work — something like a constraint on reference, that any candidate mind-independent object is only ever picked out under mental descriptions, so the phrase unperceived object is never directly about anything, and you can only ever think you're thinking it? Second, I half-remember a one-line parody from somewhere — by the same reasoning, nothing can be conceived that isn't conceived by me specifically, so wouldn't that slide all the way down to solipsism, each mind locked into its own world? Does that parody actually strike the argument's logical form, or just a strawman version of it? Explain like you're writing an email to a friend: continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. And don't be gentle with me or with Berkeley — if the argument is a sleight of hand, tell me which finger the coin is under.
