I'm writing an essay for a course on non-classical logic and want a reading of Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four through dialetheism and paraconsistency. The obvious reading of doublethink is psychological: the citizens of Oceania believe two contradictory things and train themselves not to notice the clash. But I want to press a harder question: does the novel dramatize a society in which contradictions are true, not merely believed — where the Party's arithmetic (two plus two makes five) and the Ministry of Truth's continual rewriting of the past make statements both true and false at once? Keep a crucial distinction in view: believing p and also believing not-p is not the same as believing that p and not-p are both true, and it matters whether Orwell's characters ever cross that line or are simply stuck one step short of it. Where does a dialetheist reading illuminate specific scenes — O'Brien demanding that Winston see five fingers where there are four, or Winston's daily work destroying the documentary past — and where does the novel resist it, given that Orwell plainly intends doublethink as a pathology while dialetheists like Graham Priest hold that some contradictions are simply true? And does the novel add anything to the debate that the standard argumentative treatments of dialetheism don't — for instance, a political account of who gets to decide which contradictions are true?
