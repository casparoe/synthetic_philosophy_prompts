Last week at dinner, two friends of mine argued about what our friend Daniel, who faces risky surgery next month, should believe about his prospects. (1) Helen said he should believe he will recover: patients who expect to recover fare measurably better, and a belief that helps bring about the state of affairs it represents looks like a genuine reason to hold it. (2) Marcus replied that she had confused a reason to want the belief with a reason for the belief itself: reasons for belief must bear on its truth, and a belief's beneficial effects are evidence of nothing about its truth — they are the wrong kind of reason. (3) Helen answered that where the belief is partly self-fulfilling, the world cooperates with it, so the connection to truth is present after all. (4) Marcus said the deeper obstacle is that belief is not under the control of the will: Daniel cannot simply decide to believe on practical grounds, so the advice is empty unless it becomes advice about actions — avoiding morbid reading, keeping company, cultivating routine — and reasons for those actions are not reasons for the belief. (5) Helen replied that this conceded her point: if practical considerations may rightly govern how Daniel goes about shaping his own mind, then practical reasons can, however indirectly, be reasons for belief.

Neither would yield, and I could not referee it. Please adjudicate the exchange step by step: say who is right at each of the five moves and why, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing. Please write your answer in British English.
