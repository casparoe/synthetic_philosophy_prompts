So I'm a novelist, mostly historical fiction, and I'm deep into planning a multi-generational book set in a port town over about 150 years, and something about the craft itself has been bugging me. My agent, my editor, every workshop freind of mine, and honestly every craft book I've ever read push the same thing: history in a novel has to enter through individual people making decisions in particular scenes. The genre conventions basically force it — no reader wants sixty pages on the silting of a harbor or shifts in rainfall patterns unless its dramatized through somebody's eyes, so the geology and the climate end up as painted backdrop while the actual plot runs on smugglers and an arranged marriage and a cholera outbreak. But the book I *want* to write is one where the real causes are the slow stuff: the harbor silting up, the wheat belt moving, inheritance law, a three-century shift in who controls the trade routes. Every time I try to make an individual character's choice carry genuine causal weight it feels fake to me, like a bow someone drew on top of a structure.

I've been reading around and I gather there's a French historian, Braudel of the Annales school, who split historical time into scales — a fast time of events and individuals, a middle time of economic and social cycles, and a nearly motionless time of geography and climate — and who basically thought the fast layer was the least explanatory, just the surface chop while the tides do the work. So heres my actual question: what theory of historical causation does my genre presuppose? Historical fiction, as practiced, seems to quietly commit to something like the opposite of Braudel — that history is made by choices, that individuals are where causes live, that a reader's sympathy for one person is itself a claim about what mattered. Is that right? Is it a genuine commitment, or just a marketing constraint (readers need a face) that's actually compatible with a longue-durée view of what's driving things? And do the genre's own conventions even presuppose consistent things — like, the "ordinary person swept up in history" trope treats individuals as powerless, but the "one decision changed everything" trope treats them as decisive, and my genre shamelessly uses both.

If you can reconstruct what the conventions collectively imply about how history works, I'd really like you to also push through to the practical end: what would have to change in the form of a novel if a writer took Braudel's picture seriously and made the slow structures the protagonist? Is that still a novel or does it turn into something else, and are there ways to smuggle structure-time into a book that still satisfies the conventions? Please answer in American English — my last publisher's copyeditor was British and the spelling battles were a whole thing, dont ask.
