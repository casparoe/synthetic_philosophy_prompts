After a panic attack in the spring I was diagnosed with depersonalization-derealization disorder, and for the last several months I have been living with the intermittent conviction that the person having my experiences is somehow absent. My therapist, who knows I read a lot of cognitive science, suggested I look at Douglas Hofstadter's *I Am a Strange Loop*, and told me — in so many words — that the self is a hallucination, so there is nothing there to lose sight of, and that I might find this reassuring rather than terrifying. In the book I found the line that she was presumably compressing: the "I" is a "hallucination hallucinated by a hallucination." I have to admit that at first pass this horrified me more than the disorder does. It sounded like my therapist had confirmed that the missing person is genuinely missing — that what I've been calling myself all along is an optical effect, and my symptoms are simply accurate perception.

I assume something has gone wrong with how I'm hearing the slogan, because the same sentence is apparently supposed to be consoling. So I'd like help taking it apart. Please give me a response in the following sections, in this order:

**The menu of readings** (roughly 400 words). Lay out the distinct precise theses the claim "the self is a hallucination" could be expressing — starting with readings so weak they are near-truisms, through middling readings on which the self is a constructed model or symbol that the brain builds and uses, up to the strongest readings on which there simply is no self, and perhaps the further reading that the hallucination-talk suggests a strange loop specifically rather than a mere illusion. For each reading, say whether it is plausible and what would count as evidence for or against it.

**Which reading Hofstadter holds** (roughly 150 words). Given the "hallucination hallucinated by a hallucination" formulation and the strange-loop apparatus it comes from, which of the menu's readings is his — and, importantly, does his loop structure make the self any *less* illusory than a plain hallucination, or does the self-referential character actually give the hallucination a kind of reality or causal standing that ordinary illusions lack?

**Why anyone thinks this comforts** (roughly 200 words). Explain the therapeutic logic: on which readings of the slogan does my therapist's reassurance work, and on which does the sentence become bad news — a diagnosis rather than a consolation?

**The danger readings** (roughly 250 words). Identify which readings, if true, would license the thought that depersonalization is insight — that I am perceiving correctly and the felt self is a fiction worth seeing through — and say whether any reading on the menu, Hofstadter's included, actually supports that conclusion, or whether it is a non sequitur on all of them.

**What I should actually take from the book** (roughly 100 words). Given the above, the honest takeaway for someone in my position.
