I'm a PhD student in intellectual history, and at a recent workshop a colleague waved off my reconstruction of what a 1640s pamphleteer was arguing for with Roland Barthes's line that the author is dead — as though that settled whether appeals to authorial intention are legitimate. I suspect the slogan has drifted far from the essay and now bundles together several quite different claims, but I can't sort them out myself. Could you lay out the distinct, precise theses that the death of the author could be expressing? I'd want at least the following sorted out, plus any others you can identify: the trivial ones (authors are mortal, texts outlive them, authors can't be consulted about their works anymore); the semantic ones (intentions don't fix meaning; meaning is produced by language itself or by the reader); the methodological ones (intentions may causally explain why a text exists but shouldn't constrain its interpretation); the political one (that the author's authority over meaning is a kind of tyranny, and the reader's interpretive freedom requires breaking it); and Foucault's neighboring claim about the author as a function that discourse constructs rather than a person who precedes it. For each reading, tell me whether it is plausible, and — this is what I most need — whether it actually supports dismissing a historian's attempt to recover what a writer was doing with a text. My field, from Quentin Skinner onward, is premised on intentions being at least in principle recoverable, so I want to know whether any reading of the slogan touches that premise, or whether they all attack a strawman in which the author's later testimony is treated as an interpretive trump card. Please give me a menu I could bring back to the workshop, with verdicts, rather than a defense of one reading.
