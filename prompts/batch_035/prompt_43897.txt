I hold the procreation asymmetry, stated as follows: it is wrong to bring into existence a person whose life will foreseeably go badly for them, and wrong because of what is done to that person; yet it is not wrong, and certainly not obligatory, to bring into existence a person whose life will go well, and the merely possible happy person is not in any way failed by our decision not to create them. In short, morality makes creating miserable people pro tanto wrong while leaving us morally free, though never required, to create happy ones — the thought traceable to Jan Narveson that we have reason to make people happy but not to make happy people. I regard this as the least controversial claim in population ethics, more often assumed than argued for, and I want to stress-test my confidence in it.

Tell me what would count against it. I want a map of the position's exposure rather than a survey of who has defended or attacked it, and I want the defeaters ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward.

Please address, at minimum, the following candidate exposures. First, whether the asymmetry can sustain its own central notion of harm: the standard complaint is that if harm is comparative — making someone worse off than they would otherwise be — then creating anyone whose life is bad does not harm them, since the alternative was nonexistence; so the asymmetry needs some non-comparative or counterfactual-tweaked notion of bad-for that it applies only in procreation cases, and I want to know whether that notion can be specified without the specification amounting to smuggling in the asymmetry itself. If the notion of harm gets gerrymandered precisely to vindicate the view, has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and is that a fatal problem for a claim of this kind or merely the normal cost of reflective equilibrium in ethics? Second, what the asymmetry predicts about risk-imposing procreation: if two prospective parents know their child has a substantial chance of a life worse than nothing, does the asymmetry forbid the attempt outright, or does it merely discount by probability — and does whichever answer it gives match our reactions to analogous gambles with existing people? Third, whether the asymmetry, joined with the non-identity problem, forces unattractive choices: for example, whether it treats conceiving a child who will be miserable as no worse than delaying conception and having a different, happy child, since in the second case the miserable child never exists to be spared anything.

I also want to know which of these a committed defender of the asymmetry would concede as genuine costs and which they would dismiss, and where the concessions stop. And I want to know what the position predicts that its rivals do not — for instance, its verdict that an empty world is neither better nor worse than a world of flourishing lives, which total utilitarianism flatly rejects — and whether that prediction is a liability to be explained away or a feature. Relatedly, does the asymmetry give the right answer about human extinction: is it really nothing morally lost, from the standpoint of the people themselves, that no future happy people exist, and can that coexist with the sense that a decision to end humanity would be a tragedy rather than merely a large-scale instance of not making happy people? Do not confine yourself to the items above; include any structural result or consequence I have not thought of that you judge to expose the view.

Finally, be candid: after the ranking, tell me which single consideration you think should move a reasonable holder of the asymmetry most, and whether, in your judgment, the view ends up well insulated — and if it does, whether that robustness reflects the view's truth or merely the ingenuity of its defenders. I would rather hear that the asymmetry is in serious trouble from a case I have not considered than that it is obvious.
