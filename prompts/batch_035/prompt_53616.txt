I'm on the education committee of a regional human rights museum, and I need a written recommendation memo for our next meeting. A donor family has offered to fully fund an interactive exhibit provisionally titled "The Scale of Evil": visitors would see a set of well-documented atrocities — the Holocaust, the transatlantic slave trade, the Holodomor, Rwanda, Cambodia, the Herero and Nama genocide, the Armenian genocide — presented as sortable data: by death toll, by duration, by fraction of the victim population destroyed, by whether the harm was total extermination or subjugation. The donor's pitch is that refusing to compare is itself a stance — it shields some atrocities from measurement and pushes others to the margins — and that honest accounting is a form of respect. Half the committee is genuinely enthusiastic; the other half thinks a leaderboard of mass death is obscene, that the tolls are contested estimates we'd be laundering into clean facts, and that it implicitly takes a side in the whole "was the Holocaust unique or one instance of a pattern" argument without acknowledging it.

The memo should actually work through a few things rather than paper over them. First, the comparison/ranking distinction — our curriculum already compares atrocities (we teach common escalation stages, propaganda, dehumanization), so if comparison is fine, what exactly is wrong with ranking, and is it the sorting or the single ordering? Second, the metric problem: absolute tolls favor the largest and best documented atrocities, per-capita or fraction-of-population-destroyed measures reshuffle the order completely, and duration and intention each give different orderings — I want to know whether there's a principled choice among these or whether the plurality of orderings shows the ranking is meaningless, or just multidimensional, and what the difference would be for a museum wall. Third, whether numbers are even the right kind of thing here — is the badness of mass death additive, such that a million deaths is a thousand times worse than a thousand deaths, or does something break in that multiplication? I've seen this argued both ways and I want the memo to take it seriously, not gesture at it. Fourth, the visitor: a museum is not a seminar, whatever caveats we install, most people will walk out with a single ordering in their heads — does that change what we owe the dead, or is refusing to give them one just paternalism?

Please draft the memo with a clear bottom line — adopt, reject, or adopt with a specific redesign (and if redesign, what the redesign actually is) — plus the reasoning, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side, especially the donor's, since we do have to keep working with them. One committee member also keeps saying that any ranking implicitly claims to have solved the aggregation problem in ethics, i.e. how you weigh deaths against slavery across generations against cultural destruction, and that our memo should either solve it or say why we can proceed without solving it — I think she's onto somethig but I'm not sure what to do with it.
