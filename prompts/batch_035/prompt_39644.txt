I am a novelist researching a book whose narrator is a wildlife documentary editor who gradually becomes unable to cut footage of a dying gazelle without moral unease. As background for her voice, I have drafted the essay notes below, and I would appreciate comments of a philosophical rather than literary kind.

The notes argue as follows. Nature documentaries are not neutral recordings but acts of moral editing. Their standard conventions — the chase scored as suspense, the kill as resolution, the narration that speaks of "Nature" as a designing agent whose economy excuses what happens within it, and above all the cut away from the animal that takes hours to die — train viewers to experience predation, starvation, and parasitism as the sublime movement of a system rather than as one creature's bad death following another's. This matters not only aesthetically but practically: public tolerance for intervening in wild animal welfare, whether by vaccinating populations, managing overabundant herbivores through contraception rather than letting starvation do the culling, or eventually by stranger and larger measures, will depend on whether wild suffering registers as suffering at all. A viewer taught to hear the music swell over the hunt has been taught, in effect, that the harm is part of the beauty. My notes concede an objection I'm unsure I state fairly: that the documentary conventions might express a genuine truth — that there is value in natural processes that is not reducible to the welfare of the creatures inside them — so the aestheticizing is not a distortion of the audience's moral perception but a rival account of what in nature matters.

I would like comments on three things specifically: (1) whether the central move, from claims about aesthetic framing of predation to claims about moral complacency toward it, is valid or slides between describing an audience's experience and prescribing what they should conclude; (2) whether the conceded objection is philosophically the strongest available one, or whether I've set up a strawman; and (3) whether the notes, suitably developed, would be interesting to a reader with no prior stake in wild animal suffering, or whether they only preach. Please also flag any point where I've asserted something as fact that is actually a contestable empirical or philosophical claim.
