i'm teaching a section on Wittgenstein for an intro phil course and we just hit the private language argument. next week is about the diarist thought experiment — the guy who decides to keep a notebook about a recurring private sensation, writes "S" in it whenever the sensation shows up, and claims that this little ritual is all the meaning "S" ever needed. no one else can check the entries, and he insists that's fine: he alone knows what "S" refers to. i want to open that class with a villanelle written in the diarist's voice.

the two refrains should be, roughly, (a) his act of recording — the conviction that writing "S" down pins the sensation down, that the notebook is doing real work — and (b) his appeal to the feeling itself as the only arbiter, something like memory seeming to justify itself in the absence of anything external. the trick i want is that the villanelle's form, the same two lines coming back over and over while everything else shifts around them, should enact the very thing that makes the diary useless: repetition that feels like its accumulating a record but that can never come out wrong, and so never really records anything at all. someone who knows the argument should be able to say which refrain is which and why they belong together, and someone who doesn't should still get a decent poem about a guy slowly talking himself into a circle.

a few constraints: don't name Wittgenstein anywhere in the poem, and please keep words like "ostensive" out of it too lol — i want the argument carried by the situation and the form, not by jargon. also don't resolve it at the end into "and therefore language is public"; let the closing tercet land on the diarist still convinced, or at most on a flicker of doubt, since that's actually where my students will be when the poem ends and the discussion starts
