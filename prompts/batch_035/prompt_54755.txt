At our department's formal epistemology reading group, two members spent the whole walk back arguing about how to handle uncertain evidence, and I was never sure who had the better of it. The trigger was mundane: one of them, Sofia, got a text from a friend saying a colloquium she planned to attend had been canceled, but she knows the friend sometimes confuses dates, so she ended up only about 80% confident the talk was really off. She said the right description of her situation is Jeffrey conditionalization: her experience directly raises her credence in the proposition that the talk is canceled to 0.8, and her new credence in any hypothesis H should be 0.8 times her old P(H given the talk is canceled) plus 0.2 times her old P(H given the talk is not canceled) — that the rule lets you update on evidence you are merely uncertain of, without pretending you learned some clean proposition outright.

The other member, Daniel, objected at step one. He said the 0.8 is not an input the framework hands you but an output of an unmodeled Bayesian computation: if Sofia actually knew the probability of receiving just such a text given cancellation and given no cancellation, ordinary conditioning on the text itself would deliver her posterior about the cancellation, and Jeffrey's rule merely relabels the result of that hidden calculation as a free parameter. On his view, the rule does not extend Bayesian updating to uncertain evidence; it smuggles the unmodeled part into a number and calls it progress.

Sofia replied that this mischaracterizes the motivating cases. Jeffrey's own example was of someone glimpsing a piece of cloth by candlelight and becoming unsure, say 70% confident, that it is green rather than blue — there is no further report or observation to condition on; the blurry glimpse itself is all the evidence there is, and it is not naturally a proposition in the agent's algebra. Daniel answered with what he called the rigidity problem: the rule presupposes that the experience leaves unchanged the conditional probabilities P(H given green) and P(H given blue), but any experience that makes you unsure the cloth is green — bad light, distance, your own unreliable color vision — typically also teaches you something that shifts exactly those conditionals, so the assumption fails in the very cases invoked to motivate the rule.

Sofra's next move was to deny that this indicts the rule itself. Where the conditionals are genuinely untouched, she said, the update is exactly right, and where they are not, the mistake is misdescribing your evidence, not in the rule; she added that even a fully idealized Bayesian must distinguish evidence that bears on a hypothesis only through an intermediate proposition from evidence that bears on it directly, and Jeffrey conditioning captures the direct case. Daniel then pressed a different flank: sequential Jeffrey updates on two pieces of soft evidence do not commute in general — updating on the first then the second can land you somewhere different from the reverse order — which he took to show that the rule cannot be a norm of rational response to a body of evidence, since what your evidence is should not depend on the order in which you processed it.

Sofia thought order-dependence was a feature rather than a bug: earlier soft evidence should properly color the interpretation of later soft evidence, just as it would for an ideal Bayesian receiving correlated evidence sequentially, so noncommutativity is what rational sensitivity to evidence order looks like, not an incoherence. Daniel's closing claim was that all of this concedes his point: if the rule only applies when rigidity holds, and we never know whether it holds, then formal epistemology supplies no substantive norms for credence formation at all — only coherence constraints on the credences you somehow end up with — and Jeffrey conditionalization is at best a useful idealization, not part of the epistemology of evidence. Sofia maintained that coherence plus a principled but defeasible default rule constrains rational updating in a way that falls meaningfully short of no guidance at all.

They eventually asked the rest of us to settle it, and I could not. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: assess each of Daniel's objections and each of Sofia's replies on its merits, indicate who is right about which specific points, and then say who comes out ahead overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing about how uncertain evidence and conditionalization on it are supposed to work.
