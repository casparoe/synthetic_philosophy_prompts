I'm a graduate student working on a term paper on the combination problem in panpsychism, and I've noticed that the literature tends to jump straight into evaluating particular solutions — subject-summing views, phenomenal bonding, structuralist and deflationary strategies — without ever stating what a solution is supposed to accomplish. Before I start scoring proposals against each other, I want the specification first, and I'd like you to write it. You should act as a professional philosopher of mind with deep familiarity with the panpsychism debate, and I'd ask you to begin with a short brainstorming pass over what people actually demand of a solution before you commit to the final list.

Here is the angle I want, so this doesn't turn into an encyclopedia entry. The list should be built around cases and questions rather than names of views, and at minimum it has to cover: whether the solution must explain the unity of the subject, or only the unity of a perspective (I keep running into papers that slide between these); the boundary problem — why this cluster of micro-experiences combines rather than that one, or rather than my experience and yours, and whether the answer can come from physics alone; the worry that any proposed combining relation either does real explanatory work in a way that would show up in physics, or does no work and is just a label on the emergence we were trying to avoid; and the phenomenological requirement that the solution account for consciousness as we actually find it, co-presented from a single point of view, rather than explaining it away — though I want you to treat the deflationary "there is nothing to combine" move as a live leaf and say which desideratum, if any, it fails.

For each desideratum, give the motivating case or thought experiment behind it and say whether it is non-negotiable or a prejudice of one camp. Then tell me two further things: first, whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, in which case the list itself becomes the interesting result; second, which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidate solutions. Finally, a very brief pass at how the three main strategies — subjects combining, qualities or contents combining, and deflation — fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria, since that's where the actual thinking is. No citations or bibliographies, and please don't structure this around specific authors' formulations unless a position genuinely cannot be stated without one.
