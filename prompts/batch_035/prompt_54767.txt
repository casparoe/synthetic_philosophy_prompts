I'm trying to pin down what makes a shape an 'a', and my own judgments keep escaping any single account. Here are my verdicts, stated as honestly as I can:

- The double-story 'a' of a serif text face and the single-story 'a' of my own handwriting are both 'a's, even though they share almost nothing structurally — one is a bowl atop a closed loop, the other basically a circle on a stick.
- My daughter's mirror-reversed 'a' is still an 'a', just an imperfect one.
- A blackletter 'a', which looks like nothing else in my reading life, is still an 'a'.
- The 'a' in a minimalist geometric sans — essentially a circle with a small flag — is still an 'a', and so is a captcha-distorted 'a' with noise lines struck through it.
- 'A' and 'a' are the same letter. That one already feels like trouble for any shape-based story, since they share no strokes at all.
- A glyph that could be either 'a' or 'o', set inside the word 'c_t', is an 'a' there. The very same glyph alone on a page — I honestly don't know.
- An 'a' rotated ninety degrees is not an 'a'; it's just a shape. Note that I happily accepted the mirror reversal in my daughter's writing, and I cannot explain why mirroring preserves letterhood while rotation destroys it.
- A designer abstracts a company's initial beyond recognition, so that the mark functions as that letter only by its position in the wordmark: I still count it as the letter.
- Someone who has never seen the Latin alphabet traces an 'a' stroke for stroke as a drawing exercise. The artifact is an 'a'-shaped drawing, but I keep wanting to say that they did not write an 'a'.
- The old long 's' of eighteenth-century printing is, historically, an 's': it played the letter-role of 's'. Yet on first pass my recognition files it as 'f'.
- The Greek 'α' is not the letter 'a', even though ours descends from it.

My tentative principle is two-part: an 'a' is (1) a form playing the letter-role within a writing system — historically continuous with other 'a's and interchangeable with them inside words — laid over (2) a flexible structural skeleton of a bowl with a rightward stem. The skeleton clause is already straining under the blackletter and uppercase verdicts, and the role clause gives me no answer for the isolated ambiguous glyph or the tracing exercise.

Can you find a principle that captures these verdicts, or nearly? I'd like the account stated precisely enough to deliver verdicts on new cases — in particular, a wholly novel alphabet descended from ours in which no glyph resembles its ancestor, and a hypothetical orthographic reform that merges 'a' and 'o' into a single letter. Please say which clause of the account is doing the work for which of my verdicts, which verdicts it forces me to abandon and why, and whether 'letter' and 'style' really are two independent dimensions as I assumed, or whether some of my harder cases show them bleeding into each other.
