I've been covering the collective intelligence beat for a while now — honeybee swarms choosing nest sites, slime moulds solving mazes, that sort of thing — and I keep running into the same conceptual wall, so I'm hoping you can help me climb over it instead of just describing it again in my articles. The wall, roughly: everyone can accept that an ant is basically a dumb little automaton with a handful of rules, and everyone can accept that the colony does impressively smart things, and yet the moment you suggest the colony itself is the thing that's thinking — Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary in the Ant Fugue, the anteater's friend who is literally a colony — people get squeamish. Some say it's just an illusion of intelligence, like a thermostat; others say sure, the colony has beliefs and goals, the individual ants are just its neurons. Nobody seems to have a worked-out account of what would settle it.

So before I go read another stack of papers, I want the spec first. What I'd like from you is the desiderata: what an adequate theory of when a collection of things counts as a single mind (over and above its parts) actually has to deliver. Which cases must it get right — I'd want it to say something about the ant colony, a honeybee swarm, a slime mould (one giant cell, no parts that are individually worth mentioning), a human brain (the paradigm case, where we all agree the mind is at the whole-system level), a football team executing a set play, and a market whose price is smarter than any of its traders. Which distinctions can't it collapse — like the difference between parts that merely correlate in behaviour versus parts that functionally cooperate, or between a system that represents the world and one that merely responds to it, or between "has a mind" and "acts as if it has a mind". And what it can't contradict from biology or complexity science, e.g. the colony carries on after the queen dies, individual ants get replaced constantly while the colony persists for decades, colonies have no anatomical place where information comes together, etc.

For each requirement, give me the motivating case or consideration behind it, mark which ones you think are genuinely non-negotiable and which are prejudices of one camp (I suspect the "no central workspace, no mind" intuition is in that second bucket, but tell me if you disagree). And the part I most want: tell me honestly if you think some subset of the desiderata can't be jointly satisfied — say, if full functionalist flexibility on substrate forces us to accept some embarassing things about markets or corporations, or if demanding integration rules out cases we pre-theoretically want to keep. Then, briefly, run functionalism, Dennett's intentional stance, and any integrated-information style approach against the list — just the headline verdicts, I'll do the deep dive for the piece myself. Please write in British English. (And if you can make sense of why Hofstadter's anteater insists he doesn't eat Aunt Hillary's mind, only her ants, so much the better — that bit has always struck me as doing real work, but I can't quite put my finger on why.)
