A friend of mine from a forum I frequent, who has read rather more Aumann than I have, puts it at 0.8, mostly on the strength of the Harsanyi-style thought that if two agents genuinely pooled everything they know, their priors would wash out. What I want from you is your own credence on the proposition, but far more importantly, a decomposition of how you get there. To fix terms: by the common prior assumption I mean the claim that rational agents who begin as epistemic peers should be modelled as sharing a single prior probability function, with all subsequent divergence explained by conditioning on different evidence. The sub-questions I'd tentatively propose are these, though please revise or supplement the list as you see fit: whether Aumann's agreement theorem really supports the assumption or only shows it fails to apply to us; whether Harsanyi's pool-everything reply works, given that 'everything' plausibly includes the whole of each agent's pre-communicative life; whether self-locating and anthropic credences, where a shared third-person prior plus different de se evidence may still look like prior divergence, should count for or against the assumption; whether the nonconglomerability results for countably additive measures on infinite partitions (the sort that let a shared prior yield non-shared posteriors) are a technical curiosity or a genuine crack in the 'one true prior' picture; and whether the assumption is best read as a substantive epistemological thesis at all, or merely as a modelling convention whose truth-value is a category error.

One consideration I've been chewing on, and which I suspect is where my credence diverges from my friend's, is what I privately call the exhaustive testimony problem: if what we call an agent's priors are really compressed posteriors from a lifetime of unshared evidence, then the common prior assumption is either trivially satisfiable in the limit where agents condition on each other's entire histories — at which point it describes no actual agents — or it is false of every agent who hasn't. If that's right, the interesting question stops being whether priors are common and becomes whether full evidence sharing is even coherent as an ideal. I'd like your judgement on whether this framing is sound, and where it fits in your decomposition.
