I serve on the technical governance council of a mid-sized AI lab, and in six weeks we must adopt a formal specification of the objective for our next flagship system — the document that the alignment team will treat as the ground truth for what the system is to help us achieve. Two proposals dominate the discussion, and I need a written opinion I can circulate before the vote. The first proposal endorses something in the spirit of coherent extrapolated volition: the system should serve not what people say they want today, but what they would want if they knew more, thought faster and more clearly, and had grown up farther together — with the extrapolation run per person, then aggregated across persons by some overlap or convergence procedure. The second proposal rejects extrapolation entirely as an unacceptably speculative step: the system should instead track current, actually-stated preferences, elicited through ongoing deliberative polling, with no assumption that people's "real" wishes diverge from their expressed ones. A third board member has floated a compromise under which extrapolated conclusions would be followed only where they do not trigger sustained, informed objection from a supermajority of current-day humans — a conservative override clause.

The philosophical crux, as I see it, is whether the extrapolation in the first proposal is a neutral projection or a smuggling operation. Extrapolating "what you would want if you knew more and reasoned better" seems to require substantive commitments about what counts as knowing more, what counts as reasoning better, and which errors in a person's current preferences we are licensed to call errors — and those commitments have to come from somewhere, presumably from whoever writes the extrapolation procedure. If so, the first proposal may amount to the lab's ethicists quietly legislating for everyone under the banner of "your own values, idealized." Yet the second proposal has its own embarrassment: current stated preferences are the output of ignorance, fatigue, manipulation, and coordination failures, and locking the system to them may permanently entrench the distortions — which is precisely the motivation for idealizing in the first place.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the council. It should recommend a concrete course of action among the three options, or a defensible fourth, and it should do so by confronting the smuggling worry directly rather than waving at "value uncertainty." It should say what the lab could actually do to test or constrain an extrapolation procedure before committing to it at scale, and it should give the strongest surviving consideration on the losing side a fair and explicit hearing. Assume the council members are philosophically literate but skeptical of abstraction: they will not accept a recommendation that turns on resolving metaethical disputes that have been open for a century, and they will notice if the argument quietly assumes that idealization is either value-free or impossible. Write the opinion as continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — in the manner of a formal letter addressed to the council, roughly a thousand to fifteen hundred words, ending with the recommendation stated plainly enough that it can be entered verbatim into the minutes.
