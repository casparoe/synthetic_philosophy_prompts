My grandfather died last week at 93. He was a high school history teacher for forty years and, far more importantly to him, a lifelong believer that history turns in circles rather than running along a line — he read Polybius every single summer, loved Ibn Khaldun's picture of group solidarity (asabiyya or however its spelled) building up a dynasty, the elite softening, the solidarity dissolving, the whole thing starting over somewhere else, thought Toynbee's challenge-and-response was roughly right, and had a genuine love-hate relationship with Spengler: loved the seasons metaphor for civilizations, hated that Spengler made the winter sound like a sentence being carried out. His bookshelves, his lectures, the way he narrated his own life — all cycles. When the school board cut his program in the 90s he told his students that pruned trees come back thicker, and then went and taught the same material for free at the community center for another decade.

He left a note asking that his eulogy not undo him at the last minute: no watching over us from somewhere, no better place, and none of what he called secular heaven talk — legacies marching forward, arcs of history bending, everything for a reason. Which is a problem, because that's basically the entire standard toolkit of consolation at a funeral. So I need a eulogy that comforts a grieving room — about half family who never read any of this, and a handful of his old colleagues and students who absolutely have, and who will wince if I mangle the Polybius or make it sound like he believed in literal reincarnation, which he did not — while staying honestly inside a cyclical view of things. Here's what I keep getting stuck on: in his picture it's the pattern that recurs, not the people. Spring comes back but individual flowers don't, if you see what I mean. So what is the cyclical version of "rest in peace"? I don't want the speech to dodge that tension, I want it to actually work the way he would've thought about it — maybe he survives the way spring survives winter, or through the family as a kind of ongoing solidarity, or something better that you come up with. Write it for a non-historian (me) to deliver, four or five minutes, and I'd love it to end with one line the whole room can repeat together.
