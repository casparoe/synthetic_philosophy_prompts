So last weekend me and my housemate got into a really long argument after watching a documentary about the Napoleonic Wars, and it's still bugging me. He's basically a contingency guy: he thinks history genuinely turns on tiny things — the rider's lost horseshoe nail, a general's indigestion, a message arriving an hour late — and that if you replayed history from 1800 with one small change, the world would come out radically different. I keep pushing back that this confuses two different things. In a chaotic system like the weather, small differences in initial conditions blow up precisely because nothing is watching the trajectory and correcting it. But history is full of agents who notice what's happening and adjust: if a battle is lost for want of a nail, somebody usually tries to refight it later under the same structural pressures anyway. So my hunch is that history is chaotic at the scale of weeks and battles but roughly convergent at the scale of centuries — the specific battles and biographies could have gone otherwise, but the big outcomes (which side wins in the long run, where industrialization happens, etc.) are pinned down by forces too heavy for a horseshoe to deflect. My housemate says that's just hindsight bias dressed up as a principle, and honestly I'm not sure he's wrong.

I have to write an essay for a philosophy of science course that engages exactly this question, and I want it to actually take a side rather than do the both-sides-have-a-point cop-out. So: write the essay. Is the for-want-of-a-nail picture of history defensible, or is it systematically misleading? Please lay out the strongest version of each of the three live positions — (1) real contingency at the large scale, small causes really do redirect history in ways that never get corrected; (2) overdetermination, big outcomes have so many sufficient causes lined up that the small stuff gets routed around; and (3) the middle view I sketched, contingency at the tactical scale but convergence at the structural scale. Along the way, stress-test my self-correcting-agents argument, because it has an obvious weak spot: agents can't always refight the battle. Sometimes a small event destroys the very conditions that made correction possible — a war that wipes out a whole lineage or a technological path that locks in early and forecloses the alternatives forever. I want to know whether cases like that are the exception or quietly the rule.

Dont be vague, and dont just pile up famous anecdotes — I've heard the Cleopatra's nose thing a million times and it proves nothing either way. End with a real verdict: which of the three positions comes out closest to true, what exactly separates them, and what kind of evidence could conceivably decide between them given that nobody can actually rerun history. It's fine (good, even) if the conclusion is that one position is right in some domains and another in others — but then you have to say which domains and why, not just wave at complexity.
