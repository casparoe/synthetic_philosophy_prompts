I sit on the technical committee of an aquaculture certification scheme — the kind that audits farms and lets them display a welfare label on the packaging. We certify salmon farms and, since a recent expansion, shrimp farms, and I have become puzzled by what our own requirements jointly presuppose about the nature of animal welfare. I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory for me, because I suspect it is not a theory any of us would endorse if stated plainly.

Concretely, our requirements look like this. For the fish we certify, we require that animals be rendered insensible before killing: percussive or electrical stunning is mandatory, and letting fish asphyxiate in air or in an ice slurry, which remains standard in much of the industry, is a disqualifying violation. For the shrimp we certify, by contrast, we permit a routine hatchery practice: the eyestalks of breeding females are pinched or cut off, since this accelerates ovarian maturation, and we impose no requirement for anesthesia or analgesia, nor any upper bound on how often the procedure is repeated. The same farms use cleaner fish — lumpfish and wrasse — to control sea lice, and while our salmon standards run to dozens of pages, the cleaner fish have until recently had almost no species-specific provisions at all, though they are often destroyed at the end of the season. Finally, the audit itself works by counting: mortality percentages, dissolved oxygen and ammonia levels, fin-erosion and lesion scores, stocking density. A farm passes or fails on what an auditor can record during a visit, and everything we call welfare has to surface in that checklist.

So here is my question. What theory or theories of animal welfare does this pattern of requirements presuppose, taken as a whole? A hedonic view on which welfare just is the balance of experienced pleasure and suffering would presumably condemn the eyestalk ablations outright or at least demand analgesia; a view on which welfare consists in the satisfaction of preferences or in an animal being able to live a life characteristic of its kind (the sort of view people gesture at with talk of natural behavior or telos) would presumably condemn it as a mutilation regardless of pain, and would be uneasy about the cleaner fish arrangement too. Tell me which of our requirements strictly presuppose one such theory, which are merely compatible with several of them, and where the whole package is genuinely underdetermined — such that two farms audited identically could be doing well or badly on different theories and our checklist could not tell the difference. In particular I want your view on whether the checklist method itself presupposes something substantive: that welfare is exhausted by what is behaviorally or physiologically observable during an audit, or at least adequately proxied by it. If that is a substantive commitment, say what it quietly rules out — and say what would have to change in our standards if we replaced whatever theory we are implicitly operating with by, say, a straightforwardly hedonistic one.

Two requests about how you write this. First, throughout, please separate what is settled among people who know this field from what is genuinely contested, and do not present any contested view as the consensus. In particular, the question of how the welfare frameworks on offer — hedonic accounts, preference accounts, natural-behavior or telos accounts, list-style pluralisms — relate to one another is itself a live dispute, as is how much weight auditable indicators can bear; if you tell me our scheme presupposes one of these, mark clearly that you are taking a stand on something philosophers of animal welfare argue about, not reporting a received view. Second, if you conclude that our requirements are jointly inconsistent — that no single theory could license all of them — say so plainly and locate the inconsistency, rather than smoothing it over; I would rather know our scheme is incoherent than be handed a tidy reconstruction it does not deserve.
