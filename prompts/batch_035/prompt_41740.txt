I served on a jury last year, and since then I've been taking an evening course on the law of evidence. Something about how trials handle honesty has been bothering me, and I can't find anyone who addresses it head-on. Every witness swears a solemn three-part oath: to tell the truth, nothing but the truth, and — the odd middle clause — the whole truth. Yet the actual practice of a trial is nothing like that. Testimony proceeds through narrow, lawyer-controlled questions; witnesses are routinely coached by their own attorneys to answer the question asked and no more; and a witness on cross-examination who volunteers the exculpatory context that would change the meaning of their answer is likely to be cut off. Meanwhile, perjury doctrine protects only the first and third clauses. Under the federal rule, a false statement of material fact under oath is a felony, but an answer that is literally true and engineered to implant a false impression is no crime at all. In the Supreme Court's unanimous 1973 decision in Bronston v. United States, a witness asked whether he had ever had Swiss bank accounts answered that his company had had an account in Zürich for about six months — he had one himself. The Court held this was not perjury; the remedy, it said, was not prosecution but a sharper follow-up question by counsel alert to the unresponsiveness of the answer.

So here is the pattern as I understand it. The ritual oath demands the whole truth; the criminal law punishes only departures from literal truth; the middle term — deliberately letting a false impression stand — is policed, if at all, by dialogue, on the assumption that a skilled examiner can neutralize any evasion, while nothing can neutralize a flat lie. And the same structure appears outside the courtroom: lawyers' ethics rules prohibit making false statements of fact to opposing parties, but are read to permit a lawyer to insinuate a false view of her client's bottom line or eagerness to settle, so long as every sentence is literally true — except in certain relationships, like those between fiduciaries or between insured and insurer, where genuine duties of disclosure are imposed. My instinct is that this pattern must rest on some theory of the difference between lying and merely misleading, but I can't tell whether the theory is a moral one (lying really is worse than misleading, so only lying deserves criminal punishment) or a procedural one (the difference lies in what dialogue can repair: cross-examination exists for evasions, while a lie, once believed, leaves no conversational track).

Can you help me reconstruct what this cluster of practices — the oath, the perjury rule, the follow-up-question remedy, the negotiation-ethics line between false statements of fact and strategic half-truths — actually presupposes about the lying/misleading distinction? I'd like to know what it strictly requires as opposed to what it merely sits comfortably with, and whether the parts are even consistent with one another: a system that exacts an oath to the whole truth, then declines to punish its calculated withholding, looks close to self-contradictory unless some principle reconciles them. And suppose the underlying distinction collapsed — suppose we concluded, as some philosophers argue, that misleading someone by strictly literal means is morally equivalent to lying. What, concretely, would have to give? Would perjury law have to reach answers given with intent to mislead, would negotiation ethics need a general duty of candor, and could the adversarial trial, which apparently runs on a division of labor between the witness's literal truthfulness and the examiner's job of catching implications, survive in anything like its present form?
