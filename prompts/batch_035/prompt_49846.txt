You are a moral philosopher whose work centers on the distinction between doing and allowing and on its role in clinical ethics. I sit on a hospital ethics committee, and at a recent meeting about withdrawing mechanical ventilation from a patient with irreversible respiratory failure, a senior clinician closed the discussion by saying that we would not be killing her; we would be letting nature take its course. The phrase did real work in the room: it was offered, and accepted, as establishing that withdrawal is not euthanasia and not a killing. I want to know what that proverb actually claims, because I suspect it is several claims wearing one sentence. Please think through the possibilities before committing, then spell out, in connected prose, the distinct precise theses the proverb could be expressing. Some candidates I can dimly see: that the clinicians make no causal contribution to the death; that the death issues from the underlying pathology rather than from any human act; that an omission is not an action in the relevant sense; that there exists a natural baseline course of events which medical intervention disrupts and which withdrawal merely restores; that moral responsibility for an outcome requires a positive act. For each reading you identify, tell me whether it is a platitude, whether it is defensible, whether it is simply false, and, crucially, whether it would actually deliver the conclusion the clinician wanted, namely that withdrawing the ventilator is not a killing. In working through this, please attend to some cases that seem to strain the proverb. If a patient with complete heart block has an implanted pacemaker, is deactivating the device letting nature take its course, or is it a positive act that stops the heart? If a diabetic patient stops taking insulin, almost nobody would describe the ensuing death as nature taking its course, yet the causal structure looks much like the case where a ventilator is withdrawn; does that suggest the proverb is tracking something other than causation, perhaps a conventional classification of which interventions count as defaults and which as interruptions? And consider that our patient has been ventilated for six weeks, so the sequence that unfolds after withdrawal — hypoxia, then cardiac arrest — is itself a product of prior medical decisions; in what sense is there a course of nature for us to let run when nature's script has already been rewritten several times on the ward? I would like the kind of careful, precise treatment a specialist would give this, written as continuous prose only: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, just unbroken paragraphs in the manner of a long letter.
