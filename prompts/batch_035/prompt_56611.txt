I'm a twelfth-grader who has been reading Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach on my own, and next month I'm presenting the Location of Meaning chapter at our school's philosophy club — fifteen students who haven't read the book, about twenty-five minutes, with a whiteboard. The core idea I want to land is his three-way division between the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message, and the claim that follows from it: that meaning is never simply inside a text or object but is located in the interaction between the carrier and the kind of decoder that can pick it up. I don't want you to explain the idea to me; I already think I understand it. I want a plan for explaining it to them. What has to be established first before the three-way distinction is even worth making (I suspect something like: meaning can't be located by inspecting the marks alone, which they'll resist, since they'll arrive thinking words just carry meanings the way trucks carry cargo)? Which single example should carry the whole session — a vinyl record, a message washed up in a bottle, an inscription in an unknown script, or DNA — and what makes that one better than the alternatives for this audience? What misconceptions will they predictably form — my guesses are that the frame message gets confused with a title or label, that the outer message gets confused with the language the inner message happens to be written in, and that someone will conclude meaning is entirely in the decoder rather than in the interaction — and at exactly which point in the sequence should I head each one off rather than let it settle? Finally, what should I openly refuse to bring in (I'm tempted by the Gödel machinery earlier in the book and by any serious molecular biology, but tell me if something less obvious also has to go), and what one question could I ask at the end that would let me distinguish a student who genuinely grasps where Hofstadter puts meaning from one who has merely memorized the three labels?
