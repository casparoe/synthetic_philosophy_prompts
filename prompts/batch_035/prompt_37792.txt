hey so i'm in a social epistemology reading group thats wrapping up for the semester, and our last unit has been peer disagreement — the whole equal weight vs steadfast thing. honestly our final discussion went in circles for two hours and i want to close the semester with a poem that shows *why* it went in circles, rather than a summary that pretends we settled anything. my idea is a pantoum, and the more i think about it the more the form feels made for this material: in a pantoum the same lines keep coming back but inside new stanzas, surrounded by new neighbors, so each return gets recontextualized — which is kind of exactly what happens when a conciliationist and a steadfast person argue, each one keeps restating the other's point in a frame that changes what it's doing.

in case you don't have the form on hand: quatrains rhymed abab, where the 2nd and 4th lines of each stanza return as the 1st and 3rd lines of the next stanza, and the poem's final line repeats its first line. i want exactly five stanzas, numbered 1 through 5, and nothing else — no title, no notes, just the numbered stanzas.

as for content: i want the first line to be a clean statement of the conciliationist move (something in the neighborhood of: when my epistemic equal disagrees, i meet them halfway). the steadfast rejoinder should live in the lines that circulate between stanzas — the ones that get picked up and turned around. and i want stanza 3 or 4 to hit the regress we actually got stuck on: once you and your peer disagree about how to handle disagreement itself, does conciliating about that commit you to conciliating about conciliating, and where does that stop? also somewhere in there i'd love a nod to our best fight of the night, which was over whether splitting the difference even makes sense if the truth might not lie anywhere between the two positions. the ending should not resolve anything — the last line is the first line again, but by then it shouldn't be readable the same way, and that's the whole point. if someone who knows this literature read it, they should recognize the positions precisely enough to say which lines are doing which work. also small thing: don't make either side a strawman — the steadfast person isn't arrogant and the conciliationist isn't a doormat, they're both responding to something real.
