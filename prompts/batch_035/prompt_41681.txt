In a philosophy of mind reading group, the topic turned to what makes something a joint action rather than two individual actions that happen to mesh. One member, Dana, defended a contract analogy she had picked up in a social ontology seminar. Whenever two people do something together — walking to the station side by side, co-authoring a paper, playing in a duo — she said, they are not merely two individuals with coincidentally aligned intentions. By taking the first step together, each has extended to the other a kind of guarantee, and each is now entitled to hold the other to it. The relationship is best modeled on a contract, and she deployed the analogy to support three specific claims: (1) your walking companion can demand that you not abruptly speed off ahead, in a way she could not demand that a stranger on the same sidewalk keep pace; (2) if you quit the duo unilaterally and without her concurrence, you have wronged her even if she is left no worse off than before the duo existed; and (3) the obligation runs between the two of you — you owe it to her, not to the music, the audience, or the practice of duet-playing as such. Another member, Felix, objected that the analogy manufactures obligation out of nothing: there was no offer and acceptance, nothing signed, and contracts get their force from institutions that this pair never entered; he concluded that whatever binds them, it is not anything contractlike, so claim (2) collapses. Dana replied that ordinary contracts between friends are often informal and unwritten too, and Felix has not said what a contract needs that is missing here. Please audit the contract analogy as Dana uses it. First, set out the correspondence table — what plays the role of offer, acceptance, consideration, breach, remedy, and exit clause in the walking-together and duo cases. Second, identify where the mapping breaks down, separating the disanalogies that threaten each of Dana's three conclusions from those that do not. Third, address the exchange between Dana and Felix directly: has Felix identified a genuine gap in the analogy, or has Dana adequately answered him? Finally, state which of the three conclusions, if any, survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if the contract picture is not the right vehicle, what would better capture what Dana is pointing at.
