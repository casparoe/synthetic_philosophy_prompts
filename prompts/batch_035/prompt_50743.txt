I'm a retired spectroscopist — physical chemistry, thirty-four years, mostly polymers — and since retiring I've been trying to catch up on philosophy, partly to prove to my son I can. My little reading group (two other retired scientists, all amateurs) has put together an argument about promises that I can't poke a hole in, and it concludes something I really don't want to accept.

(1) If promising creates a new obligation, it does so by creating new reasons to act — reasons that weren't there before the promise.
(2) All reasons for action trace back to consequences — harms, benefits, reliance — or to duties you already had independent of any promise.
(3) Once the promise is made, the complete situation is: you now expect me to do the thing, plans may hang on it, and the practice of trust is slightly at stake. But all of that is produced by your reliance, and your reliance would exist just the same if I'd said "count on it" without promising, or if you'd simply inferred it from my habits. "I promise" isn't a special chemical bond; it's one more way of inducing an expectation.
(4) So the obligation of a promise is exhausted by the obligation not to mess up someone's reliance — an obligation that exists whenever reliance exists, promised or not.
(5) Therefore where reliance is minimal or absent, the obligation vanishes: a promise to my dead father to keep visiting his grave binds not at all, and a promise whose breach no one could ever detect, that dents nobody's trust in anything, is free to break.

The problem is that (5) is supposed to follow from four things that each look fine, and maybe it does, but I keep coming back to the grave. My son says just bite the bullet and accept that promising is a bit of theatre. For what it's worth, my scientific instincts pull toward (2) — I've always liked reductions, temperature to mean kinetic energy and so on — but I also can't shake the sense that when I say "I promise" I'm doing something, not just reporting a prediction about myself. Someone in the group said Hume thought promises were artificial conventions, which if anything seems to help our arguement, not hurt it.

So: which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? And is there any way to keep the reduction and still be obligated to the grave?
