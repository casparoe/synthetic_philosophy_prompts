I've been keeping notes on how I actually react to fiction, because the standard puzzle everyone learns — how can I be afraid of something I know doesn't exist — never matched my own case, and I want to know whether there's one story about what's going on that fits my list, or whether my reactions are just inconsistent. Here's the list.

Watching a horror movie, I get the full fear package: heart rate up, flinching, sometimes pausing the thing because I can't take it. But if you ask me "are you afraid the creature is going to get you?" the honest answer is no. Something fear-shaped is happening in me, but it's not fear of the creature. When a character I love dies in a novel, I cry, and it feels like real grief, not a watered-down "quasi" version — meanwhile I felt basically nothing when I learned a real acquaintance I barely knew had died, which is embarassing but true. On a rewatch I still feel suspense knowing full well who lives and that the killer is behind the door. A creepypasta-style story, one that's posted as a first-person true account but that I know is fiction, scares me far more than an obviously fictional story with identical events, and the fear lingers a bit even after I remind myself it's fake. A friend once told me a harrowing story about her childhood; I was moved to tears, and when I later found out she'd made it up, my memory of being moved feels retroactively tainted — I was tricked, and somehow the emotion itself feels off now, in a way the movie-grief never does. And I can be genuinely angry at a fictional villain, rooting hard for them to lose, but I only ever get mildly annoyed at the author for writing a villain badly, even though the author is the only one who actually did anything.

My tentative principle: the emotions are real, but their objects are my thoughts rather than the fictional things — I'm not sad about the character dying, I'm sad about the thought of it, the way I can be moved contemplating mortality in the abstract. I think that handles the grief case and maybe the rewatch suspense. But it doesn't obviously handle the creepypasta case, since the thought in my head is roughly the same whether or not it's framed as true, and the fear is much stronger with the "true story" framing. And I can't tell whether it handles the flinching at all, because flinching doesn't feel like it's about a thought — it feels like it's about a thing in the room with me.

So what I want: is there a principle, or a couple of candidates, that captures most or all of these verdicts? Where mine breaks, patch it or replace it, and tell me specifically which of my reactions the resulting account has to throw out and why I might be better off giving that reaction up. Tell me what the account predicts for two cases I haven't sorted: feeling vicarious embarrassment when a fictional character does something cringe, and the fear I feel during a nightmare while I'm having it. And one thing I really want flagged: keep straight what's actually settled in this corner of philosophy versus what's still genuinely contested between the main camps — I don't want to come away thinking one of the competing accounts is the consensus view when it isn't.
