I'm doing a master's in astrobiology with a side interest in origins-of-life research, and I keep hitting the same wall in seminars: whenever someone proposes a definition of life, it looks less like a discovery and more like a device for declaring the awkward cases settled by fiat. The metabolism-first people rule viruses dead by stipulation, the evolution people quietly lose the first several hundred million years of prebiotic chemistry, and the self-organisation people can't say why a hurricane or a Bénard cell doesn't count. Before I commit to defending any one of these, I want the specification written out first, and that's what I'd like from you: the list of desiderata an adequate definition of life must meet, with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

By way of seeding, here are the tests I think any candidate has to survive, though I want you to exercise your own judgement about which of these are genuine desiderata and which are prejudices of a particular camp. The definition should say something principled about viruses, about tardigrades in cryptobiosis and seeds that have sat dormant for centuries, about enucleated red blood cells and sterile worker ants that are plainly parts of living things without themselves seeming to be living things, about HeLa cells that have outlived their donor by decades, about the autocatalytic chemistry in lipid vesicles that origins researchers think may have preceded anything we would call an organism, and about self-replicating patterns that exist only in software. It should also respect some distinctions I suspect get collapsed too easily: between being alive and being merely lifelike, between an organism and a living part of an organism, and between a simulation of life and the thing itself. And it cannot contradict what evolutionary biology tells us, which is that whatever line there is, it was almost certainly crossed gradually — so a demand for a sharp binary verdict and a demand to accommodate gradualism may be in tension, and I'd like you to flag any pair of requirements that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. My flatmate, who is a lawyer, insists any definition must deliver a yes-or-no answer, while my supervisor shrugs and says a graded verdict is fine; I'd like to know which of them is asking for something a definition owes us and which is asking for something it may not be able to give. Please also tell me which single requirement, in your view, knocks out the most contenders — that's the part I most want to see. Then, briefly, assess how a Darwinian-evolutionary definition of the sort used in space science, a metabolism-first view, and a heritable-information view each fare against your list.

Please write the whole thing in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a long letter — and please use British English throughout.
