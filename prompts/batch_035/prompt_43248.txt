I am the registrar of a mid-sized university museum, and one of my recurring jobs is deciding what counts as a single catalog object. Several times a month, something comes out of a drawer, a loan, or an excavation tray that could plausibly be accessioned as one thing or as many: a disarticulated skeleton in a shoebox, a hoard of coins found corroded together and later pried apart, a necklace whose ancient beads were restrung on modern wire by a conservator in the 1980s. Our numbering decisions have consequences — they fix insurance schedules, loan paperwork, digitization scope, and how objects are counted in annual reports — and at the moment different staff members decide differently, which auditors have begun to notice.

Please write a decision procedure my team can actually run, grounded in what metaphysicians call the special composition question: under what conditions do several objects together compose a further object? Take the main positions seriously — van Inwagen's answer that composition occurs only for living organisms, the universalist view that any objects whatever compose something, the nihilist view that composition never occurs, and the family of common-sense restricted answers (composition when the parts are in contact, or fastened, or share a fate, or form a functioning whole). Then do the translation work: say which of these positions can honestly be built into a procedure at all, which distinctions must survive the simplification into something a student assistant can apply in ten minutes, and which have to be dropped. Be candid about the fact that van Inwagen's own answer, applied literally, would tell us no artifact ever composes a further artifact — no suit of armor, no teapot with a lid, nothing — and explain what the procedure should do about that, whether it adopts a different criterion, a hybrid, or an openly pragmatic stand-in for the metaphysics.

The procedure should give the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in one catalog number versus several, and where it stops and hands the decision to the curator because what remains is a question of scholarly values — research use, display logic, conservation practice — rather than of composition. It must also cope with two real constraints: our legacy catalog is a century deep, so the procedure has to say what to do when it yields an answer that conflicts with how something has been numbered since 1912; and some lender agreements count objects in their own way, which the procedure cannot override.

Then run it on three cases. First, the shoebox skeleton: roughly five hundred loose bones and fragments with no articulation and no certainty they came from one individual. Second, the restrung necklace: forty ancient beads on modern wire, strung as a single object by a conservator forty years ago and now our most photographed item. Third, a warrior's panoply — helmet, greaves, and shield found together in one deposit, never fastened to one another, which our fundraising materials have always described as a single ensemble. At least one of these should come out in a way I will not like, and I would like you to say so plainly rather than tune the procedure to spare my feelings.

Finally, say what the procedure cannot do: the cases where it will misfire or where the metaphysics is, on honest inspection, merely decorating a decision actually made on other grounds. I would rather know that than have a procedure that pretends to more rigor than cataloging can bear.
