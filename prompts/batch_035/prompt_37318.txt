I'm compiling a set of case studies in the epistemology of mathematics, and the one I want to work through with you is the Hobbes–Wallis controversy, analyzed through the lens of Hobbes's own philosophy of geometry — not as a curiosity about a great philosopher making a mathematical fool of himself, but as a test of whether his epistemology of geometry actually explains his conduct in the dispute. Let me lay out the episode as I understand it, so we're working from the same facts. In 1655 Hobbes published De Corpore, the mathematical-physical part of his Elementa Philosophica, whose final sections included what he presented as a successful quadrature of the circle. John Wallis, the Savilian Professor of Geometry at Oxford, attacked the mathematics almost immediately in his Elenchus Geometriae Hobbianae (1655), pointing out specific errors in the constructions; Hobbes replied in Six Lessons to the Professors of the Mathematicks (1656) and a stream of further polemics, and never conceded, dying in 1679 still defending some version of his claims. It's worth keeping two mitigating facts on the table: the irrationality of pi was not proved until Lambert in 1761, so Hobbes was contradicting no known theorem — only good numerical approximations; and Wallis's methods leaned on arithmetic, indivisibles, and the emerging symbolic algebra that Hobbes distrusted on principle, so the quarrel was never purely about a figure. I also understand that the dispute carried political charge — Hobbes the notorious royalist, Wallis and the Oxford circle having done cryptographic work for the Parliamentarian side during the Civil War — though I'd like you to keep that in the background rather than making it the story.

The theory to apply is Hobbes's view, stated most explicitly in his polemics against Wallis himself, that we have demonstrative knowledge only of what we ourselves generate: geometric figures are traces of the motions of bodies (or imagined bodies), so in geometry we know the causes because we make the effects — whereas in natural philosophy we know the effects and must seek the construction. This is why geometry is the paradigm science for Hobbes, and why he thought the same constructive method could be carried into civil philosophy, as he claims to have done in Leviathan. So here is what I want from you. First, a disciplined mapping: on this view, what should have counted, for Hobbes, as evidence for and against a proposed quadrature in 1655? What did his epistemology predict about his conduct — his confidence in a construction he had generated himself, his resistance to Wallis's arithmetic and algebraic style of refutation, his refusal to concede over two decades? Where does the mapping strain or break: his view licenses confidence in constructive method, but does it anywhere license immunity to a specific, checkable flaw in a construction, and if not, does the theory actually explain his behavior, or only rationalize it after the fact?

Second, run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode show about the theory? I can see at least three possible verdicts and I want you to commit to one with reasons — that it confirms the theory (his conduct flowed coherently from his epistemology, and a man who believes knowledge is self-construction will indeed treat an external examiner's objection as beside the point); that it exposes an anomaly (the theory never anticipated adversarial checking by others, or the possibility that a construction one "makes" can nonetheless be misdescribed by its maker); or that the episode reveals the theory was never really about cases like this. And be explicit about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak — for instance, does Hobbes's epistemology of geometry say anything at all about how disputes between geometricians should be resolved, or is it in that respect a blank? Finally, tell me honestly whether a different lens does better here — peer disagreement between a near-competent amateur and the leading geometer of the day, or the sociology of a mathematical community policing its standards — or whether Hobbes's own theory, applied carefully, is genuinely the most illuminating frame. I'd rather have an answer that defends a firm verdict and admits the strain points than one that hedges everything into agreement.
