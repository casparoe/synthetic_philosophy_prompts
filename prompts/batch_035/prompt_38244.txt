I'm assembling closing material for a unit in my value theory seminar on the logic of betterness, and I want to end the discussion of spectrum arguments — the Temkin- and Rachels-style challenges to the transitivity of "better than" — not with another handout but with a villanelle that is itself the argument.

The setup the poem should stage: a descending ladder of options in which each rung is only marginally worse than the one above it, while the first and last rungs are wildly far apart (a stubbed toe traded for a slightly longer ache, traded in turn for something a little worse, on down to something dreadful — choose your own ladder, but adjacent rungs must be plausibly indifferent and the endpoints grotesquely unequal). Each of the five tercets should carry one rung, or one stretch, of the descent, so that the poem read top to bottom is the spectrum. The closing quatrain should set the first rung directly beside the last and let the gap do the talking.

Use the two refrains as the two premises whose conjunction generates the paradox. The first refrain, returning verbatim every time, is the small-step claim: no single step makes a real difference. The second refrain is the running tally — what has actually accumulated by this point in the descent — and here I want you to use the traditional license to vary a refrain by a hair, but deliberately: exactly one word (or one small phrase) changes at each of its appearances, and each change should be individually negligible the way the steps of the spectrum are. In the final couplet the two refrains stand side by side, and the distance between the fixed one and the much-traveled one should be visible on the page. I care more about this refrain discipline than about meter, though keep the lines comparably weighted so the refrains scan the same at each return.

Do not end in a shrug. The poem should commit to one diagnosis and carry that commitment structurally. The live options, as I see them: transitivity fails for betterness, and the chain is real, so the concept rather than the intuition has to give; or the no-real-difference premise fails, because imperceptibility is not indifference and tiny differences remain differences, so the chain was never a threat; or the two endpoints are not genuinely comparable, so the argument never gets started. Pick one (or defend a fourth) and make the form argue for it — a reader who knows the spectrum literature should be able to tell from the poem alone which move you made, and should find it accurate rather than merely atmospheric.

After the poem, append exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with nothing before or after them: the first three reconstructing the poem's argument as its premises and inferential steps in order, the fourth stating plainly which premise the poem rejects, and the fifth answering the strongest objection to that rejection.
