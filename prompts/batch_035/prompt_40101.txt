I've been writing down my gut reactions to claims of the form "the theory wasn't wrong, it was early" — mostly about Malthus and his descendants in the long-run-prediction business — and I want to know whether there's a principled distinction underneath them or whether I'm just being partisan. Here are my verdicts.

1. Malthus, 1798. Population presses on subsistence. England escaped not by repealing the mechanism but through the fertility transition, emigration, and industrial agriculture, and the mechanism still runs wherever that transition hasn't happened; it also arguably ran in England itself for the half-century after the Essay, when wages barely moved while output grew. My verdict: "right but early" is legitimate here.

2. Jevons, The Coal Question, 1865. Britain's prosperity rested on cheap coal; with demand compounding and seams deepening, growth would be checked within a generation or two. No such check came — but British coal output did peak in 1913, within his window, and British relative decline followed. His defenders say: right about the dependence and about the peak, wrong about the consequences. My verdict: I'm inclined to count this as legitimate too.

3. Ehrlich, The Population Bomb, 1968. Predicted that hundreds of millions would starve to death in the 1970s and 1980s regardless of what anyone did. The decades passed, the famine was rescheduled, then quietly generalized to "sometime this century." My verdict: illegitimate. The theory was discredited, and every reissue made it worse.

Now the tension I can't resolve. Cases 2 and 3 are structurally identical: a resource or population constraint given a deadline, technology or history beating the deadline, and the date sliding. Worse, Ehrlich's mechanism is literally Malthus's mechanism, and I excuse Malthus while condemning Ehrlich for the same mechanism with the same kind of miss. And two more verdicts to constrain any principle:

4. Keynes, Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren, 1930. Predicted living standards several times higher within a hundred years — roughly right — and a working week of about fifteen hours — wrong. My verdict: this is not "right but early"; I split it into two claims, credit the confirmed one, and let the refuted one stand refuted. Nothing was early; the hours claim failed on its merits, and no clock was moved because there was nothing left to move.

5. An acquaintance's model called a financial crash for 2011; the crash came in 2020; he now claims vindication. My verdict: absurd. But structurally this looks like Jevons with smaller numbers, and I can't say why it isn't.

I've half-formed some candidate criteria, none of which work: (a) the author must have declared in advance which parts of the prediction were load-bearing and which were mere clock-reading — but Ehrlich could retort that his deadlines were rhetorical framing, and I have no evidence about what he privately thought; (b) the missed prediction must be compensated by independent corroboration of the mechanism elsewhere — Malthus has this in spades, but Ehrlich could point at every famine that did occur, and I don't know how to keep that move from counting; (c) the rescue must cost the theory something — a clause surrendered, not just a date moved — but I can't tell whether Malthus and Jevons paid that price or merely never set a firm enough date to have to move one.

What I want from you: a criterion, or a small set of them, stated precisely enough to decide new cases; its verdicts on three I haven't yet formed opinions about — a defender of The Limits to Growth saying the 1972 simulations were right but early, someone saying secular-stagnation predictions of the 2010s were right but early, and an AI-takeoff forecaster moving a 2025 deadline to 2030; and an honest accounting of which of my five verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and which of my verdicts it forces me to change. If you think the honest answer is that "right but early" is not a coherent category at all, and that cases 1 and 2 are really something else wearing its clothes, say that and say what they are.
