I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums since roughly the Overcoming Bias days, and over that stretch I have kept returning to a comparison I have never managed to complete to my own satisfaction: Camus's absurdism versus the contemporary literature on meaning in life, and Susan Wolf's fitting-fulfillment view in particular. On the absurdist side there is the confrontation between our demand for meaning and the universe's silence, the rejection of what Camus called philosophical suicide, and the closing injunction to imagine Sisyphus happy. On the other side there is Wolf's proposal that meaning in life arises from a fit between loving what one does and what one does being worth loving — subjective attraction to genuinely worthwhile projects. Please give me a genuine comparison of the two positions and then a verdict on which is closer to a complete account.

A few things I would like the comparison to settle. First, whether these are even competing theories. Camus sometimes reads as offering not an account of meaning but a normative stance toward its absence: live without appeal, in revolt, with passion. Wolf is offering a condition under which a life counts as meaningful. Is the disagreement substantive, or are they answering different questions that merely happen to share a word?

Second, a run of test cases our community actually argues about, on which the two views seem to diverge. The wirehead or experience-machine life: most of us judge the blissed-out existence to be meaningless, which looks like evidence for Wolf's objectivity condition. The researcher who spends her best decades on a theory that turns out to be false, but whose failure maps the terrain for everyone who comes after: Wolf's condition appears to make her life meaningful, but meaning now depends on downstream facts she could not survey from inside her life — does that cohere with the absurdist insistence that whatever redeems a life must be available from within it? The numerology devotee who experiences exactly the absorption and sense of contribution Wolf describes, aimed at projects of no worth whatsoever. And Sisyphus himself, on whom the two views return flatly opposed verdicts.

Third, Nagel's objection that the felt absurdity of life comes not from cosmic indifference but from our peculiar ability to step back and view our own projects from nowhere in particular. If Nagel is right, the absurdist's premise looks misdiagnosed. Does Camus's conclusion survive that correction, or does the diagnosis have to be reattached to the prescription?

Finally, my own tentative patch, which I would like tested rather than praised: naturalize Wolf's objectivity condition as whatever would survive reflection by an agent who had fully internalized the absurdist's external view. If the patch is incoherent — and I half suspect it is, since it seems to collapse into either Wolf or Camus depending on how the reflection is run — I want to know which of the two it collapses into, and which of the test cases above forces the choice.
