I write a column on the history of ideas, and I've spent the last few months reading about how rituals and customs come to exist, mostly because I kept tripping over cases where "ancient tradition" turned out to mean "invented within the last two centuries, often by a committee or a marketing department." Before I commit to a thesis for the piece, I want to be honest about my own reactions to particular cases, because I've noticed they don't line up neatly, and I'd like your help extracting whatever principle is actually driving them. So, my verdicts. The system of a distinct tartan for each Scottish clan, presented in a book from the 1840s that attributed the patterns to medieval manuscripts: invented, and somehow offensively so. The Olympic torch relay from Olympia: invented for the 1936 Berlin Games, and I'd say it remains invented no matter how telegenic it has become. The diamond engagement ring: invented by an advertising campaign, full stop. The American national holiday of Thanksgiving, proclaimed by Lincoln during the Civil War: invented, though this one bothers me less. The Japanese tea ceremony: genuine, even though it was substantially codified by a single master in the late sixteenth century, which is not much older than some of the clan tartans. The Passover seder: genuinely ancient in its basic structure. And then the case I keep going back and forth on: the Bastille Day ceremonies, instituted by the French Republic in 1880 as a deliberate act of national construction, now old enough that nobody who remembers its founding is alive — is that still invented, and if it isn't, at what point did it stop being so?

My tentative principle, such as it is, is that a tradition is "invented" when it lies about its own age, or lets people believe something false about it — the tartans came wrapped in bogus medieval manuscripts, the torch relay gestures at ancient Greece, the diamond campaign implied eternal custom. The tea ceremony makes no claim beyond its founder, so it's genuine even though it was designed. But I can see that this can't be the whole story. Lincoln's Thanksgiving never claimed to be older than 1863, yet my instinct still files it as invented, while the tea ceremony, designed by one man with the same self-knowledge, gets a pass. And the sincerity test seems to let the Bastille Day case flip over time without anything epistemic changing: everyone in 1880 knew it was new, everyone now assumes it's old, and my verdict wobbles accordingly, which suggests age or collective forgetting is doing work my principle doesn't acknowledge. There's also a nagging functional explanation I half-believe, from reading Hobsbawm and Anderson: maybe what really offends me is a tradition engineered to serve nation-building or commerce, and the tea ceremony gets excused because it was engineered to serve something spiritual instead — though that would make my verdicts look less like history and more like taste.

So here is what I'd like from you. State the candidate principles precisely, tell me which of my verdicts each one captures and which it forces me to abandon, and be honest about the trade-off between a complicated principle that saves everything and a simple one that costs me a verdict or two — I suspect the tea ceremony verdict is the one a clean principle would sacrifice, and I'd like to know if I should let it go. Then apply the surviving principle to a couple of cases I haven't worked through: the kilt itself, which I'm told exists in its familiar form largely thanks to an English factory owner in the eighteenth century yet is now inseparable from Scottish identity; and the Pledge of Allegiance, composed in the 1890s for a school celebration and now treated with something close to liturgical gravity. I should also mention that my editor wants the piece in continuous prose, so please respond the same way: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text or tables, just flowing paragraphs like a long letter.
