I'm putting together a proposal for a one-semester graduate term paper (~6000 words) on the problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation theory, and I have a half-baked idea I want you to turn into a proper proposal document.

The idea: everyone treats "old evidence" as a single problem — Einstein already knew about the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion before general relativity was formulated, yet GR's explaining that anomaly sure seems to confirm GR, and vanilla Bayesianism (where confirming evidence must raise your probability via conditionalisation) has trouble saying how that works. But I suspect two different situations are getting run together here. Sometimes the agent never fully believed E in the first place — their credence in E has since gone up because it got replicated or the measurement was tightened up. And sometimes they were confident of E all along but only just discovered that H entails or predicts it — which looks like a failure of logical omniscience, not a failure to have acquired evidence at all. My hunch is that the standard fixes each quietly work for only one of these cases: the "what would your credence have been without E" counterfactual line makes sense for the first case but is weird for the second (you can't subtract evidence you never doubted), and the "you learned the theory-to-evidence connection, so conditionalise on the discovered entailment" line makes sense for the second but does nothing for the first.

Please turn this into a proposal with: a precise statement of the research question; why the existing positions leave it open (without doing a citation-heavy lit review — just describe the positions, I'll fill in references later); the method, meaning what I'd actually do month by month, including how you'd formalise the two cases, e.g. in a toy agent with explicit credences, and how you'd test whether a given piece of formal machinery fits one case, the other, or both; what a positive result would look like; and an honest section on what would count as the project failing — for instance if the two cases turn out to be formally indistinguishable once you write them down, or if the "conditionalise on the entailment" route just collapses into a standard solution dressed up in new words.

Two requests on the epistemics. First, please keep a clear running distinction between what is basically settled among people who know this literature and what is genuinely contested — e.g. whether logical omniscience is the real culprit in the old-evidence problem is a live dispute, so don't present either side of that as consensus. Second, if my two-case split itself rests on a confusion (maybe there's a reason Bayesians treat these as one problem?), flag that rather than papering over it — I'd much rather find out now than in month three. Assume I know basic probability theory but not much formal epistemology beyond an intro course.
