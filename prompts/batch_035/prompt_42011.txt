I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and I'm writing a dissertation chapter on chance. Before my committee meeting next month I want a consistency check on my own package of views, because it feels like it should fall apart but I can't actually locate the seam. Here's the set. (a) I'm on board with the Principal Principle: if all I learn is that the objective chance of some event at a time is p, and nothing inadmissible, then my credence in the event should be p. (b) I'm sympathetic to Bohmian mechanics — deterministic hidden variables, the whole future fixed by the current state plus the laws, no fundamental dynamical probabilities anywhere. (c) I still think it's rational for me to have credence around 1/2 that the coin on my desk lands heads if I flip it, and like 0.99 that the soufflé I just put in the oven rises, and crucially these credences feel like they're tracking something objective about the setup — if I learned the coin was biased toward tails that would be a discovery about the coin, not just an update in my ignorance. (d) I think chance is one thing: at a time, for an event, there's the chance, full stop — not one chance relative to me and a different chance relative to the grad student in the lab next door who know more about the oven. If chances varied with who's evaluating them, they'd just be credences with a fancy name. (e) I also hold that there's no in-principle barrier to some agent knowing the current microstate of a system — physics doesn't forbid it, it's just very hard.

The resident skeptic on my committee says (b) plus (a) means every chance is 0 or 1, so my 0.99 soufflé credence directly violates the Principal Principle and one of the two has to go. I have a feeling that's too quick — I never actually learn the fundamental chance while standing in the kitchen, so it's not obvious (a) ever gets a grip on me — but I can't turn that feeling into a position, and the moment I say "well, the 0.99 is tracking something objective," I can hear him asking "tracking what, exactly, in a deterministic world?" and I don't have an answer that doesn't cost me (d), which I'd genuinely like to keep, though I can't tell how much of that is philosophy and how much is just me not liking the sound of "chance is relative."

What I want is an audit, not a lecture on all the things Bohmians are supposed to believe: which pairs of these are really in tension and how badly, which ones only look like they are, and for each real tension, what the cheapest fix is and what it costs. Rank them by severity rather than listing everything as equally bad. And if the package can be made consistent, tell me what I'd have to add to do it — I don't mind adding machinery, I mind pretending nothing was added.
