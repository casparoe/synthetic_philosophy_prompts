As a philosopher who teaches philosophy of science but does not work in formal epistemology, I recently sat in on a guest lecture aimed at undergraduates in which the speaker characterized van Fraassen's Reflection principle roughly as follows: "Reflection is van Fraassen's principle of deference to one's future self. It says that a rational agent should believe now whatever her later self is going to believe, since the later self will have had more time and more evidence and is therefore more likely to be right. Van Fraassen proved that an agent who violates this deference is diachronically incoherent: she is committed to inconsistent probability assignments across time, in just the way an agent with inconsistent betting odds can be Dutch-booked and drained of her money by a clever bookie. The principle is really an extension of the rule of conditionalization — it says that rational agents update by conditionalizing on new evidence, and it requires them to anticipate doing so. In the end, though, van Fraassen conceded that the principle was too strong to hold without exception, and he quietly dropped it from his later work."

Parts of this struck me as off — particularly the deference framing, which does not match my (admittedly informal) understanding of how van Fraassen motivates the principle — but I do not trust my own footing here, and the speaker was otherwise impressive. Before I say anything about Reflection to my own students, I would like a careful audit. Please go through the speaker's summary claim by claim and tell me: which parts a defender of Reflection, or someone who works in formal epistemology, would accept as a fair gloss; which parts conflate distinct claims that the view keeps apart or overstate a more modest thesis; and which parts are simply false. In particular, I would like to know whether the Dutch-book consequence as stated follows from anything accurate in the summary, and what a charitable but correct statement of the principle's relationship to conditionalization would look like. Finally, since I am half-inclined to accept the deference reading simply because it is the version I have absorbed from casual conversation, please restate the principle in a form I could give to students that anticipates and blocks the misreadings the speaker seems to have fallen into.
