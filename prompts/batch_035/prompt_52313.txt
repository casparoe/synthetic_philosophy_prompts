For the last three years I have helped run a Saturday discussion group at our community centre — about eighteen adults, a mix of retirees, shift workers and parents who come after the school run. None of us has any academic background; I spent my working life in insurance claims, and the group runs on coffee and whatever question someone brings in that week. A question I have been circling for months, entirely on my own, is this: whenever a group decides something by voting, the most important decision has already been made before anyone raises a hand — namely, who counts as being in the group. I first felt this at our building's owners' association, which votes on rules that bind the tenants as much as the owners, while the tenants get no vote; but if we let the tenants vote, why not the shopkeeper downstairs whose trade our rules affect, and if him, why not the whole city, and if not the whole city, then who draws the line, and by what right, since whoever draws it has already decided the outcome. On my own I have run into what look like dead ends from every direction. Rule one: everyone affected by the decision gets a say — but everything affects someone, so the association swells until the entire planet votes on where our bins go. Rule two: only those who can actually be compelled to obey — tidy, but it quietly drops people who benefit or suffer without being coerced, and it hands victory to whoever is good at dodging enforcement. Rule three: only those competent to understand the choice — which turns out to mean whoever I have decided has understood it, a suspiciously convenient way of silencing whoever disagrees. Rule four: those who consented — but I never consented to being born into the groups that rule me, and neither did anyone else.

What I would like from you is a game we can actually run in one ninety-minute session with those eighteen people, in a room with movable tables. My strong preference is that the problem arrive through the rules rather than through me: participants should make decisions, get rewarded under a rule for who counts, and only later discover that the rule for who counts was itself the real decision — and that it was made by someone, in someone's interest, without ever being voted on. I would like real but small stakes (nothing involving money — perhaps points that decide who picks the refreshments for the following month), and I would like the composition of the voting group to change at least once mid-game, so that people experience being moved from insider to outsider, or the reverse, by someone else's stroke of a pen. I have one half-formed mechanic you may keep or discard: the winners of an early round get to write the rules for who votes in the next round. When I imagine running that, the room simply gets angry; perhaps the anger is the lesson, but I would honestly rather you design the whole thing from scratch.

Please include, alongside the rules and materials, a debrief that maps specific moments from the game onto specific parts of the problem — above all, separating what we decided from who we counted, which I expect most participants will not have distinguished even once. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be blunt about which parts of the real problem a ninety-minute game cannot possibly show, so that I can decide whether to raise them in the debrief myself. I should say that I do not have an answer of my own that I want the game to smuggle in — I find each of my four rules appealing in one situation and monstrous in the next — so if the game ends up quietly favouring one of them, tell me that too, because I would rather know than be flattered.
