I hold Berkeley's theory of money, the one he develops in The Querist in the 1730s through numbered questions about Ireland's poverty: money has no intrinsic value; its true idea is, in his words, "altogether that of a ticket or counter"; and its whole use lies in stirring up industry, money being valuable only as it "stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour." On this view, wealth is labour and not bullion; a country can be rich without a mine; and commerce could in principle be carried on in paper, since Berkeley asks whether, the denominations being retained, "although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained." I hold the modernized version: what backs a currency is collective confidence, the material it is struck or printed on is a detail, and what matters is the flow — Berkeley's thought that less money swiftly circulating is, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating.

What would count against this? Don't explain the theory back to me and don't point me to literature. I want a map of its exposure. Which observations, historical episodes, or regularities would a defender of the view have to concede genuinely count against it — and how do they rank, from decisive to merely awkward? I can see some candidates myself and want them assessed rather than assumed: the recurring collapse of pure fiat regimes through over-issue (does that confirm the theory, on the ground that the convention was withdrawn, or reveal something the theory is silent on?); hyperinflated notes that still bear their denominations while buying nothing, which looks like the convention persisting without the value; gold hoards, and now cryptographic tokens, held for decades outside any issuing authority by people with no intention of ever redeeming them for anyone's labour; and whether the historical record supports the thought that money typically arises by spontaneous exchange out of a commodity with non-monetary use, rather than being instituted and sustained by authority — and what an answer either way would do to Berkeley's story. Distinguish what a committed Berkeleyan would concede as counting against the theory from what defenders routinely wave off, and say which of those dismissals are legitimate. Finally, the question I care about most: has the view been insulated into unfalsifiability? Its defenders can re-describe nearly anything — an idle hoard becomes a ticket to command labour, a collapse becomes a convention withdrawn — and I want to know whether that manoeuvring is a defect in a theory of this kind, or simply what any functionalist theory of a social institution has to accept. If it helps you order things, what I would personally find most decisive is a case where a community sustains a medium of exchange that demonstrably fails to mobilize industry, or rejects one that would. Keep the whole answer under 800 words.
