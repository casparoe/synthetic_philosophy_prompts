I'm putting together a talk for a philosophy of science reading group that works through Imre Lakatos, and I want to close with a counterfactual exercise rather than another exegesis session. Your job is to make that exercise as rigorous as counterfactuals about a dead philosopher's opinions can be.

Setup: Lakatos died in 1974, roughly four decades before the current era of large-scale deep learning. That field has since organized itself into something that looks, at least superficially, like a research programme in his sense. The putative hard core is the bet that Richard Sutton compacted in his 2019 essay "The Bitter Lesson": general-purpose architectures trained with more parameters, more data, and more compute reliably outperform methods built on hand-crafted domain knowledge. The protective belt would then include the auxiliary theories that absorb failures without touching the core — tokenization and data-mixing recipes, architectural tweaks, fine-tuning and alignment patches, evaluations that get adjusted when models do embarrassingly or embarrassingly well on them. And there is a plausible rival programme: mechanistic interpretability, whose practitioners often describe their work in almost inverted terms — they claim scaling cannot explain what the models do, and that reverse-engineering the circuits is the only route to understanding, prediction, and control.

Task: reconstruct how Lakatos would have evaluated this situation, using his methodology of research programmes as the reconstruction engine. Before writing the talk, brainstorm internally and settle the following questions, then present your conclusions with the reasoning behind each:

1. Progressive or degenerating? Work out what, in Lakatos's framework, would count as a novel prediction for the scaling programme. He distinguished sharply between theories that anticipate phenomena and theories that accommodate them post hoc; benchmark evaluations are a genuine puzzle here, since benchmarks are authored before they are run, but often turn out to be contaminated by training data or trivially gamed. Does Lakatos have the resources to rule on whether benchmark wins are novel predictive successes or dressed-up accommodation? Consider also that he judged programmes on a temporally extended view — a programme may stagnate and then spring forward. On the evidence you actually know about, would he score the scaling programme as currently progressive, currently degenerating, or too early to call?

2. Would he have a concept for the present state of interpretability research — a rival programme competing for the same problem domain, a degenerating offshoot of the parent, or part of the parent's protective belt? He had a category for cases where one programme's hard core is quietly preserved inside another's; think about whether interpretability practitioners are refuting the core, ignoring it, or parasitically relying on it.

3. Where does his framework visibly strain? Identify at least two respects in which this situation lacks anything Lakatos gave his machinery for. Two candidates I suspect are relevant but want your judgment on: whether he has a category for a programme whose "theoretical" content is mostly an empirical regularity (the scaling laws) rather than an explanatory mechanism, and whether he has any notion of a field whose internal history has been recorded too publicly, in preprints and release notes, to permit the sort of rational reconstruction he liked to perform. But you may find better candidates; if so, name them, and say what you would have to assume about Lakatos's broader commitments to stretch the framework over the gap.

Conventions: throughout, clearly separate what Lakatos's published methodology strictly entails from what you are extrapolating on his behalf. Flag every extrapolation as such; a talk that confidently reports Lakatos's opinions on transformer scaling laws would embarrass the speaker, and the audience will include people who know his work well. No citations, no bibliography — I have his texts and will handle references myself. Write the talk as you would deliver it: a short framing of the exercise, the three verdicts with argument, and a closing paragraph on what the exercise teaches about the limits of applying Lakatos's categories to a field that is engineering-driven rather than theory-driven. Take your time; precision here matters more than coverage.
