For an upper-level course I teach called "Correlation, Causation, and Paradox," aimed at mixed philosophy and statistics students, please write one midterm exam question on Simpson's paradox, together with a model answer and a marking scheme out of 20 marks. The question must go beyond spotting the reversal. Construct a fresh, numerically explicit dataset for it — a fictional university comparing online versus in-person teaching, where online sections produce better outcomes within every single department yet lose in the aggregate — and then attach two mutually exclusive institutional backstories to the same table: one in which students were assigned to a format on the basis of their department, so that department acts as a confounder; and one in which students chose a format first, with the departmental composition of each section forming as a downstream consequence, so that department acts as a mediator. The question should then ask, under each backstory, which marginal — the aggregated or the disaggregated — answers the causal question of which format would teach better, with students required to justify their verdicts using a causal diagram. The marking scheme should allocate only a few marks to correctly detecting the reversal itself, give the bulk of the marks to the confounder/mediator discrimination and the graph-based justification, and close with a one-line note naming the mistake you expect strong-but-not-excellent students to make — most plausibly, the assumption that the disaggregated answer is always the causally correct one, which is precisely the lesson students absorb from the standard textbook presentation of the Berkeley graduate admissions case.
