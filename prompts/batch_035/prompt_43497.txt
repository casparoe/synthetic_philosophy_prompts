I write historical fiction, and my editor — let's call her Dana — has very firm instincts about when you're entitled to *believe* something, which she mostly deploys to shut down my fact-checking anxiety at 11pm. Her track record, from arguments we've actually had: she says I'm perfectly rational to believe my lottery ticket will lose (odds of 1 in 40 million against winning), though she'd never say I *know* it will lose. She says a patient is rational to believe "I'm fine" off a diagnostic test that's 99.9% reliable, and that the doctor saying it is fine too. She is on record as saying it was rational of her, for every single claim in her own last nonfiction book, to believe that that claim is true — she checked each one individually — while also believing, and announcing in the preface, that the book almost certainly contains at least one error somewhere. And when I pressed her with "so you beleive every claim in the book AND you believe at least one claim in the book is false," she called that a cheap trick with the word 'and' rather than a contradiction she was actually committed to.

Now the immediate problem. My new novel has a historical afterword with 90 period details in it — what people ate, which streets existed in 1883, what a mourning brooch was for — and my researcher has verified each of the 90 individually to her satisfaction. But she also told me flat out that with 90 claims like this, statistically a couple are probably wrong, though she can't say which ones. Dana has to rule on two things: whether I'm rational to believe every single detail is correct, and separately whether I can write in the afterword "every detail here is accurate."

Predict her verdicts on both, and tell me what rule she's actually running. I want you to be honest that more than one rule fits her record so far, since her lottery ruling and her preface ruling don't obviously come from the same principle — lay out the candidate rules, say what each one predicts for my afterword, give me your best guess with reasons, and then give me the further case that would actually tell the rules apart. For what it's worth, my own suspicion is that what matters is that in the lottery she knows the exact structure of the situation (exactly one ticket wins, she just doesn't know which) while in my afterword nobody knows which details are the wrong ones or even how many — but I genuinely can't tell whether that difference does any real work or whether it's just vibes, and she'd eat me alive in the margins if I guessed wrong.
