I'm a clinical psychology doctoral student transitioning to dissertation work, and my project sits between philosophy of mind and psychotherapy research. I take eliminative materialism — the Churchlands' claim that beliefs and desires are posits of a folk psychological theory that mature neuroscience might displace rather than vindicate — seriously enough that I want the dissertation to engage its consequences for clinical practice. But I don't want to spend three years on implications that turn out not to follow from the view at all, or that would change nothing in the clinic even if they did follow.

Here is the task. Take the alleged practical implications of eliminative materialism for psychotherapy and psychiatry, and sort them into three categories: those that genuinely follow (or would follow if the view turned out true), those that follow but are overrated in how much they would actually change practice, and those that look like consequences but are non sequiturs. Among the candidates I've been circling — add or drop as you see fit:

1. Cognitive restructuring as practiced in CBT presupposes that beliefs are real internal states with causal power; an eliminativist should therefore expect cognitive techniques to be superseded by, or at least reducible to, exposure-based and purely behavioral techniques that never appeal to the contents of a patient's attitudes.
2. Patient self-reports of beliefs and desires lose their standing as data about inner states and become, at best, more behavior to be explained — which would change what "insight-oriented" therapy can even coherently promise.
3. Diagnostic categories framed in terms of propositional attitudes, such as delusions conceived as false beliefs, inherit the shaky status of the attitudes they invoke, whereas categories framed in terms of affect, arousal, and observable behavior are untouched by the eliminativist's argument.
4. The therapeutic alliance runs on shared folk psychology — therapist and patient understanding each other by attributing beliefs and desires — so if eliminativism were true, a working mechanism of change would be threatened, not merely a vocabulary.
5. Capacity and consent assessments in psychiatry and law presuppose that patients literally have the beliefs and desires their decisions are taken to express, so eliminativism would destabilize the normative basis of respecting refusals.

Second, rank the items you placed in the first two categories by how much they deserve to shape my dissertation, and state your criteria explicitly. The ones I'd expect you to weigh: how much philosophical work the implication presupposes versus generates; how tractable it is for someone trained in clinical research rather than neuroscience; how neglected it is relative to the well-worn general debate about whether folk psychology is a theory; and how the ranking would shift under a different framing of what is at stake.

That last point deserves emphasis. Much depends, in my view, on which rival to belief-realism is live in the clinic. If the realistic alternative to attitudes-as-inner-states is Dennett-style instrumentalism — the intentional stance as a predictive strategy we are free to keep — then several of the dramatic implications collapse into choices about vocabulary, and I want to know which of my five candidates survive even on that weaker reading. Treat this as a constraint on your analysis: for each item, say whether it depends on full-blooded eliminativism or would follow equally from mere instrumentalism about belief-talk.

Finally, tell me which of the five you would cut outright as a dissertation topic, and propose one question that is not on my list and that you judge more promising than any of them. I am looking for something in the weeds of clinical practice rather than another pass at the standard debate — an angle a philosopher without therapy experience would be unlikely to generate, but one that could be defended without empirical claims I cannot back up. Throughout, I want the reasoning on display, not just verdicts: for every sorting and ranking decision, exhibit the inference, and flag the premise in it that a defender of the practice — a CBT theorist, a phenomenologically inclined clinician, a forensic psychiatrist — would most plausibly contest.
