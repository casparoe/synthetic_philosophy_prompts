Our metaethics reading group ends the year with a tradition: each member circulates a short position statement, and the others spend the final session trying to knock it apart. Mine is drafted, and before I send it out I'd like an outside opinion on whether it can survive contact — not with the counterarguments to each view individually, which I've mostly worked through, but with itself. Here is the set. (1) I am a robust realist about ethics: there are mind-independent moral facts, and they are not reducible to facts about our attitudes or to any natural inventory. (2) I think the Darwinian dilemma pressed by Sharon Street is basically right as an epistemological challenge: natural selection tuned our evaluative tendencies to fitness rather than to moral truth, and it would be an enormous coincidence if the belief patterns that promoted survival and reproduction on the Pleistocene savannah also tracked independent moral facts. (3) At the same time, I accept the companions-in-guilt response: our mathematical capacities have an evolutionary genealogy too — counting, estimating quantities, navigating space — and nobody concludes that arithmetic is debunked by its origin, so I don't see why ethics should be treated differently. (4) Yet I also hold that there is a genuine disanalogy between ethics and mathematics, namely persistent, deep disagreement: cultures and centuries disagree about fundamental moral questions in a way they simply do not disagree about whether seven is prime, and I think this difference matters for how debunking arguments apply. (5) I nonetheless believe moral progress is real and not merely change: the abolition of slavery was a matter of discovering an independent moral fact, not a shift in preference, and I would say the same about the fading of torture as routine judicial punishment. (6) Finally, I hold that reflective equilibrium is the correct method in ethics — intuitions count as data, defeasible data, and the route to truth is mutual adjustment between principles and considered judgments.

Please treat the whole set as the object of the audit. Which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, which only look to be, and for each real tension, say exactly what would have to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set? I'd rather not get a flat list where everything is flagged as equally problematic: rank the tensions by severity, identify which of the apparent ones actually dissolve on closer inspection, and, if I were to make only one revision, tell me which single change would clean up the most.
