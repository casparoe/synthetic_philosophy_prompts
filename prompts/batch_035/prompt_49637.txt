I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on formal decision theory, and our unit on infinite expectations has produced a disagreement with my seminar partner that I cannot resolve. I'd like a third opinion, in the form of an actual credence with the reasoning laid bare.

First, the setup, so the question is precise. In the St. Petersburg game, a fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads; if that happens on toss n, the payoff is 2^n dollars, and the expectation series 1 + 1 + 1 + ... diverges. In the Pasadena game, introduced by Harris Nover and Alan Hájek in 2004, the flipping procedure is the same, but the payoff for first heads on toss n is (-1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n dollars, so rewards and penalties alternate and grow without bound. Since toss n ends the game with probability 2^(-n), the expectation series is 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2 — about 69 cents — but only conditionally. By the rearrangement theorem for conditionally convergent series, enumerating the very same outcomes in a different order makes the series sum to any real number you like, or diverge. And nothing in the physical gamble depends on how the outcomes are listed: the coin does not care about our bookkeeping.

Here is the twist that keeps us arguing. Imagine the casino lets you choose, before the flip, between two ticket formats. Format A lists the outcomes in the natural order of the tosses. Format B lists each reward outcome followed by two penalty outcomes — outcome 1, then outcomes 2 and 4, then outcome 3, then outcomes 6 and 8, and so on — which still covers every outcome exactly once, but whose series converges to (1/2) ln 2, roughly 35 cents. The two tickets are physically identical: same coin, same payoffs, same probabilities. If the fair price of the game is what the series says, you should value the two tickets differently, which seems absurd; if it is not what the series says, it is unclear what it is. My partner's rejoinder is that the natural order is not mere bookkeeping — it is the order of the tosses themselves, the temporal order in which the experiment actually unfolds — so ln 2 is privileged after all. I find this tempting but suspicious: the coin has no memory of an order of hypothetical outcomes, only of what actually happens.

So, the proposition I want your credence on: the Pasadena game has a determinate rational fair price for a risk-neutral agent with unbounded, linear utility for money — a single number p such that she should be indifferent between playing and being handed p dollars — with that price fixed independently of how the outcomes happen to be enumerated. My credence is about 0.35; my partner's is about 0.8, since she thinks the natural-order sum simply is what expectation means and that rearrangement is a mathematical curiosity irrelevant to the defined random variable.

Please give me your own number, but more importantly decompose it.
