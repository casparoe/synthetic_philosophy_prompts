My friend has accumulated stable verdicts on cases of honesty over years of arguments: lying to a pursuing killer about where his intended victim is hiding is not wrong; telling a friend her novel is brilliant when it is mediocre is a small but genuine wrong; lying on an insurance claim is a serious wrong; a doctor who conceals a terminal diagnosis at the family's request, against the patient's explicit wish to be told, has done something seriously wrong; telling a friend's child that her drawing is wonderful is not wrong at all; and volunteering a humiliating truth nobody asked for — telling a colleague, unasked, that his ideas bore everyone — is a real wrong. Three new cases have come up, and I want to predict her verdicts before we argue about them. (1) A dying friend instructs me plainly: I want you to tell me that my son forgave me, whether or not it is true; do not tell me otherwise. I say it, and it is false. (2) A used-car seller's enthusiasm is expected and discounted by everyone, but amid the patter he states, as flat fact, that the car was never crashed, when it was. (3) A friend has instructed me never to tell her bad news about her health; a routine test shows something serious but treatable if caught early, and I tell her over her standing instruction. What will she say about each? I'm less interested in the three answers than in the rule she seems to be running: is it a generalist principle — and if so, which candidate principles fit her record equally well — or does the record point toward something more particularist, where a feature like deception or paternalism changes its moral character with context? And which of the three cases would best tell the candidate rules apart?
