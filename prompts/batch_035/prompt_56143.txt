So I write for the science and ideas desk at a mid-size outlet, and I'm doing a piece on psychiatric diagnosis. The researcher I've been interviewing keeps reaching for this same image: good science carves nature at its joints, the way a skilled butcher cuts where the animal is already divided instead of hacking through bone. Her point being that chemistry found real joints — the periodic table, water turning out to be H2O — while the DSM just hacks. Depression, ADHD, these are cuts of convenience, not joints, and that's supposedly why psych meds are such a mess: we're treating cuts, not kinds.

I sort of buy it, but I have a nagging feeling the analogy is carrying more weight than it should, and I don't want to hand it to my readers unexamined. Can you audit it for me? What maps onto what — who's the butcher, what's the carcass, what exactly is a joint supposed to be (an essence? a cluster of properties that travel together? something else?), and is there a knife in the picture at all, or does the carving happen for free once you find the structure? Then tell me where the picture breaks. Planets seem like a decent stress test — the IAU demoted Pluto in 2006 and it looked like a committee decision, a knife fight, not the discovery of a joint, though maybe that's wrong and there really is a joints fact of the matter there. Species are another one, with ring species and hybrids blurring things. And "life" itself, given how viruses sit astride whatever line you want to draw.

Then the honest question: does the conclusion my researcher wants — that psychiatry's categories are second-class because they don't track joints — actually survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Or does the butcher image quietly assume there's exactly one right way to carve everything up, an assumption that maybe even chemistry doesn't support (is "isotope" a joint? is "acid"?). If the carving picture can't support her conclusion, is there a better analogy she could be using — something that captures why chemical kinds feel more robust than clinical ones without implying there's One True Carving — or is the robustness itself the thing I should be questioning? I need to know what I can actually claim in print.
