I'm teaching an upper-level seminar on moral responsibility and writing the take-home final for it. I want one essay question on standing to blame that can't be answered just by reciting that hypocrites lack standing. The question should center on a case I've been kicking around: someone who blames a friend for, say, cruel gossip, when the blamer committed the same wrong years ago, has since genuinely reformed, and makes no secret of their past. Call them the reformed hypocrite. They're not dishonest, not currently guilty of the same thing, not hiding anything — so if they've still lost standing, I want to know what exactly the standing was protecting. The question should force students to first say what standing to blame even is (a right? an authority? just a propriety condition on when blame is appropriate?), then use the reformed hypocrite to stress-test whichever answer they give. Write the question itself, a model answer of about 600 words that takes an actual position rather than surveying the options, and a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain — e.g. distinguishing loss of standing from the blameworthiness of the blame itself, or explaining why mere past inconsistency shouldn't cost you anything now. One more thing: keep the whole response as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no numbered lists, even in the marking scheme. And no name-dropping papers in the model answer — my students won't have citations on a take-home anyway, and I want it to model argument, not bibliography.
