I'm a PhD student in political theory serving on the graduate student assembly's governance committee, and we have to settle a boundary dispute before our March referendum on raising the student activities fee to fund a childcare subsidy. The assembly's charter says the referendum passes by majority vote of "the graduate student body," but nobody has ever defined that body, and the outcome depends heavily on how we do. The fee, if passed, would be charged to anyone registered for at least one graduate course; the subsidy would be available only to students with children. Four groups are contested: students on parental or medical leave (not currently paying the fee, but eligible for the subsidy), students in their final semester writing dissertations who are technically registered but off campus, part-time master's students in a professional program who use almost none of the activities the fee funds, and visiting exchange students who will leave before any subsidy is paid out. A faction on the committee wants to just use the registrar's headcount because "registered students" is administratively clean; another faction argues that everyone affected by the outcome should vote, including, on their principle, non-student partners of students with children.

Draft the written recommendation I'll circulate before the meeting. It should take a clear position on which of the four contested groups gets a ballot and which don't, and it should ground that position in a principled account of what entitles someone to a say—e.g., being subject to the outcome's coercion, bearing its costs, having one's interests affected, or being able to hold the body accountable afterward—rather than just enumerating who seems sympathetic. It should also explain why the "affected parties" principle, pushed to its limit, proves too much for a body like ours, and say where the registrar's-headcount fallback is genuinely defensible versus merely convenient. Please keep in mind that the philosophical literature on the boundary problem offers no consensus resolution; separate what is widely accepted among theorists from what remains genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the settled answer. Write in American English.
