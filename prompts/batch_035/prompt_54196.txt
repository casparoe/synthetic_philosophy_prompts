I've been reading LessWrong and adjacent forums since about 2010, and the house style trained me to distrust questions about categories: if a question seems to admit no answer, the Sequences say, dissolve it — show it rests on a confused model. For years I assumed the special composition question (under what conditions do some things compose one further thing?) was exactly that sort of question, and that my object-level judgments were cached human ontology. But my verdicts on particular cases don't dissolve under inspection; they pattern-match with distressing consistency, and not toward either of the two answers the dissolution is supposed to leave me with. So I've done what the rationalists tell you to do: written down my gut verdicts on a spread of cases, and I want to know whether there's a principle behind them, and if so what it is.

My verdicts, stated as a real person would have them:

1. My desk: composed. Obvious.
2. A murmuration of starlings, thousands of birds turning as one: not composed. Many birds, however choreographed.
3. The same starlings roosting on a wire, wing to wing, motionless: still not composed. Contact does nothing for me here.
4. A freight train: composed — even though the cars are joined only by couplings.
5. A total-gridlock traffic jam, cars a meter apart, engines off, hours old: not composed, even though it is denser and more stationary than the train. This one I can't justify and won't pretend to.
6. Loose gravel in a pile: not composed. The same gravel mixed with cement and cured into a slab: composed. Composition switched on somewhere in the pouring and curing, and I can't say when.
7. Two people slow-dancing, hands clasped, sustained: not composed. Conjoined twins joined at the hip: I say two objects, but if they share a liver I wobble, and if they share a heart I say one.
8. A coral reef: composed. I realize this sits badly with the starlings, a reef being mostly a colony of tiny animals plus rock, but there it is.
9. The ship of Theseus, planks replaced one at a time over years: one object the whole way through. And the pile of old planks in the shipyard is not an object, and — the part I care about — the ship and the pile do not compose some larger object, even though the pile exists only because of the ship.
10. A datacenter running a single model: ten thousand GPUs, one set of weights, coolant pumps humming. Inclined to say the whole building is composed, one object — in a way I would not say about the same building during a power outage, when it is just a warehouse of parts.

I know the standard poles: the nihilist answer (composition never occurs, or never outside living things) and the universalist answer (any things whatsoever compose something). My verdicts agree with each somewhere and disagree with both elsewhere, which is why I'm asking.

Please find the principle, or principles, behind these verdicts — reflective equilibrium, done on request. Two to three candidates, stated precisely enough to generate verdicts on cases I haven't listed. A principle that can't pay rent in predictions about new cases is a clause-machine, not a principle, so show me what your best candidate predicts. And be honest about where the rationalist dissolution is right: if some of my verdicts are just human ontology projected onto the world, tell me which ones and by what criterion, rather than rescuing the rest by giving the principle one clause per case.

Give me any prose discussion you need first, keeping it under about six hundred words, and then the structured summary as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

  "principles": [ { "name": string, "statement": string (precise enough to generate new verdicts), "motivating_cases": [case numbers from my list] } ],
