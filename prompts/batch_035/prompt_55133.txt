I'm taking a metaphysics of science course, and we've just finished the unit on laws of nature, where the big fault line was Humeanism versus its opponents. In the seminar the debate kept circling back to one analogy that a classmate of mine now treats as decisive, and I'd like to see it properly audited rather than just asserted. Her version goes like this: the word "law" in "law of nature" is a leftover from a legal picture, so we should settle the whole dispute by looking at what actual laws of the land are like. A statute is governing in the full-blooded sense — it exists independently of anyone's behavior, and conduct can conform or fail to conform to it, with sanctions or at least illegality as the mark of the failure. But the "laws" a demographer or an actuary talks about — that the residents of a certain district have voted the same way for sixty years, that mortality rises with age — are not governing anything; they are just our best summaries of patterns in the data, and nobody thinks those summaries constrain anybody. She then says the Humean is simply claiming that laws of nature are like the second kind of law, and that once you see that, the governing picture looks like a metaphysical illusion: a pattern cannot push a planet around, any more than a bar chart can.

She's using the analogy to argue for the Humean conclusion — that governing laws are spooks, and that the Best System picture is not a fallback but the truth about what lawhood always was. My worry is that I can't tell which parts of the legal source domain the analogy is entitled to carry over. In the legal case the contrast feels vivid partly because statutes have legislatures, penalties, and enforcement — features that, if anything, a divine-command voluntarist about laws would happily accept and a modern anti-Humean would reject. So it's not obvious that the analogy is neutral between the two camps, or even aimed at the right target: it may be ridiculing a divine-legislator view that no one in the seminar holds, while leaving Armstrong-style necessitation untouched. On the other hand, maybe the analogy does carry a real insight — that everything we ever do to confirm or apply a law is compatible with its being merely an exceptionally good summary, so the "governing" bit never does any epistemic work.
