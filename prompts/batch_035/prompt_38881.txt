I work in rational choice theory, and the argument over whether money pump arguments succeed has, in one form or another, been running since the 1950s and in its modern form since the early 1990s, without either side giving ground. I want a diagnosis of the standoff rather than another round of it.

The setup, briefly: if an agent's preferences are cyclic — she prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A — a trader can offer her a sequence of trades, each individually acceptable, that returns her to her starting option while pocketing a fee at every step. The standard inference is that rational preferences must be acyclic, indeed transitive, on pain of exploitation. The critics have a family of replies, the proponents a family of counter-replies, and the whole exchange has been static for decades:

1. The awareness reply: a rational agent who notices the pattern will refuse the later trades, so no pump ever runs. Proponents respond with one-shot pumps: if the trader can offer all the trades at once, or if each trade is independently acceptable given the agent's preferences at that moment, then refusal requires the agent's current choice to depend on her own future choices. That leads to the next reply.

2. The resolute choice reply: a rational agent is entitled to bind herself — to decline a trade she now prefers because she committed earlier to a plan. The proponent's rejoinder is that then the agent's standing preferences are not her all-things-considered preferences, or that commitment merely relocates the intransitivity into the machinery of self-binding rather than answering the argument.

3. The contestability reply: pumpability is too contingent a property to ground a requirement of rationality — whether the pump runs depends on the trader's opportunities, the agent's foresight, and whether trades are available jointly or only sequentially — so if anything is irrational, it is the intransitivity itself, not the vulnerability. Proponents answer by tightening the pump's preconditions until exploitation requires nothing but the preferences.

My hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the two sides are answering different questions: the proponents are asking what norms govern an agent's preferences at a time, treating the pump as a dramatization of intransitivity, while the critics are asking what norms govern a choosing agent extended through time, for whom the operative requirement is consistency in the plan rather than in the options. If that is right, the dispute is partly verbal — but only partly, since a substantive question would remain about whether an agent whose time-sliced preferences are cyclic, but whose whole-life pattern of choice is coherent, has anything wrong with her at all.

So: is the disagreement verbal, a difference about the relata of the rationality requirement (options versus plans), a difference about standards of proof (what counts as establishing a requirement rather than merely indicating one), or a clash of bedrock intuitions about what rationality is for? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a formal distinction, a decision-theoretic result, an agreed class of test cases, a change of question — or is there reason to expect none? Please answer in American English, and engage the positions as I have described them rather than reciting the standard textbook treatment of transitivity.
