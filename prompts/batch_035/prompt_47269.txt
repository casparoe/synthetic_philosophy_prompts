I've been in an epistemology reading group for about a year now, and the conciliationism vs steadfastness debate over peer disagreement has completely stalled, and I'm trying to work out what kind of stalemate it actually is. Quick setup so we're on the same page: you and a colleague are equally competent and equally well informed, you've pooled all your evidence, and on some question — say whether some inference is formally valid, or who influenced whom in a piece of intellectual history — you come down on one side and she comes down on the other. The conciliationists (the Elga-style equal weight crowd) say you should each give the other's view some weight and move at least partway; the steadfasters (Kelly and company) say you can keep your confidence, at least when you have an independent antecedent case that you're the one who's got it right. The moves have been identical for over a decade: the conciliationists say that "she's probably wrong because I'm probably right" is just bootstrapping, and the steadfasters reply that if disagreement always forced a retreat, nobody could stably believe anything in philosophy, religion, or politics, since for every smart informed person on one side there's another on the other. Nobody in the group has budged an inch, and honestly neither has the literature as far as I can tell.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to actually evaluate rather than just nod along to: the disagreement is verbal in a slightly sneaky way. Maybe both camps secretly agree about the credences — everyone converges on something near the middle in the hard cases — and what they're really fighting over is what to *do* with a middling credence: what counts as "justified belief" or "reasonable confidence" when you're hovering around 0.55 after the disagreement. The steadfasters read "belief" with a threshold that tolerates that; the conciliationists don't. If that's right, the debate should really dissolve into a debate about the concept of belief itself — no easier, but at least a different and more tractable one. But I can also see a rival diagnosis: the camps genuinely disagree about higher-order evidence, i.e. whether the bare fact that she disagrees with you is itself evidence that you're mistaken, and that's a substantive question, not a verbal one. There's even a third option, that they're answering two different questions under one heading — what's rational from the inside, versus what's objectively likely from the God's-eye view. So: which is it, and why? And whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would count as progress here — a worked-out theory of belief thresholds, a test case both sides would have to accept, or nothing at all — and be honest if you think no progress should be expected. One last thing: the disagreement itself is a peice of evidence about how hard this is, so factor that in if you like.

Please write your answer in British English, and no reading lists or citations — just reason it through from what you know.
