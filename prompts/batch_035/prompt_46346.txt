I keep a running folder of cases where an autonomous system causes harm, along with my own gut verdicts on each, and I want to know whether there's a principle behind the pattern — specifically, when a "responsibility gap" opens up (a situation where the harm seems to escape anyone's responsibility altogether) versus when responsibility just gets reshuffled onto whoever's nearest. Here's the folder, with my verdicts:

A self-driving car at a known-tricky junction misclassifies a cyclist and injures them. The manufacturer knew this failure mode was rare-but-possible. Verdict: not a gap. Responsibility lands on the manufacturer, and partly on whoever certified the car for that kind of junction in the first place.

A military drone strike kills civilians. There was a human authoriser, but he had about eight seconds, three overlapping sensor feeds, and a checklist designed for much slower decisions. Verdict: genuine gap. Nobody in that loop had the practical ability to do otherwise than what they did, even though nominally a human "decided".

A trading firm's algorithm triggers a flash crash. Nobody at the firm wanted or foresaw that specific outcome, but they did choose to deploy that class of algorithm because it was fast, and they knew the risk class existed in general. Verdict: not a gap — but notice that no individual is culpable either. The firm, as a firm, is responsible. So: responsibility without a responsible human. Where does that fit?

A surgical robot in autonomous mode makes a judgement call (which nerve to route around) that no human reviewed, and it's a call a trained surgeon would probably have made differently. The hospital chose autonomous mode for throughput reasons. Verdict: honestly unsure. I want to say gap, but the throughput decision keeps pulling responsibility back onto the hospital, and i cant tell which feeling should win.

Now the awkward parts. For the drone I want to say BOTH that it's a gap AND that the officers who put the authoriser in that position — rushed timeline, bad checklist — are responsible for the deaths. But if people further up are responsible for the outcome, in what sense did responsibility "escape" anyone? It seems to have just moved. So here's my tentative principle, and I'd like it stress-tested: a gap opens exactly when, at the moment of harm, no agent had the time, information, and attention needed to have done otherwise. This saves the drone verdict, but it seems to make a gap just… a tragic accident? Is that a bug or the whole point?

And one more thing the principle has to digest. It says the trading case isnt a gap because the firm could have not deployed the algorithm — which I accept — but notice that locates the crucial control at deployment time, not at the moment of harm. Is that consistent with denying gap-status to the car while granting it to the drone, given that the drone's rushed checklist was itself a negligent deployment decision? I go back and forth on this every time i open the folder.

So: is there a principle that captures all of this? What does it say about the surgical robot, and about a case I havent listed — a system so fast that human veto is physically impossible, where the designers knowingly designed the veto away? And please be straight with me about which of my verdicts has to go.
