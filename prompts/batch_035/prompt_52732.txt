After a career in geophysics, I have spent my retirement working through philosophy of language and philosophy of science, and I have arrived at a set of positions concerning truth that I would like audited for consistency. Each of them seems to me independently plausible; my suspicion is that at least two cannot be held together, but I do not know which, and I would rather be shown where the real strain lies than be given a survey of the literature. The set is as follows:

1. Deflationism about truth. The truth predicate's entire function is to underwrite the equivalence instances — it is true that snow is white if and only if snow is white — and thereby to permit endorsements we could not otherwise express, such as that everything in the last IPCC assessment report is true. There is no further property of truth for a theory to uncover.

2. Scientific realism. The empirical success of our mature theories is best explained by their being approximately true, and this is a substantive inference to the best explanation of the same kind as any inference in science. I also hold that approximation comes in degrees: Newtonian gravitation is closer to the truth than, say, Lorentz's ether theory, and that ranking is itself something we have grounds to believe.

3. Tarski's undefinability theorem, as I understand it, shows that a consistent language strong enough for arithmetic cannot define its own truth predicate; truth must be relativized — truth-in-a-language — with the definition framed in a stronger metalanguage. When I first worked through this, it struck me as a discovery about the structure of truth, not a notational fact.

4. Some claims about the future are now neither true nor false. Consider the claim that the global mean sea level in 2300 will be at least one metre above its 2020 level. No amount of further computation performed today determines it; I am inclined to say the claim currently lacks a truth value and acquires one only when the world does.

5. Truth is the aim of inquiry. The methods of science — controls, replication, pre-registration, and the rest — are justified by their tendency to deliver true beliefs, and I would resist any philosophy of science on which truth drops out of the story of why we proceed as we do.

6. There is a single truth concept operating across all domains: the truth of the prime number theorem is the same property as the truth of the ideal gas law, and truth in mathematics and truth in physics mark differences of subject matter, not different kinds of truth.

What I would like from you is this: identify which pairs among these commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious. For each, say what the cheapest revision would be and what that revision costs elsewhere in the set. Finally, tell me which single change, if I made exactly one, would clean up the most. Please be specific about which commitments do the pulling and which merely look like they do. I am not attached to any particular member of the set; treat the set as a whole as the thing that must stand or fall together.
