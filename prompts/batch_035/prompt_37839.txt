I'm the organizer of a small, funded working group on moral change — two philosophers, one historian, one political scientist, roughly three years. We want to understand how practices like dueling, Chinese foot-binding, and Atlantic slavery went from being defensible within their societies' own moral vocabularies to being seen as intolerable, and we're working from the premise (familiar from Kwame Anthony Appiah's account of moral revolutions) that mere argument rarely suffices: the wrongness of dueling and slavery had been argued for centuries before anything changed, and what seems to have shifted was a society's honor structure — what counted as a marker of standing flipped into a marker of shame, with material interest following status rather than leading it. We'd like your help setting the research agenda.

Which open questions in this area should our group prioritize, and in what order? Please defend the ordering rather than just surveying it. Among the candidates we've been arguing about internally: (1) the selection problem — every famous case is a success, so the mechanism we extract may be an artifact of retrospective storytelling; how do we construct a comparison class of practices that were loudly condemned and did not die, and can that even be done without smuggling in hindsight? (2) the construct validity worry — that "honor" does no real explanatory work, since any change in status can be redescribed as interest, so how would we tell an honor-driven revolution from an ordinary prestige scramble or a shift in factor prices? (3) the mechanism's generality and predictive use — whether anything in the account lets us say which currently contested practices (industrial animal farming, carceral punishment, the assignment of citizenship by birth) are near a tipping point, or whether it is only ever retrospective; and (4) whether argument is ever causally load-bearing, given the standard story that dueling died not because it was condemned as wrong but because it became ridiculous. For each question you rank highly, tell us what makes it upstream of the others, whether it's tractable for a four-person team with no experiments and a small number of historical cases, and what evidence we could realistically produce in three years that would move it up or down your list. We'd also like to know which of these you'd deliberately deprioritize, and why — including if you think the whole honor framing is a dead end and something else should replace question (2) at the top.
