I've been reading Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert, the 1758 one where he tries to talk Geneva out of the theater d'Alembert had just proposed for the city in his Encyclopédie article, and I've started keeping a scorecard of how he rules on different entertainments, because I can't tell if the rulings add up to a single principle.

Against: the Geneva theater itself — it corrupts a small republic who's citizens should be working, wastes money, turns people into spectators of other people's lives. Molière gets a whole section, especially the Misanthrope, where the honest man Alceste is the joke and the smooth talker comes out on top — Rousseau thinks the play is genius and morally backwards at the same time. Tragedy too, sort of: his complaint there is that we weep for imaginary sufferers and then walk home past real ones feeling virtuous without having done anything. Also the acting profession as such, and he's very worked up about theater handing women social power over men.

For: weirdly, he never calls for closing the Paris theaters — his argument is that they're fine there, a big monarchy's morals are already what they are, it's a small republic that can't afford one. And at the end he gets genuinely excited about public festivals: open-air gatherings with no stage and no performers, because the citizens are the show themselves, the Spartan kind of thing. He loves this one Genevan example of a neighborhood militia drilling in the square with the women watching from the windows, and holds that up as the model spectacle for a republic.

Okay so here's the case I want him to rule on, and I want you to predict the ruling from his track record alone. A small republic, Geneva-like, starts an anual open-air fête: no professionals, no stage, no spectators — everyone who shows up joins the games and the dancing, and the matrons of each quarter organize the pairs. But it's funded by the rich merchant families as a display of civic splendor, and in practice they show up in lavish costumes while the poorer families come in their work clothes, so by mid-afternoon it reads as the rich parading past the poor. What's his verdict? I can feel the pull in both directions from his record but I don't trust myself to pick.

More to the point: what rules actually fit his whole scorecard, what does each one predict about my fête, which prediction do you believe and why, and — the part I actually care about — what further case, described concretely, would do the most to tell those rules apart?
