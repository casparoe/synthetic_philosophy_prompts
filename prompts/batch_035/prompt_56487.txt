At my company we do three things that I've started to suspect pull in different directions, and I'd like help thinking through what each one quietly assumes about responsibility.

First, HR runs an annual resilience workshop built on ideas the facilitator attributes to Stoicism. The core exercise is a version of what Stoics apparently called the dichotomy of control: you list your work stressors, sort them into things you control and things you don't, and you're taught to stop investing emotional energy in the second column. The framing is that your frustration about a slipped release date is your problem to manage, not the release date's.

Second, my performance review scores me heavily on outcomes: revenue attributable to my projects, incidents on my services, delivery against quarterly goals. In practice a lot of those outcomes depend on things I plainly don't control — vendor outages, a reorg that changes my team's scope, another team's migration slipping and taking my launch date with it. The review doesn't distinguish; a missed number is a missed number, with a small box for "context" that reviewers are told not to lean on.

Third, engineering culture here (like most of the industry) uses blameless postmortems: after an incident, the writeup deliberately avoids saying that any individual made a bad call, even when someone did click the button that took down prod, and the remediation items are changes to systems and process rather than to people.

Here's what I want analyzed. What view of responsibility — of what a person is answerable for — does each of these three practices presuppose, if any? The workshop seems to say that only your responses are yours; the review seems to say outcomes are yours regardless of control; the postmortem seems to say that even conduct well within your control isn't treated as yours once the system is big enough. Are these three commitments genuinely inconsistent, or is there a principled way to slot each practice into a different domain (emotional life, compensation, safety engineering) such that no contradiction emerges? For each practice, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with — for instance, maybe the review can be defended without any claim that outcomes track control, say as a betting or incentive story rather than a desert story. Finally, tell me which single change to which practice you'd make first if you wanted the whole package to rest on one coherent theory, and what that theory would be.
