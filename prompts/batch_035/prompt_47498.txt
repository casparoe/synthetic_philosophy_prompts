I have a set of verdicts about when two things that occupy the same place at the same time are one thing and when they are two, and I'd like to know whether any single principle lies behind them.

Goliath, a statue, and Lumpl, the piece of clay it is made of: two things — the clay existed for days before the sculptor shaped it, so they can't be identical. The house at the corner and the brick structure composing it: one thing — I can't bring myself to believe my street hosts twice as many large objects as it appears to. A person and her living body: one thing — if my body steps on your foot, I stepped on your foot. A commemorative amulet and the piece of gold it was cast from: two things, though I admit the case feels structurally identical to the house one. A club and the set of its founding members: two things, obviously. The Mississippi River and the aggregate of water molecules in it at the moment: two things, since the river survives the molecules passing through and being replaced.

And then one I keep circling: after my grandmother's death, I said that her body remained in the room, which sounds like the person and the body were two things after all — but I also unhesitatingly say she was buried in Cincinnati, which seems to require the opposite.

Please tell me whether there is a principle that captures these verdicts, stated precisely enough to decide new cases. If one verdict has to go, say which and why. Then apply the resulting account to two cases I haven't mentioned: whether a written constitution and the printed document it's inscribed in are one thing or two, and whether a tornado and the swirling column of air constituting it are one thing or two.

Lead with the principle (or the statement that no single one works), then give your verdicts on each of my cases under it, and close with the two new cases. Please keep the whole response under 500 words.
