Here's something I keep bumping into in the IRL literature and I want to think it through philosophically. The standard setup: you watch a human act (driving demos, joystick trajectories, clicks, whatever), and you infer the reward function that would make that behavior (near-)optimal. Almost nobody assumes the human is literally optimal though -- instead the default is to model the human as noisy-rational, sometimes called Boltzmann-rational, where the probability of an action goes up exponentially with its value, so worse choices still get made occasionally. And when the demonstrations still look too messy to fit, researchers do one of two things: they bolt on a fancier noise/bias model (the human had cognitive biases, limited planning depth, myopia), or they reframe the whole thing as "reward misspecification" -- a puzzle to be solved by better modeling so that the behavior comes out as evidence of *some* stable preference ordering. What basically never happens is concluding "this person just doesn't have a coherent preference here, stop fitting one."

So my question: what does this whole practice presuppose about human minds? Is it committed to something like: every bit of purposive-looking behavior is evidence of a latent, coherent preference structure that exists independently of our modeling of it? Can a practicing Humean get on board (preferences are just whatever explains behavior, so if behavior looks inconsistent, the preferences just *are* weird), or does the noise-modeling move secretly smuggle in a substantive view -- that inconsistency is noise *around* a signal rather than just what preferences sometimes are? And relatedly, it seems like the practice quietly rules out certain familiar things: akrasia (knowing better and doing worse), adaptive preferences (sour grapes -- the fox "preferring" not to have them only after failing), preferences that are genuinely in transition mid-demo. All of those get reclassified as bias parameters to be estimated and corrected away, so the recovered preference is what the person *would* have with all their biases removed -- which starts sounding less like inference and more like an idealization, an implicit view of the "true self" as the unbiased self.

A few things I'd want an answer to hit: (1) reconstruct the implicit theory of agency as precisely as you can, (2) distinguish what the practice *strictly* presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably -- e.g., maybe Boltzmann-rationality is compatible with a deflationary "as-if" instrumentalism where reward functions are just predictive instruments and nobody's committed to them existing in the head, but the misspecification-fixing response seems to break that reading, no? (3) whether there's a live alternative -- some view of preference on which inconsistency is first-class data rather than noise -- and what the ML practice would concretely have to change to take it seriously (e.g. what happens to the confidence you report in the inferred reward). Bonus if you can say something about whether the same commitments show up in the preference-model side of RLHF, where the human is modeled as a noisy comparator of two trajectories.
