Earlier this week a friend and I spent the better part of an evening trying to fix a boundary between legitimate persuasion and manipulation, and while we never settled it, we did converge on a two-part test that felt more precise than the usual appeals to "deception" or "pressure." A tactic is suspect, we tentatively agreed, to the extent that (a) its effect on the target runs through mechanisms the target would not endorse on reflection — call this the endorsement gap — and (b) its efficacy would diminish if the target simply noticed it being used — call this the exposure sensitivity. My friend insisted the two measures come apart often enough to be scored separately; I suspect they usually travel together.

I would like you to adjudicate this by applying our test to the following tactics. For each one, assign two sub-scores from 0 to 10, one for endorsement gap and one for exposure sensitivity, along with an overall manipulation score, and justify each set of scores in a sentence or two. Wherever the two sub-scores diverge by three points or more, say so explicitly, since those cases are precisely our disagreement. Finally, sort the tactics into three tiers by overall manipulation score, and state in one sentence what the tactics in each tier have in common.

The tactics:

1. A charity appeal describes one named child who needs clean water rather than citing statistics about the number of children without access to it.
2. A salesperson mentions, truthfully, that another customer looked at the same item that morning, in the hope that fear of missing out will close the sale that day.
3. A doctor describes a treatment's benefit as a relative risk reduction without also stating the absolute risk reduction.
4. An automated shopping assistant compliments the user's discernment before recommending a product.
5. A requester poses a series of small questions the target will answer affirmatively before making the larger request they actually care about.
6. A petitioner deliberately times an unwelcome request to coincide with the target's visibly good mood, having done nothing to bring that mood about.
7. A host arranging a dinner seating plan deliberately places a guest who will make a rival's best points sound unattractive, so that the target concludes against the rival while feeling the conclusion is her own.
8. An advocate presents only true facts, but selected so that the target will draw a conclusion those facts do not actually support.
9. An employer offers a choice of benefits packages that includes one option no one would rationally select, placed there so that one of the remaining options looks better by comparison.

Please also address our underlying question directly: do the endorsement gap and exposure sensitivity measures genuinely come apart, or is one of us clinging to a distinction that a single dimension would capture just as well? If they come apart, explain what the divergent cases show about which of us was right.
