Over the years my closest friend from college has accumulated a strikingly consistent set of rulings on when a critic is entitled to blame someone for a wrong — when the critic "has standing," as she puts it — and I've started keeping a list because the pattern is real even though she'd deny she has a theory. Her verdicts so far: her stepfather, who checks his texts at red lights nearly every day and insists that red lights "don't really count," delivered a furious lecture to her cousin about texting while driving, and she said the lecture had no standing, even though she agreed with every word of it on the merits — his own habits disqualified him from being the one to say them. Her uncle, sober for fifteen years after two DUIs in his twenties and the first to say his own record was worse than anyone's, sharply rebuked her brother for driving home tipsy from a wedding, and she insisted the uncle had full standing: the past made the rebuke costlier to deliver, not illegitimate. And her officemate, who eats meat at nearly every meal but maintains that what he does at the grocery store is "a different thing entirely," berated her cousin over photos from a trophy hunt, and she ruled he had no standing either, though she granted the trophy hunt is worse than a factory-farmed steak. In every case she takes care to separate whether the critic is right from whether the critic may be the one saying it, and she always concedes the first while sometimes denying the second.

Three new situations have come up in her circle, and before we discuss them at Thanksgiving I've bet her brother that I can predict her rulings in advance. First: the marketing director at my firm, whose agency runs campaigns for sports-betting apps, has never placed a bet in his life, personally disapproves of gambling, and would condemn his own hypothetical gambling without hesitation — but he has lit into his younger brother for burning through his savings on parlays. Second: a member of my book club gave up gossiping a year ago and has held to it, but she still maintains that everything she used to spread about a former colleague was justified and that she'd do the same again in those circumstances; last week she sharply blamed another member for gossiping about a couple's separation. Third: my neighbor, who freely admits he drives five to seven miles over the limit on the highway the way almost everyone does and considers it mildly wrong, has publicly condemned another neighbor for racing through our school zone.

Predict her verdict on each of the three, and explain the rule she seems to be running: whether standing is lost by sharing the vice (even in diluted form), by contributing to the prevalence of the wrong (as opposed to committing it oneself), by exempting oneself from a standard one applies to others, or something else entirely. Say which of the three cases would best tell those candidate rules apart, give your best guess as to which rule she's actually operating with and why, and describe the one further case — a hypothetical is fine — that would settle the matter for good.
