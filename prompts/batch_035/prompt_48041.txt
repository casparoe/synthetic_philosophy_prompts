I'm a high school junior, and at my school's philosophy club we have a yearly tradition: each member submits a written statement of their real views on a chosen topic, everyone receives a copy a week in advance, and one meeting is spent interrogating it. This year I volunteered to write on how far our duties to others extend. I would like someone to audit my position for consistency before I submit it. Here is the set of commitments as I currently hold them:

1. I am a consequentialist: what makes an action right or wrong is its effects on the welfare of those affected, and I think the right act is the one with the best expected consequences, given the evidence available to me at the time of acting.

2. I accept the cluelessness arguments I have come across: the long-run effects of nearly anything we do are unknowable. Every action slightly alters the timing and course of events, and thereby changes which particular people will later exist; and for almost any act one can tell a serious story on which it prevents vast amounts of good and an equally serious one on which it causes vast harm, with no principled way to assign probabilities to either.

3. Because of (2), I believe our duties extend only to the foreseeable future, and that the rational way to be a consequentialist is to support interventions with solid evidence of near-term benefit. That is why I donate part of my allowance to a charity that treats children for intestinal worms, and why I am unpersuaded by arguments for prioritizing the very long term, such as reducing the risk of human extinction this century — those arguments rest on speculation about effects centuries out, and speculation is exactly what (2) says we cannot do.

4. I believe buying meat from factory farms is seriously wrong, and that this is true of the individual shopper's single purchase, not merely of the collective practice — the fact that my one purchase would not change anything by itself does not excuse me.

5. I believe every eligible citizen has a duty to vote, including in elections where one vote is all but certain not to affect the outcome, and I would defend this duty in the way that starts from what would happen if everyone reasoned that their own vote did not matter.

6. I believe that where two options are genuinely balanced on the evidence — no foreseeable difference in welfare, and equally weighty unknown long-run effects on both sides — it is permissible to choose on personal grounds, for instance by taking the more meaningful career even if no impartial observer could certify it as the most beneficial one.

Individually, each of these feels right to me, and I can defend any one of them in the abstract. But I have started to worry that some of them are held together by habit rather than argument — that the reasoning I rely on for one commitment would, if I applied it consistently, demolish another. I cannot see clearly which pairs are genuinely inconsistent, which merely look inconsistent, or where the problem lies in an argument I am using rather than in the belief it is supposed to support. Before I submit this to the club, please go through the six commitments: identify which pairs or subsets are actually in tension, rank those tensions by severity, distinguish them from any that dissolve once stated carefully, and for each genuine one say what would have to give — which commitment should be revised or dropped, or how the least costly repair would work. I would rather find the cracks myself now than watch the club find them for me.
