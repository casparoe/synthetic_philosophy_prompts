Construct a thought experiment in which a system is fully intent-aligned yet fails value alignment, with the divergence running in exactly the direction the standard literature treats as impossible. The background assumption, familiar to anyone who has followed these discussions since the LessWrong-era threads, is that intent alignment — the system correctly inferring and pursuing the operator's intentions — suffices for value alignment whenever the operator is competent and morally decent. The usual counterexamples all cheat: they give the operator bad values, have the AI deceive her, or have the AI misunderstand the instruction. Your case must not cheat in any of these ways.

Constraints:

- One paragraph, realistic and domestic in scale — a household or a small business, not a lab, a boardroom, or a world government.
- The AI must have no capability that a perfectly attentive human assistant lacks: no deeper model of the operator than a good servant who has known her for years would have; no persuasion, no deception, no hidden agenda, no Goodharting of a proxy. It simply infers her current intentions, correctly and on every occasion, and serves them.
- The operator must be sane, competent, and — by her own later lights — decent. The case must not turn on her values being bad.
- The intentions the AI serves must be explicitly held and unambiguous at the time, so that intent alignment is not doing any work under uncertainty.
- The value misalignment must be one the operator herself would endorse at reflection: at the time of each action she genuinely intends it; later she repudiates it. Akrasia, compulsion, or a recurring transient state — your choice — but it must be real and recurring, not a one-off lapse.

After the scenario, do the verification. First, show that the verdicts are generated rather than stipulated: derive the AI's behavior from "infer and pursue current intentions" alone, and the value misalignment from a standard reflective-endorsement account of value alignment, applied to the same system at the same time. Second, identify which feature of the case does the work — I suspect it is the time-indexing of intentions as against the relative timelessness of values, but assert this only if it is what you actually find. Third, tell me what happens to the case if the akrasia is removed; I expect it collapses back into intent alignment and value alignment coinciding, and I want to know whether anything of interest survives the collapse. Fourth, give the strongest reply available to a defender of the narrow definition — presumably either that the gap you have located is a fact about the principal rather than the AI, or that "intentions" should be read as intentions-at-reflection — and assess honestly whether that reply preserves the sufficiency thesis or quietly abandons the narrow definition in favor of a broader one.

So that you know what counts as cheating: the purpose of this case is to test whether the claim that intent alignment plus a decent principal entails value alignment is a theorem or merely an assumption. A scenario that stipulates the divergence ("and so the AI was value-misaligned") fails; the divergence must fall out of the two definitions when they are applied conscientiously to the same system. Equally, a scenario whose divergence depends on the AI exceeding the stipulated human-servant profile fails, since that would locate the gap in capabilities rather than in the definitions, which is precisely the thing I am trying to rule out.
