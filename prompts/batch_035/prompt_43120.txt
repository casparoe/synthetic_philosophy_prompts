I'm on my school's debate team, and this quarter's resolution concerns whether organized humanitarian intervention is morally justified. In preparing for it I've been reading about karma as a theory of moral desert, and my debate partner has built an argument that, if it works, seems to settle the resolution in the negative — which is exactly why I don't trust it. He lays it out like this:

(1) Karma, understood as a theory of moral desert, holds that a person's condition — illness, poverty, grief, loss — is not brute luck but the ripened consequence of that person's own past actions, in this life or earlier ones. What befalls you is, at bottom, what you have earned.

(2) To say that someone deserves a suffering is to say that the suffering is owed to them as a matter of justice — not merely permitted, but required by desert, the way a sentence is owed to a convicted criminal.

(3) If a suffering is owed to someone as a matter of justice, then preventing that suffering is interfering with justice, and interfering with justice is pro tanto wrong.

(4) So on karma, medicine, famine relief, and even the small everyday compassion of comforting a grieving friend are pro tanto wrong.

(5) But these are among the least deniable goods we know of. Comforting a friend in grief is not even slightly wrong.

(6) Therefore karma, understood as a theory of moral desert, is false.

I'll admit (4) and (5) sit very strangely together, and my working suspicion is that the argument proves too much. When I pushed back at our practice round, our coach offered the standard reply: a defender of karma can say that compassionate action itself generates good karma, so relieving suffering isn't interfering with the ledger — it's adding to it. But I can't tell whether that's a real answer or just a relabeling. If the universe owes the sick their sickness, then my comforting them earns me credit for tampering with what justice required — like collecting a bounty for springing prisoners. Then again, maybe that analogy is itself confused, since on karma there is no court, no statute, and no administrator whose sentence is being frustrated; there's just a causal process, and nobody thinks you "interfere with gravity" by catching a falling vase.

I want to know which premise or step I should reject, and what rejecting it costs. I'd most like to keep (5), and I'd also like to keep (1) if that's possible, because if we weaken (1) too far, karma stops being a theory of moral desert and becomes the mere claim that actions have consequences — something even a thorough skeptic about desert could accept. Please identify the weakest step, explain why it looked fine to us when we first wrote the argument down, and then price the alternatives: what exactly does a defender of karma have to give up to escape the conclusion, and does the coach's reply work, or does it just move the problem somewhere else?
