Hi, so I'm a novelist, been writing literary fiction for about twenty years, and I've recently fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole via a craft problem. I kept noticing that when a reader "misunderstands" one of my characters, I often can't point to any single line in the text they got wrong — it's more like they fixed on a different overall picture of the person, and then every line landed differently under that picture. A friend who did a philosophy degree told me this is basically Davidson's radical interpretation: the field linguist with no shared language and no prior psychology who has to assign beliefs, desires, and meanings to a speaker all at once, with the whole thing held together by the principle of charity. Great, I thought. But then I stumbled on Dennett's intentional stance, which on the surface sounds like the same move — treat the system as a rational agent and predict what it'll do — and yet the vibes are clearly different, and I can't put my finger on why.

So here's what I'd like you to do: compare radical interpretation and the intentional stance properly, not just as two labels for rationalizing. Where do they genuinely converge — is the principle of charity doing the same job as Dennett's rationality assumption, or is one a constraint on interpretation and the other a strategy for prediction? Where do they split — my guess is it's something about Davidson treating interpretation as constitutive of what belief and meaning even are, while Dennett is more of an instrumentalist who's happy to ascribe beliefs to a thermostat without thinking anything deep is being uncovered, but I want you to spell that out and tell me if I've got it backwards. And does the indeterminacy thing matter here — Davidson's line that there's no single fact of the matter about whether someone believes there's a coffee cup on the table or believes that cup-on-table plus some trivial variant, versus Dennett's various indeterminacy claims about whether an information-processing system really has a particular belief at all?

Then please press on the novelist angle, because that's what I actually care about: fictional characters. When a reader interprets Hamlet, is that closer to the field linguist case — some interpretive work is genuinely indeterminate but wrong is still wrong, since the whole creature can't be mostly mistaken about its world, whatever a made-up world amounts to — or closer to taking the intentional stance toward a chess engine, where the ascriptions are useful fictions with nothing deeper underneath? If reader interpretation is constitutive in the Davidsonian way, does that give me a principled distinction between a novel and a prediction machine, or does the instrumentalist reading make characters just very elaborate thermostats we're fond of? I want the comparison to earn its keep, not just restate both theories at me — tell me which of the two actually explains what happens when two readers disagree about my character and each is, in some annoying sense, right. No reading lists please, just the thinking.
