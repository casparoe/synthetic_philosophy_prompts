I've been reading about Good's theorem — the result that an expected-utility maximizer can never be made worse off, in expectation, by acquiring costless information before choosing: whatever you'd learn, you can always do at least as well as before, because you retain the option of ignoring the information. I gather the standard proof leans on the independence/substitution structure of expected utility, in particular on flattening compound gambles into simple ones. Meanwhile, Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory deliberately gives up independence in order to accommodate Allais-style preferences, reweighting outcomes according to how bad they rank within the gamble. Here is what I can't get straight: can Good's theorem fail for a risk-weighted agent, so that she rationally declines free information in some cases? If it can, please walk me through why — identify exactly which step of the expected-utility proof no longer goes through — and then assess whether refusing costless information is really a cost of REU theory, or whether the agent's refusal is defensible as consistent with the global-risk attitude that REU is designed to respect rather than a mere dynamic inconsistency.
