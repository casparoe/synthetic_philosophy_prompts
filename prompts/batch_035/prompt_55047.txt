Two colleagues of mine have been arguing for close to a decade about what utility even is, and they've finally agreed to do a proper adversarial collaboration. I need you to draft the joint statement they'd both sign.

Camp one: a hardline revealed-preference guy. Thinks utility is nothing over and above a representation of choice behavior, doesnt believe there's a quantity in the head worth calling utility, and thinks interpersonal comparisons are basically meaningless.

Camp two: a wellbeing economist. Thinks life-satisfaction surveys and hour-to-hour affect reports pick up a real, roughly measurable quantity, and that this quantity can legitimately override what people actually choose when the two diverge.

The context they're advising on: a ministry deciding whether to run cost-benefit analysis for non-market policies (air quality, a sleep-health program, a loneliness initiative) on wellbeing data instead of money-equivalents. The test case that keeps derailing their meetings: a proposed community program where hardly anyone voluntarily signs up (low revealed demand), participants rate their overall life satisfaction slightly lower after joining, but their moment-to-moment reported affect improves substantially. Every data source points a different direction, and the ministry wants a recommendation.

Produce: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can genuinely sign -- about preferences, choice, measurement, whatever they truly share; (2) the exact residual disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording; (3) the cruxes -- subquestions such that settling one would move at least one of them -- and for each crux, a concrete test, dataset, or argument both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance; and (4) if part of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why. Format as numbered agreed-claims followed by a disagreements table, and keep it to something they could actually circulate.
