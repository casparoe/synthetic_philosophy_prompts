I've been invited to give the keynote at a small two-day workshop — roughly forty attendees — on historical analogy and democratic stability. The audience splits unevenly: just over half are ancient historians of the late Republic who regard the whole modern-analogy industry as intellectually disreputable; the rest are comparative political scientists who work on democratic backsliding and know Rome mostly from secondary summaries and popular nonfiction; and a scattering of program officers from a democracy-support foundation that funds work on institutional resilience. My thesis, in brief: the Republic's collapse is best understood as the failure of an informal-norm layer that regulated aristocratic competition — the unwritten mos maiorum conventions governing iterative office-seeking, tribunician restraint, and the treatment of defeated rivals — with the familiar structural changes (the removal of the Carthaginian check, wealth flowing in from provincial empire, land dislocation, the professionalization of the legions) functioning as load on that layer rather than as independent causes. On my reading, the escalating recourse to the senatus consultum ultimum marks the moment formal-legal instruments began substituting for norms that no longer held. And the only thing worth exporting to modern democracies is the feedback dynamic itself — what happens once a critical faction discovers that violating an unwritten rule pays off and can be countered only by a rival violation — not the surface parallels (polarization, inequality, elite overproduction) that the popular backsliding literature leans on.

Prepare my objection-prep sheet for the question period: roughly eight objections I should actually expect from this specific audience, ordered by how much damage each would do if well delivered. The historians' objections should be phrased the way late-Republic specialists actually argue: evidential caution about a thin and hostile source tradition, periodization gripes about compressing a seventy-year arc from Tiberius Gracchus's tribunate in 133 BCE to Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon in 49 BCE into a single "collapse," and the standing complaint that Republican elections were competitions among aristocrats, not anything that could be called democratic. The political scientists' objections should come in their own idiom: selection on the dependent variable, the unit-of-analysis problem, and the charge that "norms" is a label for unexplained stability rather than an explanation of it. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply I could plausibly deliver, note what I should concede up front, and flag where the reply is soft. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks, give its strongest form, and say honestly whether I have any answer to it. Then be blunt about the single weakest point in my thesis as stated. Finally, tell me where the two halves of the audience will predictably talk past each other, and how I might use that divergence in the room rather than merely get caught between them.
