I teach an upper-level seminar on well-being (14 students, one 75-minute session) and I want an in-class activity that makes the heterogeneity problem for hedonism land as a lived experience rather than a paragraph in a reading. Design it as follows: give me the rules and materials, centered on a deck of short episode cards describing first-person experiences (a runner's high, losing track of time in a difficult proof, the taste of a peach, absorbed conversation, schadenfreude, the satisfied feeling after a burden is lifted, sensory absorption in music, and so on — include at least a dozen, and engineer the deck so that sensory and attitudinal pleasures cross-cut with episodes that are intense but shallow and episodes that are barely felt but deeply wanted). The core mechanic should be a counterexample round: teams must propose a feature that all and only the genuine pleasures share, and the opposing team scores by producing either a card or a plausible invented episode that breaks the feature. Make them feel the pull of three escape routes — circularity ("it's just what's pleasant"), deflationary family resemblance, and the move to desire-satisfaction — and arrange the deck so each escape fails on some card. Then give me a debrief that maps what happened onto the actual philosophical question: whether the failure to find a common thread shows hedonism false, or merely non-reductive, or merely a measurement problem for welfare economics. Include a timeline, a note on what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it, and an honest statement of which part of the problem (e.g., whether the heterogeneity is real or an artifact of introspection) the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply. Please keep the whole design under 700 words.
