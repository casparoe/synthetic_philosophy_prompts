I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and next month I'm on a panel at a media-ethics conference defending a view I half-jokingly call "belief laundering": when a reporter learns her story rested on a retracted paper, or her source burned another reporter, or five experts she showed the draft to split right down the middle, she should lower her confidence in the story even though she can't find a single flaw in her original reasoning -- and even if that leaves her believing p while beleiving her evidence doesn't really support p. So basically I'm taking the conciliationist side of higher-order evidence and I'm fine biting the bullet on epistemic akrasia, at least sometimes. The room will be mostly working journalists, plus two or three epistemologists who actually publish on peer disagreement, and a handful of scientists who think the data speaks for itself. My fear is the epistemologists call akrasia flatly irrational and the scientists say only first-order evidence counts, and the journalists just throw up their hands with "so we can never publish anything?" Prep my Q&A sheet: the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased how that crowd would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. And include the one question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks.
