My best friend and I spent most of last night arguing about eliminative materialism, and we've agreed to stop trading the same seven arguments and instead structure our dispute properly. He has taken up the Churchland line: beliefs and desires are theoretical posits of a folk psychology, a proto-theory that explains and predicts behavior roughly the way phlogiston theory once explained combustion — serviceable for its time, wrong in its ontology, and destined to be replaced by a mature neuroscience whose categories may not even map cleanly onto anything we would call a belief or a desire. I held the realist position associated with Fodor: propositional-attitude psychology is by far our best instrument for predicting and explaining behavior, science does not discard its most successful theory, and the eliminativist owes us the missing alternative — a neurocomputational story that does the explanatory work attitudes now do, which none has delivered. Write the joint statement of our adversarial collaboration. It should contain: a numbered section of claims we can both sign (about folk psychology's genuine predictive and practical success, about what a mature neuroscience would have to accomplish before any elimination could count as progress, and about introspection being fallible while remaining evidentially nonempty); a table of our exact residual disagreements, each phrased so that the side holding it would accept the wording — for instance whether a theory's predictive success is defeasible evidence that its posits are real, whether phlogiston and caloric are the right precedents or misleading ones, and where the burden of proof lies; the cruxes; and, for each crux, the kind of empirical finding, thought experiment, or argument we would both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Finally, tell us candidly whether any part of our year-long argument turns out to be merely verbal, and if so, which part and why.
