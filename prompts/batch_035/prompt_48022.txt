I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. I read Descartes' Meditations last year, then fell into the secondary literature on the cogito, and I've noticed a debate that appears to have been going in circles for a very long time. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on whether Descartes was right.

Here is the dispute as I've reconstructed it. One camp reads the cogito as an inference — thinking is occurring; whatever thinks exists; therefore I exist — and its critics object that the first premise already presupposes the very "I" it was supposed to establish, so the argument begs the question. A second camp, in a tradition running through Hintikka's reading of the cogito as performance rather than inference, answers that no inference is going on at all: an assertion of one's own existence, made while thinking, is self-verifying, so the question-begging charge misfires. A third camp, tracing back to Lichtenberg, holds that both sides overshoot: the meditator is entitled at most to the claim that thinking is happening — it thinks, in roughly the way we say it rains — so the cogito proves the existence of thought, not of a thinker; and the Cartesian rejoinder is that thought without a subject is not even intelligible. Layered on top is a further dispute about what follows even if the cogito succeeds: existence of a thinker only at moments of thinking, or of a persisting thinking substance.

Everyone has had their say for decades, all the moves are on the table, and nobody moves. My amateur hypothesis is that this is two debates run under a single heading: an epistemological question about what the cogito can be used to ground, and a semantic question about whether ascribing a thought to oneself already presupposes a subject — and the parties trade knockdown arguments endlessly because a win on one question doesn't transfer to the other. But I'm not confident. It might instead be a genuine substantive dispute about the ontology of the self, or partly a verbal one about what earns the right to the word "I."

So: diagnose the disagreement. Is it verbal, substantive, hidden-factual, or two different questions wearing one name? Support the diagnosis with what each camp actually claims, not a survey of positions. Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a conceptual distinction, data (for instance, the clinical phenomenon where patients report thoughts occurring in them that they insist are not theirs), a formal model of self-ascription — or else argue that no progress should be expected and why. Since I come at this from building distributed systems, I'd welcome an analogy to how a process's log entries relate to the process itself if it genuinely clarifies the logic, but don't let the analogy do the philosophy's work.
