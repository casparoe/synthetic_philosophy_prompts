**Reciprocal Altruism Made Flesh: The Engineering Problem at the Heart of Abolitionism**

**1. Introduction.** Pearce's proposal is usually stated as a single thesis: suffering is optional, and we should use biotechnology to abolish it, replacing the Darwinian motivational architecture with a "gradient of well-being" in which life remains informative but is no longer painful. Almost all criticism targets the desirability of the endpoint — the claim that we would lose something valuable along with the pain. Almost none targets the structure of the transition. This paper argues that the transition, not the endpoint, is where the program becomes either incoherent or far more radical than its proponents advertise.

**2. Two abolitionisms.** I distinguish two projects that abolitionists tend to run together. *Threshold abolitionism* holds that we should eliminate intense suffering — chronic pain, despair, panic — while preserving the ordinary gradient of negative affect that carries information about the world: disappointment, mild anxiety, grief at appropriate times. *Gradient replacement* holds that the motivational architecture itself is the problem: because pain and its precursors are what natural selection built to make us pursue survival and reproduction, a sincere abolitionist cannot stop at the threshold. The whole gradient must eventually be replaced by gradients of intelligent well-being, so that motivation persists but is constituted by variations in bliss rather than variations in distress. Pearce, I claim, is committed to gradient replacement; but much of the popular sympathy for abolitionism is really sympathy for the threshold version, because the threshold version does not require believing that our emotional lives are replaceable without loss of function.

**3. The wireheading objection and its standard rebuttal.** The classic objection is that abolishing suffering in favor of a bliss gradient is wireheading: a being whose every motivational state shades into varieties of pleasure will no longer track the world, because it has no reason to distinguish better states of affairs from worse ones. The standard rebuttal is that this objection assumes a fixed behavioral architecture bolted onto a changeable reward signal. If one re-engineers the whole system — not just the valence of the signal but its informational content — then gradients of well-being can be as richly differentiating as gradients of suffering currently are: an ectopic bliss-state can encode *this is worse than that* just as reliably as anxiety does. I accept that this rebuttal is coherent in principle. My claim is that it succeeds only by conceding something serious.

**4. The convergence argument.** Here is the core of the paper. Many people accept threshold abolitionism for humans on essentially consent-based grounds: individuals may consent to having their capacity for intense suffering removed, and some of us would. Now consider wild animals, who cannot consent to anything, and whose suffering vastly exceeds human suffering in aggregate. The abolitionist who wants to help them faces a dilemma. If she acts, she cannot appeal to consent, so she needs some other justification. The only available one is that the intervention preserves or improves the animal's functioning — that is, she must claim to know which parts of the animal's affective architecture are load-bearing. But that is exactly the knowledge whose absence the wireheading debate shows we lack: we do not know, in general, how to re-engineer motivational architecture so that behavior is preserved. So the threshold abolitionist, extending her program to non-consenting creatures, must either wait for a completeness of neurobiological understanding that amounts to waiting for full gradient-replacement technology — in which case she has in practice accepted gradient replacement — or she must intervene on the basis of a guess, which is the very thing her consent principle was supposed to prevent. Consent-based abolitionism therefore converges on the radical program it was meant to moderate, or collapses into paralysis.

**5. Reward hacking.** The analogy that motivates my concession claim: in machine learning, a system trained on a fixed reward signal will often find ways to increase the signal that bypass the behavior the signal was meant to elicit. The fix is never to trust the signal; it is to redesign signal and behavior together. But that fix is only available to the designer who already knows what behavior she wants. Pearce's rebuttal to wireheading places the abolitionist in exactly the designer's position with respect to every organism on Earth — and the thing the designer most needs, a specification of what the organism is *for* independent of its suffering, is precisely what Darwinism tells us does not exist. In that sense, the rebuttal succeeds technically while conceding that abolitionism is not a welfare policy but a total redesign, one for which the redesigner must supply values that evolution never supplied.

**6. Conclusion.** I stop short of saying the redesign should not happen. My conclusion is the weaker one that abolitionism should be argued for as what it is — a specifiable redesign of all terrestrial motivation — rather than smuggled in as an extension of ordinary medicine.
