Since my mother died two years ago I have been trying to work out, on my own and without any training in philosophy, whether death is bad for the person who dies. I have read a little here and there, but mostly I have just argued with myself on long walks, and I have now written down six positions I have settled into. I would like someone to check whether they can all be true together.

1. A harm needs a victim who exists when the harm occurs. Nothing can be bad for me at a time when I do not exist, so nothing that happens after my death can be bad for me.
2. Being deprived of things is a genuine harm in its own right. Someone paralyzed in an accident is harmed not only by the pain at the moment of the crash but by everything she will never get to do again; the lost possibilities themselves are part of what makes her worse off.
3. Death can be bad for the person who dies. Dying at twenty-five in an accident is worse for that person than dying at ninety-five after a full life, and what makes it worse is precisely the stretch of good life that was cut off.
4. The dead can be wronged. If I promise my mother to scatter her ashes at sea and then quietly throw them in the rubbish, I have done something to her, not merely done something distasteful in front of the living — the promise was made to her, and she is the one the betrayal concerns.
5. My nonexistence before I was born was not bad for me, and I cannot find any difference between that endless past and the endless nonexistence that will follow my death that would make one of them bad for me and not the other.
6. Even if death turns out to be bad for the person who dies, it is irrational to fear being dead — although it is perfectly rational to fear the process of dying, and to grieve.

Here is my trouble. When I go through the list, some pairs seem to cooperate (2 and 3 feel like the same insight), some seem to be at war (1 seems to attack 3 and possibly 4), and 5 seems to attack 3 all over again from a different direction. But whenever I try to follow any of these threads I end up walking in circles — for instance, in the case of the paralyzed woman it is her future existence that carries the lost possibilities, so maybe I can say the harm of death somehow happens before the dying is complete, but I cannot make that precise without feeling like I am cheating.

Please treat the six positions as a set and tell me: which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, how severe each genuine tension is, and what the cheapest way of resolving each one would be, including what that resolution costs me elsewhere in the list. I am not asking you to pick one belief for me to abandon; I want to understand the structure of the problem well enough to see what my options actually are.
