I'm a PhD student working on nineteenth- and twentieth-century European philosophy, and this term I've been running a reading group on Nietzsche's On the Genealogy of Morality for a mix of graduate students and a couple of faculty members. For our final session I'd like, instead of another interpretive essay, a diagnostic instrument: a decision tree that starts from commitments a reflective person can hold before knowing any of the labels, and ends at a named position on the question the Genealogy is usually taken to raise — what can a causal history of our moral concepts show about their authority?

A few constraints on the construction.

Build the tree from the logical space first, and locate the named positions in it afterward. That is: fix the internal nodes as questions whose answers carve up the space, work out which combinations of answers are possible, and only then place the actual positions into regions of that space, treating each as a position to be located rather than an item on a list to be worked through. Where a combination of answers has no occupant, note it and give a one-sentence diagnosis of why — because the view is incoherent, because it would be unattractive to anyone, or simply because no one has explored it. And where two positions you have been asked to include cannot in principle be separated by any node you can devise, flag that explicitly rather than inventing a spurious question to paper over the gap.

Each internal node should be a concrete case or thought experiment on which the positions genuinely divide, phrased so that someone could answer it without knowing which leaf it leads to. No node may simply ask whether the respondent is a realist, an expressivist, or anything of the sort. Please include, in roughly this dialectical order: a warm-up in which someone learns that their disgust at a food was conditioned by an incident in childhood, and where it is clear that the causal story does nothing to undermine the aversion — the tree has to say why that case is unlike the moral ones, since that disanalogy is where most of the interesting positions live; a case in the spirit of Leiter, in which a person's moral judgments turn out to be predicted by facts about their temperament and formative circumstances, on the analogy of dismissing a verdict on a sauce that tracked the taster's palate rather than anything in the sauce; and Nietzsche's own material deployed as cases rather than as doctrine — the noble evaluation that names its own subjects good and everyone else bad; the inversion by which the powerless come to call their own condition and its causes good and the powerful evil; cruelty turned inward as a guilty conscience; and the ascetic ideal, under which a will that no longer wants any of the world's goods comes to prefer wanting nothing at all to not wanting. Paraphrase all of this; nothing in the instrument should be enclosed in quotation marks, since I don't want it inheriting translation disputes.

Two nodes I specifically want, because I think the standard debates run their subject matter together. The first splits on whether a debunking genealogy must show something epistemically corrupting about the value's formation — that the belief would have been acquired whether or not it was warranted — as opposed to merely something unflattering about its originators; positions that look identical on every other axis come apart here. The second splits on whether abandoning the value is even available to the person whose values are in question — whether some values are identity-forming in a way that makes revision not mistaken but impossible — and the leaves should reflect that this practical question can cut orthogonally to all the epistemic ones. If you think either of these proposed distinctions is confused, say so in the key rather than quietly dropping it.

The leaves must include, somewhere in the tree: the error-theoretic debunker, for whom the genealogy explains away a discourse that purports to represent facts about which there are no facts; the Leiter-style naturalist, on whom the genealogy establishes not that there are no values but that the value of a way of life is relative to the type of person living it, so that a single evaluation can be vindicated for one type and discredited for another; the vindicatory or tracking genealogy in the style of Williams, on which a genealogy that shows a practice answering to the human needs it arose to serve can confirm rather than undermine its authority — you may treat Craig's expressivist reading of the first essay as its own leaf or fold it in here, your call; the non-genetic realist, who holds that origins are strictly irrelevant to authority and greets every genealogy with a shrug; and two readings of Nietzsche on truth, since at some point the tree has to turn on itself — the Clark-style revisionist, on whom Nietzsche abandons the early view that truth-claims are fictions and can therefore debunk morality while keeping the ordinary concept of truth, and the reading on which the will to truth is the last descendant of the ascetic ideal, so that the genealogist's own inquiry is caught in the genealogy and the tree cannot certify itself. Roughly six to eight leaves in all; you may add one or two beyond these if the logical space requires them, but name whatever you add.

Refer to positions by scholar's name alone — Leiter, Clark, Craig, Williams, Reginster if you use him — with no titles, dates, page references, or bibliography. The instrument is meant to be used on someone who has read the Genealogy carefully but none of the secondary literature, so no label may appear anywhere a respondent would encounter it before reaching a leaf.

Finally, the key at the end should carry an epistemic annotation, kept separate from the instrument itself: mark which of your characterizations of these positions would be treated as settled by people who work on this material, and which are genuinely contested. In particular, don't present any one scholar's reconstruction as the consensus view of what Nietzsche held — whether Leiter's naturalism captures Nietzsche's actual method, whether Clark is right that the early view of truth is abandoned, and whether Williams's contrast between vindicatory and destructive genealogies is even the right cut are all live disputes, and I'd like the key to say so where it applies rather than adjudicating silently. If any leaf rests on a reading you would not confidently call standard, say which ones you would hedge.
