I want a thought experiment built to spec on the Judy Benjamin side of things — updating on conditionals — because the stock example is so worn out that models just regurgitate it, and I want a fresh case engineered for a particular job.

The stock example, quickly, so we're self-contained (paraphrasing, don't treat this as gospel): van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin is dropped in an area cut two ways — Red territory vs Blue territory, and Second Company sector vs Headquarters sector — with a flat prior over the four quadrants. Her radio tells her only this: if you're in Red territory, the odds are 3 to 1 that you're in the Headquarters sector. She's learned no proposition outright — just a conditional about what things are like within Red — so plain conditionalization has nothing to grip. The classic puzzle is what the message does to her probability of being in Red territory at all.

For this exercise, take these three update rules as given — I'm stipulating them, so don't worry about matching some author's exact version:

1. Material conditioning: read "if A, then B" as the material conditional, not-A-or-B, and condition on that.
2. Adams-style conditioning: freeze everything outside A; keep the total mass on A fixed; rescale within A so B's odds come out as stated, relative proportions inside A otherwise preserved.
3. Imaging-style update: take the mass sitting on A-and-not-B and slide it onto A-and-B, everything outside A untouched.

Here's the build request. Give me an everyday civilian scenario — a doctor, a mechanic, a sommelier, a hiker, whatever, just not the military-radio one — meeting these specs:

- A prior with at least three cells — A-and-B, A-and-not-B, and not-A — with round numbers you can do in your head.
- The evidence is a restricted odds claim exactly of the Judy Benjamin shape, from a sensible but fallible informant. No oracles, no stipulated omniscience.
- There's a target proposition T that the conditional doesn't explicitly mention, and the three rules must deliver three genuinely different posteriors for T. (With a flat prior some pairs of rules can tie — engineer it so they don't.)
- At least one of the three verdicts should be flatly unacceptable, not just slightly off. I want a reader to recoil.
- The case has to generate the verdicts from the rules, not stipulate them. If you catch yourself smuggling in which rule is right via the scenario's details, start over.

After the scenario, walk through all three updates with actual numbers, and say which rule you think wins and why. If your intuition about the right answer in your own scenario wobbles when you push on it, say so rather than papering over it.
