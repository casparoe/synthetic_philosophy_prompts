An economist friend of mine claims the centipede game refutes a foundational assumption of noncooperative game theory, and his argument looked airtight for about a day after he showed it to me. I'll reconstruct it as he presented it, since I can no longer tell which piece to distrust.

The game: two players alternate moves. At each turn the mover may take or pass. Taking ends the game immediately, with the taker receiving the larger of the two accumulated pots; passing grows both pots, and after a fixed, commonly known number of moves the game ends with a division favoring whoever passed last. The pots grow steeply: if both pass to the end, each receives several times what any intermediate take would yield; but the growth is such that a player who expects her opponent to take on the very next move does better to take first, and at the final decision node taking is strictly better than passing no matter what.

(1) Rationality here is sequential: a rational player, at any node she actually reaches, chooses the move that maximizes her expected payoff given the beliefs she holds at that node.
(2) It is common knowledge that both players are rational in this sense.
(3) At the final node, taking strictly dominates passing, so a rational mover takes.
(4) At the penultimate node, the mover believes (3) — she knows her opponent is rational and can run the same argument — so she expects that passing will be followed by a take that leaves her worse off than taking now; she takes.
(5) The same step, iterated, applies at every node: each player believes the next mover would take, so each takes at the first opportunity.
(6) Therefore the first mover takes on the very first move, and both players leave with the smallest payoffs the game allows, though a few more passes would have made each several times richer.
(7) Now the twist my friend insisted on. The beliefs that drive steps (4) through (5) are beliefs about nodes that (6) says will never be reached. Worse, if one of those nodes were reached, the observed passes would themselves be evidence against (2): the players' own earlier moves would violate the very induction they are all supposed to share. So the argument works only if each player holds, at every node, beliefs about counterfactual play that actually arriving at those nodes would undermine.
(8) My friend concludes that common knowledge of rationality and sequential rationality are jointly unsatisfiable in this game — and indeed in any game of perfect information with more than one move — so the assumption game theory builds on is incoherent wherever agents reason about each other's reasoning. Chess, sequential bargaining, hold-up models: the foundations fail everywhere at once.
(9) He adds, cheerfully, that (6) makes rationality self-defeating in a further sense: two agents known to be rational walk away with the crumbs, while two agents each suspected of even slight sentimentality walk off rich. A theory of rationality that guarantees its adherents the worst jointly available outcome is doing something wrong.

(8) and (9) seem to prove too much — game theorists rely on these foundations daily — but I cannot locate the flaw. Which step do you reject? If you would rather accept (6) and swallow its price, say so and say what the price is. For whatever you give up, I want to know why it looked fine in isolation and what abandoning it costs elsewhere in game theory and in our thinking about agents who model each other. I'd confess a preference: I would rather keep (1) and (2) intact than accept any patch to the belief attributions in (4) through (5), but I don't know whether that preference is affordable. And please tell me whether (8)'s generalization is actually forced: does the problem really infect every perfect-information game, or is there something specific about the centipede's payoff structure doing the work?
