I spent the better part of my career in intensive-care physiology, and since retiring I have been reading my way into the philosophy of medicine. The literature on the definition of death strikes me as oddly unexperimental: almost all of it is argued from the standard cases, where the competing definitions happen to agree, so the disagreements only ever surface in footnotes. An old habit from the laboratory is to change one variable and hold the rest fixed, and that is the kind of thing I would like to see attempted here.

Construct a thought experiment, set in an ordinary intensive-care unit using only technology that exists today, in which the three leading formulations of death — whole-brain death, circulatory death under an irreversibility standard, and circulatory death under a permanence standard — give different verdicts about one and the same patient, at the same time. Your scenario should move through at least two stages, so that the patient passes from one contested state into another (I have in mind such things as extracorporeal perfusion maintaining circulation after catastrophic and confirmed destruction of the entire brain; a patient whose native cardiac function has permanently ceased but whose circulation is carried entirely by an implanted device while consciousness is preserved; or the interval after withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment in a donation-after-circulatory-death protocol, where the question of whether function might resume bears on how the standard is read — but you need not use my examples). The patient should remain a single continuous individual throughout, not three patients in a trench coat.

Constraints: nothing speculative — no machines that do not exist, no stipulated miracles; the verdicts must fall out of the definitions themselves rather than being asserted into the scenario; and no stipulating that a definition "obviously" gives a verdict without showing the reasoning from inside it. If you find that the three standards cannot honestly be pried apart, come as close as you can and tell me exactly where and why the construction strains.

After the scenario, do three things. First, walk through each definition in turn: state its verdict, derive that verdict from the definition's own terms, and identify which single physiological feature of the case is doing the work. Second, tell me which definition comes out least damaged, and be honest about whether the construction merely happened to favor it — I want the design rationale, not advocacy. Third, give the defender of the definition that fares worst their best reply: what would they say to reject the construction rather than accept the verdict? Finally, and this matters to me: tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to fail — which verdict you would reassign if one specific physiological or definitional premise turned out otherwise, and what that premise is.
