I've been going down the rabbit hole of decision problems where expected value doesn't come out as a number at all -- St. Petersburg variants with heavy tails, the Pasadena game where good and bad outcomes both sum to infinity so you can get any "expected value" you want just by rearranging the order of summation. Before I read another proposed resolution, I want the spec these proposals are being graded against, because I suspect half the disagreement in this literature is people using different implicit requirements. So write the desiderata: what an adequate decision theory for cases with undefined or divergent expectations has to deliver. Which verdicts must it give or at least not give (does it have to tell me whether to pay $50 to play Pasadena, or is refusing to have an opinion acceptable, and if so what do I do when I actually have to choose?), which invariances it has to respect (relabeling states, reordering the series, splitting one outcome into two), what it has to preserve of ordinary expected-value reasoning for the well-behaved cases, and what it should say about dominance arguments and about playing chained or repeated copies of the game. Give the case or argument behind each requirement, and flag which ones you think a theory could reasonably refuse to meet -- my own hunch is that "returns a determinate action in every choice" is a prejudice of one camp rather than a genuine desideratum, but push back on me if you think that's wrong. One more thing to consider: a friend claims any fix that works by truncating or discounting the tail somewhere will get embarrassed by a two-envelope-flavored case where the cutoff itself changes what's rational, so include a requirement stress-testing that if you buy it, and say why if you don't. Skip scorecarding the existing proposals against your list, I'll do that part myself once I know what the list should be.
