I teach a second-year ethics course with a unit on moral judgment and its history, and I'm drafting the final exam while our external examiner reviews the paper. Last year's question on the 'man of his time' defence was, frankly, a disaster: students simply recited the standard back-and-forth — someone says we shouldn't judge Jefferson or Churchill by today's standards, someone replies that abolitionists existed then too, done — and the marks didn't discriminate between students who understood the issue and students who had memorised the labels. This year I want a question that forces them to actually do some philosophy.

Here's the structure I'd like you to fill in. The exam question should centre on a composite, fictional figure — call him Havelock Grant — who in the 1910s campaigned for old-age pensions, prison reform, and women's suffrage, and who also publicly endorsed compulsory sterilisation of people he described as 'feeble-minded' and campaigned to exclude non-white immigrants. The university is deciding whether to keep his name on a building, and a committee member has offered the 'man of his time' defence on his behalf. The question must make students (a) distinguish the explanatory use of historical context from its justificatory or excusatory use, (b) confront the fact that the defence proves either too much or too little: if it excuses Grant's eugenics because many contemporaries shared it, then whether abolitionists existed in 1820 or anti-eugenics voices existed in 1915 should matter, and students must say exactly why or why not, and (c) apply their verdict to a forward-looking case: our grandchildren judge us as we judge Grant, and at least one currently mainstream practice — students should choose their own candidate, though industrial animal farming is the obvious one — strikes them as obviously monstrous. Does a student who accepts the defence for Grant have the resources to resist a parallel 'woman of her time' defence offered on her own behalf, or does consistency require her to accept it? The question should be answerable in 75 minutes.

Please give me three things: the exam question itself, as it would appear on the paper, including the Grant case written out as a brief scenario; a model answer of roughly 650 words that resolves the question rather than merely surveying positions — I want to see what a committed, defensible answer looks like, not a balanced 'on the one hand' mush; and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth 4 marks each (total 20), where for each point you name the specific move a top answer must contain, the weaker version that should earn a B but not an A, and a one-line note on how students typically fumble it. One constraint on the marking scheme: the A/B distinction should turn on whether students can state a principled criterion for when prevailing-norm facts mitigate blame — something like a visibility or availability-of-moral-argument condition — rather than merely gesturing at 'context matters'. I'd rather the criterion be one the students have to construct in the exam than anything we covered in lectures, since that's precisely what last year's paper failed to test.
