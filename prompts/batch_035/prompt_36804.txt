As a science and ideas journalist, I end up covering a fair number of disagreements — between researchers, between experts and publics, between quantitative modelers and people who distrust the models — and over the past few years I have noticed a particular one-liner migrating from the decision-theory literature into conference Q&As and podcast panels: "rational agents cannot agree to disagree." Last month, at a session on climate-risk modeling, an economist used it to brush off a meteorologist who had just said, to her face, that she did not find his model credible: if they were both rational, he said, persistent disagreement between them was impossible, so one of them was ipso facto not a rational agent, and he knew which one. The audience laughed; the meteorologist was effectively disqualified from the conversation; the panel moved on. I could tell something was off about the move — it felt like the slogan was being made to do more work than the theorem behind it can carry — but I did not have the apparatus to say precisely what was smuggled in.

So I would like the slogan taken apart. I understand that the underlying result, Robert Aumann's 1976 paper "Agreeing to Disagree," is genuinely a theorem: if two people have the same priors, and their posteriors for some event are common knowledge, then the posteriors are equal. But the conversational one-liner clearly trades on a gap between that statement and what listeners hear. Please lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan could be expressing, as a menu I can keep at my desk: for each reading, state it as a proposition, say whether it is a mathematical truth, an empirical hypothesis, or a normative demand, and assess whether it is plausible and, crucially, whether it supports the move my economist made. I have some candidate readings to get you started, and I would like your own additions: (1) the bare theorem itself, about agents with a common prior whose posteriors for an event are common knowledge — but note that in the panel exchange, nothing remotely like common knowledge was established; each party merely heard the other's conclusion once; (2) the stronger claim that whenever two people persist in disagreement, at least one is being irrational, which would license the disqualification — though this seems to require that differences in prior beliefs, in what counts as evidence, and in trust in sources are themselves always irrational, which I suspect is where the real controversy lives; (3) a claim about the direction of inference — that observed persistent disagreement is itself evidence that at least one party lacks a common prior with you, and therefore evidence about whom to trust, without identifying which party is irrational; (4) a claim about process — that rational agents who find themselves disagreeing cannot agree to *stop* discussing, since announcing your posterior and learning the other's is itself an exchange of information that should move you, so a polite truce is strictly irrational; and (5) something weaker and almost sociological: that when experts disagree, this signals the disagreement is located in premises or values rather than in the reasoning, and that the honest response is to identify the premises rather than to argue about the conclusions. I would also like you to address two things my desk dictionary of economics cannot settle for me: whether the common prior assumption is doing all the work in the theorem, such that the slogan is best read as a disguised assumption rather than a conclusion; and whether the theorem says anything at all about the case I actually cover most often, where the disputants disagree not about a probability but about which model of the world is even worth building. Conclude by telling me, plainly, which reading the conference-panel crowd is almost certainly relying on, and what a journalist is entitled to infer from watching two experts disagree — as opposed to what my economist claimed he was entitled to infer.
