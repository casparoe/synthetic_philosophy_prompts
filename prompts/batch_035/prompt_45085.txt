I do demand forecasting for a mid-sized grocery chain — every week we decide how much of each product to stock, and both over- and under-ordering cost real money, so this is a decision we make constantly, not a one-off. The old-timers here say just trust last year's numbers, the data-science hires say the world changes and the past is a biased sample, and since we've never had a rule it gets settled by whoever argues loudest. My understanding is the no-free-lunch theorems and Hume's induction problem are basically the formal versions of this exact argument, but a theorem doesn't tell an analyst what to do on a tuesday morning. So turn it into a procedure: exactly five numbered steps an analyst can run on any forecasting question in under and hour — what to check at each step, what counts as passing, and where the step has to end in a human judgment call because no checkable answer exists. Each step a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion. It needs to cover both ends: cases where we have years of similar history, and cases like a new oat-milk brand with three weeks of sales or a flour spike that started for no reason anyone can name, where the procedure should say something more useful than "flip a coin."
