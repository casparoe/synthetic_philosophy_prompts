I write literary fiction, and one of the first things you learn in a workshop is that a scene doesn't argue — it arranges. Which details you include, which you withhold, whose eyes the reader borrows, what the room smells like: none of that states a thesis, and yet by the end of the paragraph the reader has been moved somewhere specific while feeling they walked there on their own. Lately I've been reading philosophy for pleasure, and I keep recognizing my own trade in it. Someone wants to defend a broad claim — that we are never responsible for anything, that a person could survive having their memories transplanted, that some creature's suffering doesn't count — and instead of arguing, they first tell a tiny story: a machine, an island, a surgeon, a village. The story is unfailingly concrete and loaded, exactly the way a novelist loads a scene. The audience reads it, feels something — pity, revulsion, the certainty that of course one would press the button — and only then does the arguer return to collect: the feeling is presented as the verdict. The general thesis never had to do any work; the framing did it all, and the audience's confidence in the thesis is really confidence in a scene they know full well was invented.

What I can't find a name for is the specific division of labor. The hypothetical carries the entire evidential burden, the subsequent argument only harvests the response, and the response survives even after people concede that the scenario was rigged — rather the way a reader will cry at a death they know is fake. When I've pressed friends on this, they offer words like rhetoric or begging the question, but neither seems right: rhetoric usually aims at inclining you, while this somehow extracts what feels like a considered judgment; and nothing is circular in the strict sense, since the premise (your reaction) and the conclusion (the thesis) are different things. I also want to distinguish the flip side, which may or may not be the same concept: the objector's move of saying that any reaction to a deliberately staged scenario is worth nothing as evidence, because for any reaction the teller could have staged the opposite — the claim, roughly, that the method is self-undermining.

Is there an established concept — or a pair of them — for this arrangement, the one covering the proponent's practice of mining assent from a crafted hypothetical and the other covering the objection that scenes prove nothing about the world they were built in? If philosophers have named and argued about these moves, I'd like to know what the names are, who fights about them, and how the concepts are carved: in particular, whether the reaction is supposed to count as evidence about the actual world, about our concepts, or merely about the storyteller. And if there is no settled term for the division-of-labor version specifically, I would be grateful if you could coin one and mark its edges — I need to distinguish it from a plain analogy, from a merely vivid illustration of an independently established point, and from the ordinary novelist's business of making readers feel things, which after all makes no claim to be true.
