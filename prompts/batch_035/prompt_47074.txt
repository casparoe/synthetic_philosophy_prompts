I'm teaching an intro ethics unit on the doing/allowing distinction and I keep running into students who treat "killing vs letting die" as just the same line drawn twice — the assumption being that killings are doings and lettings-die are allowings, so once you've located one distinction you've located the other. I want a thought experiment that breaks that runthrough in a way they can't hand-wave.

Specs: construct a case where an agent kills someone *without* doing anything, or does something that clearly counts as a doing yet is a letting-die — you pick the direction, but the two distinctions have to come apart cleanly, not just blur. Constraints: realistic medical setting (a hospital, a field clinic, a pandemic triage tent — no demons, no perfect computers, no sci-fi organs), one paragraph for the scenario itself, and critically the mechanism can't be the standard "switch off the ventilator" move, since that device is exactly what my students already have canned answers for. I want something structurally different — e.g. a setup where keeping the patient alive requires continuous positive action from the agent so that inaction itself produces the death, or some other contrivance that genuinely crosses the two distinctions instead of running them in parallel.

After the scenario, three things. First, defend the classifications: say which side of each distinction the agent's behavior falls on and argue for it — I'll notice if you just stipulate "this is a killing" without showing why the taxonomy puts it there. Second, walk through what this does to the popular hybrid view that killing is worse than letting die *because* killing is a doing: can a defender keep that rationale here, or are they forced to either bite a bullet or quietly redefine "doing" mid-argument (and if the latter, what stops the redefinition from being ad hoc)? Third, tell me honestly which single feature of the case is doing all the work, and what happens to the crossing if you delete that feature.

One more constraint: don't build the case by simply making the letting-die variant nicer than the killing variant (extra suffering, bad motives, etc.) — it has to survive the bare-difference treatment, where you equalize everything except the do/allow structure itself. If you can't meet that while keeping it realistic, say so and explain which constraint you'd relax and why.
