I'm about to begin a two-year postdoc on moral emotions, with a background in social psychology and a solid working knowledge of ancient Greek philosophy, and I have to choose now which open questions in the guilt-versus-shame debate are actually worth my time. To make the choice concrete, here is how the terrain looks to me — though part of what I want from you is a verdict on whether I've carved it up correctly, and I'd welcome the claim that the most important question is one I haven't listed.

First, there's the question of how the distinction itself should be drawn. The dominant psychological tradition, running through June Tangney's work on shame-proneness and guilt-proneness, separates the two emotions by target: guilt is about the act (roughly, the thought that one did a bad thing) and motivates repair and apology, while shame is about the self (the thought that one is bad) and motivates hiding, withdrawal, and defensiveness — which is why that tradition treats guilt as the moral workhorse and shame as mostly corrosive. Bernard Williams, in Shame and Necessity, draws the line somewhere else entirely: shame is fundamentally the emotion of being seen — by a real or internalized observer whose view of one matters — and on his account it can register failures of character that guilt, focused on specific acts and rules, cannot reach. He also thought the Greek shame culture label, coined in Dodds's era and then applied wholesale to Japan by Ruth Benedict, was a slander on the Greeks: their shame was not mere conformity to external opinion. So I'd face an early fork: adopt the psychological taxonomy, adopt something like Williams's, or argue the two are tracking different cuts of the same phenomenon and try to say how they interlock.

Second, there's the causal claim the practical interest rests on: that guilt produces amends-making while shame produces concealment and passivity. The supporting evidence comes largely from self-report and questionnaire studies of emotional style, and I'm not sure how much of it would survive experimental designs that actually induce the emotions and observe behavior — or whether the asymmetry holds for vicarious shame and guilt about others' conduct, which is where most real moral-emotional life outside the lab seems to happen.

Third, the question of whether a culture can choose its balance of these emotions. Benedict's guilt-culture/shame-culture dichotomy is discredited as ethnography, but some weakened version keeps resurfacing — in comparative criminology, in talk about the role of apology and confession in East Asian versus Scandinavian penal systems, in debates over reintegrative shaming as an alternative to incarceration of the sort John Braithwaite proposed. Whether anything in that space is a genuine cultural parameter that policy can shift, as opposed to a durable disposition that policy merely reflects, seems both unresolved and upstream of a lot of applied work.

Fourth, the normative question of which emotion a liberal state ought to cultivate, if either. There's a real tension here: the psychological evidence that shame is comparatively destructive sits alongside the Williams-style thought that shame is the only emotion that reaches character and self-respect, and alongside the observable fact that states and publics keep reaching for shaming anyway — most visibly in online pile-ons, where the practice looks like Braithwaite's reintegrative shaming with the reintegration surgically removed.

What I want from you is a defended agenda, not a survey. Commit to a ranking of these questions — including, if you think it belongs, one I haven't listed — and argue for it using explicit criteria: which question is upstream of which, so that settling it first would change how the others are even posed; which are tractable with my methods and background, as opposed to belonging to neuroscientists or anthropologists; which are already crowded and which are neglected; and which, however interesting, could be deferred for two years without much loss. I care more about the reasoning than the list, and I want you to take definite positions rather than laying out options for me to choose among: tell me what I should work on first, what you'd cut, and what development in the next few years would make you revise the ranking.
