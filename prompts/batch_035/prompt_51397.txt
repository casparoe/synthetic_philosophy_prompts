I'm a philosophy PhD student, and my comprehensive exam next month requires me to defend a written position on objective chance in front of a committee that includes a very sharp Humean about laws and an equally sharp anti-Humean. I've drafted my commitments and want an audit before they take me apart. Act as a professional philosopher of probability who has supervised many dissertations in this area; think carefully, and begin by brainstorming how the commitments might interact with one another rather than going down the list in order.

My position is:

(1) Humeanism about laws: the laws, including the probabilistic ones, supervene on the total mosaic of particular facts — they are whatever best systematizes that mosaic.
(2) Realism about chance: when I say the chance of this atom decaying within the hour is 0.3, I am stating a fully objective, mind-independent fact, not reporting a credence or a convention.
(3) The Principal Principle: if I learn that the objective chance of an outcome is p, and my other information is admissible, then my credence in that outcome should be p.
(4) Undermining futures are possible: because best-system chances are fixed by the whole of history, there can be a possible course of events that has some nonzero chance now, but which, were it to unfold, would be part of a global pattern whose best system assigns different chances to present events.
(5) Regularity: rational credence should never be 0 or 1 on any proposition that could turn out either way.

A friend in the program read the draft, said there is a famous clash hiding in it that is fatal to one of these five, and then refused to elaborate — which was annoying, but also convenient, since the exam is supposed to be my own work. Please audit the set: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which merely appear to be, and rank the tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say exactly what would have to give and what abandoning it costs me.
