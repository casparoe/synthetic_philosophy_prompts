At my school, and at every school I've heard of, there's a rule that if someone damages the lockers or leaves a mess in the changing rooms and nobody owns up, the whole form gets detention, or the whole form loses a privilege like a trip. Teachers openly say this is to pressure the culprit into confessing and to make everyone police each other. Yet the very same teachers would never suspend one student for something another student did, and when they punish someone directly they always insist on finding out exactly who did it — they'd think it was outrageous to punish a student who provably did nothing wrong. So the school seems to run on two inconsistent ideas: sometimes only the guilty may be punished, and sometimes the innocent are punished on purpose as a tool. What I'd like to know is what theory of punishment each half of this practice presupposes. Does collective punishment only make sense if punishment is justified by its consequences (deterring future locker damage, extracting confessions), and does individual desert-based punishment only make sense on a retributivist view where the guilty deserve it and the innocent never do? Or is that too quick — could a consistent consequentialist defend both practices, or a consistent retributivist tolerate collective punishment in some limited way? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of punishment my school seems to be operating with, say which parts of the practice strictly require which commitments and which parts are compatible with several different theories, and explain whether the apparent inconsistency is real or whether some single underlying view could license both. Answer in British English, and please write in fairly plain language — I'm in year 12 and haven't studied philosophy.
