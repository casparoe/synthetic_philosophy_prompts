You are an expert in probability theory and the philosophy of decision theory, with a gift for spotting exactly where a clever-looking chain of reasoning quietly changes its terms. I am a high school senior who reads about paradoxes for fun, and I need your help finding a flaw in an argument my cousin insists is airtight. Please think very carefully before answering — this deserves slow reasoning, not reflexes.

The background, in case it matters: there are two envelopes, one contains twice as much money as the other, and after you pick one you are offered the chance to switch. There is a famous bad argument that you should switch: if your envelope contains A, the other contains either 2A or A/2, each with probability 1/2, so the expected value of switching is (1/2)(2A) + (1/2)(A/2) = 5A/4, which exceeds A. (By symmetry you could then switch back, which is why it is famous.) My cousin, a statistics graduate student, says he knows precisely what is wrong with that argument, and his repair proves something much stronger that I am convinced cannot be right. His argument:

(1) The 5A/4 argument's only mistake is using the single symbol A for "the amount in my envelope" while that amount ranges over two different scenarios, as if it had one fixed value across both.

(2) The correct description conditions on S, the amount in the smaller envelope: your envelope holds either S or 2S, each with probability 1/2.

(3) On that description, staying and switching both have expected value (1/2)(S) + (1/2)(2S) = 3S/2, so for every possible value of S, the expected gain from switching is exactly zero.

(4) Since the expected gain is zero at every possible value of S, switching has no value anywhere in the game, however you describe it — including after you open your envelope and see what is inside.

(5) Therefore no strategy whatsoever, even one that looks inside the envelope before deciding, can profit from switching; any apparent profit is just the 5A/4 fallacy dressed up in new clothes.

Here is my problem. Suppose the host makes one announcement before you choose: the smaller envelope contains at least $10. Now you open your envelope and see $11. If yours were the larger envelope, the smaller would contain $5.50, which the announcement rules out. So yours is the smaller envelope, the other holds $22, and switching is guaranteed to double your money. That is a strategy that looks inside the envelope and profits from switching — exactly what step (5) forbids. Worse for my cousin, his own repair still works perfectly in this announced version of the game: steps (2) and (3) go through unchanged. So I cannot dismiss my counterexample by saying it changes the game in a way that breaks his repair.

I want to keep (2) and (3) — the 3S/2 computation looks airtight to me — and I am certain (5) is false, which leaves (1) or (4), but I cannot see what is false about either. First, whether my counterexample can somehow be dismissed as not really being the two-envelope game. Second, whether the 5A/4 computation and the 3S/2 computation are genuinely incompatible, or whether they are computing different quantities. Third, and most importantly, how it can possibly be that switching has zero expected gain conditional on each value of S, while having a guaranteed positive gain conditional on seeing $11 — what exactly does step (4) confuse these two kinds of conditioning about?
