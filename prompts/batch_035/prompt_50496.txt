I'm in a college seminar on ethics that has spent the past two weeks on aggregation problems, and this week we discussed the famous thought experiment pitting a barely-felt annoyance — a speck of dust in the eye, gone in an instant — occurring in an astronomically large number of people against fifty years of torture inflicted on a single person. I came down on the side of the specks, and in the discussion I leaned on an analogy that felt powerful at the time but that I now can't fully defend: aggregation, I argued, works like a bank's balance sheet. Each speck is a tiny debit posted to a separate account; no account holder is ever meaningfully worse off; and yet the institution's total position is what matters for decision-making. A bank that ignored millions of small fees because each was individually negligible would go bust as surely as one that ignored a single catastrophic loss. A classmate pushed back that the analogy cheats at exactly the crucial step: money pooled across accounts is pooled somewhere — the total exists, in a vault, as someone's asset — whereas there is no "central bank of suffering" into which all those little debits are deposited, since each speck is experienced only by the person it lands on, and no one ever feels the sum.

I'd like a proper audit of my analogy. First, a correspondence table: what plays the role of the accounts, the account holders, the debits, the bank's total, and the banker who reads the balance sheet — and I want you to flag which of these have no counterpart at all in the moral case. Second, the breakdowns: which disanalogies actually matter for the use I put the analogy to, namely justifying the choice of torture over specks, as opposed to which are harmless decoration. My classmate's complaint, as I understand it, is that the total on my ledger is experienced by no one, so the analogy works only if one has already assumed an impersonal standpoint from which aggregate suffering is a real quantity — which is precisely the point in dispute — meaning the analogy may be smuggling in its own conclusion. Tell me whether that charge is fair or whether there is some legitimate reading of the balance sheet on which the total does real work. Third, I'd like to know whether my conclusion can survive without the analogy at all, or whether it was carrying the entire argument. And if the bank picture is beyond repair, propose a better comparison: some situation where many small bads distributed across separate persons plausibly do add up to something worse than a single concentrated bad, and where we can explain why without appealing to any ledger.
