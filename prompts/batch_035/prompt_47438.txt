"Explain why already-known ('old') evidence appears to be a problem for Bayesian confirmation theory, using the general relativity / Mercury perihelion case, and critically assess one proposed solution."

Here is one student's answer, pasted as-is:

"Bayesianism says that evidence E confirms hypothesis H when P(H|E) > P(H), which works out to P(E|H) > P(E). Glymour pointed out in his 1980 book that this makes trouble for old evidence. Take general relativity and Mercury's perihelion: the anomalous advance was known since the 1850s, so by 1915, when Einstein published GR, any agent already knew E, hence P(E) = 1. The problem is that P(E) = 1 whenever E is old — that's just what old evidence means for a Bayesian. And since GR entails the perihelion advance, P(E|H) = 1 as well, so E confirms H to degree zero: the posterior just equals the prior. Yet intuitively the perihelion fit is a big part of why GR beat its Newtonian rivals, so something is off.

The best solution, due to Howson and Urbach, is to notice that there is a piece of genuinely new information in the story: the logical fact that H entails E. The agent didn't know that entailment before H was formulated (she isn't logically omniscient), so she can conditionalize on learning that H entails E, and that can raise P(H) in the ordinary way. Old evidence thereby becomes new evidence, in a roundabout sense. Some people instead go in for a counterfactual approach, pretending you never learned E in the first place, but that seems to me clearly worse — it answers a question about an agent that doesn't exist. Finally, since confirmation is always relative to a background corpus K, and the formalism doesn't fix which K to use, the Bayesian is free to select the background distribution that yields the intuitively correct verdict, so the problem fully dissolves."
