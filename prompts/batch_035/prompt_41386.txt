I serve on the interpretive panel of a small regional museum, and we're finalizing the labels for a new prehistory gallery that opens in four months. The decision before us concerns one display case, and it has split the panel down the middle. The finds come from a local excavation: a pit deposit of about forty polished stone axes, all nearly identical, none showing microscopic use-wear, sealed under a layer of burnt material; in the surrounding settlement deposits, axes of the same type turn up constantly but worn and repaired. A few meters away, a single adult burial contains one of these axes, the only grave good in the cemetery. No writing, no organic remains that speak to us, no continuing tradition we can trace the objects into.

Our lead curator wants the label to describe this as a ritual deposit made by a community with ritual specialists — a "shrine" with a suggested reconstruction drawing of robed figures placing the axes — and wants the burial framed as a person of special standing, maybe a kind of priest. Another panel member argues this is exactly the old archaeological habit of reaching for "ritual" whenever we can't infer a function, and that we should say only what we can strictly establish: that unused tools were deliberately buried together, and leave it at that. A third position, which I find myself partly drawn to, is that the two options aren't symmetric: the "we only describe the deposit" label quietly smuggles in its own interpretation, since calling the objects "unused tools" still reads them through a tool paradigm rather than considering that they may have been made as something else from the start. What we're really wrestling with, though nobody on the panel puts it this way, is what kind of inference from objects to beliefs and institutions is legitimate at all — how much weight analogy from better-documented societies can bear, what a deliberate deposit can and cannot tell us about the people who made it, and whether positing an institution like a specialist class ever earns its keep versus explaining the same pattern with fewer posits.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate to the panel before our next meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action on the label text, the reconstruction drawing, and how to handle the burial — something specific enough that we could vote on it line by line — and it should give the reasoning in terms the panel can act on, not just philosophy-of-archaeology vocabulary. It must engage the strongest version of the side it rejects, not a caricature: if you side with the ritual reading, take seriously the worry that we'd be projecting institutions onto patternless ambiguity; if you side with restraint, take seriously the point that refusing to interpret is itself an interpretation. Where the memo relies on a judgment call rather than something that follows, flag it as such, and close with a brief note stating how confident the memo is in its central recommendation and which specific step in its reasoning it is least sure of.
