I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of cognitive science — sixteen students, one 90-minute session on Dennett's stances and whether it makes sense to attribute beliefs to AI systems. The trouble is that my students can recite the intentional/design/physical stance distinction and still treat the question as settled either way: either the chatbot "really believes" things or it's all quotation marks. I want them to discover, through play, that stance-talk is first a prediction strategy, and only afterwards a metaphysics.

Design an in-class prediction game along these lines (improve them as you see fit). Three students, briefed beforehand, will play "the system" in a back room: they respond to prompts by following a hidden rulebook you should draft for me, with some genuinely mechanical rules (fixed answers for certain input classes, an error condition, an indeterminate coin-flip case) and some that are strategy-like (give the answer the evaluator seems to want; express more confidence the more uncertain the rulebook is). The other students are split into predictor teams with asymmetric information: one team gets the rulebook minus its strategy clauses, one team gets only transcripts of the system's past answers plus the right to submit follow-up questions, and one team gets both, but must pay a point cost every time it consults the transcripts — the point is to make each team feel the tradeoff between treating the system as a mechanism and treating it as an agent with beliefs and goals. Teams earn points for correctly predicting outputs to novel inputs; you supply the inputs, the scoring, and a way to run the whole thing with index cards and a whiteboard rather than any actual software. Somewhere in the middle there should be a round where the strategy clauses dominate — where the system's answer tracks the evaluator's apparent preference rather than anything truth-like — so that the team relying on belief-attribution either wins big or is led astray, and the class can argue about which happened and why.

Then give me the debrief: which moments in the game map onto which stance, where intentional-stance attribution earned its keep even though everyone knew it was a roomful of students with a rulebook, and how to handle the student who will insist that the sycophancy round proves the system "really wanted approval." Also include what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the confederates breaking character, one team sandbagging, timing — and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: the real epistemic opacity of trained systems, where nobody has the rulebook at all, and the facilitator must supply that by fiat.
