Third-year PhD student in org sociology here, and I keep getting assigned to teach the "success studies" unit — the airport-book genre where somebody takes a sample of companies that already won (Good to Great, Built to Last, that whole lineage), interviews them, finds some shared traits, and prescribes those traits to everyone else as the recipe. Everyone in my cohort knows the standard critique — selection on the dependent variable, survivorship bias, the halo effect, regression to the mean — but what I actually want to articulate for my students is the view of explanation the whole genre quietly rests on. The thing that bugs me most is how, inside these books, the future looks inevitable from somewhere in the past: every ex ante gamble gets retold as foresight, every lucky break as strategy. Hindsight, narrative fallacy, whatever you want to call it — the sense that once you know the ending, the path to it stops looking contingent.

So here's my ask: what philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Give me exactly five numbered commitments, each one or two sentences, no introduction and no conclusion. For each one, say in the same breath whether the genre strictly needs it — i.e., the books couldn't exist in anything like their current form without it — or whether it merely sits comforable with it and some rival view would license the same recommendations. I'd rather get the in-the-weeds ones that actually do the work (retrospective determinism, whatever happens to the availability of alternative futures once the outcome is fixed, whether "traits" are even stable enough to be causes here) and not just "they assume correlation is causation," which my students will have heard a million times already.
