I am the teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on theories of distributive justice, and I want to build a one-page decision tree that sorts students into the position on equality they actually hold, rather than the one they can name. The course has covered the luck egalitarian family and its critics, so the leaves should include at least the following: brute-luck egalitarianism of resources built on a hypothetical insurance market in the manner of Dworkin; equality of opportunity for welfare in the manner of Arneson; equality of access to advantage in the manner of Cohen; plain welfare egalitarianism; and, as exit positions rather than variants, relational or democratic egalitarianism in the manner of Anderson and Scheffler, prioritarianism in the manner of Parfit, sufficientarianism in the manner of Frankfurt, and a strict desert-based view on which entitlements track contribution or effort alone. A reader who ends at an exit leaf should be able to see which luck egalitarian view they came closest to before leaving.

Please construct the tree from the logical space first, not from the list of names. That is, begin with the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space, and only afterwards note where each named thinker sits. Every internal node must be a concrete case or a comparison between cases, phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing which position it leads to; nothing of the form do you believe in equality of resources versus welfare. The tree must route through, at minimum, these decision points, which you should render vividly rather than abstractly:

1. The auction on a desert island: two shipwreck survivors divide all the island's resources by bidding with equal clamshell currency under an envy test, and the question of whether any remaining inequality after that procedure requires further correction.
2. The surfer: someone who could work full time deliberately surfs a few hours a day and lives frugally, while a neighbor works long hours at a job she dislikes; whether, and in what units, the state should even things out between them.
3. Expensive tastes: one person cultivates, knowingly and over years, a taste for champagne and caviar; another, through no cultivation of her own, cannot feel satisfaction with anything less than the finest food. Whether either has a claim on others, and whether for the same reason.
4. Chosen risk versus birthright: a skier disabled after ignoring posted avalanche warnings, contrasted with someone born with the identical injury. Whether the correction owed tracks the source of the disadvantage, its foreseeability, or only its severity.
5. The metric: two people with identical resources, one of whom is content and one of whom finds nothing in life satisfying; whether the fact that they are equally resourced settles the question of their equality.
6. The negligent motorist stranded in a snowstorm of his own making: whether the state's rescue should be conditional, whether the rescuers may treat him as the author of his plight, and whether charity administered with that attitude degrades the recipient.
7. The ascetic: someone whose religious commitments lead her to fast into ill health, and whose resulting need for care is predictable but bound up with what she regards as her salvation.
8. Below the threshold: two people both badly off, where the only possible transfer goes from the somewhat less poor of the two to the much poorer, and whether the absolute gap between them or their position relative to everyone else determines whether the transfer is required.

Where the combinations of answers leave cells unoccupied, say so explicitly and diagnose the vacancy in a sentence: is the position incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? I would particularly like you to address whether any coherent leaf exists for someone who thinks the insurance market device fixes the right distribution but denies that its results track anything about what people are responsible for, since I suspect that cell is empty for an interesting reason.

Where the tree cannot separate two named leaves without a question that would be unanswerable for someone innocent of the labels, flag the pair rather than inventing a fake separation, and say what additional case would be needed to pull them apart. I expect the Arneson and Cohen leaves to be hard to distinguish, and I would rather the tree admit this than smuggle in a question only a reader of the primary literature could parse.

Constraints: keep the tree to eight decision points and at most ten leaves; give a one-sentence rationale after each node explaining what the case is engineered to reveal; close with a scoring key that maps complete answer paths to leaves and, importantly, gives instructions for handling students whose paths crisscross the tree — those students are exactly the ones the discussion section is for, so the key should tell me how to describe their view to them in terms of the tension between their own answers, not in terms of the thinker they half remember. Assume no library access and no citations: the cases and the reasoning have to carry everything.
