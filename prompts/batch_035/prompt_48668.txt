My friend is really into Dennett's old Atlantic essay on counterfeit people and keeps using it in arguments. The idea, roughly: counterfeiting money is a felony not because any one fake bill is so harmful, but because it works by eroding trust in the medium itself, and now AI lets anyone counterfeit *people* — bots that pass as humans in digital environments — so we should treat fake personas as a comparably serious offense, much worse than ordinary fraud. He leans on this to claim that even a harmless fake persona does something uniquely bad, that a disclosure at the end of the conversation doesn't cure anything, etc. How far does the analogy really hold? Lay out what maps onto what (the currency = our practice of trusting persons? the treasury? the teller's inspection?), then where it breaks down — a counterfeit bill is worth nothing while a chatbot might actually give you a good poem or decent advice, money's value is conventional and rests on keeping fakes out entirely, etc. — and separate the disanalogies that matter for his conclusions from the ones that don't. Does anything survive once those are on the table? And if the analogy is doing more harm than good, is there a better one?
