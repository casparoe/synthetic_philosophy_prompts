I've been trying to sort out, just from my own reactions to specific situations, when something counts as a genuine preference that deserves respect and when it's really a bias I should be working against. I'll lay out my verdicts and hope someone can find the principle behind them, because I can't quite state one myself.

My friend grew up without much money and now, as an adult with a decent income, he genuinely prefers diners and cheap food and feels out of place in fancy restaurants. That seems like it's just his taste, and it would be rude to tell him he's wrong about what he likes. My cousin, on the other hand, grew up in a family that looked down on manual labor, and now she says she'd hate working a trade even though she's good with her hands and it would suit her. That feels like a bias — something implanted in her that she'd be better off shedding. The strange part is that both preferences were formed by their upbringings and both are entirely sincere.

More cases, with my gut verdicts. Someone who will date within their own race only, and says it's just what attracts them: bias, clearly. An amputee athlete who says she wouldn't take a cure if offered, that she's come to like her life as it is: preference, and I feel I owe her respect for it. A person bitten by a dog as a child who avoids all dogs, even gentle ones: bias — the fear is real, but it's overgeneralized. A woman raised in a traditional community who sincerely prefers being a homemaker and says feminism should include her choice: here I flip-flop constantly. Me preferring the music I grew up with to anything new: preference, obviously, and I resent anyone calling it a closed mind. And someone bothered by sharing a bathroom with a trans coworker: bias, I'd say, even if the discomfort is sincerely felt.

My first attempt at a principle was: it's a bias if the person would disavow it given full and accurate information, and a preference otherwise. But this fails almost immediately. My dog-avoiding friend knows full well that most dogs are harmless and doesn't disavow the avoidance. And the amputee presumably wouldn't have chosen amputation in advance, yet I still count her current preference as genuine, so by my own test it shouldn't be. My second attempt — it's a bias if it harms others, a preference if it only concerns oneself — also stumbles, because my cousin's job preference harms no one else but still strikes me as a bias, and I'm not sure "harm" is even the right word for who a person dates or which bathrooms she uses.

So I'd like a principle, or a few candidates, stated precisely enough to apply to new cases. For each candidate, please say which of my verdicts it rests on, where it predicts something I haven't considered, and which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. I'm prepared to be told that some of my verdicts are inconsistent — the pair I'd most suspect is my friend with the diner taste versus my cousin with the trade-job aversion, since I can't articulate what separates them. And please tell me what the best principle says about the homemaker case, since that's the one I most want a stable answer to.
