A bit of background: I'm a retired chemist who fell into philosophy of science after I stopped doing bench work, and my reading group now spends whole meetings labelling research programmes progressive or degenerating, Lakatos-style. Our track record of rulings so far: Newton's programme, progressive; Ptolemaic astronomy with epicycles patched to fit each new observation, degenerating; psychoanalysis, degenerating, since it can accomodate any outcome after the fact; general relativity's treatment of Mercury's perihelion, progressive, even though the anomaly was known for decades before 1915 and Einstein was aware of it; Bohr's 1913 atom, progressive, even though the spectral lines were long known and Bohr had the Balmer formula in view while building the model; string theory, which we refuse to call degenerating despite the shortage of confirmed risky predictions, because nothing in it looks like epicycle patching. Next month we have to rule on a case from my old field: a programme that was first built to fit a batch of data, then turned out to retrodict an old anomaly none of us had even heard of, then made two bold predictions that both failed, with each failure handled by adding a free parameter justified by general symmetry considerations rather than by fudging that particular case, and no risky predictions since. What should we say, if we want to be consistant with ourselves? I have a suspicion our past verdicts fit more than one rule and this case is exactly where the rules come apart -- if so, name the rules, say what each one predicts here, give me your best guess along with your reasons, and tell me what further episode would most cleanly tell the rules apart. Continuous prose please, no headings, bullet points, bold or tables -- write it like an email. And British English if you can; I absorbed quite enough American spellings from the journals for one lifetime.
