I am a PhD student in political economy and the graduate representative on my university's Student Union Council, which controls a modest capital budget of its own. At next month's meeting we vote on a proposal, drafted by two members of the economics faculty, to run a one-year futarchy-style pilot governing roughly ten percent of that budget — enough to fund one mid-sized capital project. The proposal has three parts. First, the Council votes, by ordinary majority, to adopt a mission metric; the current candidate is the year-over-year change in the Union's existing end-of-year satisfaction survey, administered to a random sample of students. Second, each of the three shortlisted projects — a twenty-four-hour study pavilion, an expansion of the gym, and a full refurbishment of the performance space — will be priced in a continuous play-money prediction market open to all students and staff, run through an automated market maker so that thin trading does not prevent prices from existing at all; the market pays out on which project would produce the largest one-year increase in the mission metric if adopted. Third, the Council binds itself in advance to select whichever project the market ranks highest at the close of trading, subject to an escape clause for manifest market failure, which the proposal leaves undefined.

Two amendments will be moved from the floor. One would bar from trading anyone with a financial stake in a vendor likely to bid on the works. It is prompted by the news that an alumnus whose firm would almost certainly bid on the gym expansion has offered to seed the market with a large liquidity subsidy, and that his letter to us leans on the argument that attempted manipulation is a gift to honest traders, who profit by correcting the price. The second amendment would replace the satisfaction survey with a composite of three metrics, on the ground that a single survey is too easy to game by students who trade in the market and then want their position to pay out.

Meanwhile the Council's own sceptics have raised, among others, the following: that a few hundred mostly amateur traders cannot be assumed to beat the Council's own judgement, which is after all informed by professional facilities staff; that the satisfaction survey measures satisfaction only among students who use Union facilities at all, so the metric can be raised by projects that build a small devoted user base while leaving the median student untouched; and that the undefined escape clause poisons the pilot in advance, because rational traders will price in the probability that the Council exercises it.

I have been asked to circulate a written opinion before the meeting, and I want it to be the sort of document the two economists could recognise as a serious engagement with the strongest case for their proposal, and that the sceptics could recognise as more than futurism. Please draft a decision memo of no more than 900 words recommending one of three courses — approve the pilot as drafted, reject it, or approve it with specified conditions — with reasons the proposers and the sceptics could actually respond to, and an honest statement of the strongest consideration on whichever side loses, stated so that a proponent of that side could sign off on the summary of their view. The memo should address the escape clause explicitly rather than ignoring it, and should say whether the amendments fix the problems they are aimed at or merely relocate them. Whatever the reasoning above it, the memo must end with a final line in exactly this form, containing one of the three labels and nothing else: \answer{APPROVE}, \answer{REJECT}, or \answer{APPROVE-WITH-CONDITIONS}.
