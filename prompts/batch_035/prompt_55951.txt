I'm TA-ing an upper-level undergraduate course in philosophy of language, and I'd appreciate a second opinion on one of the exam answers before I finalize grades. The exam question was: "Explain how, on Kripke's view, an identity statement such as 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' can be both necessary and a posteriori, and explain why this is supposed to be a problem for descriptivism about proper names."

The student wrote:

"Kripke argues that names like 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' are rigid designators: in every possible world, they pick out the same object, the planet Venus. Since both names are rigid, 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is true in every possible world if it is true in the actual world, so if it is true at all, it is necessary. But it is not knowable from the meanings of the words alone: its truth was discovered empirically by Babylonian astronomers, so it is a posteriori. That is the necessary a posteriori. Descriptivism, associated with Frege and Russell, holds that a name's reference is fixed by a definite description the speaker associates with it, for example 'the heavenly body that appears first in the evening sky' for 'Hesperus.' Kripke's modal argument against descriptivism is that definite descriptions typically pick out different objects in different possible worlds, so a description-based name would be non-rigid; and since the identity statement is necessary while any statement built from the corresponding descriptions, like 'the evening star is the morning star,' is contingent, descriptivism cannot capture the modal facts. Kripke instead proposed the causal theory of reference, on which a name's reference is transmitted through a historical chain going back to an initial baptism of the object; on this view, once both names are seen to refer to Venus, the identity holds by virtue of meaning, which is why 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is analytic a posteriori. The same reasoning extends to natural kind terms: Kripke and Putnam argue that 'water is H2O' is contingent a posteriori, since Putnam's Twin Earth scenario shows that the watery stuff in our world could have turned out to be XYZ instead."
