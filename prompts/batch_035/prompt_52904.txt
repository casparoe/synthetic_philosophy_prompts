I'm a journalist who writes about economics and the history of ideas, and our weekend magazine is putting together its annual satire issue. I've been assigned the advice-column parody slot, and I've settled on a target I think deserves it: the sometimes smug confidence with which revealed preference talk gets deployed — the idea that whatever you do, you've thereby shown that's what you wanted, so your complaints about your own life are somehow inadmissible evidence. Write me the column: roughly 550 to 700 words, three or four letters plus replies, from an agony aunt I'll call Aunt Utility, who has absorbed the revealed-preference worldview so completely that she cannot be told anything about her readers that their behavior hasn't already told her.

The humor has to come from the economics being right, not garbled, so that an economist laughs rather than winces. Aunt Utility should work with genuinely held doctrine: preferences are ordinal, so she refuses to say a reader is "twice as unhappy" or quantify anyone's welfare at all; interpersonal comparisons of utility are off-limits, so any letter asking whether one person's life is going better than another's gets ruled out of order as a category error; the only admissible data are choices and prices, never introspection, self-report, or regret — she treats a stated desire as noise unless it's been paid for. She should lean on at least one consistency axiom by name (GARP or the strong axiom, or at minimum the money pump) — one letter-writer, say a man who cycles through memberships he never uses, gets briskly diagnosed as either violating revealed-preference axioms or having tastes the columnist declines to call irrational, since de gustibus non est disputandum is the house motto. A letter from someone in a bad relationship, or who keeps hitting snooze, should get the classic move: the snooze button was purchased, therefore preferred, therefore the complaining is a rounding error. Somewhere in the piece she should decline to give actual advice on the grounds that advising is incoherent — if the reader takes her advice, that only reveals a preference for taking advice, which she cannot have caused, since utility functions are what they are.

That last point — the circularity of defining welfare as whatever is revealed by choice — is the thing I most want to land, so please give it to a reader rather than to Aunt Utility: a letter from someone who writes in to point out that the column has never once told her anything she didn't already do, and Aunt Utility's serene reply. Keep the voice deadpan and sincere throughout — she is not a hypocrite, she genuinely believes she is helping, and the irony should stay legible without a single wink. A lay reader should find it funny; a microeconomist should find it fair. Format it as a proper column: a masthead-style tagline for Aunt Utility, the letters in italics or set off clearly, and her replies beneath each.
