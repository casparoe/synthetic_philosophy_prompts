I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of a decade, mostly for the arguments about civilizational fragility and what does or doesn't count as an existential risk. No line recurs in those arguments more often than the Easter Island one, in the form it circulates on forums: the islanders cut down the last tree knowing it was the last, and with it they cut down their own civilization. It's usually traced back to Jared Diamond's popular 2005 book on how societies fail, but by the time it reaches a comment thread it has shed all attribution and all detail, and it's doing a lot of quiet work. Last week someone used it to argue that collapse from ecological overshoot is (a) easy to reach, (b) self-inflicted, and (c) total and irreversible, and therefore that a technological civilization on a closed planet should treat resource exhaustion followed by terminal collapse as the default trajectory. I have a suspicion the slogan supports far less of that than it seems to, but I can't cleanly separate what's being claimed from what's defensible.

So that we're working from the same material, here is what I take to be on the table. The standard story: Polynesian settlers deforested the island in service of agriculture and the moai economy, topsoil eroded, agriculture and deep-water canoe fishing collapsed, and the population crashed into endemic warfare and famine well before Europeans arrived in 1722. The revisionist story: rats that came with the settlers, rather than deliberate felling, did most of the deforestation by preventing palm regeneration; the island also suffered severe droughts during the Little Ice Age; the catastrophic population loss came not from internal ecocide but from Peruvian slave raids in the early 1860s and introduced disease, which reduced the native population to a low of about 111 by 1877; and the islanders themselves remained a functioning society that adapted, rather than a doomed remnant. The wider anti-essentialist view, applied also to the Maya and the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, holds that the word collapse names a transition in political form — the end of dynastic monument-building in the southern Maya lowlands, the unraveling of a palace-centered trade network around 1200 BCE — rather than a demographic or cultural death, so that societies mostly don't fail; they reorganize, and it looks like failure mainly to whoever preferred the old form. And there is the view that collapse can be a rational economizing simplification once returns on complexity go negative, which is not obviously a mistake at all.

What I want is a disambiguation with verdicts, laid out as a menu I could take back to that thread. Identify the distinct precise theses the last-tree line could be expressing: an empirical claim about what physically happened on Rapa Nui; a causal claim that deforestation, whatever drove it, produced a pre-contact societal collapse; a general law that resource overshoot tends to terminate complex societies; an analogy claiming that Earth is materially like a remote island in the relevant respects; and any others you think the line smuggles in. For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, substantive but contested, or on current evidence false. Then the part I actually need: say which of the readings, if any, would support the poster's conclusions that collapse is easy, self-inflicted, and irreversible — including whether the Earth-as-island analogy survives the observation that Rapa Nui's nearest inhabited neighbor was thousands of kilometers away, which is unlike almost any constraint a global civilization faces. Close by saying which reading people who repeat the line probably have in mind, and whether that popular reading survives the revisionist evidence. Please state how confident you are in each verdict, single out the point you are least sure of, and be explicit wherever the honest answer is that the evidence is genuinely unsettled rather than pretending a menu item has been settled.
