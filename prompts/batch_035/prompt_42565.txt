Ok so I'm building a final-unit essay question for my phil of science course, and before I inflict it on students I want a model answer in hand so I know what I'm actually asking for. Here's the assignment: write an essay on whether the principle of indifference is dead, or whether it can be rebuilt in some restriced form that's still worth having.

The essay should work two pressure points, concretely, not just name-drop them. First, Bertrand's chord paradox: several different ways of "randomly" choosing a chord on a circle give different probabilities that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle — I want at least two of the constructions actually derived in the text, with the arithmetic visible. Second, the wine/water paradox, which the essay should state for the reader rather than assume: a glass holds some mixture of wine and water, and all we're told is that the ratio of wine to water lies between 1:1 and 2:1. Apply indifference once directly to the fraction of the glass that is wine, and once to the wine-to-water ratio itself; the two assignments then disagree about the probability that the glass is more than three-fifths wine. Have the essay work that out in the open too, so the reader sees the inconsistency land rather than being told about it.

After that I don't want a survey — I want a verdict. The candidates the essay has to take seriously: (i) the principle is beyond repair and probabilities need some extra-logical source (frequencies, betting behavior, whatever); (ii) the paradoxes just show indifference is incomplete, not wrong — you add a transformation-invariance requirement, in the spirit of Jaynes, so indifference only goes over parameterizations where the problem is symmetric, which arguably settles Bertrand but looks shakier when you run it at wine/water; (iii) a middle line where indifference is demoted from logic to convention, a prior-setting device with no claim to objectivity. Pick one, defend it against the strongest version of the others, and no ending on a "further work is needed" cop-out. A conditional verdict is fine if that's what the argument supports, but it has to be a real verdict.

Two things at the very end, as short standalone bits. First, a paragraph stating how confident the essay is in each of its main claims — rough odds are fine, I just want calibration, not bravado. Second, one sentence flagging the single point the essay is least sure of, the place a sharp grader should push. Don't bury that inside the last paragraph.

Length: whatever the argument needs, roughly 1200-1800 words. And no citations, no reading list, no bibliography — argue it out on the page; I can't have the answer leaning on sources nobody can check.
