I'm writing a spoof piece for my decision theory reading group's little zine (don't ask), and I want it to actually get the math right. The target is a coin-flip gambling game modeled on the one philosophers of decision theory call the Pasadena game — a cousin of the St. Petersburg game where the expectation blows up to infinity. In my version the rules are: a fair coin is flipped until the first heads; if the first heads lands on toss n, the house pays the player (-2)^n / n dollars. So heads on the first toss loses you $2, tails-then-heads wins you $2, heads on toss three loses you $8/3, and so on, each term growing in magnitude but with alternating signs. The expected value is the sum of (-1)^n/n — the alternating harmonic series, which comes out to about minus 69 cents if you add the terms in the natural order (i.e., ordered by which toss they correspond to). The catch, and the whole joke, is that this series is only conditionally convergent: by the rearrangement theorem you can reorder the same outcomes so the "expected value" comes out to anything you like, or diverges. Which means the casino's marketing department can truthfully advertise the identical game as a rip-off, a windfall, or perfectly fair, just by picking a different bookkeeping order for tallying the same terms.

So, the ask: write a promotional brochure for a casino introducing this game at a new table, around 650 words. Structure it as glossy promo copy up top (an excited headline, a paragraph or two of hype about "the first casino game whose fairness is a matter of accounting"), then an FAQ, then numbered clauses of terms and conditions in shrinking fine print. I want the comedy to come from the real mathematics — conditional convergence, rearrangement changing the sum, the contrast with the St. Petersburg table across the floor whose expected value is merely infinite — and not from generic casino jokes, so that someone who knows the decision theory literature laughs instead of winces. Somewhere in there give me at least one quoted statement from a fictional executive, e.g. a VP of Expected Value Communications, who is entirely sincere. And here's where I want you to commit: the house has to actually pick one ordering that it uses for official purposes — payouts, advertising, regulatory filings — and stick with it for the whole brochure, even as other departments quietly use different orderings in their own sections. Don't let the brochure itself notice or apologize for the inconsistency; the reader should spot it, not the copywriter.

After the brochure, add a short note, maybe 4 sentences, on where the piece takes liberties: in particular whether the "just reorder the terms" trick is literally available to a casino, given that the natural ordering falls out of the game itself (outcomes are indexed by toss number), or whether the brochure's move only works as a metaphor for something else — like selectively truncating the series or reweighting which outcomes you mention. Take a definite position on that in the note rather than laying out the possibilities for me to choose between, and same goes for the comedy: make definite choices, no hedging. Also feel free to double-check my arithmetic in the note if any of it is off — if the minus-69-cents figure or the payoff schedule is wrong, say so plainly, I'd rather know than print it.
