I'm a machine learning engineer who spends most of my time on federated learning and model merging: taking clients that fine-tune the same base model on different local data shards and averaging their weights, and worrying about when the merged model beats the ensemble and when it turns to garbage. I recently got into a long argument about Aumann's agreement theorem — the result that two ideal Bayesian agents with a common prior and common knowledge of each other's information cannot disagree — and my instinct was that I already know this stuff: same base model, different shards, merge and you get something that dominates either client alone. But my friend insists the theorem has nothing to do with weight averaging and that I'm flattering myself by analogy. So I want a real translation, not a hand-wave. Restate Aumann's theorem and the debate around it — the common prior assumption, information partitions, the notion of common knowledge, the follow-up result that agents who repeatedly announce posteriors and update on each other eventually converge, and the standard objections that real people have neither a common prior nor common knowledge of each other's rationality — in the vocabulary I actually use: base models, shards, non-IID data, federated averaging, model soups versus ensembles, calibration, logits versus argmax. Where the mapping is loose, say so rather than quietly pretending it's tight.

Then give me the residue report, because I can already see trouble spots. A common prior is at best "fine-tuned from the same base," but base models absorb priors only approximately, and a model's information isn't a partition anyone can point at — it's smeared across billions of opaque weights, so "common knowledge of each other's information structure" seems to have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Is that a real gap in what my field can say, or just a counterpart nobody has bothered to formalize? And the reverse question: is there anything in my world that the philosophers' setup cannot express — for instance, that averaging destroys information that ensembling preserves, or that disagreement between two checkpoints is ill-posed because their outputs aren't credences in any clean sense? I want an honest verdict on whether my friend is right that the analogy is superficial, or whether the theorem is telling my field something about when federated averaging should and shouldn't be expected to work that we haven't internalized.
