So here's my situation. I'm a philosophy professor who's been voluntold to run a two-hour session for our cognitive science program's incoming PhD students — a mix of psych, neuroscience, and a couple of CS kids — on Dennett's heterophenomenology as a method for the science of consciousness. Smart kids, but almost none of them have read any philosophy of mind. The neuro kids arrive thinking introspective reports are just data about the brain, full stop, and the psych kids arrive vaguely insulted on behalf of thier research participants. Don't explain heterophenomenology to me — I've been fighting about it for twenty years and I know what I think. What I want is a plan for the session.

1. Sequencing. Does the method even make sense to this crowd before they've seen what it's a method FOR — do I have to set up the Cartesian theater and the multiple drafts picture first so they can feel the pull of what heterophenomenology is trying to stay neutral between, or can I present it as a plain methodological proposal and save the metaphysics for the second hour? What depends on what, and where's the mistake people make by getting the order wrong?

2. The carrying example. I'm inclined to build the whole two hours around ONE phenomenon rather than hopping around. Color phi? The cutaneous rabbit? Something else entirely? Which one actually does the work of showing why the subject's reports are a text to be interpreted rather than a measurement to be read off, and which one is more likely to backfire on me with this particular audience?

3. Misconceptions. I can predict some — "this is just behaviorism with a fancier name," "Dennett denies consciousness exists so why is the lab next door studying it," maybe "heterophenomenology means we get to ignore what people say." But tell me which ones will actually surface with this crowd, roughly in what order, and where in the sequence I should head each one off rather than letting it fester until Q&A. Also, one or two of the CS kids will almost certainly raise LLM self-reports, and honestly that's not a dumb question — tell me whether to invite that comparison at a specific point or park it, and why.

4. What I should openly refuse to cover at this level — zombies, the Hard Problem, the whole qualia wars — and how to say "not today" without sounding like I'm dodging.

5. One concrete check at the end that would show me they actually got the idea, not just that they can parrot "third-person method" back at me.

Keep in mind these students have to actually deploy something like this method in their own first-year projects, so the session can't be a history lesson.
