I would like to work out what, if anything, my pleasures have in common, and I have been recording my honest reactions to cases in the hope that they add up to some coherent account. Here they are, with my verdicts.

The satisfaction that arrives when a proof I have been stuck on finally clicks into place: a paradigm pleasure — my best days have been made mostly of such moments. The taste of cold watermelon on a hot afternoon: also a paradigm, and it seems to share nothing with the proof except that I wanted both while they were happening. The hour after a migraine lifts: I am inclined to call it pleasure, but on reflection it may be nothing more than the cessation of pain — a relief rather than a pleasure, unless relief is a pleasure. Schadenfreude at a rival's public comeuppance: unmistakably a pleasure in its intensity and vivacity, and yet I resent counting it as one; if someone were summing the pleasures of my life, I would not want it included. The state of being absorbed in a hard climb: clearly good while it lasts, yet I could not report what it felt like except by describing the climbing — I notice its feel only when something interrupts it. Anticipating a holiday weeks before it begins: a pleasure now, before anything good has occurred, though it is hard to say what is pleasant about it beyond picturing something pleasant. The feeling of being listened to by an old friend: a pleasure, but a calm one, less a jolt than the background hum of things going right.

There are two verdicts I am confident about and cannot reconcile. First, I believe the question "which of these two is the greater pleasure?" is well-formed across all the cases: asked to compare the proof-click with the watermelon, I want to answer, even if unsure, and I would answer confidently when comparing the watermelon with the hour after the migraine. Second, I believe the watermelon and the proof-click share nothing at all in the way they feel, and that the resemblance between the watermelon and a good back-scratch is far closer than either's resemblance to anything intellectual. It seems to follow that if two states share nothing, there is nothing for a single common quantity to measure; yet I cannot give up the thought that the comparative question makes sense.

Let me also offer a tentative principle of my own, to be tested rather than adopted: a pleasure is the felt process of some capacity of mine doing its work unhindered — the palate discriminating, the intellect connecting, the imagination representing. This would explain the absorption case nicely, since the feel of the climb would just be the feel of capacities at work, which is why it registers only at the edges; and it would handle the migraine case by counting relief as pleasure, if recovery is the body's capacities resuming their work. But it seems to classify the schadenfreude as a full pleasure — some capacity of mine is working just fine when I savor a rival's downfall — and that is not the verdict I want.

What I would like from you: a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough to have consequences; for each clause, the case above that motivates it; its verdicts on at least two cases I have not listed — say, the pleasure of being moved to tears by music, and the pleasure of vividly imagining an insult delivered to someone who has wronged me; and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts your favored principle cannot accommodate, so that I know what I am being asked to give up. If you conclude that my two confident verdicts cannot both be true, tell me which one to abandon and what the comparative question becomes once I have abandoned it.
