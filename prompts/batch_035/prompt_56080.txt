I'm preparing a position statement on the foundations of expected utility theory for a decision-theory reading group, and I'd like an audit of the package before I circulate it. Here are the commitments I've arrived at:

1. The Dutch book argument is sound in its essentials. If an agent's credences violate the probability calculus, a bookie can offer her a set of bets, each of which she will accept, whose joint effect is a guaranteed loss; that susceptibility to sure loss is precisely what makes the credences irrational, and it is the only thing the argument needs.

2. The money-pump argument for transitivity, by contrast, fails. Pumps work only against agents who choose myopically, one decision at a time. An agent with cyclic preferences who sees the pump coming, or who can commit in advance to refusing further trades, never loses anything — so the sure loss never materializes, and transitivity is left without its argument.

3. I would make the Allais choices myself — taking the sure thing in one pair of options while preferring the riskier gamble in the matched pair — and I don't regard this as a mistake to be explained away.

4. Rationality nonetheless requires maximizing expected utility.

5. Representation theorems are interpretive rather than normative. Given enough consistency in an agent's preferences, they show that there exist a probability function and a utility function relative to which the agent is an EU maximizer; they do not show that an agent ought to be one. This is not a defect to be repaired but the right way to read the results.

6. Incomplete preferences are rationally permissible. An agent who cannot rank two options, and who is neither indifferent between them nor confused, is not on that account irrational.

I'm confident these are each defensible individually — that's why I hold them — but I have a persistent sense that the package leaks somewhere, and I can't localize it. I suspect the two sure-loss arguments (1 and 2) are being treated differently in a way I haven't earned, and I have no clear story about where 4 is supposed to come from given what I say in 3 and 5, but I don't want to assume those are the real problems rather than the salient ones.

Please treat the set as the object. Identify which pairs or larger subsets are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the revision costs elsewhere in the set. I'd also like to know which single change would clean up the most. Two constraints on the audit: don't repair my position by attributing to me views I haven't stated (for instance, I have not endorsed any argument from scoring rules, so don't quietly import one), and don't tell me a tension is resolved by appeal to an argument I'd have to accept on your authority — if a fix requires a substantive premise, name it as such.
