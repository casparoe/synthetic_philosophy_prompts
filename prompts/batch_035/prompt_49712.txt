Write an essay taking a side on futarchy — Robin Hanson's "vote on values, bet on beliefs" scheme where citizens set a national welfare metric by voting, and prediction markets decide which policies will maximize it. Most discussions stop at the obvious worries (rich actors manipulating thin markets, people being bad at specifying what they value in a single number). Go deeper than that. In particular I want you to grapple with the self-referential corner of the design: the metric itself is chosen by the same population whose beliefs the markets are supposed to correct, and voters who are systematically irrational about, say, immigration or drug policy might be equally irrational about whether GDP per capita or life satisfaction or "quality-adjusted national happiness" is the thing they want maximized. If voters can't be trusted to reason about policies, why trust their meta-level reasoning about what the policies are for? Is this fatal, or is it just the ordinary regress problem every political system faces (constitutions are also written by the people the constitution governs)?

You should also deal with what I think is the most interesting practical objection: markets are good at aggregating dispersed info about verifiable near-term outcomes, but futarchy needs them to price long-horizon causal effects of policies on a national metric over decades — long-dated conditional contracts that almost nobody has incentive to trade accurately, where the manipulator's losses only materialize after everyone involved is out of office or dead. Does the arbitrage-against-manipulators argument survive when the payoff horizon is 20+ years?

Weigh both sides honestly, but don't end with a wishy-washy "more research is needed." Defend an actual verdict — whether that's "futarchy is unworkable and here's the deep reason", "it works but only with these guardrails" (be specific about which ones and why they're enough), or something in between like "futarchy is better thought of as an advisory layer than a decision procedure." No citations or reading lists needed, just your reasoning. Length: whatever the argument needs, but roughly essay-length.
