Someone in my ethics reading group keeps deploying 'one thought too many' (she traces it to Bernard Williams's case of the man who can save either his wife or a stranger from drowning, and who supposedly shouldn't even deliberate about it) to shut down any thinking that smells of impartiality. Latest round: I asked whether I was wrong to take my sister's side in a family dispute without first weighing up whose grievance was actually more justified, and she said even posing that question is the thought too many -- as though wondering about it already means I don't really care about my sister. I have the feeling this little phrase is doing several different jobs at once and I'd like to see them pulled apart. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims 'one thought too many' could stand for: psychological ones about what actually goes through a loving or virtuous person's head at the moment of action, ones about what counts as an appropriate ground or explanation, ones about decision procedures versus criteria of rightness, and ones about what the act of deliberating itself does to the relationship or reveals about the agent's character? For each, say whether it's plausible, whether it's trivial or substantive, and whether it actually supports using the phrase the way she did -- to stop the deliberation before it starts rather than to say anything about how it should come out. No headings, bullet points, bold text or tables please; write it as continuous prose, like an email to me.
