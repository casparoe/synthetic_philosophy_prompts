Last week my walking group turned into a philosophy seminar, and I'm out of my depth. Quick background on me: I'm a retired biochemist, 38 years in pharma R&D, mostly enzyme kinetics, and since retiring I've been reading philosophy on the porch — figured after a career of measuring things I'd finally find out what health actually is. Turns out nobody agrees! Anyway. Our friend Margaret, who is 74, still beats everyone at the crossword, but has started losing words mid-sentence — the ususal age stuff. A longevity clinic has now prescribed her donepezil (an Alzheimer's drug) off-label, basically as a memory sharpener, and at dinner my daughter (an ICU nurse) and my old labmate Ray got into it over whether that's medicine or something else:

(1) My daughter said it's enhancement, full stop. Donepezil treats a disease, Alzheimer's; Margaret doesn't have a disease, she has a normal brain aging normally, so the pill isn't therapy any more than EPO in a healthy cyclist is therapy. Medicine treats sickness, it doesn't upgrade the well.

(2) Ray said that criterion proves too much. Cataract surgery, hip replacements, hearing aids — none of those treat a disease in her sense; they just push back on aging, and nobody calls Gran's cataract surgery an enhancement. If treating the biology of aging counts as enhancement, then half of geriatric medicine is.

(3) She said the real difference is restoration: surgery brings Gran back to species-typical function, which is why it counts as therapy. But Margaret isn't below what's typical for a healthy 74-year-old — if anything she's above it. So if Margaret's pill is therapy, the target must be something like a healthy 40-year-old's memory, and at that point you've just defined enhancement for old people as therapy with extra steps.

(4) Ray said every line in medicine is statistical and fuzzy anyway — hypertension is a cutoff on a population curve, cholesterol too — and we treat those without agonizing. So either fuzziness is fine here too, or the whole therapy/enhancement distinction is useless and the only real questions are whether the drug is safe and whether it works.

(5) She said if you throw the distinction out, you can't say why insurance should cover Margaret's memory boost but not a 25-year-old's, and medicine ends up as a branch of cosmetic self-optimization with a stethoscope attached.

I promised them a verdict, but I spent my career measuring reaction rates, where you can at least tell what's being catalyzed — this one I can't referee. Please go through the five moves one by one, rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, then tell me who's right about what overall and whether there's something both of them are missing. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble and no restating my question back at me. American English please. Apologies for typos, I'm typing this on my grandson's ipad.
