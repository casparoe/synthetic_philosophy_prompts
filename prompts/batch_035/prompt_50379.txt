Something I can't stop noticing: insurance contracts and tort law still use the category "act of God" — hurricances, lightning strikes, floods with no human cause. The category does real work: it splits losses into ones where some human might be at fault (and thus sueable, or chargeable a higher premium) and ones where nobody is, and coverage often turns on which side of the line a loss falls. What view of evil — or at least of bad events — does that practice quietly presuppose? The line it draws maps almost exactly onto the theological distinction between moral and natural evil, which is the same line the free will defense leans on to explain why God isn't on the hook for storms. But the insurer doesn't seem to need God at all: "no human agent was causally involved" looks like it does all the actual work, God or no God. So does the doctrine presuppose anything theological, or is it just a secular fault-tracking rule in vestments? And here's the puzzle: theodicies use the moral/natural boundary to defend God's goodness, while insurers use the very same boundary to decide who can be sued — same line, opposite jobs. Reconstruct what the practice actually needs, whether the theological and legal readings come apart in real cases (they seem to — a loss caused by human negligence during a storm, or a "natural" disaster a human could reasonably have prevented or mitigated), say where the practice is underdetermined between rival views, and tell me what an insurer who took the theological reading seriously would have to change about how they write and price policies.
