I am a retired physical chemist who spent most of my career running spectroscopy labs, and over the past few years I have been working through philosophy and linguistics as a late-life education. The debate over whether the language one speaks shapes the way one thinks fascinates me, partly because it has the shape of a scientific controversy — competing hypotheses, ambiguous experiments, and a lot of people talking past each other about what would even count as evidence — and I have found that the standard textbook framing (a 'strong' version, a 'weak' version, everyone nowadays endorsing the weak one) papers over the disagreements that actually do the work.

I would like you to construct a decision tree that sorts a person into a position on this question. The specifications:

1. The tree should start from a single shared commitment, which I propose should be something like: 'Human languages differ in the distinctions they grammatically and lexically encode, and experimental psychology can in principle tell us something about whether this matters for thought.' Anyone who rejects that can be dismissed at the root with a brief note on where they go.

2. Every internal node must be a question answerable from intuitions about concrete cases, phrased so that someone could answer it without knowing which position it leads to. Use cases like the following, or others of the same kind: whether a speaker of a language with two basic words for blue experiences a stronger perceptual boundary between the shades than a speaker with one; whether a community that uses compass-direction terms instead of left/right keeps better track of where objects are; whether someone whose language lacks a convenient way of expressing exact quantities above a small number could nonetheless keep an accurate inventory if their livelihood depended on it; whether describing a bridge with a grammatically feminine noun makes the bridge seem more graceful when the conversation moves to another language later that day. No node may simply ask 'do you believe language determines thought?'

3. At least one node should concern methodology, since this is where I keep losing my grip: when an apparent language effect on, say, color discrimination disappears once subjects are given a concurrent verbal-interference task, does that show the effect is shallow (language merely a lens used at the moment of judgment) or genuinely linguistic (language causally active in the moment)? Please make the tree handle the distinction between effects that are online and effects that are developmental habits, because I suspect many people's 'positions' are really stances on that distinction and nothing else.

4. The leaves should include at least: the strong deterministic view; the moderate 'language as habit of attention' view; a universalist view on which thinking proceeds in a medium independent of any natural language; and a view on which language and thought co-evolve or are too intertwined for the causal question to have a clean answer. Where a leaf is better split, split it, and where your questions cannot separate two named positions — for instance, two different grounds someone might have for expecting cross-linguistic universals — say so explicitly rather than pretending the tree draws the line.

5. Finally, identify at least one combination of node answers that the logical space permits but that no one seems to occupy — for example, holding both that language deeply structures thought and that translation between languages is essentially transparent — and diagnose whether the absence is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

I intend to use the tree on myself, so where a node forces a choice I don't understand, tell me what I would need to know to understand it.
