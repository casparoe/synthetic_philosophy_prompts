Next month I'm presenting to the technical standards committee of an international aquaculture certification body — the group that writes the production standards farms must meet to carry the label. The committee is about a dozen people: mostly aquaculture scientists and fish-health veterinarians who think in terms of auditable performance indicators (growth rates, feed conversion ratios, mortality curves, water-quality parameters), plus one food-safety auditor, plus a philosopher of biology who was hired recently after consumer and NGO pressure. I'm an animal-welfare scientist by training, not a production person, and they know it. My proposal is that the next revision of the standard extend its welfare criteria from finfish to decapod crustaceans, shrimp and prawns above all, with four concrete requirements: a phase-out of eyestalk ablation of breeding females in hatcheries; a cap on stocking density tied to mandatory water-quality triggers; a requirement that animals be rendered insensible by electrical or otherwise validated stunning before killing, replacing the current practice of chilling or immersion in fresh water without stunning; and a set of on-farm welfare indicators that go beyond production metrics (antennal and shell damage, abnormal swimming or surfacing, moribund animals left in the water). My argument leans on the sentience evidence that has accumulated for decapods — nociception, motivational trade-offs, protective behavior, giving up food to avoid a noxious stimulus — and on a precautionary line: where the evidence of sentience is mixed but the cost of wrongly denying it is borne by the animal, the standard should not wait for certainty.

I've sat in on one of their meetings, and the mood toward the proposal is skeptical rather than hostile: they think of themselves as pragmatists who will not put anything in the standard that a small farm in the global South cannot measure, afford, or pass an audit against. Prepare my Q&A sheet accordingly. Give me the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise — not the ones I would get from animal-rights campaigners or from a philosophy department, and not strawmen — ordered by how much damage each could do if badly handled. Phrase each objection the way a production scientist or a committee veterinarian would actually phrase it (about auditability, about the seed-supply consequences of ending ablation, about whether density per se is even the right variable as opposed to management, about capital cost and reliability of stunning equipment in warm-water species, about what "insensible" can even mean for an animal with a decentralized nervous system), and pair each with a two-minute reply plus a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which points I should concede up front rather than fight. And include the one question I am quietly hoping nobody asks, with my best answer in case it comes anyway. The prep sheet should be practical and speakable under time pressure, not an essay, and it should be written in American English throughout. Please do not include a bibliography or reading list; any reply that needs evidence should point to the kind of study or observation involved rather than to specific citations.
