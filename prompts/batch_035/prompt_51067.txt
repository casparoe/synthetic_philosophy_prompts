criminology phd student here, and I need help untangling something that keeps happening in my policing seminar. We're doing a unit on defund/abolition debates, and the Weber line about the state having a "monopoly on legitimate violence" keeps getting invoked, but it seems to do totally different work depending on who's talking. In one discussion last week it got used three ways almost simultaneously:

(1) One classmate, basically a reformist, used it to say that police violence is legitimate insofar as it *is* state violence — the monopoly is what makes it lawful and proper, so the whole question is just how to hold officers accountable *within* the monopoly (better oversight, consent decrees, etc.). The slogan, for her, explained why we reform the police rather than replace them.

(2) An abolitionist classmate treated the same slogan as naming the disease: the concentration of the capacity for violence in one institution is the problem, so the point of abolition is to *break up* the monopoly and disperse legitimate force back into communities (restorative processes, unarmed responders, whatever). Same sentence, opposite conclusion.

(3) A third classmate (sociology background) rolled his eyes at both and said the line is just a definition from the sociology of statehood — a way of marking what counts as a state at all, useful for comparing strong states with failed ones, and neither of them can squeeze anything normative out of a definition.

I'm not even sure these people disagree about the slogan vs. about policing itself. Please take on the role of a political philosopher and think carefully before answering — maybe start with some brainstorming and then organize it. What I want is the distinct precise claims the slogan could actually be making: definitional readings, empirical readings, normative readings, whatever else you can come up with — laid out as a numbered menu from most trivial to most radical. For each reading, say whether it's plausibly true, whether it's trivial or substantive, and, crucially, which of the three uses above it would actually support (e.g., which reading makes my classmate (1)'s move valid, and is that reading one anyone should accept?). Also tell me whether any of the three classmates are making a mistake that the *other* readings would expose — like whether the abolitionist can even use Weber's line for her purposes, since on some readings it arguably says nothing about how much violence a state should have or who should wield it. I want something I can actually bring to seminar, so please be rigorous about which conclusion follows from which reading and which don't.
