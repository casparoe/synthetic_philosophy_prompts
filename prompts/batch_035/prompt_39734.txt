I hold a three-year research fellowship in philosophy, with a doctorate in proof theory and a solid working knowledge of axiomatic choice theory, and I must settle on a single research agenda rather than dabbling. The area that keeps drawing me back is the use of money-pump arguments to establish diachronic requirements on rational preference. The textbook story is that an agent whose preferences form a cycle — it prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A — can be offered a sequence of trades, each one individually acceptable by its own lights, that leaves it back where it started but poorer, and that this vulnerability to exploitation shows cyclic preferences to be irrational. But the story is far more fragile than the textbooks let on. If the agent foresees the whole sequence and reasons by backward induction, it refuses to trade at all; if it can commit in advance to a plan, it never opens itself to exploitation; and if the would-be pump must cover any transaction cost, many cycles become unprofitable to milk. So the argument's force seems to turn on auxiliary assumptions about myopia, foresight and commitment that are themselves the very things in dispute.

Against that background, I would like you to tell me which open questions in this area most deserve three years of my effort, and to defend the ordering. I have a provisional list, which you should feel free to revise, reorder or reject outright. First, whether diachronic norms can be derived from synchronic ones at all, or whether they form a distinct class of requirement needing their own defence — I take this to be upstream of everything else, since if there simply are no diachronic norms, the money-pump literature dissolves rather than gets repaired. Second, whether the familiar ways of blocking the pump — sophisticated choice by backward induction, resolute choice through prior commitment, or merely refusing to trade with a known exploiter — are themselves rationally permissible, or whether each generates pathologies that could serve as indirect pump arguments of a new kind. Third, the over-generation worry: it appears that patient traders can profit from agents with merely incomplete preferences too, since an agent indifferent between two options will sometimes pay a small fee to have the choice made for it, so if exploitability suffices for irrationality, the method would apparently prove that rationality demands complete preferences — a conclusion that strikes me, and many others, as a reductio of the method rather than a discovery, though I would want to know whether that reaction can be made principled. Fourth, how much of the literature survives de-idealisation: whether anything can be said about bounded agents with cognitive costs and limited foresight, or whether these arguments are intrinsically arguments about ideal agents and lose all their force under realistic constraints. Fifth, and most speculatively, whether the money-pump style of argument is a special case of the Dutch-book style of argument for probabilism, such that credences and preferences are being regulated by one unified norm of invulnerability rather than two unrelated ones.

For each question you keep on the list, I want your reasons for its position: how much downstream work in decision theory turns on it, whether it is tractable for someone with my background in logic and formal choice theory rather than in psychology or economics, and what would have to happen over the next few years — a new counterexample, a formal result, a shift in how the debate is framed — for it to move up or down. I also want to know which, if any, of these I should drop entirely because settling them would change nothing downstream, and whether there is a question I have not listed that belongs above all of them. Please write your answer as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — the sort of thing that could stand in a long letter to a colleague.
