I referee for a journal that has been receiving a steady trickle of cliodynamics papers — secular-cycles fits, structural-demographic models, one even a proposed "early-warning index" for state collapse — and my worry is that we reviewers have no shared sense of what such a theory would actually have to deliver before it counts as a theory of history rather than a very elaborate curve-fit. So before I write another review, I want the specification the field should be held to. Please write the desiderata list.

Purpose to fix the standard: a macrohistorical theory good enough to base policy on — say, for a government deciding whether its own society is entering a disintegrative phase a decade out — not merely good enough to organize the past. Requirements I would expect you to consider (add or reject these as you see fit, but say why):

- Out-of-sample prediction, where "the 2020 instability forecast" is the standard showcase — but distinguish genuine prospective tests from retrodictions dressed as forecasts, and specify what would count as a fail given how coarse the predicted quantity is.
- Small-n statistics: what predictive or explanatory bar can even be met when the number of independent agrarian polities with usable data is on the order of tens, cycles take centuries, and every case is a confounder's playground.
- Mechanism versus pattern: whether the theory must identify individually measurable causal processes (elite overproduction, wage stagnation, state fiscal distress) that can be checked case by case, or whether validated cycle-regularity without mechanism discrimination would suffice — and whether those can come apart in this domain.
- Reflexivity: a publicly believed theory of instability is a new input to the system it models, in roughly the way the Lucas critique says macroeconomic policy models are destabilized by being believed. The theory must say something about whether it is self-fulfilling, self-defeating, or neutral with respect to its own publication.
- Comparability across societies and eras: the theory needs operationalizations of "elite," "overproduction," "popular immiseration," and "state capacity" that mean something comparable in Plantagenet England, Qing China, and the contemporary United States.
- Data endogeneity: much of the underlying evidence is itself historical reconstruction shaped by the interests of whoever recorded it; what standard should that impose?

For each requirement, give the motivating case or consideration in a line or two, mark it non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp (dynamical-systems people will weight mechanism differently than the historical sociologists, presumably), and — this is the part I most want — flag any subset of the desiderata you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable. My own hunch is that reflexivity plus policy-grade predictive usefulness may be jointly unreachable, and I'd like to see whether you agree or can defuse it. Close by naming the single requirement that, if enforced, would knock out the most of what currently gets published under the cliodynamics banner. Keep it under 650 words; a compact annotated list is exactly right, and scorecards of Turchin versus the historical sociologists are not — I'll do that part in the review myself.
