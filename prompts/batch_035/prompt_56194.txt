I keep getting into arguments about how to discount the far future — climate mostly, but also infrastructure and public health — and everyone just shouts a number (1.4%? 3%? 5%?) like that settles it. Before anyone argues about the number I want the spec: what does a social discount rate or discount function actually have to get right to be usable in intergenerational cost-benefit analysis? Suspects I want on the list, but thought through, not just listed: no pure time preference, since we're supposed to be impartial between persons regardless of when they live (but then what role, if any, is left for extinction risk — say); time-consistency, so planners can't keep re-running the analysis every decade to procrastinate on mitigation, and whether that rules out the declining-discount-rate idea or just complicates it; sane behavior at the extremes — the present shouldn't be a dictator, but neither should the year 2400; honesty about the one elasticity parameter doing triple duty as risk aversion, intertemporal substitution, and aversion to inequality across time — either accept that or flag it as a real cost; and whether discounting a dollar, a life-year, and an ecosystem are even the same operation. For each requirement give the case or argument behind it, mark which are non-negotiable versus camp preferences, and — the part I most want — tell me whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, because it smells like it (no pure time preference + consistency with observed rates of return + an exponential form?). Then one short paragraph, no more, on how ethical-first and market-rate approaches each do against the list; skip the full scorecard.
