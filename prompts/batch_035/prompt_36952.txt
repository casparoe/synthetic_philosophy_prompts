I take part in a small reading group on Indian epistemology — mostly the Nyāya-sūtra with some later commentary — and last Thursday two members, Priya and Marcus, spent the entire session stuck on tarka. Some background, so the dispute makes sense: in classical Nyāya a pramāṇa is a means of genuine knowledge — a causal route to true cognition — and the school recognizes exactly four: perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna), and testimony (śabda). Tarka is the mind's procedure of testing a proposition by supposing its negation and showing that the supposition lands in absurdity — something like a reductio. The sūtra's list of sixteen categories gives tarka its own slot, distinct from the pramāṇas, and the standard line is that tarka is not itself a pramāṇa but an auxiliary that helps establish vyāpti, the invariable concomitration (e.g. smoke with fire) that licenses an inference. Priya and Marcus could not agree on whether that standard line holds up.

Priya argued that tarka should simply be counted as a fifth pramāṇa. A pramāṇa is whatever produces knowledge, and tarka patently does: when you genuinely see that denying the smoke–fire connection would wreck things you cannot give up, you come out knowing the connection holds — settled, confident, and in fact right. She added that everyone already relies on this: if vyāpti is what licenses inference, and tarka is what settles vyāpti against its counterexamples, then inference is standing on tarka, and it is odd to keep reclassifying a ladder that bears the whole weight as a mere accessory.

Marcus replied that tarka is parasitic through and through. A reductio works only by borrowing premises whose truth was established some other way — by perception, inference, or testimony — so treating tarka as a pramāṇa would make a means of knowledge partly ground itself, which is incoherent. In any case, he said, tarka never shows that something is true; it shows that its negation is untenable given what you already accept. It delivers nothing independent — only the impossibility of rejecting what was, in a sense, already in hand.

Priya came back hard on the parasitism point. Testimony, she said, is parasitic in exactly the same way: the hearer's knowledge depends wholly on the speaker's knowledge, which the speaker acquired through his own pramāṇas, yet śabda is a full pramāṇa for the hearer, because it is an independent causal route for that cognizer. So why isn't tarka the same for me? The materials — my perceptions, my memories, my standing assumptions — may come from elsewhere, but within me, at that moment, the reductio is what actually causes the new cognition that the connection holds; nothing else in me generates it then.

Marcus's last word was that this shows the analogy fails. A pramāṇa, he said, is a repeatable, general-purpose channel connecting cognizers to the world — testimony works because of a standing relation to a competent, trustworthy speaker — whereas tarka is a private mental maneuver whose reliability just piggybacks on whatever you fed it. Worse, if tarka is a pramāṇa, then wishful thinking dressed up as reductio must count too: ruling something out because denying it leads to an unacceptable result is exactly the form of "it can't be raining, because rain would ruin the picnic." Priya answered that there is a principled line between the two: tarka requires that the consequence be an impossibility, not merely something unwelcome — logical disaster, not a wet blanket. Neither would budge.

I followed both of them the whole way and honestly cannot referee it. Please take the exchange round by round and tell me who gets the better of each move and why — not merely who wins overall — and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing, whether about the Nyāya texts or about what a pramāṇa is supposed to be in the first place.
