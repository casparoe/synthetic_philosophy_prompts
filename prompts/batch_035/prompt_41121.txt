For some twenty years, the argument over whether the British empire in India nets out positive or negative has run through the same exchanges without visibly moving anyone. On one side is the revisionist ledger associated with Niall Ferguson: the empire knit the world into a system of trade and capital flows, built railways and ports on a scale India would not have managed alone, exported the rule of law, the English language, and parliamentary institutions, and, after 1807, spent naval resources suppressing the Atlantic slave trade — so that on balance empire accelerated the spread of modernity beyond what any plausible alternative would have achieved. On the other side is the rebuttal Shashi Tharoor has made famous: a wealthy manufacturing economy was deliberately deindustrialized to serve Lancashire's mills, wealth was drained for nearly two centuries through taxation, monopoly, and manipulated trade, famines culminating in Bengal in 1943 killed millions under an administration that plainly did not weigh Indian lives equally, and the final exit was managed so badly that it ended in Partition. Each side grants the other's facts and disputes their bookkeeping: the railways were real, but Indians paid for them and they were laid to move raw materials out; the famines were real, but pre-colonial India also knew hunger. The debate has become a fixture of public history, and it is completely stuck.

I want a diagnosis of the stuckness, not a verdict on the ledger. My working hypothesis is that the stall comes from the counterfactual baseline, which no evidence could settle. Tharoor's implicit comparison is India as it plausibly would have been without conquest — a major manufacturing and trading power that modernized on its own terms, more like Japan or Siam. Ferguson's implicit comparison is what other great powers would have done with the same dominance, or a world of autarkic empires without the trade and capital flows Britain imposed. Both baselines are projections; neither can be tested; so each side's arithmetic is unfalsifiable and the debate cannot move. But I am not confident in this diagnosis, because it does not explain two things. First, the two books seem to keep different ledgers entirely: Ferguson's is global, crediting the empire for effects on the modern world at large, while Tharoor's is Indian, debiting it for what was done to one society. If the sides are literally totaling different accounts, the disagreement may be less about counterfactuals than about whose balance sheet the question is asking about in the first place. Second, both sides accept the same rhetorical form — that a centuries-long process with millions of participants and billions of affected lives can be entered on a single balance sheet, with 'the empire' as the responsible agent — and it is not obvious that this form makes sense at all. Amartya Sen's treatment of famines, for instance, evaluates specific policies and decision-making structures rather than empires, and if that is the right way to think about the record, the aggregate question may be ill-posed.

So tell me, using what each side actually claims: is this a substantive empirical disagreement that happens to outrun the available evidence, a hidden normative one about the unit of moral accounting, a verbal one about what gets attributed to 'the empire' as such, or two groups answering different questions under a single heading? Test my counterfactual hypothesis against the alternative diagnoses and say where it fails. And then say what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a settled econometric estimate of the drain, a sharper distinction between evaluating policies and evaluating structures, a change of question — or an argument that no progress should be expected, because the debate persists for reasons internal to the genre: two very able public intellectuals addressing audiences who want a verdict on the past rather than an analysis of it.
