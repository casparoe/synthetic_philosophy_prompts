Our reading group has spent the term on Hofstadter's discussion of where meaning is located, and at the last meeting one member closed with an argument he claims shows that no message ever contains any meaning at all. The conclusion seems to me to prove far too much, but I have been unable to find the defective step, and I would like help diagnosing it and pricing the alternatives.

His argument, as I have it in my notes:

(1) Before the content of any message can be extracted, the receiver must first take the object to be a message and take it a certain way: the frame message — that this is a message at all, decode me — and the outer message — the hints about which decoding machinery to bring to bear, carried in the look of the script, the engraving of a record — must be picked up before the inner message, the content, is even in view.
(2) The frame and outer messages cannot themselves be stated in any language, since any such statement would already need decoding, and the regress never bottoms out; so they can work only by triggering structures the receiver already has.
(3) What merely triggers pre-existing machinery contributes no content to what that machinery then produces; a button on a vending machine contributes nothing to the can it releases.
(4) The inner message is reached only through the frame and outer messages; so whatever understanding the receiver ends up with was produced entirely by the receiver's own machinery, merely set going by the object.
(5) What is wholly produced by the receiver cannot be located even partly in the object.
(6) Therefore no message contains any meaning at all: a page of Japanese is in itself just ink; the golden record is in itself just grooves; DNA is in itself just a molecule; and the meaning of each lies entirely in whoever reads it.

He then draws out what I take to be the too-much: that on (6) it is never the original that a translation is faithful or unfaithful to, only our habits of response; that a misprinted recipe and its correction are, in themselves, the same object; and that it was never true that the Rosetta Stone meant anything to anyone before Champollion — there was only Champollion, meaning something at it. I can accept that meaning is partly an attribution of the reader. I cannot accept that it is entirely located in readers, and I do not believe he can either, since he still gets cross when he is misquoted.

Where does the argument go wrong? Tell me which step to reject; explain why that step sounded fine when it was first stated; and set out the price of rejecting each of the live rivals, including what accepting his conclusion outright would commit us to. If I must give something up, I would sooner keep (1) and lose (4) than keep (4) and lose (1), but I cannot see how (4) fails if (3) stands, so I may be wrong about where the cost really falls.

In your answer, please keep separate what is settled among people who know this material and what is genuinely contested — in particular, do not present any one account of where meaning is located (in the object, in the reader, or in neither) as if it were the consensus when it is not. And please answer in British English.
