I work for a small foundation that funds speculative theoretical research, and we are considering supporting three early-career philosophers for three years on work connected to the idea of "free-floating rationales" — Dennett's term, from his 2017 book on the evolution of minds, for reasons that exist without any reasoner entertaining them: the rationale of a spider's web is out there in the design logic of the thing, though no spider ever grasped it. Please take the role of a senior philosopher of mind and metaethicist who has spent a career in this territory and knows exactly where the live problems are buried.

Before ranking anything, spend a paragraph or two brainstorming the full space of open questions in this area, so we can see what fell out of the ranking and why. Then give us a defended agenda: which open questions around reasons-without-reasoners should our three researchers work on, in what order, and — this is the part we actually care about — on what grounds?

Among the candidates we have been debating internally, so you can push back on each, none of these framings is settled:

1. Whether free-floating rationales are normative reasons in any sense, or merely compressed explanations dressed up in normative clothing — whether "there is a reason for the bacterium to tumble less when nutrient gradients rise" means anything over and above "tumbling less when gradients rise is what selection favored."

2. The underdetermination problem. When we attribute a rationale to a structure whose selection history is partially erased, what licenses the attribution at all? Our program officer has been calling the hard cases "orphaned rationales": traits like the human chin, or social practices whose original function has decayed, where any story about the reason the thing exists is at least as plausibly a just-so rationalization after the fact as a discovery of a real reason that has been sitting there all along. Is there a principled line between recovering a rationale and inventing one, and does the line live in the evidence, in the metaphysics, or in the standards of the relevant science?

3. Whether rationales can conflict in the absence of a reasoner to do the weighing. Two selection pressures favor the same trait for incompatible ends, or a trait retains a rationale from one environment that it has forfeited in another; it is tempting to say one rationale "defeats" the other, but it is unclear what that could mean with nobody home to adjudicate.

4. Whether artifacts designed by systems that do not understand what they are doing — and, more pointedly, artifacts produced by large machine-learned models — acquire free-floating rationales in the same sense organisms have them, and whether that question is a real metaphysical problem or just a colorful way of restating questions about function and etiology that philosophy of biology already handles.

That list is not exhaustive and is not supposed to be authoritative; prune it, add to it, and tell us if some of these four are really the same question in different clothes. For each item that survives, say what makes it worth three person-years, which other questions depend on settling it first, how tractable it is with the tools philosophers actually have, and what developments — conceptual, empirical, or from adjacent fields — would move it up or down the list. Be willing to say that something matters mainly because getting it wrong would quietly poison work downstream. We want the reasoning, not a survey, and we are explicitly not asking for a reading list.
