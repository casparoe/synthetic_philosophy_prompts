I'm a political theory PhD student, and my dissertation chapter is on the ethics of exclusion — the club picture, where a state's right to exclude is supposed to come from something like freedom of association, versus the open-borders view that exclusion needs a justification it never manages to produce. My advisor's one big note on the last draft was that it's all thought experiments and no actual cases, so I've been trying to anchor the chapter in a case study, and after going back and fourth for a while I've landed on a weird one: The Grapes of Wrath.

Here's what got me excited about it. The Joads are not foreigners. They're American citizens, English-speaking, crossing an internal state line, and they get everything short of an actual border: cars turned back at the California line, Hoovervilles burned, deputies and vigilantes organized by the growers' association, and a propaganda machine that invents a new slur ("Okie") to do the work that "foreigner" usually does. Every standard justification for border exclusion that leans on foreignness — a distinct people, a different language, not members of our cooperative scheme — is off the table, and yet the rhetoric sounds exactly like anti-immigrant rhetoric. So my question: does the novel show that the operative principle isn't cultural self-determination but the defense of wages and property, i.e. that the right to exclude is really a right of owners against the destitute? And then there's the twist that scares me: the government camp at Weedpatch. There the migrants form their own association — committees, rules about who gets to stay, and the Saturday dance where they know the growers have sent men in to provoke a riot and get the camp shut down. That camp excludes people too, and honestly the club theory of exclusion seems to fit the camp better than it fits California itself. Which would mean the novel vindicates the principle my chapter attacks while damning its usual application.

What I want is a reading I can actually put in the chapter: one that takes both the novel and the debate seriously, that doesn't reduce the book to a sticker on my argument, and that's honest about where the book resists me — the "we" passages strike me as a bit too idealized, the Rose of Sharon ending gestures at an ethic of need that leaves no room for association rights, and Steinbeck never really asks whether the growers were partly right about scarcity. And since neither of us has the text in hand right now, work from the scenes at the level of common knowledge and don't fake quotes or page numbers. Point me to the two or three scenes I should hang the chapter on, and tell me what the novel adds to the exclusion debate that the arguments by themselves dont.
