Exam question (25 points): In "Of Conspiracy Theories" (1999), Brian Keeley argues that even a "minimalist" conspiracy theory — one involving only a small group acting in secret for some nefarious end — tends to be unwarranted. Reconstruct the central line of reasoning by which he reaches this conclusion, explaining the role played by (i) the way conspiracy theories handle "errant data" and (ii) what he calls the "conspiracy theory of error." Then take a position in the generalism/particularism debate: does the historical record of proven conspiracies (Watergate, COINTELPRO, and the like) show that conspiracy theories cannot reasonably be dismissed as a class, or is there nonetheless something epistemically distinctive about them that warrants a general attitude of suspicion?

Student's answer: Keeley defines a conspiracy theory minimally as an explanation that posits a small group of powerful people acting in secret to bring about an event, typically for a hidden and nefarious purpose. This is important because on this definition nothing is irrational about conspiracy theories as such: after all, conspiracies of exactly this shape really do happen, so positing one can't be a mistake in principle. Keeley's real argument is that in practice conspiracy theories end up unwarranted anyway, for two reasons. First, conspiracy theories exhibit a distinctive relationship to errant data — pieces of evidence that don't fit the official explanation. The problem is that conspiracy theories cannot accommodate errant data: since the conspirators control the flow of information, any anomalous evidence is just absorbed or suppressed, so the theory ends up with no way to process the data that would disconfirm it. This makes them unfalsifiable, and for Keeley the unfalsifiability is itself a mark against them. Second, Keeley describes what he calls the "conspiracy theory of error": the theory explains why most people don't believe it, namely that the conspirators have planted misleading evidence or manipulated the media. So the absence of confirming evidence and the presence of contrary evidence both get explained away. Keeley concludes that conspiracy theories are irrational by definition, since a theory that can explain away any possible counterevidence isn't a genuine explanation at all.

On the generalism question: Popper is the classic generalist, holding that conspiracy theories rest on the assumption that social outcomes are always intended by someone, and this "conspiracy theory of society" is bad methodology. But particularists like Coady are right that this doesn't settle anything about individual conspiracy theories. Watergate was a real conspiracy that was investigated and confirmed; COINTELPRO similarly came out in the open through documents and testimony. The fact that conspiracies have been exposed proves that secret collusion happens and can be discovered, so generalism is wrong. Once we accept particularism, the right policy is to give official and conspiratorial explanations equal prior credence: since either kind of explanation can turn out true, neither deserves a head start, and we should simply follow the evidence in each case. The reason this is hard in practice is underdetermination: for any event, one can always construct a conspiratorial alternative compatible with all the available evidence, so no amount of evidence can eliminate the conspiracy hypothesis. What particularists conclude is that we should therefore withhold assent from official stories as a rule, because for each of them there is always at least one live conspiratorial rival. This is what makes particularism the epistemically safest position: it treats all explanations as innocent until proven guilty, which is the only attitude consistent with genuine open-mindedness.
