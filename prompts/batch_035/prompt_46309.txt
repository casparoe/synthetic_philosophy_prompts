I'm a cog sci PhD student and I have to give a eulogy for my grandmother, and I'm stuck enough that I'm asking for help writing it, so here goes. Some background: my grandma died three weeks ago at 88. She was a computer scientist who worked on early machine translation in the 70s, and for the last few decades of her life she was deeply, sincerely invested in the Hofstadter picture of the self -- the idea that an "I" is a kind of looping pattern a brain builds about itself, and that these patterns get partially copied into the brains of people who know you well, so that a person is in a real (if scattered and fuzzy) sense distributed across many heads, and doesn't fully vanish when any one head stops. This wasn't a casual thing for her, she read I Am a Strange Loop so many times the spine broke and she gave talks about it at her retirement community, of all places. She left instructions that her memorial not contain anything she would have considered false. And here's the problem: she would have considered BOTH of the standard funeral moves false. "She's in a better place" -- false, she was a hard materialist. "She's gone, but her memory lives on in us" as a kind of consolation-flavored metaphor -- also false as far as she was concerned, or at least way too weak: for her the claim that parts of her keep running in other brains was meant literally, like a program running on fewer machines after one crashes, not as a greeting-card gesture.

The audience is genuinely mixed. Maybe a third of the room are her old colleagues and former students, people who will catch every strange-loop reference and will notice immediately if I get the idea wrong or water it down. The rest are family, including my uncle, who thinks all of this is a fancy coping mechanism grandma built to avoid facing death, and who rolls his eyes whenever anyone says "she's still with us in a way" -- to him that's exactly the mush people say at funerals when they have nothing real to say. He's grieving hard and I am not going to inflict a lecture on him. My mother is somewhere in the middle: she wants comfort and she doesn't want to hear a philosophy seminar, but she also doesn't want the mush, out of respect for grandma.

So: please write me a eulogy, about five minutes when spoken, that does the following. It comforts a grieving room without saying anything grandma would have marked as false. It gives her actual view of the distributed self real content -- enough that the colleagues nod instead of wince -- without becoming a lecture, and in language my uncle and the non-philosopher relatives can follow. And ideally it should convert "she's still with us in a way" from the mush my uncle hates into something he could actually believe or at least respect, without pretending the situation is less sad than it is. Grandma would have hated a eulogy that used her metaphysics as a painkiller. If it helps, one concrete thing about her: she was famous for finishing other people's sentences, badly and confidently, and she'd be the first to admit that the copy of her in my head does the same thing. Please write it in American English, and feel free to tell me where I'd need to personalize with actual anecdotes, I'd rather slot those in myself.
