I'm putting together notes for a reading group on formal epistemology, and I keep running into something that I can't quite articulate to my own satisfaction. On the surface, Kyburg's lottery paradox and Makinson's preface paradox seem like the same problem wearing two different coats: in each case you have a large set of propositions, each of which is individually plausible enough to rationally accept, and yet accepting them all together commits you to something absurd (in the lottery, a flat contradiction via "exactly one ticket wins"; in the preface, an explicit tension between the book's claims and the book's preface). But the standard responses to the two paradoxes diverge strangely. For the lottery, many philosophers are happy to say we never really believe "ticket #47 will lose" no matter how high its probability is -- the proposition somehow never earns full belief -- while for the preface, many of those same philosophers insist the author really does believe each of the claims in the book, and the preface's "at least one of these contains an error" is somehow not in conflict with that. I'd like you to explain, as carefully and intuitively as you can, exactly where the structural symmetry between the two cases breaks. Is the difference that lottery propositions rest on a known statistical distribution with no individuating evidence, while the preface author's claims are each backed by independent investigation? Is it about the grounds of the second-order belief -- the lottery player derives "one ticket wins" by definition, whereas the author derives "somewhere in this book I erred" only from a track-record induction about authors in general? Or is the symmetry only apparent, and a consistent position must treat both paradoxes the same way, accepting conjunction closure failures for both or for neither?

For context: one member of my group claims the preface is just a lottery in disguise, on the grounds that once you fix an error rate for academic books, each individual claim inherits a known probability of being false, exactly as each lottery ticket inherits its probability of losing. I suspect this is wrong -- it feels like it ignores that the author has item-specific evidence for each claim, whereas the lottery player has nothing that distinguishes ticket #47 from ticket #12 -- but I'd like a principled diagnosis of why, if I'm right, it matters whether the acceptance-relevant probability is "bare" statistics or a mixture of statistics and particular evidence. Relatedly, tell me whether you think the pair of paradoxes, taken together, supports distinguishing belief from some weaker attitude like "rational acceptance," or whether instead they show that any threshold-based account of belief is in trouble no matter how the attitudes are sliced. Please keep the discussion in American English, and favor plain-language explanations over formalism -- our group has only a modest background in probability theory. No need for a bibliography; I'm interested in the reasoning itself.
