You are to act as a professional philosopher with a deep sideline interest in the craft of fiction, someone equally at home in metaphysics and in a workshop discussing draft problems. Please think carefully and systematically: begin by briefly brainstorming the different questions buried in the situation below before committing to advice, and make sure your answer distinguishes them rather than collapsing them into one.

I am a novelist, and what I assumed was a craft problem has turned into what strikes me as a genuinely philosophical one. My current novel is written in close third person, and my protagonist does something dreadful at the midpoint: during a flood, he has one clear chance to pull his brother out of the water, hesitates, and lets the moment pass, later lying about it to his mother. I built him carefully for this. I gave him a violent, alcoholic father, a temperament I designed to be fearful under pressure, a string of formative humiliations I arranged in earlier chapters so that his hesitation would feel, to the reader, like the terrible summing-up of a whole life. In other words, every trait and circumstance that leads to the drowning, I chose; nothing about him that matters was left to him. A trusted reader has now objected that it is incoherent for my other characters to blame him — and, she argues, incoherent for readers to blame him too — because the person ultimately responsible for what he is, is me, and it would be monstrous to blame anyone for choices their maker made for them. She says blame scenes in fiction only work because readers momentarily forget the author's hand; the emotion is real but the object is an illusion, a trick of the medium.

I suspect she is wrong, or at least not wholly right, but I cannot say why, and the failure to say why is now blocking the entire second half of the book, which is structured around an act of forgiveness that has to feel earned. So my questions, and I would be grateful for careful, non-evasive treatment of each: First, if my character cannot coherently be blamed because I authored everything he is, does the same reasoning apply to people outside fiction, whose author is instead heredity and circumstance? If blame is coherent in one case and not the other, what exactly is the difference — is it that I could have written him otherwise while nature cannot revise? That I intended the outcome? Something about my standing relative to him? Second, if my friend is right that blame of characters rides on the reader forgetting the author, is blame of real people in any better position, or does it ride on an analogous forgetting about upbringing and luck — and if so, is that forgetting a failure, or might it be something more like the correct registration of a distinction I am failing to draw? Third, and most practically: should I write the blame scenes and the forgiveness straight, on the assumption that whatever makes everyday blame coherent carries over to characters, or does the novel need to acknowledge her point somehow — and if blame of a wholly authored person can still be meaningful, what precisely makes it meaningful, such that I can write scenes that reflect it? Please be concrete about how any philosophical position you invoke would actually cash out in prose.
