I'm a science journalist and a neighborhood library has roped me into running a 40-minute evening session on Dennett's old brain-transplant piece, the one where they take his brain out, keep it in a vat, and send his body into a tunnel to disarm a warhead, everything hooked up by radio. Audience is mostly retirees and book-club types, no philosophy at all, and they'll show up thinking this is a story about whether the tech is possible. Don't explain the story to me — I know it. I need a plan for how to explain it to them.

What has to be in place before the story even registers as a puzzle instead of just sci-fi? My instinct is they first need the three-way split between where your body is, where your brain is, and where you seem to be from the inside, since the story only bites once those can come apart — but tell me if that ordering is wrong or if something needs to come even earlier. Then: which beat of the story is actually carrying the idea. I assume it's the bit where the engineers re-route his point of view to follow the body instead of the brain, and he suddenly finds himself down in the tunnel even though his brain never left the vat — if so, how do I set that up so it lands as disturbing rather than as a fun trick?

Misconceptions I expect: so the answer is that you just ARE your brain, and the opposite one, so this proves the self isn't physical after all. Where in the sequence do I head each off, and are there others I'm not anticipating from that crowd?

Also: the ending, where they may have put someone else's brain in his skull and he can't tell from the inside. How do I handle that without the room filing it as a cheap gotcha — is it best saved for the last five minutes, or does it work better as the actual conclusion everything else was driving toward?

Give me one question I can ask at the end to check whether they got the real point rather than just enjoyed the ride, and one thing I should openly say I'm not going to cover in 40 minutes. Last thing: should I open with the story itself, or cold with the question where am I — or does that question not even make sense to them until the story has done its work?
