I'm a retired physicist — most of my career was condensed matter experiments, which basically means decades of staring at error bars — and since retiring I've fallen down the philosophy rabit hole, currently in the economics-and-probability wing. The standard story I keep running into about Keynes is that there are really two Keyneses. The early one, in his Treatise on Probability, treats probability as a logical relation between propositions — something like a degree of rational belief fixed by the evidence, objective in the sense that any rational person seeing the same evidence should agree. Then, so the story goes, Ramsey's critique plus the mess of the real economy knocked that over, and the later Keynes of the General Theory era ended up saying that for the big questions of life and investment the probabilities can't even be quantified — there's just radical uncertainty, and we muddle through with conventions, confidence, and animal spirits. Textbooks and blog posts present this as a conversion experience. My suspicion, after reading around a bit, is that this is too tidy, but I'd like to see the argument worked out properly rather than just asserted by a crank with a physics degree.

So here's the position I want you to defend, and I want you to take it seriously as a scholar would: the later Keynes's doctrine of uncertainty is a natural development of commitments already explicit in the Treatise, not a repudiation of them. The resources I have in mind are the parts people usually skip when summarizing the book — his admission that some probabilities are non-numerical and not even comparable to each other, and his distinction between how probable the evidence makes a conclusion and how much evidence there actually is, the thing he calls the weight of the argument. My half-formed thought is that once you take non-numerical probabilities and weight seriously, the later emphasis on unquantifiable uncertainty looks less like a Damascene conversion and more like the same picture with the spotlight moved onto the cases where the logical machinery runs out of steam. But I might be projecting my error-bar instincts onto him, so argue it, don't just sympathize.

A few ground rules for how I'd like you to go about it. Take the role of a professional philosopher who knows the history of probability theory and of economic thought, and think carefully before writing — maybe start with a short brainstorm of the strongest objections so you know what you're up against. Briefly steelman the two-Keyneses story first, so the argument actually engages something rather than knocking down a strawman, and then show where it goes wrong or at least gets importantly incomplete. Work from what you know of the ideas and arguments; don't include page numbers or a citation apparatus — I'll chase the sources down at the library myself. And because I'm an error-bar guy at heart, finish by telling me what would count as evidence against your reading: some fact about the texts or about how the later Keynes talks that, if it turned up, would sink the continuity thesis. I don't want a story that can't lose.
