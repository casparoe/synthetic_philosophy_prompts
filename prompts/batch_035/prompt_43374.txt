For the past several years I've been following the argument over bootstrapping and easy knowledge, and I've come to suspect it has quietly stalled: the arguments on all sides are on the table, everybody knows them, and nobody's position has moved. I want a diagnosis of the stall, not a survey of it. Let me lay out how I see the dispute, since the diagnosis has to engage it.

The bootstrapper reasons roughly as follows, using a reliable method R. R says the object is red. She infers: this R-reading was accurate. After enough rounds of this, she concludes inductively that R is reliable, and now she has (she thinks) warrant for trusting R in the first place. Critics such as Stewart Cohen and Jonathan Vogel say this reasoning makes reliability or the lack of epistemic circularity too "easy" to obtain, and that a theory of warrant on which the bootstrapper counts as knowing thereby fails. The standard responses are by now well-worn: deny closure or transmission of warrant across the relevant inferences; accept that the bootstrapper really does acquire warrant but insist it is useless or inaccessible; or bite the bullet and say easy knowledge is no worse than ordinary inductive reasoning ever is. Each response has its counterarguments, all published, all answered, all answered back — and here we sit.

To make the stall concrete, consider a variant I've been mulling. A radiation detector has a test function: press a button and the device displays ACCURATE or INACCURATE after running a self-diagnostic. The self-diagnostic is itself highly reliable. A technician may now bootstrap entirely inside the device: on each use, she notes the reading, later checks the self-diagnostic, and builds an inductive case that the whole system — sensor plus diagnostic — is accurate. Nothing she does is epistemically circular in the crude sense, since the diagnostic checks the sensor and something else checks the diagnostic, and so on down. Yet the intuition that she has gained nothing substantive is, at least in me, undiminished. If you think she has gained nothing, it matters enormously whether your reason is the same as your reason in the classic case, or whether the classic case depended on something the device case lacks.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not really about closure, transmission, or reliabilism at all. It's that the two camps are answering different questions under one heading. One camp is evaluating the bootstrapper's reasoning from the first-person perspective of what she can appeal to in support of her trust in R; the other is evaluating it from the third-person perspective of whether a belief-forming process with that structure tends to get things right. On my hypothesis, both camps are right about their own question, and the appearance of direct contradiction is a kind of illusion. But I'm not confident of this, and I want it stress-tested.
