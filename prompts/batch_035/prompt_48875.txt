Every biology course I have taken begins the same way: the teacher gives us a checklist of the properties of life — cellular organization, metabolism, growth, reproduction, response to stimuli, homeostasis — and we use it to sort samples into living and nonliving. But the practice wobbles as soon as it gets interesting. Viruses reproduce and evolve, yet we are told they are not alive, apparently because they fail some checklist items while meeting others; prions are dismissed as misfolded proteins even though they spread; and when I asked whether a frozen embryo, or a seed dormant for centuries, or a self-replicating computer program was alive, I was told that this is just semantics — even though the whole unit had treated the border between life and nonlife as something that could be located with a checklist. I have since learned that my school's sequence is typical, and that no textbook we use ever defines life itself, only lists its features. What view of what life is does this teaching practice presuppose — that life is a natural kind with a real boundary that the checklist approximates, that it is just chemistry complicated enough that the checklist is a practical convention, or something else? Is the practice even consistent, given that it applies the checklist strictly to viruses but waves off harder cases as semantics, and if not, which of its parts presuppose what? Please tell me which parts of the practice strictly follow from the view it presupposes and which are compatible with several rival definitions, and what would actually have to change in the classroom if we replaced the implicit view with one explicit definition — say, one based on metabolism, or on the capacity to evolve, or on maintaining order against thermodynamic decay.
