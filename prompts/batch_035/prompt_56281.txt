I'm grading final exams for an upper-level undergraduate course in philosophy of mathematics, and I'd like a second opinion on one essay before I commit to a mark. The question I set was:

"The Church-Turing thesis states that every effectively calculable function is computable by a Turing machine. One sometimes hears the following assessment: since the thesis concerns an informal notion, it cannot be proven; therefore it is best understood as a stipulative definition of 'effectively calculable,' and its acceptance among mathematicians is a matter of convention rather than evidence. Is this a fair account of the thesis's epistemic status? What kind of consideration, if any, could count for or against it?"

One student's answer, reproduced in full:

"The assessment gets something right and something wrong. It is right that the thesis cannot be proven: 'effectively calculable' is an informal notion, and no theorem can connect an informal notion to Turing machines without presupposing some formal analysis of it. But the conclusion does not follow, because the thesis is plainly not arbitrary — it has content, and we can ask whether that content is true. The strongest evidence for it is the convergence of the independently proposed formal analyses: Turing machines, Church's lambda-definability, and Herbrand-Gödel general recursiveness all turned out to be equivalent, and each was motivated by the same pre-formal notion of a finite, mechanical procedure. So acceptance is not mere convention; it is better described as an empirical hypothesis, confirmed by observation the way any hypothesis about human abilities would be. Gödel, who first proposed the thesis in 1931, was initially skeptical that recursiveness captured calculability but was persuaded by Turing's analysis. Since the thesis is empirical, it is falsifiable in the ordinary way: if physicists discovered a machine that computed a non-recursive function — say, by completing infinitely many steps in finite time — the thesis would be refuted, just as a failed prediction refutes any scientific hypothesis. In fact our ordinary computing experience already counts as evidence, since every model of computation ever actually built computes only recursive functions. One might worry that Turing's analysis is circular, since he arrived at his machines by reflecting on what a human clerk does; but this is not a genuine circularity, it is simply how any definition of an informal concept has to proceed."
