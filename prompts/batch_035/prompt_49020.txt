I spent most of my working life in chemical physics, where probabilities never much troubled me: they were weights over phase space, or spreads in a distribution of measurement outcomes, and one used them without ceremony. Now that I am retired and reading philosophy in earnest, I have run into something I cannot get straight on my own. It concerns David Lewis's Principal Principle — the norm saying, roughly, that if you know the objective chance of an outcome is p, and your other information is of the right, admissible sort, your credence in that outcome should be p. On its face this looks like nothing more than good laboratory practice: when the model says a decay has probability p in the next hour, you believe it to degree p. But Lewis discovered that the principle, in its original form, is inconsistent, and he himself called the problem the Big Bad Bug. I understand that the trouble has to do with chance functions that undermine themselves — possible candidate chance distributions that assign low probability to their own being the true chances — and that the problem is supposed to bite especially hard on the Humean view that chances are fixed by patterns in the events themselves, so that a future full of heads is what makes the coin fair. What I would like, please, is an explanation pitched at someone with my background, organized in sections as follows. First, "The Principle Stated Plainly" (~150 words): the principle, what admissibility is doing, and why it initially strikes a scientist as trivially correct. Second, "The Bug" (~300 words): a worked-through illustration of the self-undermining problem, showing exactly where the contradiction in the original principle comes from. Third, "The Repairs" (~450 words): the main fixes on offer — Lewis's own reformulations conditioning on history and laws rather than on chance hypotheses, the thought that rival chance claims fail to be admissible, and views on which one defers instead to something like the actual course of events — with a note on what each repair gives up. Fourth, "What This Means for the Working Scientist" (~200 words): whether my habit of sliding from a model's probabilities to my own degrees of belief was always hiding a version of the same problem — in particular, whether typicality arguments in statistical mechanics, which look so much like deference to chance, inherit the bug when a measure assigns small measure to its own typicality claims. Fifth, "Confidence and Doubts" (~100 words): please say how confident you are in the readings you have given, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of — I would rather know where the ground is soft than receive a polished surface.
