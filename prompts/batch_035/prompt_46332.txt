For the past two years I have been part of a small community group that meets monthly at our public library to discuss how local institutions use automated scoring — bail and parole risk scores, credit scoring, insurance pricing. I have no academic background (my training is in electrical work), but I have worked through enough of this on my own to understand the core mathematical fact that our discussions keep circling back to: when two groups have different underlying rates of whatever is being predicted, no score can be equally well calibrated for both groups and at the same time have equal false positive and false negative rates across them. Some fairness demands are mathematically incompatible, so everyone has to choose, and I have noticed that people in our group often don't know which one they are actually choosing.

I want to build a "which fairness view do you actually hold?" quiz for the group's website. Please take the role of someone who has both worked through the mathematics of fairness criteria and explained them to non-specialists, and please think carefully before writing: first work out which concrete cases genuinely split the main positions, and build the quiz from those, rather than from questions whose answers everyone would give the same.

The quiz should have ten questions. Each question should be a short, concrete case (a bail hearing, a loan application, an insurance pool, a hiring screen), stated in plain language a non-academic can follow, with two or three answer options. No question should name any position or use its standard label; the reader should be sorted by what they say about the cases, not by which word they identify with. The cases should collectively cover at least the following situations, phrased however you think best:

- a score that is perfectly calibrated within each group but produces clearly unequal false positive rates, used in a setting where a false positive means jail time;
- a rule that would achieve equal selection rates across groups but only by approving loans to applicants the score says are likely to default;
- two applicants identical on every feature the institution can legitimately consider, who differ only in group membership;
- removal of the protected attribute from the data when correlated features still carry its signal;
- deliberate use of group membership as an input in order to benefit a disadvantaged group;
- a situation where the unequal base rates themselves are the legacy of past injustice, raising the question of whether the score should be judged against the world as it is;
- a situation that tempts one to say the whole enterprise of predicting people's futures is the problem, not any particular score.

The scoring key should map patterns of answers onto at least these positions: the accuracy-first view that a well-calibrated score is already fair; demographic parity; equal error rates (whether in the full form or only for the positive class); the view that fairness means treating similar individuals similarly; the view that the unfairness lies upstream in the base rates and the world, not in the score; and the view that the right response to the incompatibility results is to stop using prediction for such decisions at all. For each position, give one sentence on its most awkward commitment. If certain answer combinations do not correspond to any coherent position, say so in the key rather than forcing a label onto them, and diagnose briefly why nobody holds that combination.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order and rough lengths: (1) "Before you begin," a plain-language introduction for quiz-takers, around 150 words, explaining calibration, false positive rates, and base rates without jargon; (2) "The ten cases," the questions themselves, each case two to four sentences plus its options; (3) "The scoring key," one short paragraph per position; (4) "What this quiz cannot separate," around 150 words on the positions or distinctions the cases fail to pull apart, so readers know where the instrument is blind.
