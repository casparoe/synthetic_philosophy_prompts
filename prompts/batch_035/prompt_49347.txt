I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and there's a pattern in how pest control is regulated that I can't quite put my finger on, so I want your help articulating what's underneath it. Here is the practice, stated as concretely as I can.

In most countries, the same animal's legal treatment flips depending on a label. A mouse in a laboratory is a research subject: euthanasia follows mandated protocols, and an oversight committee has to approve what happens to it. The same mouse in a pantry is a "pest," and glue boards, anticoagulant baits that kill over several days, and snap traps are sold over the counter with no welfare requirement at all. Regulators test pesticides for safety to humans, pets, and "non-target" wildlife, and they require efficacy — a product that fails to kill its target species can be removed from the market as ineffective — but nowhere in the process is the suffering of the target organism itself a criterion, except inverted: being lethal to the pest is a condition of staying on sale. Meanwhile "pest" is purely relational. It's not a biological category but a role — an animal is a pest insofar as it interferes with human purposes — so the same insect can acquire protection the moment it's recast as a pollinator and lose it the moment it's recast as a nuisance.

My question: what theory of animal minds and of moral status does this scheme presuppose in order to be coherent? Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. For each item, name one commitment the practice needs in order to make sense, and note in the same breath what rival view it quietly rules out. Make the fifth item the one that sits worst with something else the same legal system does — I want to see where the presuppositions stop being consistent with each other. Keep the language plain; if a technical term is unavoidable, define it inline.
