In debates about Zeno's paradoxes of motion, a claim I keep meeting — most recently in a popular science book I'm reading — is that the paradoxes were dissolved by the mathematics of infinite series: the successive catch-up stages of Achilles and the tortoise form a geometric series with a finite sum, so the whole race can be completed in a finite time, and the puzzle evaporates. The book goes further: it says the arrow paradox is covered as well, since calculus lets us define a speed at an instant, and it concludes that Zeno was simply groping toward the concept of a limit two thousand years early. I have two doubts the book never addresses. First, a finite sum tells me the total time is finite, but it seems silent on whether infinitely many distinct acts — one per catch-up stage — can all be completed; the sum being small doesn't obviously mean the doing gets done. Second, the arrow argument looks like it is about what is true at each instant, not about adding anything up, so I can't see how a series touches it. Please audit the analogy. Set out the correspondence: what in the paradox plays the role of the series, the terms, the sum, and the limit. Then say where the mapping carries real weight, where it breaks — above all at the step from "the sum is finite" to "the tasks are completed" — and whether the derivative genuinely answers the arrow or only restates the question in different symbols. Finally, judge whether the book's conclusion, that Zeno was refuted rather than merely reinterpreted, survives the disanalogies; and if not, say what the mathematics of limits did actually accomplish for Zeno, stated carefully.
