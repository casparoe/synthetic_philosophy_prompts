The unit covered first-order vs higher-order theories of consciousness, Rosenthal's HOT theory, inner-sense (HOP) views, and the misrepresentation objections. The midterm question was:

"Explain Rosenthal's higher-order thought theory of what makes a mental state conscious. Distinguish it from inner-sense theories of consciousness. Then present the misrepresentation objection to higher-order theories, describe the two forms it takes, and assess how well the higher-order theorist can answer each. Finally, state whether Rosenthal's theory is meant to solve the hard problem of consciousness, and why or why not."

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Rosenthal's higher-order thought theory says a mental state is conscious when there is a higher-order thought about it, i.e. a thought to the effect that I am currently in that state. The guiding intuition is that consciousness isn't just having a state but being aware of it — the transitivity principle. When I am in an unconscious pain, the pain still exists and does its causal work, but there is no thought about it, so it is not a conscious state. The HOTs are produced by a kind of inner attention that scans the first-order states and generates thoughts about whichever ones are active.

Importantly, the HOT itself need not be conscious. If it had to be, we would get an infinite regress, since a third-order thought would be needed to make the HOT conscious, and so on up. Rosenthal holds that HOTs are typically unconscious, becoming conscious only if some third-order state represents them. This is what lets the theory be non-circular.

Inner-sense theories agree that consciousness involves higher-order representation, but they claim the awareness is perceptual: an internal scanner produces higher-order perceptions of first-order states. So the dispute between Rosenthal and the inner-sense camp is about whether the higher-order relation is cognitive (thought) or perceptual (monitoring).

The misrepresentation problem: higher-order representations can be inaccurate, and since the HOT is what confers consciousness, conscious experience can come apart from first-order reality. There are two cases. In one, the HOT attaches to the wrong first-order state — say there's a red-sensation and a green-sensation in adjacent locations and the HOT picks out the green one, so I consciously experience green while the actual first-order state is red. In the other case, there is no suitable first-order state at all. But this second case reduces to the first, since the HOT can simply misrepresent whatever nearby states exist.

Can Rosenthal handle this? For the first case, yes: HOTs are thoughts, and thoughts can misdescribe their objects, so false conscious experiences are possible just as false beliefs are. The theory never promised infallible introspection, so this is a feature, not a bug.

Finally, the theory does solve the hard problem. It feels like something to be in pain because I am having a thought representing me as in pain, and once the higher-order representation is in place, the phenomenal character is fully explained: the raw feel just is whatever content the HOT carries. So physicalists should be happy — consciousness reduces to facts about higher-order thoughts."
