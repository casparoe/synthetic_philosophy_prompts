For four decades I worked as an experimental chemist, and something I only found the vocabulary for after retiring into philosophy is this: almost nothing I "kled" — no, let me put it plainly. Almost nothing I knew about the natural world was knowledge I had gained by my own observation. I had personally verified perhaps a few hundred claims over my whole career; the rest — the melting points of every compound but a handful, the values of the constants, the entire literature of the fields adjacent to mine, the reliability of instruments I used daily but had never taken apart — I took from journals, textbooks, calibration certificates, and colleagues. Yet the official ideology we taught our students was that scientific knowledge comes from observation and experiment. The only school of philosophy I have found that takes the actual epistemic situation seriously is Nyāya, which counts reliable testimony (śabda) as a basic pramāṇa alongside perception and inference, grounded in the competence and sincerity of the speaker, rather than as something reducible to inference from one's own experience. So my question is this: what epistemology does the day-to-day practice of a laboratory actually presuppose? Does it need testimony to be a basic source of knowledge in the Nyāya sense, or can the reductionist — who says my trust in a journal article is just an inference from my past record of journals generally not lying to me — accommodate the practice just as well, with nothing left over? Is the practice even internally consistent, given that we demand replication of headline results while accepting thousands of background facts on pure trust, and given that an instrument reading sits awkwardly between something I perceive and something I take on testimony from the instrument's designers? I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory, tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several rival accounts of testimony would equally license it — and say what would have to change in the working laboratory if we replaced the implicit view with a strictly verificationist one.
