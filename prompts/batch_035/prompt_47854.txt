I teach a graduate seminar on social epistemology, and I have come to dread the unit on peer disagreement — not because the students find it boring, but because it always ends the same way. We work through the classic cases (the disputed restaurant tip, the arithmetic answer checked between colleagues, the twin who has read all the same evidence on some contested empirical question), we get the standard positions on the table — the equal-weight view on which you should split the difference with a recognized peer, the steadfast or "right reasons" view on which you may keep your confidence if you in fact have the stronger grounds, and the various hybrids that tell you to weigh the disagreement as higher-order evidence without letting it dominate — and then the seminar fractures. By the last session the conciliationists and the steadfasters are exactly where they started, and, being social epistemologists, they are perfectly aware of the irony.

My question is not who is right. It is what kind of disagreement this is, and why it refuses to close. The moves are all well known and have been for two decades: conciliationists argue from the symmetry of the peers' epistemic positions and insist that you cannot count your own reliability twice; steadfasters reply that the symmetry is broken by the first-order evidence itself, that the peer's dissent is just one more piece of evidence to weigh, and that conciliationism, applied to the debate about conciliationism, seems to recommend that neither side should conciliate. Neither line has budged the other. Is this a substantive disagreement about the epistemic force of higher-order evidence, a verbal one about what "peer" means, or two research programs answering different questions — one about what it is rational to believe, one about what a well-functioning agent should do — under a single label?

Let me offer a hypothesis of my own, from a student's term paper I found unusually sharp. She argued that the debate stalls because the cases are stipulated at the wrong level of detail: "your friend is your epistemic peer, confronted with the same evidence, and confidently disagrees" underdetermines the causal process by which each of you arrived at your belief — a quick perceptual judgment, a long explicit calculation, a memory of prior study, a hasty reading of an argument. Verdicts about the cases flip depending on which process we silently imagine, she claimed, so what presents itself as a fight about peer disagreement is really a fight about the reliability of belief-forming processes, with the word "peer" smuggling in whatever process assumptions each philosopher needs. I cannot decide whether this is a genuine diagnosis or a restatement of the impasse one level down.

Please diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says — including the stubborn fact of the disagreement itself, which the steadfasters cite as a counterexample and the conciliationists defuse by denying peerhood among the disputants — and tell me, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a sharper taxonomy of cases, a formal model, a distinction, or an argument that this particular debate should not be expected to terminate at all. And if you think my student is onto something, say why the process-level reading hasn't already settled it.
