In July 2010 a user posting as Roko argued on the LessWrong forum that a future benevolent superintelligent AI might precommit to punishing people who had learned of that very possibility but did not help bring the AI into existence. The site's founder, Eliezer Yudkowsky, deleted the post and banned discussion of it for several years, on the stated ground that the argument constituted an information hazard for anyone who reasoned about it incautiously. The deleted idea became known as Roko's basilisk, and the decade of mockery and fascination that followed is now part of the episode too.

Analyze this episode through the lens of decision theory — specifically counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail. I want a careful mapping, not hand-waving. In the counterfactual mugging, Omega flips a coin before you exist, pays you a large sum on heads only if it predicted in advance that you would pay on tails; in XOR blackmail, the blackmailer is known to have consulted your own decision algorithm before deciding whether to send the letter, and paying is what makes the letter's arrival costly. Say explicitly who or what occupies each role in the basilisk scenario: who is the predictor, what plays the part of the coin flip, what exactly is being paid and to whom, and which algorithm's outputs are being consulted — the future AI's, or your own, or both. Note also that the basilisk seems to combine the two structures rather than instantiate one of them, and say whether that combination is coherent or whether it destroys the force of each component: if the punishment only ever targets those who heard the threat, is the scenario blackmail with no blackmailer present at the time of the decision, or a mugging where the predictor is a hypothesis rather than an agent?

Then run the analysis in the other direction. Functional decision theory is often advertised on the grounds that it refuses to pay in XOR blackmail while still paying in the counterfactual mugging; does the basilisk episode give that claim a stress test, or is it a case the theory never anticipated because the "blackmailer" is merely counterfactually hypothesized? Was the deletion itself a decision-theoretically defensible act, or a reflexive failure to apply the site's own advertised decision theory? And what should the episode's tabloid afterlife — the fact that the argument became famous precisely because it was censored — teach us about whether blackmail-type arguments can be neutralized at all?

Please distinguish clearly between what is settled in this literature and what is genuinely contested. For instance, the verdicts that causal, evidential, and functional decision theories deliver on the counterfactual mugging and on XOR blackmail are settled results in the sense that they follow from the theories' own commitments, but whether one should in fact pay in either case remains disputed among decision theorists, and whether acausal trade between agents at different times is anything more than a mathematical curiosity is contested as well. Do not present one decision theory's answer as the consensus, and do not treat the coherence of the basilisk scenario itself as a settled matter when it is not.
