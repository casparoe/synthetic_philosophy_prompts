I've been keeping a list of cases where someone has to do two things and can't do both, and marking my gut call on each: genuine moral dilemma (they really ought to do both, and doing one doesn't erase the other) or not a real dilemma (one duty just outweighs or defeats the other, so only one thing is actually required). My verdicts so far, and I'd love your help figuring out if there's a principle behind them.

You're a professional moral philosopher, so take this seriously, brainstorm a few candidate principles before committing, and then give me the best one or two, stated precisely.

The cases and my calls:

1. Sophie in Sophie's Choice forced to pick which child dies: genuine dilemma. Whichever she picks, she's left with a residual wrong, or at least something that isn't just regret.
2. A doctor with one ventilator and two dying patients who both need it: NOT a genuine dilemma. Tragic, but one option is simply the right one (say, the one with better prognosis) and that's it.
3. Sartre's student torn between joining the Free French and staying with his mother who lives for him: genuine dilemma, though I'm shakier here.
4. Bernard Williams's Jim, offered to shoot one captive himself so the other twenty go free: NOT a dilemma. He shouldn't shoot, even at that price.
5. A lawyer who knows her client is guilty and another man is about to be wrongly convicted, but confidentiality rules forbid revealing it: genuine dilemma.
6. Agamemnon having to kill Iphigenia to sail to Troy: hmm, I keep flip-flopping. Part of me says no dilemma, just don't kill your daughter, war can wait.
7. A parent who promised each of two kids, separately, to be at their events on the same day: NOT a dilemma, just apologize to one of them, feels clearly different from Sophie.

Here's my tentative principle, tear it apart if needed: a dilemma is only genuine when the two requirements come from different people's claims on you (my child's claim vs. my other child's claim, my client's vs. the innocent man's), and not when one side is just the world's brute badness, like a shortage of ventilators. But I can see holes: Sartre's student doesn't exactly have both duties flowing from personal claims, yet my call is that it's genuine. And Agamemnon seems to involve claims on both sides too, which my principle would make genuine, against my flip-flopping gut.

So: is there a principle that fits most or all of these? What would it say about a case I haven't considered, like a firefighter who can save either a stranger or, with equal effort, five strangers (no personal claim involved)? And honestly tell me which of my verdicts your principle can't save, and whether the principle that saves everything is worth its complexity.

Please write in American English, and keep it under 400 words -- tight, no filler. No citations or reading lists needed, just your reasoning.
