My friend defends conspiracy theorizing by analogy to the history of science. Semmelweis, she notes, had the data on handwashing and was nonetheless hounded out of the profession; the medical and tobacco establishments denied for decades what their own internal documents eventually confirmed. On her telling, the conspiracy theorist is doing exactly what the vindicated heretic did — refusing to defer to a consensus that has demonstrably failed before — so the reflexive epistemic penalty we attach to conspiracy theories puts us on the wrong side of history. She even anticipates the standard reply (Semmelweis had evidence; most conspiracy theorists don't) and has an answer ready: Semmelweis's evidence didn't help him at the time, so evidence-versus-no-evidence can't be what separates the heretic from the crank.
