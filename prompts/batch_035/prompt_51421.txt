My study group in a second-year philosophy of mathematics module has produced an argument that I suspect proves far too much, and I cannot locate the false move. The starting point is the epistemological horn of Benacerraf's old dilemma: if we give arithmetic a face-value, standard semantics, then a sentence like "there are infinitely many primes" says that a claim about abstract objects is true, and abstract objects stand in no causal relations to anything; yet our beliefs about the primes were produced by entirely ordinary causal processes — teachers, textbooks, blackboards — with no abstract object anywhere at the far end of the chain. My friend thinks this amounts to a proof of nominalism, and she has laid it out as follows. (1) If platonism is true, the truths of arithmetic are truths about causally inert, non-spatiotemporal objects. (2) A belief counts as knowledge only if there is an appropriate dependence between the fact believed and the believer's arriving at that belief — paradigmatically a causal or otherwise reliable connection. (3) There is no causal connection between abstract objects and anything in our brains. (4) So if platonism is true, none of our arithmetical beliefs amount to knowledge. (5) We do have arithmetical knowledge. (6) So platonism is false.

So far I was prepared to follow her; giving up numbers as objects struck me as a modest price. But last week someone in my moral philosophy tutorial ran what is recognisably the same schema against moral non-naturalism, and it occurred to me that nothing in the argument restricts premise (2) to mathematics. Consider my belief that the match would have lit had it been struck. The causal history of that belief runs entirely through events in the actual world; the counterfactual fact itself, being about a non-actual event, causes nothing and is caused by nothing. If (2) is correct, I do not know that either. For that matter, if the ethical facts (on the non-naturalist picture) are causally idle, the schema sweeps them away too, and it is not obvious to me that it stops there.

I would rather keep my arithmetical knowledge, my knowledge of what would have happened, and whatever ethical knowledge I have, so I suspect (2) is the weak link — but I cannot see how to weaken it without simply handing the platonist a victory. If the "appropriate dependence" is allowed to be non-causal, what stops there being such a dependence between the abstract realm and our brains, and doesn't the dilemma then lose its point entirely? Which step of the argument should be rejected, what does rejecting it cost, and is there a principled repair that explains why mathematics, counterfactuals, and ethics stand or fall together, or failing that, why they do not? I would be grateful for a diagnosis that names the precise step, explains why it looked fine, and prices the alternatives honestly rather than simply declaring victory for one camp.
