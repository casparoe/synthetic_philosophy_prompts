You are a philosopher of science with a specialty in the methodology of consciousness research. Think carefully, and before committing to anything, brainstorm candidate requirements and prune the ones that turn out to be the prejudice of one camp rather than a genuine demand — I want the surviving list, not the raw pile.

I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, planning a long feature on the scientific study of consciousness. Every researcher I interview insists their method is the scientific one, while accusing the others of begging the question. Rather than referee the interviews, I want the spec first: what any adequate method for a science of consciousness has to deliver.

Write the desiderata, with the case behind each:

- the phenomena it must handle, including the hard ones — subjects who confabulate reasons after the fact, blindsight patients who report seeing nothing while guessing better than chance, dreams, and beings who cannot report at all (infants, animals, the anesthetized);
- the cases it must get right — where the method has to be able to disagree with the subject, and where it must not;
- the distinctions it cannot collapse — between what a subject says and what is actually going on in that subject; between taking reports as data and taking them as evidence;
- the findings from psychology and neuroscience it cannot contradict.

Mark which requirements you treat as non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular school dressed up as demands. Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want to see. My own hunch is that "the method must stay neutral on whether experience outruns what subjects can report" and "the method must be able, in principle, to settle what experience is like" pull against each other, but give me your judgment, not mine. Close with one short paragraph on how Dennett's heterophenomenology fares against the list and where its critics allege it cheats — no further scorecard, and no reading lists.
