A colleague of mine who works on decision theory walked me into the following argument, and I cannot find the exit. Each step looked fine when I checked it individually, but the conclusion seems to prove far too much, and I would like a careful diagnosis of where the chain breaks.

The argument runs as follows:

(1) Aumann's agreement theorem (1976): if two agents start from a common prior, update by Bayesian conditioning on their private evidence, and their posterior probabilities for some proposition are common knowledge between them, then those posteriors must be equal.

(2) Take two honest, reflective people who have each absorbed the same large body of public evidence but hold different degrees of belief in some proposition p. If they sincerely tell each other their current degrees of belief, in a setting where each can see that the other is being sincere, then each learns the other's posterior, each knows the other has learned it, and so on up the hierarchy — the announced posteriors become common knowledge.

(3) An announced posterior is itself evidence. On hearing it, a Bayesian conditions on it, so the announced figure is no longer that speaker's current posterior. But if the two iterate — announce, update, announce again — the process must settle, since every round merely adds to their stock of shared evidence, and what it settles into is a pair of equal posteriors that are common knowledge between them.

(4) So two honest Bayesians with a common prior cannot persistently disagree: once they have argued the matter through, agreement is forced, and any disagreement that survives the argument entails that the parties are not both honest Bayesians with a common prior.

(5) Now consider any long-running dispute between experts who have access to the same evidence — think of two economists who have read all the same papers and still disagree about the sign of some policy effect, decades in. Their disagreement has survived prolonged argument, so by (4) either at least one of them is being dishonest or irrational, or their priors differ.

(6) If their priors differ, then, since priors are not themselves derived from evidence, there is no rational ground for preferring one prior to the other. So there is no fact of the matter about which party is closer to the truth, and rational persuasion across the disagreement is impossible in principle.

(7) Therefore rational debate between rival experts is either redundant — shared priors meant the agreement was forced in advance — or impossible — distinct priors leave no rational route from one side to the other. In neither case can debate do what we observe it doing: running for years, staying rational throughout, and ending with one side genuinely persuaded by the other.

This proves too much for my taste. It would entail that scholarly controversy is a sham whenever it persists, that a juror who maintains a dissent after deliberation must regard every dissenting juror as dishonest or irrational, that polling experts is pointless, and that every scientist who remains in good-faith disagreement with a colleague is thereby committed to attributing irrationality to her. It also licenses a corrosive symmetry: whenever your opponent disagrees with you after you have laid out your case, you may conclude, without further work, that she is irrational — no need to locate her mistake.

I would rather keep (1) than anything else: the theorem is a theorem, so if something must go, it should be a step on the application side. My suspicion is that the trouble lies in (3) or (6), but I cannot name it precisely, and I notice that (3) looks like it is trading on an ambiguity between the posterior someone announces and the posterior someone holds at the moment the announcement is absorbed. So: which step should I reject? Explain why that step looked fine, what rejecting it costs — in particular what the cheapest rejection does to the idea that prolonged honest disagreement between rational people stands in need of explanation — and say whether the resulting position leaves the theorem with any real purchase on actual disputes, or reduces it to a statement about an idealization that almost never describes anyone.
