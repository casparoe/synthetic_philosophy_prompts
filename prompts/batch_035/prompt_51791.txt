So I have no philosophy background at all, just a guy who reads physics stuff for fun and got sucked into a rabbit hole. I've been turning this one over for months and I want someone to actually explain it to me, not just define terms.

The question, as I understand it: are the laws of nature like rules that make stuff happen, or are they just descriptions of the stuff that happens? Like, does the universe "obey" F=ma, or is F=ma just the compressed summary of what matter happens to do, the way a sports record book isn't causing the games?

I get that the "summary" camp (I think it's called Humean, after that Scottish guy) has a specific move: laws are whatever the best systematization of all the facts would output, where "best" trades off simplicity against how much it covers. And I get the other camp says no, there's something extra, laws govern or constrain or whatever.

But here's the thing I keep snagging on, and this is my own worry so tell me if it's dumb. "Best system" -- best for whom? Simplicity isn't a fact about the universe, it seems like a fact about us, or about what language we're writing the theory in. Like, suppose I invent a predicate "flurg" that applies to exactly the electron-like things and exactly the gravitational behaviors or whatever, carefully gerrymandered. In flurg-language my physics is two axioms long. Super simple! Obviously that's cheating, but the only way I can see to rule it out is to say only "natural" or "fundamental" properties count as legitimate vocabulary. And that feels like smuggling in exactly the thing we were trying to avoid -- those natural properties look suspiciously like little laws, or at least like structure the Humean wasn't supposed to be committed to. So my worry is the summary camp only works by quietly borrowing from the rules camp. Does this sink the whole project, or can they bite some bullet here? (I've also half-convinced myself the two camps are just fighting about the word "because" -- like maybe there's nothing to disagree about once you stop asking whether laws "do" anything, and it's all about what counts as an explanation. Is that a real position or am I confusing myself?)

Second thing, and this is the part I actually care about most: does any of this matter outside the seminar room? Like, if the rules camp is right, does anything change for how we think about what could have been otherwise (I gather this connects to whether the constants of nature could've been different), or about what a physics theory even is? And here's a wrinkle I haven't seen addressed anywhere: it seems like the rules camp owes us an account of how laws get their grip, and the summary camp owes us the vocabulary thing, so maybe each side's problem is just the other side's problem wearing a hat. If that's right, what would even count as progress?

Please walk me through this like I'm smart but untrained -- I can handle the ideas, just don't assume I've read anyone. And I'd rather you take an actual side at the end than give me the "philosophers disagree" shrug. But also, tell me straight: what would change your answer, and where's the part of your reasoning most likely to be wrong? Like if someone showed you X, you'd flip to the other camp. I want to know what the load-bearing stuff is, not just the conclusion.
