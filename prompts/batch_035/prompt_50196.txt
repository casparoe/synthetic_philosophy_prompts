in EA-adjacent discussions i keep running into this: utilitarianism is just moral bookkeeping. pleasures are credits, pains are debits, and the right act (or better world) is whichever runs the biggest surplus. people deploy it to say a world with tons of mild pleasure plus a few cases of torture beats a world of moderate contentment with no torture, so long as the surplus is bigger. someone in my reading group pushed back that this is exactly where lexical threshold negative utilitarianism bites: pains above some intensity threshold can't be bought off by any amount of pleasure, no matter how much you pile on — as if there's a currency the pain column just doesn't accept.

can you audit the bookkeeping analogy for me? lay out what maps onto what (the two columns, the netting operation, what it means for an account to be 'in the black'), then where the ledger picture breaks down, what it quietly smuggles in about the commensurability of pleasure and pain, and whether the surplus conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. also curious whether there's a better analogy for the lexical threshold view — maybe pain above the threshold is less like a debt and more like a price nobody's allowed to pay, or whatever you think the honest comparison is.
