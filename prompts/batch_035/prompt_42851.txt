I run a discussion group for second-year undergrads — basically a pub philosophy night, about fifteen people, mostly not philosophy majors — and this term's topic is what you're allowed to assert. I want one of those 'which position do you actually hold?' quizzes: eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three options, and a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto views. People should get sorted by what they say about the cases, not by which label they like, so no question can be 'do you think assertion is governed by a knowledge norm?'.

Leaves the key needs to have: the knowledge norm, the truth norm, the justification / reasonable-belief norm, the belief (sincerity) norm, and the no-distinctive-norm view on which assertion just inherits general epistemic norms plus whatever rules the practice happens to have. You can add a pluralist 'different norms in different contexts' leaf if it helps, but mark it clearly as such.

Cases the quiz has to include, because these are where the views actually come apart:

- The lottery one: a million-ticket fair lottery, the draw has already happened, your friend's ticket lost, and you say to them 'you lost'. True and near-certain — but assertable?
- A Moorean sentence: 'It's raining, but I don't believe it is.'
- Gettier-style: someone asserts p, p is true, they're justified in believing it, but only by luck. Proper assertion or improper?
- False but justified: someone is confident, well-supported, and wrong. Can that still be a perfectly fine assertion?
- Concessive knowledge: 'I know the shop closes at six, but it might be closed.'
- Retraction etiquette: someone asserts something true that they had no business believing, and when challenged says 'fair, I shouldn't have said that'. Is the practice of faulting such assertions evidence about the norm, or just noise?
- Machines: my phone's weather app prints 'rain tomorrow' off a 55% forecast, and a chatbot tells me a fact while believing nothing about anything. Are these assertions at all, and if so which norm could they even violate? I want this one in because it goes past the standard cases everyone's seen.
- Stakes: the same sentence ('the meeting's at three') said on the same evidence when the cost of being wrong is trivial vs ruinous.

For each question, write options a non-philosopher can pick without knowing where they lead — 'yes you may say it', 'no, you shouldn't', 'you may, but you owe an explanation if pressed' — that kind of thing.

Two more asks. First, in the scoring key, separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this: e.g. flag whether treating lottery propositions as unknowable is a consensus or a working assumption that some reject, and dont present any one norm as the consensus view, because it isnt. Second, flag any pairs of leaves the quiz can't really separate (I'd guess truth vs justification will blur whenever justified beliefs happen to be true) and any answer combinations that don't map onto a leaf — and say whether nobody's in that corner because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

It needs to be runnable in about twenty minutes at a pub: people answer by holding up cards, and the key has to be checkable by them on the spot without me hovering over anyone's shoulder.
