I chair the Standing Rules Committee of a professional association with roughly 4,000 members, and I also serve as its parliamentarian. Our charter can be amended by the membership, and — this is what makes the job genuinely hard — the amendment procedure is itself written into the charter and can therefore be amended by it. Article VII currently provides that a proposed amendment becomes binding if (i) it is submitted with signatures from two percent of the membership and (ii) it is approved by a two-thirds majority in an election conducted under our election bylaws, which the charter also permits us to amend. We get about a dozen amendment proposals a year, mostly drafted by non-lawyer members. Our committee has five people — a tax lawyer, a retired high-school principal, an archivist, a project manager, and me — none of whom is a logician. We budget one hour of review per proposal, at least three of the five must sign the resulting written opinion, and every opinion is published to the full membership. When a ruling is challenged it goes to an internal appeals tribunal, and we look terrible when our reasoning turns out to have been improvised.

I want you to act as an expert in legal philosophy, the logic of normative systems, and practical parliamentary procedure — the rare person who can both explain the deep theory and then actually use it. Before writing anything, take some time to brainstorm: what are the distinct ways a self-referential amendment can go wrong (self-validation, self-nullification, self-binding of future majorities, conflicts between a rule and the rule that authorizes it, the regress that starts once you ask what validates the validating rules)? Say which of these failure modes a one-hour committee procedure must catch, and which it can safely ignore as exotic. Then produce the deliverable.

The deliverable is a decision procedure my committee can actually run: an ordered list of questions to ask about any proposed amendment, with, for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer, what outputs a definite "valid," what outputs a definite "invalid," and — crucially — where the procedure must stop and hand the question to a person because it has turned into a question of values or institutional self-definition rather than one of logic. It must make the distinction between the validity of a rule's content and the validity of its enactment usable by non-experts, and it must be honest that no finite checklist can close the regress of validation completely; the procedure should say what it does at the point where the regress would start, rather than pretending the point doesn't arrive.

Then run the procedure on three proposals we have actually faced, with details changed. For each, show the output at every step, identify at which step the paradox surfaces, and give your recommendation:

1. An entrenchment clause: "Article III of this charter shall not be amended or repealed by any vote, resolution, or procedure of any kind" — itself proposed for adoption under Article VII.

2. A proposal raising the ratification threshold for all future amendments from two-thirds to ninety percent, and stating that it "takes effect immediately upon its own adoption by a two-thirds vote." Read one way, it validates itself; read another way, it can never take effect at all.

3. A clean-elections amendment declaring that "no motion voted upon in an electronic election shall be valid" — which we ratified through an electronic election, so that on a literal reading it un-ratifies its own ratification.

Include among the three at least one outcome the committee will not like — a proposal the committee is politically invested in passing that the procedure nonetheless blocks, or a proposal it wants to kill that the procedure lets through — and tell me which it is and why. Then close with the limitations section: where the procedure will misfire, where "valid" and "wise" come apart and which member of the committee has to absorb that difference, and what you would say to the member who argues, in open session, that our own authority to issue validity opinions at all is granted by the very Article VII that proposal 2 would neuter. Do not substitute name-dropping for argument — if you invoke incompleteness or self-reference results, show me the specific analogy and where it strains. Write it all in plain, formal English that a project manager can follow on a Tuesday night.
