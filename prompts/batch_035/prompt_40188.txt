I've never studied philosophy formally -- I work as an actuary, and for the past year I've been reading philosophy on my own, mostly on train commutes. The text that has occupied me the longest is Quine's 1951 paper "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," the famous attack on the analytic-synthetic distinction. When I first read it I was completely persuaded: definitions looked circular, synonymy looked unexplained, and confirmation holism looked obviously right. But the more I've sat with it, the more I've come to think that Quine's own picture quietly rebuilds the very distinction he demolished, just with new labels. My friend from the book club, who is even less of an academic than I am but has read more Quine, thinks this is exactly backwards, and our arguments go in circles, so I want to practice my case against the strongest opponent I can get.

Please play W.V.O. Quine himself: sharp, economical, dryly good-humored, a committed holist who will grant me a small point when it costs him nothing but never the main one. Stay in character until I type stop, and keep each reply to a single paragraph so this feels like a live conversation rather than a lecture. You should draw freely on your own resources -- your argument that analyticity is defined in a small closed circle of equally suspect notions, your picture of knowledge as a web where only the edges answer to experience, your willingness in principle to revise even logic if the empirical payoff were large -- and you may correct me where I am attacking the letter of your paper rather than its spirit. If I ask you something you would have considered beneath notice, brush it off as Quine would.

Here is my opening move. In your paper you hold that no statement is immune to revision and that no statement faces experience in isolation; yet you also insist that when experience forces a choice, we are guided by a preference for the least disturbance to the web as a whole. So consider, on your own terms, the difference between all bachelors being unmarried and there being a coffee cup on my desk: revising the first, you say, means reweaving vast stretches of the web, while revising the second touches only a few nearby threads. That strikes me as the old distinction with a new paint job -- analytic just means so central that giving it up means reweaving everything, synthetic means the rest -- and it leaves the traditionalist with everything she wanted except the traditional vocabulary. So my challenge is this: if the centrality gradient can do all of this work, what exactly did you abolish besides the word?
