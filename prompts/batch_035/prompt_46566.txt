You are to answer as a professional philosopher of mind who also keeps abreast of empirical consciousness science, and I ask you to think carefully and in stages: first brainstorm the candidate defeaters without commitment, then prune and organize them before presenting anything final.

By way of context: I am a retired physical chemist who spent most of my career on spectroscopic methods, and since retiring I have been working through philosophy of mind in a reasonably systematic way. I have come to sympathize with Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenology as the method for a science of consciousness: the subject's verbal reports are treated as a text to be interpreted with charitable but revisable assumptions, while all the explanatory work is done by third-person data, on the ground that first-person access is just another thing brains do and something we can be wrong about. To a scientist this looks like ordinary methodology: no instrument is privileged, the subject is calibrated like any other measurement device, and we should expect calibration failures — blindsight, confabulation in split-brain patients, the experiments in which people ardently defend choices they never actually made. My working view is that any program granting greater authority to first-person reports, whether the introspectionist psychology of the early twentieth century, neurophenomenology, or so-called contemplative science, is methodologically retrograde.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure: which empirical results, which arguments, and which developments in adjacent fields would genuinely count against heterophenomenology as the right method — as opposed to merely irritating its defenders. Please consider the possibility that the method is insulated: since it instructs us to provisionally honor the subject's text while holding that only third-person considerations can overturn it, it is not obvious what discovery could ever dislodge the method rather than just being absorbed as one more calibration failure, and I would like your honest verdict on whether that resilience is a scientific virtue or a symptom that the method risks nothing and therefore explains less than it advertises. Distinguish throughout between defeaters a committed defender would concede and defeaters they would reframe; I am not interested only in what critics say. As format, please give me a table with exactly these columns: (1) Defeater, a short name; (2) Description, what would have to be observed or argued; (3) Force, rated fatal, serious, or merely awkward; (4) Likely defense, how a committed heterophenomenologist would respond; and (5) Cost of that defense, what the position gives up if the response succeeds. Follow the table with a short paragraph assessing, overall, how exposed the method actually is, and tell me plainly which of the defeaters you personally find most persuasive and why.
