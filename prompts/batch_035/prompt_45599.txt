I'm putting together a presentation for a graduate seminar on theories of brain function, and as background I watched a lecture series by a computational neuroscience postdoc on predictive processing and the free energy principle. In his wrap-up video he compresses the entire framework into a single paragraph, and I came away suspecting the whole thing is just behaviorism dressed up in probability theory — but I can't tell whether that's his fault or the framework's, since I don't know the literature well enough to locate the errors. The paragraph, as I noted it down, went roughly like this: "Predictive processing says the brain is constantly generating predictions about its sensory input and only updates when predictions fail, so perception amounts to controlled hallucination and surprise is the enemy. The free energy principle generalizes this to all of biology: any system that endures has to minimize free energy, and free energy here is the same quantity as in thermodynamics, just applied to the system's beliefs about its environment. Since an organism following the principle avoids everything unexpected, the natural endpoint is a state of minimal stimulation — the theory predicts we should all prefer to sit in dark, quiet rooms, and it escapes this only by stipulating priors that happen to match what we actually do. And because the principle is supposed to govern every self-organizing system, it covers thermostats and crystals as much as brains, so it can't tell us anything specific about cognition. Finally, since free energy is defined in terms of the system's own generative model, the principle is unfalsifiable — whatever the organism does counts as minimization after the fact, which is why serious scientists dismiss it."

I'd like an audit of this paragraph, because my instinct is to side with the dismissive reading, and I want to know whether that instinct is tracking real problems or just his compression. Please respond as exactly five numbered points, one per claim, in the order I encountered them: (1) perception as prediction with surprise as the enemy, (2) the identification of free energy with the thermodynamic quantity, (3) the dark-room endpoint and the "stipulated priors" escape, (4) the thermostat charge that the principle says nothing about cognition, and (5) the unfalsifiability charge. For each, a sentence or two stating whether a theorist actually working in this area would accept it as stated, wince at it as a familiar simplification, or reject it outright — and, where the claim is partly right, whether the conclusion he draws from it survives the correction. No introduction or conclusion; just the five items.
