I've been reading rationalist blogs and comment threads since the Overcoming Bias days, and one analogy keeps resurfacing in discussions about whether religion makes people better and whether post-religious societies can sustain trust. It's the cut-flower picture: secular morality, on this view, is a flower severed from its religious roots. It still looks fresh for a while — it lives off whatever moral capital the faith that produced it accumulated — but nothing new is coming up the stem, and it is only a matter of time before it wilts. The conclusion this is deployed to support is that countries undergoing secularization are drawing down a finite stock of honesty, trust, and self-restraint, and that once the capital runs out, no purely secular mechanism will regenerate it. I've seen it used by religious apologists, but also, more interestingly, by secular pessimists who concede the premise and simply expect the decline.

I'm not asking you to adjudicate the empirical sociology wholesale. I'm asking for an audit of the analogy, which is doing a lot of quiet work.

First, lay out the correspondence table. What plays the role of the root (theology? weekly enforced practice? supernatural monitoring? belonging?), the stem (institutions? habit? guilt?), the stored nutrients (moral capital — but what is that, concretely: trust levels? honesty norms? fertility? charitable giving?), and the wilting (what observable would count as the flower dying — falling trust statistics, rising defection in prisoner's dilemmas, or something else)? Note where the correspondences are ambiguous, because I suspect the analogy survives scrutiny precisely by never fixing what the nutrients are.

Second, the disanalogies. The one I keep turning over is this: gardeners also work with cuttings. A cutting is a piece severed from the parent plant that, given the right conditions, grows its own roots. The cut-flower analogy assumes the biological fact that severed flowers never re-root, and smuggles it in as a social fact — that norms, once detached from their religious source, cannot grow new anchoring in, say, constitutions, professional ethics, or mutual monitoring. That's the crux, and I'd like you to address it head-on: is a post-religious society a cut flower or a cutting, and is that an empirical question the analogy can't settle but pretends to? Distinguish for me, too, between disanalogies that matter for the intended use (forecasting whether secular trust sustains) and ones that don't (flowers can't choose, plants lack doctrine, etc.).

Third, tell me what the analogy smuggles in about the direction of dependence: it assumes the religion produced the morality rather than the morality, or something else, selecting for and stabilizing the religion — the way an adaptationist might say the root grew to serve the flower rather than vice versa. If the causal arrow is reversed or mutual, what happens to the wilting forecast?

Finally, the verdict. Once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion the analogy was used to support survive on independent grounds, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if you think there's a better comparison in circulation or worth constructing — a coral reef, a market, a commons, a muscle that atrophies without exercise — tell me what it is and whether it fares any better under the same audit.

Please write the response in American English.
