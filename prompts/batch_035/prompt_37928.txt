Quick context before the ask. I'm a journalist covering science and ideas for a mid-size regional outlet, and my next column is about the fight our city council is having over a one-time resilience grant. The money can go to exactly one of two things: upgrading the flood levee behind the old cannery, or buying out and preserving Miller's Orchard, the last working orchard in the city limits, which would otherwise get sold to a logistics company for a distribution hub. The levee engineer on the council, who I'll call Dana, ran a cost-benefit model that puts a dollar figure on both options -- flood damages avoided on one side, and on the other the orchard's value priced through property values, estimated tourism, and one of those contingent-valuation surveys where you ask people what they'd pay to keep it. Dana's not a cartoon, she actually thinks that refusing to price things is a way of hiding your preferences from scrutiny. The council member on the other side, let's say Marcus, keeps saying the two options are "not on the same scale" -- he doesn't claim the orchard is worth more, he claims the comparison itself is confused, that some things are neither better, nor worse, nor exactly equal, and that picking between them is less like measuring and more like deciding who you're going to be.

My editor wants me to try something other than the usual he-said-she-said, so here's what I'm after: write the dialogue between Dana and Marcus, set maybe over coffee after a long council session. Dana should press her strongest line -- that incommensurability talk is usually a rhetorical shield, that once you refuse to weigh things you're just outsourcing the choice to whoever shouts loudest, and that the levee side could play the same game if it wanted to ("how do you price a child's life downstream, Marcus?"). Marcus should respond to that rather than repeat himself -- he should distinguish what he's claiming from plain indecisiveness, explain what he thinks people are actually doing when they make a choice like this without any further fact to appeal to, and ideally force Dana to admit where her spreadsheet quietly sneaks in judgments that aren't themselves measurements. Both of them should be smart and arguing in good faith, and they should actually land some hits on each other. I don't need it wrapped up with a neat verdict -- if anything I'd like it to end unresolved but with the disagreement clearer than it started, since that's honestly where I am after three weeks of covering this thing. Keep it to something like 600-900 words, and feel free to have them use the orchard and the levee as their concrete example throughout rather than floating off into abstractions.
