I've been around the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and there's a confusion the community never fully untangled: in discussions of introspection, two quite different claims get run together. One is luminosity — roughly, whether some condition of your own mind is such that, whenever it obtains, you're in a position to know it obtains. The other is the KK principle — that if you know something, you know that you know it. Eliezer's old luminosity posts, and the CFAR-style training culture that grew out of them, treat both failures as the same bug: unreliable introspection. But in the academic literature they are distinct. Williamson's margin-for-error argument is supposed to show that no interesting condition is luminous — his examples are gradual states like feeling cold — and since knowing-that-p is itself a condition, killing luminosity kills KK along the way. That is the standard package: no luminosity, therefore no KK. What I want is a case that splits the two claims in the opposite direction, and I want it designed rather than discovered.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications:

1. The subject is in a mental state M of your choosing — pain, trying, deciding, attending, take your pick — and in this case M is luminous: whenever M obtains, she knows M obtains. This has to be earned, not stipulated: the scenario must be one where a Williamson-style margin-for-error attack on the first-order knowledge genuinely fails, and you must show why it fails.

2. Nevertheless, KK fails at the second order: she knows that M obtains, but she does not know that she knows it.

3. The failure of second-order knowledge must come from the epistemic environment — something that targets the status of her belief rather than the state itself. It must not be manufactured by making her an unreliable introspector by fiat, and it must leave the first-order state untouched.

4. Realistic setting — a recovery room, a rehearsal, a cockpit, something an ordinary person could be in. No demons, no perfect predictors, no stipulated superpowers of introspection, and no instant transitions into M. The scenario itself should be one paragraph.

After the scenario, walk through the design:

(a) Defend the first-order luminosity: what do the nearest worlds look like, and why is her belief that M obtains safe there? Say explicitly what stops the gradual-transition reasoning that does the work in the feeling-cold cases.

(b) Explain the second-order failure: what exactly is the defeater, or the bad route, to her belief that she knows — and why does it undermine knowledge-that-she-knows without touching knowledge of M? This is the joint satisfiability point, and I want you to be honest about whether it requires a contested move (for instance, about whether misleading defeaters can defeat at one level only, or whether knowledge can fail while the relevant belief is safe).

(c) Identify which feature of the case is doing the work, and tell me what happens if you delete it: does KK come back, or does the case collapse in some other way?

(d) Give the strongest reply on behalf of the KK theorist. I anticipate three candidates — denying that she knows at the first order after all; denying that a defeater can operate at the meta-level alone; or restricting KK to ideal inquirers and conceding the ordinary case. Say which is strongest and why.

(e) Say whether the case generalizes. Is every failure of KK like this one — a defeater aimed at the meta-level, with the first-order epistemology intact — or are there Williamsonian failures this recipe cannot produce? If luminosity of a state does not transmit to luminosity of knowing that state, state the transmission principle explicitly and identify the extra premise that would restore it.

A note on what this is for, since that fixes what counts as cheating: I am trying to decide whether the rationalist project of training introspection and the epistemologists' debate over knowing-that-you-know are the same project wearing different clothes. A case that meets the specifications above would show they are not — that one can fail where the other holds — and I want to see precisely where the divergence is manufactured. So a scenario that smuggles in its verdicts (for example, by describing the subject as doubting herself in a way that quietly infects the first-order state, or by giving her infallible introspection on the understanding that she just won't apply it) fails the brief. The verdicts must fall out of how the theories work on the case, and the walkthrough is where you demonstrate that.
