My philosophy of science reading group has spent a month on Goodman's new riddle of induction and has ended up in exactly the place the literature seems to have ended up: everyone restates their position with increasing confidence and nobody changes their mind. To set the scene briefly, in case you need the recap: Goodman's point is that all the emeralds observed so far being green is equally good evidence that they are "grue" — green if examined before some future time, blue otherwise — since the very same observations satisfy both hypotheses. The old riddle asked what justifies induction at all; the new riddle asks why we project "green" rather than "grue," and the standard answers split into camps my group keeps rotating through without converging. There are the naturalness people, who say "grue" is somehow an illegitimate, gerrymandered, or disjunctive predicate and only natural, projectible properties deserve induction. There are Goodman's own followers, who answer with entrenchment — "green" has a history of successful past projections that "grue" lacks — which strikes the rest of us as changing the subject from justification to psychology. There are the Bayesians, who say we simply assign "all emeralds are green" a higher prior, and that priors don't need to be justified one by one. And there are the dissolvers, who say the whole thing is a verbal trick resting on a time-indexed predicate and that once you see what "grue" actually is, there's no residual puzzle.

Here's my problem: each side has a standard counter, and the counters have been available for decades. The naturalness camp gets asked what "natural" means without either circularity (natural = the kind of thing we project) or a heavy-duty metaphysics of kinds that many of them disown. The entrenchment camp gets told that past successful projection of "green" only helps if we were entitled to project it in the past, which is the question again. The Bayesians get told that assigning the flat hypothesis a higher prior just because it's "simpler" either presupposes a notion of simplicity that grue was designed to make mysterious, or falls back on the very entrenchment story they were supposed to be replacing. So my group is stuck, and I notice the field looks similarly stuck: the responses are all well known, all well answered, and nobody has moved since roughly my professor's graduate career.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another tour of the positions. My working hypothesis is that the camps aren't actually disagreeing about one thing: the entrenchment camp is answering a descriptive question — why do humans in fact project "green"? — while the naturalness and simplicity camps are answering a normative or metaphysical one about what makes a hypothesis worthy of induction, and the dissolvers are answering a third question about predicate languages, and each side treats the others' answers as if they were failed attempts at its own question. But I'm not sure this is right, because the defenders of entrenchment and of Bayesian priors usually insist they are giving justifications, not merely explanations of our behavior. So: is this a substantive disagreement about induction, a verbal one about what counts as "justifying" a projection, or several distinct questions traveling under the single name "the new riddle"? And given whatever diagnosis you settle on, tell me what would actually count as progress here — a sharper distinction, a formal result, some empirical finding about how humans or ideal learners generalize, or an argument that the impasse is permanent and the riddle is doing something other than waiting to be solved.
