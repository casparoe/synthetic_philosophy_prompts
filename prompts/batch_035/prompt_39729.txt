I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm kicking the tires on a piece about how "fair split" algorithms are quietly showing up in divorce-mediation apps and treaty simulators, which is how I ended up auditing a game theory course at the university here and getting in over my head. The session on axiomatic bargaining did me in. Afterwards my friend — an econ postdoc I play pickup basketball with — walked me into an argument about the Nash solution versus the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution that I still can't find the exit to. Setup in case it's been a while: two parties, a convex set of feasible utility pairs (you can randomize between deals, hence convex), a disagreement point, and the whole question is just which point in the set counts as the fair split. You can normalize so that no-deal is (0,0) for both.

His argument: (1) if you delete an option that the fair split wouldn't have chosen anyway, the fair split shouldn't move — a restaurant running out of the one dish you weren't going to order doesn't change what you order; (2) never settle on a deal when a deal that's better for both of you is sitting right there; (3) mirror-image parties get a mirror-image split; (4) utility numbers are just bookkeeping for preferences, so stretching or shifting either party's units shouldn't change the actual agreement. (5) Nash's theorem: the only rule satisfying those four is the one that maximizes the product of the two players' gains over no-deal. (6) So the Kalai–Smorodinsky rule — pick the frontier point where each party comes away with the same fraction of their best-case payoff — must be trading on something wierd, because it lets possibilities nobody would ever agree to drag the split around.

And honestly, you can see his point. Take the set with corners (0,0), (1,0), (0,1), and (0.8, 0.9). Both parties' best case is 1, so Kalai–Smorodinsky picks the frontier point with equal coordinates, roughly (0.82, 0.82). Now delete the (0.8, 0.9) option — which K-S itself does not pick — and the "fair" split crashes to (0.5, 0.5). A never-chosen option was propping up the whole thing. (My friend does admit that the Nash bargainer in that same set picks exactly (0.8, 0.9), so "never-chosen" seems to depend on who's doing the choosing; he calls that a feature of his argument, and it bugs me in a way I cant articulate.) Here's why I can't just concede, though — I went home and checked his own rule against the intuition that if one side's possibilities get weakly better at every level of the other side's utility, that side's share shouldn't fall. Set A has a Pareto frontier (straight segments) running (0,1), (0.4, 0.8), (1, 0): Nash picks (0.5, 0.67). Set B's frontier runs (0,1), (0.4, 0.8), (0.8, 0.45), (1, 0) — it sits on or above set A's frontier at every level of player 1's utility, and player 1's best case is unchanged, so player 2's options got weakly better everywhere. Yet Nash now picks about (0.66, 0.58): player 2's share fell from 0.67 to 0.58 *because her prospects improved*. Kalai–Smorodinsky, for what it's worth, rises from about (0.57, 0.57) to (0.61, 0.61) there. My friend says the two principles are provably incompatible once you fix Pareto, symmetry, and scale invariance, so I can't keep both no matter what.

So which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd much rather keep (1), which feels like plain sanity, but I have this itch that the restaurant analogy is smuggling in an assumption about bargaining that just isn't true — something about there being a "what we'd order anyway" independent of the menu, when the menu might be part of what fixes our claims on each other. And if I'm right that something's off between (1) and the axiom Nash actually needs, I'd like to know exactly where the everyday version and the formal one come apart. Convince me one way or the other.
