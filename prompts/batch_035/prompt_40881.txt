I host a small podcast about why people do dumb stuff against their own better judgement (it started as a joke about my unused gym membership and somehow we're 40 episodes in now). Next week's guest is a behavioral scientist who thinks the whole ancient puzzle of weakness of will was basically solved once we understood hyperbolic discounting -- the bit where we value a cookie now over health in ten years way more than we value a cookie tomorrow over health in ten years and a day. His line is that there's no spooky "akrasia" and no broken will: when you pick the cake, that's just your real preference at that moment, and the old philosophers were chasing a ghost. When I push back he says something like "your present self and future self are basically two different people negotiating." I find this pretty convincing on paper but also weirdly insulting to my actual experience of lying in bed at 7am having a full internal argument about getting up and losing it.

Draft me eight to ten interview questions that go past having him restate the view. I want at least one that presses on the phenomenology -- the guilt, the sensation of arguing with yourself and caving -- and asks what a discount curve is supposed to *feel* like from the inside, since a curve that explains my choice doesn't obviously explain my shame about it. At least one should be about the tiny-timescale version: when I decide at 9am to skip dessert tonight and then order it at 9pm, the payoff is only hours away either way, so what exactly got discounted between breakfast and dinner? And one on the cases where giving in isn't even fun -- like procrastinating on my taxes in a miserable, anxious doomscrolling way -- because that really doesn't look like choosing a smaller-sooner reward unless misery itself counts as the reward.

For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply he's most likely to give (he leans hard on the multiple-selves framing and on the hot/cold empathy gap stuff, so anticipate those). And if you can fit in one closing question about whether his story takes the criticism out of weakness of will -- like, if caving was my genuine preference all along, is there anything left to explain, or did the akrasia just dissolve into ordinary choice -- and whether he thinks that means the ancient skeptics who said weakness of will is impossible were accidentally right, I'd love to end on that. Keep the questions conversational enough that I can actually ask them on air, not like a reading quiz.
