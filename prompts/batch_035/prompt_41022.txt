Some background, so the exchange makes sense. There is a well-known visual illusion, the colour phi phenomenon. A red spot is flashed briefly at one position on a screen; then, after a short interval, a green spot is flashed at a second position some distance away. Nothing moves — the screen is dark in between. Yet observers typically report seeing a single spot that travels from the first position to the second and changes colour partway across, usually from red to green. In Consciousness Explained (1991), Dennett uses this case to stage a clash between two explanations, which he labels with political metaphors. On the Stalinesque picture, the brain holds the incoming signals in a buffer for a moment, does some editing — inserting the illusory trajectory and the mid-route colour change — and only then delivers the result to consciousness; the experience is fabricated in advance, so that it never needs correcting afterwards. On the Orwellian picture, the experience is produced in real time, just as it seems to the subject; what gets falsified afterwards is the memory of it, in the way the Ministry of Truth in Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four rewrites yesterday's newspapers so the record never disagrees with the present line.

Now the disagreement. Two friends of mine in a postgraduate reading group — call them Mara and Jules — got stuck on this. Here is roughly how it went, as best as I can reconstruct it.

Mara: The Stalinesque picture has to be right, and the timing shows it. At the moment the red spot is flashed, the visual system has no way of knowing that a green spot is about to appear, or where. So it cannot at that moment put a colour-changing moving object into the experience. The only way the experience can contain the full trajectory — including the colour change, which depends on the second flash — is if the brain waits, assembles the whole sequence, and only then hands it over to consciousness. So the experience of the moving spot arrives later than it seems to, and what the subject is conscious of is the edited version.

Jules: That is too quick. Everything we are conscious of already lags behind the world by some tens of milliseconds — nerve conduction and cortical processing take time. Once you grant that, the delay you need is not an extra commitment; it is just the ordinary lag we already need for any experience at all. So your observation does not decide between your picture and mine. And I think mine is right: the subject did experience the moving, colour-changing spot roughly when it seemed they did; what is rewritten afterwards is the memory, not the experience. The brain is the Ministry of Truth: the event happened, and it is the record that is doctored.

Mara: If the experience happened in real time and only the memory was rewritten, then before the second flash is processed, the experience must already have contained the first stretch of the trajectory — the spot partway across the screen. But at that point the visual system could not yet know the trajectory, since it depends on where and when the second spot appears. So on your view the real-time experience would have to be partly about the future. Unless you want to say the brain is clairvoyant, the content of the experience cannot be fixed that early. It has to be fixed afterwards — which is exactly my picture.

Jules: I am not saying the content is fixed early. I am saying there is a fact about when the experience occurs, and that fact does not line up neatly with when its content is fixed. On my view the whole sequence is experienced as a unit, with the ordinary few-dozen-millisecond lag, and the editing you describe is simply part of how the sequence gets built before it enters experience at all. I will even grant you something smaller: that the later memory record is tidied up as well. But a real difference between us remains, and it matters. On your view, the subject was never conscious of anything that later gets revised — there is no first draft. On mine, there was an original experience, and it is the record of it that is falsified.

Mara: Then answer me this. On your view, was the subject conscious of the spot changing colour before the green flash had been processed in the brain? If yes, the experience contained information that was not yet available to the system. If no, then the original experience you keep defending did not include the very thing we are trying to explain — the colour change — and I no longer know what that original experience is supposed to have been. I am beginning to suspect there is no fact of the matter about when the content entered consciousness. Perhaps that is the real lesson of the case: the question we are fighting over presupposes some inner moment of presentation that does not exist, and the Stalinesque and Orwellian pictures are just two ways of drawing a line the brain never draws.

Jules: There I cannot follow you. Whatever else consciousness is, it cannot be indeterminate in its own chronology. Either the subject experienced the motion at a given time or they did not. If all this Multiple Drafts talk means that there is no answer to that question, it has not resolved our disagreement; it has declined to answer it. Experiences may be hard to date from the outside, but an episode of experience is not the kind of thing that can lack a time of occurrence — that is part of what it is to be an episode.

They broke off there. I could not follow all of it, and I honestly do not know who came out ahead.
