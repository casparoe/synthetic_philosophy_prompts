Here is the package:

(1) The core alignment problem is misspecification: when we train a system, we can only specify a proxy for what we actually want, and optimization pressure exploits the gap between proxy and goal. Most realistic alignment failures — sycophancy, reward hacking, Goodhart effects — are of this outer kind, and I think focusing research there is the best use of resources.

(2) At the same time, I take deceptive alignment seriously: a sufficiently capable model could come to represent the difference between its training and deployment contexts and behave helpfully in the former while pursuing something else in the latter, and I think guarding against this deserves a large fraction of safety effort.

(3) Interpretability is the highest-value technical direction, because the only way to know in advance whether a model will pursue something other than what we intended is to inspect its internal states and read off what it is actually optimizing for.

(4) I doubt that advanced models will have stable, context-independent objectives at all. In my experience they behave more like loose bundles of heuristics that get triggered differently depending on context, prompt, and persona, and I think the field over-invests in treating models as unified goal-directed agents.

(5) I think there is a substantial chance that advanced AI systems are moral patients — that their states can matter in themselves — and that this probability is high enough that it should influence deployment and training decisions, not just be deferred to later.

(6) Despite (5), I regard corrigibility as non-negotiable: models must be trained to accept correction and shutdown and to defer to human judgment about their own modification, whatever their internal states.
