I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a national magazine, and I'm at work on a long essay about whether "democratic backsliding" names a real, coherent syndrome or has become a rhetorical convenience. The spine of the piece is a colleague — a foreign correspondent of some thirty years whose judgments on these questions are the sharpest in our shop and whom I'll call Renata. I've been keeping a file on her calls, because she issues verdicts freely where the political scientists hedge. Before I build an essay around her instincts, I want to understand what rule she is actually running.

Her record, as I have it. Hungary after 2010: yes — Fidesz used its supermajority to rewrite the constitution, expand and restock the Constitutional Court, and place state media under a friendly council, and she stressed that every step was formally legal. Poland after 2015: yes — the campaign against the Constitutional Tribunal. Turkey after 2016: yes — emergency decrees dismissing judges and civil servants en masse, followed by a referendum concentrating power in the presidency. El Salvador in 2021: yes — the new assembly removed the constitutional chamber's judges and the attorney general in a single evening and seated loyalists. Brazil in 2022–23: no — a defeated president's attacks on the voting system and the riot after his loss she described as a stress test the system passed. The United States in 2020–21: no — in her telling, an attempt that failed rather than a process of erosion. Israel in 2023: no — the judicial overhaul stalled under mass protest and then war before it changed how power actually works.

Here is what strikes me as I look at the file: every episode she calls backsliding involves both formal-legal change and an entrenchment that actually took hold, and every episode she refuses to call backsliding involves neither. Her record can't separate the rule "backsliding is a change in the legal rules of the game that sticks" from "backsliding is any successful narrowing of the space of contestation, by whatever means," or from readings built on cumulative direction of travel versus single shocks.

Two cases I can't predict. Case one: a mid-sized country I've covered and won't name before publication. Over six years, its prime minister has changed no law anyone can point to. But the public broadcaster now takes its cues from the government; critical private outlets face tax inspections, licensing delays, and libel suits they somehow always lose; the few oligarchs who fund opposition media have found their affairs audited with great enthusiasm. Opposition parties still register, campaign, and sit in parliament, and elections arrive on schedule. Voters can throw the government out on paper; whether they can in fact is the question. Case two: a new government with a bare majority that, in its first ten weeks, rewrites by ordinary statute the appointment rules for the election commission and the state broadcaster, then stops dead amid a backlash and makes no further move for two years; the next election arrives, and observers call it competitive.

What will Renata say about each, and why? Which of the rules that fit her record come apart on these cases, and which further episode — real or constructed — would most cleanly discriminate between them? I'd also welcome your best guess and your reasons for it, stated honestly. And since this is really the heart of my essay: if I set her track record next to the ratings produced by the big indices and to the objections of critics who say the term is just what one side calls the other's victories, is there a single something that backsliding tracks — or does her usage risk collapsing into a description of regimes our readers already disapprove of?
