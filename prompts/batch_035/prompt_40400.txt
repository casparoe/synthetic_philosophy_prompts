I'm a third-year undergraduate enrolled in a seminar on artificial minds and machine ethics, and I've chosen to write my final paper on whether reinforcement learning agents could suffer — specifically on the cluster of issues around treating reward signals as a form of valence. The professor has asked us not to survey the literature but to identify the questions in our topic that most deserve attention and to argue for that ranking. Since I can develop only two or three questions in the depth the paper requires, I want to get the prioritization right, and I'd like your help thinking it through.

To be concrete about what I take the open questions in this area to be, here is my tentative list (please tell me if I'm missing something important or if some of these are better treated as sub-parts of one question):

1. Whether reward, in the machine-learning sense, has the right structural features to be valence at all. A reward signal is a scalar used for credit assignment: it tells the learning algorithm which states and actions to revisit or avoid. But in humans, plenty of signals with that corrective function appear not to be experienced as anything — e.g., the error signals used in motor adaptation. Why think the scalar at the heart of an RL loop is any more of a candidate for felt quality than those? The one consideration cutting the other way that I know of is the distinction affective neuroscience draws between "wanting" and "liking": the systems that drive learning and the systems that constitute felt pleasure apparently come apart even in animals, which suggests that a signal's role in shaping behavior doesn't by itself settle its valence status — but it doesn't tell me which side of that divide an artificial reward signal falls on.

2. Whether negative valence would even amount to suffering. My intuition is that suffering involves more than momentary unpleasant signals: perhaps the capacity to represent a state as persisting over time, some minimal model of oneself as the one in the state, or at least the difference between escapable and inescapable aversive situations. A simple tabular agent receiving a stream of -1s during training doesn't seem to be doing anything beyond adjusting a table. But a model-based agent with a world model that simulates futures, and that "prefers" some of those futures over others, at least raises the question of whether it represents those futures as bad-for-it rather than merely as low-value. Is the difference between these cases a matter of degree or of kind, and does that matter morally?

3. Whether the substrate matters. In biological organisms, valence appears tightly bound up with the regulation of the organism's own body — interoception, homeostasis, the fact that the signals are about the state of the living system itself. On a view like that, no amount of architectural sophistication in an agent whose "body" is an emulator would produce valence, because the signal is never about anything the system needs to survive. Alternatively, this might be a prejudice for our own carbon-based implementation of the function. This question seems to me the least tractable of the three, since I can't see what would count as evidence either way, but I may be wrong about that.

4. How to act under the resulting moral uncertainty. Even granting that these questions stay open, we face a decision about how much weight to give to the possibility of suffering in systems that clearly lack the features we consider paradigmati — and there is a cost to over-attribution as well as under-attribution, since diluting moral concern wastes protection on things that don't warrant it and undermines the case for protecting things that do. I worry this question is downstream of 1–3, though it's also the only one that's urgent in any way, since it's the one that actually governs what anyone should do now.

For the prioritization: my paper is around 7,000 words, and beyond the paper I'm deciding whether to pursue something in this neighborhood for graduate work, so I care about which of these will still be open and still be interesting in five years, not just which can carry a term paper. Please rank the questions and defend the ranking. For each, I'd like to know what makes it worth the space rather than merely unresolved — the criteria I have in mind are importance, tractability for someone with philosophy training but only a working knowledge of RL (not the ability to run experiments), how many of the other questions depend on its answer, and whether anything in the next few years (e.g., wider deployment of agents with learned world models and long-horizon training) would move it up or down the list. I'm especially interested in whether you think question 4 is genuinely downstream of the others, as I suspect, or whether the uncertainty about what to do is in some way prior to the metaphysics — and if you'd add, remove, or merge anything on my list, please say so and explain how that changes the ordering. I'd rather the answer focus on the reasoning than on naming positions or authors.
