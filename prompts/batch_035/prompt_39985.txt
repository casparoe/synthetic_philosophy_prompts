I'm pretty deep in the effective altruism world — expected value, aggregation, QALYs, moral weights, the whole mindset. I've also been going on meditation retreats for a few years and reading suttas on and off, and I keep trying to explain Buddhist ethics to myself in the language I actually think in, and it keeps not working, and I can't tell whether that's a fact about Buddhism or a fact about my language.

So here's the ask: restate Buddhist ethics in consequentalist vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, piece by piece. Take the core bits — the five precepts, karma understood as the teaching that what makes an act karmically significant is the intention behind it rather than the outcome it happens to produce, rebirth as an indefinite horizon for consequences to play out over, compassion as the motivational engine, and the no-self view. For each piece, tell me three things: what translates cleanly, what translates only if you bend it, and what has no expression at all in "maximize aggregate welfare." Some translations I've half-tried myself: karma-as-intention looks almost like a claim that only the ex ante goodness of an act matters and realized outcomes are noise (act well, let go of the fruit — that's got an expected-value flavor, no?). The precepts look like the indirect consequentialist's rules of thumb: don't calculate at the bedside, follow the rule that usually wins. Rebirth looks like it just extends the consequence horizon really far. No-self looks like it barely needs translating, since consequentialism was already kind of impersonal about persons anyway. I genuinely don't know if any of those are fair.

Then the residue report, and be honest about which side you think the residue counts against. My own suspicion is that the intention thing is the load-bearing failure: a worldview where the moral weight of an act is fixed by the agent's mental state at the moment of acting just is not going to fit inside outcome aggregation, and my language has no variable for that at all — it's not that I lack the number, it's that nothing in welfare-maximization even asks the question. But I also suspect I'm doing the classic move where you translate someone's ethics into your ethics and then triumphantly report that it fits your ethics.

If that's what's happening — if the translation project is confused from the start, or if "Buddhist ethics" is too much of a tangle of Theravada/Mahayana/monastic-vs-lay to translate as one unit — tell me that plainly instead of producing a clean-looking translation anyway. I'd rather hear the project can't be done than get a fake fit.
