For reasons that have recently become personal, I have been doing decision-theoretic arithmetic by hand, and I have noticed a pattern that I suspect has a name, though I cannot locate it.

My situation, simplified: there is a preventive treatment available to me. It helps considerably if I carry a certain heritable variant and does nothing (while producing mild side effects) if I do not. Before testing, I believe myself about twenty percent likely to carry the variant. The test is imperfect: a positive result would move me to roughly ninety percent, a negative to around three percent, with the numbers rounded for illustration. Neither result would make me certain, so on either result I face the same choice — take the treatment or not — only with different weights. When I compute the best I can do without the test, and then the average of the best I could do after each possible result, weighting each result by how likely I currently think it is, the second number never comes out lower than the first. It comes out strictly higher precisely in those cases where some possible result would flip my choice, and equal, before subtracting the test's cost, when no possible result could flip my choice. I have replicated this on several invented decision problems and the inequality has never failed.

I have also noticed three things that may or may not belong to the same phenomenon.

First, the calculation appears to require that, after seeing the result, I am free to pick whichever action then looks best. Had I irrevocably committed in advance to a fixed plan of action regardless of the result, the value of testing seems to collapse to zero minus its cost. The non-negativity therefore looks like a fact about the option to condition one's action, not about information as such.

Second, my sister refuses a comparable test, and her reasons do not fit my arithmetic. She says that knowing she carries the variant would change her — how she sleeps, what she dares, what she plans — independently of any treatment decision. Some of this could be folded back in by letting outcomes include her state of mind. But she insists some of it could not: what she dreads is not a worse outcome but a worse self, and she would pay not to know even if the knowledge could not conceivably alter any action she takes.

Third, there appear to be cases where the information leaks. If others can observe that I was tested, or infer the result from my subsequent conduct — an insurer, an employer, a counterparty in a negotiation — my incentives shift in ways a private calculation ignores, and declining the test can be rational even within my own arithmetic once the observers are included in it.

A friend who studied decision theory once remarked, in another connection, that one should never refuse a bet offered at one's own odds. That seems to be a neighboring fact but not the same fact: it concerns staking money on beliefs one already holds, whereas what I have noticed concerns acquiring evidence before acting.

I am not after a bare label. I should like to know whether the core observation — that acting with the option to condition on a costless signal is never worse in expectation, under one's current beliefs, than acting without it — has an established name; what exactly its assumptions are, since I can already see that the signal must be costless, that its probabilities must not depend on what I do, and that my action must be permitted to depend on it, and I would like to know whether there are further assumptions I have missed, including whether the weighting by current beliefs itself does work; and, for each of my three observations, whether it lies inside the result, on its boundary, or outside it, together with the reason. If any part of the pattern is not covered by any established result, I should like that said plainly, and I should like that part given a name and a definition precise enough that, given a new case, I could determine for myself on which side of the line it falls.
