You are a professional philosopher of history with expertise in both ancient and modern theories of historical cycles, asked to help a graduate student prepare for a seminar discussion.

Here is my situation. My reading group has been working through cyclical and linear conceptions of history, with selections from Polybius on the cycle of constitutional forms, Ibn Khaldun on the rise and decay of dynasties, Spengler on the life-spans of cultures, and Toynbee on challenge and response. During a debate about whether the last few centuries are best read as a progressive, roughly linear story — say, of expanding moral concern or consolidating legal order — a fellow student shut down the progressive reading with the slogan "history repeats itself," gesturing at our Ibn Khaldun week as if that settled it. Someone else countered by quoting Marx's quip from the opening of The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte, where Marx writes that Hegel observed that all great world-historic facts and personages appear, so to speak, twice, adding that "the first time as tragedy, the second time as farce." The counterer claimed that Marx's line, far from asserting repetition, actually denies it: recurrence with a change of register — tragedy giving way to farce — is not the same event happening twice, so the slogan as used must be false. I left the session unsure what exactly was being asserted, denied, or precisified, and I want to come to the next meeting with the analytic distinctions laid out.

Please think carefully and begin with some brief private brainstorming about what work the slogan was being made to do in that exchange, before writing your answer. Then give me a numbered list I can bring to seminar. For the slogan "history repeats itself," lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, ordered roughly from weakest to strongest. Candidates you should consider and distinguish include, at least: (a) a near-tautology to the effect that the same kinds of situation keep arising, so it is always instructive to study the past; (b) a loose inductive claim that certain patterns — Ibn Khaldun's dynastic consolidation and softening across generations, or constitutional degeneration of the sort Polybius anatomized — have recurred often enough that we should expect them again; (c) a claim about generational forgetting, on which repetition is driven by the turnover of people rather than by any structure in events themselves; (d) a strong metaphysical thesis in the neighborhood of Spengler, on which entire cultures are organisms with fixed life-cycles, such that later civilizations must pass through stages that earlier ones passed through; and (e) a claim, in the spirit of Toynbee, that history is driven by a recurring mechanism — challenges met or not met — whose operation across cases explains whatever rhyme we observe.

For each reading, tell me: whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false; what evidence would bear on it, given that we cannot rerun history; and, crucially, whether it would actually support the use my classmate put it to — namely, refuting a progressive reading of the last few centuries. Pay particular attention to whether any of these readings is in genuine tension with a linear-progressive story, or whether cyclical and linear claims can simply be true at different scales or of different variables (dynasties cycling while, say, some cumulative trend persists underneath). Finally, address the Marx exchange directly: does the tragedy-then-farce line refute any of the readings on your list, does it precisify one of them (and if so, which), or does it change the subject from recurrence of events to recurrence of self-interpretations — actors borrowing the costumes and vocabularies of their predecessors? Close by saying which reading people who repeat the slogan most probably intend, and whether that intended reading survives scrutiny. Please write in clear prose, keep the numbered list tight and quotable, and do not pad the answer with biographical background on the theorists; I need the distinctions, not the textbook.
