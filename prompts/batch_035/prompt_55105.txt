Write a dialogue between two political theorists who disagree about how a collective — specifically, a three-judge court deciding a contract case — should aggregate its members' judgments into a group decision.

The case is an instance of the discursive dilemma. Liability requires two premises: that a valid contract existed, and that the defendant breached it. Judge A holds that both premises are true and votes liable. Judge B holds that the contract was valid but was not breached, and votes not liable. Judge C holds that there was a breach but that no valid contract existed, and votes not liable. Thus a majority affirms each premise, while a majority rejects liability.

One character argues that the court must speak with a single consistent voice, and therefore should decide the premises by majority vote and derive the conclusion from them — even though this means overriding the majority's vote on the conclusion itself. The other character argues that each proposition, premise or conclusion, deserves an independent majority vote, and that any procedure which lets judges vote on some propositions but not others smuggles in a hidden agenda-setter who decides which propositions get decided directly. Both characters should press their strongest objections on the other: for instance, that premise-based decisions are manipulable and condescending toward the dissenting judges, versus that majority voting on all propositions yields a court that simultaneously asserts P, asserts Q, and denies the consequence of P and Q — an absurd collective agent. They should also consider whether the disagreement is really about consistency or about whether the court is a single agent at all. The dialogue should end without a tidy resolution, but with each character having sharpened the other's position.
