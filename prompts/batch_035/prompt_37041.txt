I've been reading up on that old debate between the judge Devlin and the professor Hart about whether the law should punish things that are immoral but don't hurt anybody (it came up arguing with my brother-in-law about why adultery laws are still on the books in some states). His argument is basically Devlin's: society is held together by a shared moral code the way a church is held together by its creed — people can deviate privately all they want, but once the code erodes the whole community comes apart. So on his view the law is allowed to punish private vice just like it punishes treason, since both are supposed threats to society's survival. Can you audit this analogy for me? I want the correspondence table — what exactly maps onto what (society = church? moral code = creed? the private sinner = the heretic?) — then where the mapping breaks. Stuff I can already half-see: a heretic publicly renounces the creed while the deviant keeps everything behind closed doors, you can leave a church but not a society, and societies seem to have survived plenty of moral change (slavery) without dissolving. The big question though: does "society needs SOME shared morality to hold together" actually get you to "society needs intolerance of THIS particular conduct", or is that a scope jump the analogy is smuggling in? Basically I want to know whether my brother-in-law's conclusion survives once all the disanalogies are on the table, or wether the analogy was doing all the work, and if it fails what's a better analogy for the job.
