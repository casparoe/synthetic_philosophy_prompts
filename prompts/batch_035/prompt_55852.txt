you're a satirist with serious training in the history of ethics, so I'm hoping you can help me with a piece for my utilitarianism seminar. We're reading Bentham's unpublished manuscripts on sexual morality — the ones where he argues, shockingly early, that consensual acts condemned as "unnatural" produce pleasure and no pain for anyone, that the disgust people feel is a cause of the condemnation rather than an argument for it, and that the legal punishment is really the only injury in the whole picture. I want to pair that with a parody so my students can feel the dialectic instead of just summarizing it.

Write me a parody agony-aunt column, around 600 words, formatted as 3 or 4 exchanges of letter and reply. The columnist is a pompous traditionalist moralist with a newspaper column, and every letter comes from the same maddeningly calm utilitarian correspondent who keeps politely asking him to specify exactly which pains, suffered by whom, the condemned conduct is supposed to cause. The columnist has to keep manufacturing harms — divine displeasure, the corruption of the young, the weakening of some national moral fibre, population effects, slippery slopes toward acts even more unspeakable — and the reader should get to watch each argument deflate in real time as the utilitarian applies the felicific calculus to it.

The humor has to come from the actual dialectic, not from making the columnist an idiot. His moves should be the real moves people made (and frankly still make) when they can't produce a harmed party, just compressed and slightly exaggerated, so a historian of ethics would nod along instead of wincing. The utilitarian correspondent should never be smug — the comedy works better if he's genuinely earnest and a little apologetic about having to keep asking. End the final exchange with the columnist falling back on pure disgust, stated as if it were a conclusion rather than a feeling, and the utilitarian warmly thanking him for finally saying so plainly.

One more constraint: don't let the piece become a sermon on tolerance. It's satire of an argumentative strategy, not a victory lap, and it should stay deadpan throughout.

Before you write, do a short brainstorm: list the harm-fabrications that would be most historically plausible for a moralist of roughly Bentham's era, and rank them by how funny they are at the moment they collapse. Then write the piece. Keep the columnist's voice period-appropriate but readable — think Victorian newspaper columnist with a high opinion of his own delicacy, seperate paragraphs for each reply, and a sign-off flourish each time that gets a little more strained as the column goes on. Thats the arc I care about most: each reply confident, each one slightly less so, until the last one is basically just the queasiness with a bow on it.
