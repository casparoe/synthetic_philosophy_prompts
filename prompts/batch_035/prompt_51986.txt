I'm writing a paper section arguing that the usual story about the leveling-down objection -- that it hits egalitarianism but leaves prioritarianism untouched -- is too quick, and I want a concrete case to anchor it. Help me build the case.

Here's the view I want it to target. I'm calling it relative prioritarianism: it keeps the prioritarian idea that benefits to the worse off count for more, but it makes the weights depend on relative standing instead of absolute welfare. A benefit to someone counts for more the further below the group average they are, and a loss to someone counts for less the further above average they are -- with nothing ruling out that a loss to someone far enough above average comes out neutral, or even as a small improvement. The motivation I want built in: comparative deprivation. The same bad toothache seems more urgent when you're the only one suffering in the room than when everyone around you is in the same boat, so urgency should track local context, not some cosmic welfare scale.

Now the case. Requirements:

1. Realistic, no sci-fi, one paragraph. A hospital or hospice setting seems natural but I'm open to other ideas.
2. The leveling down must be pure: one person ends up worse off and absolutely nobody gains anything -- no budget savings, no changes in anyone's knowledge or attitudes, no side effects on third parties. This is the hard part. Real cases usually smuggle in some compensating good, and I want all of those scrubbed out.
3. Small numbers, arithmetic doable in your head.
4. Then the walkthrough: state what (a) a total utilitarian, (b) an ordinary prioritarian with fixed, seperable concave weights, (c) a telic egalitarian (the kind that thinks inequality itself is bad, apart from its effects), and (d) the relative prioritarian each say about the two states, with actual arithmetic. For (b), the verdict should come out the same no matter which concave weighting you pick -- point out why, if you can.
5. Tell me which feature of the case or of the view is doing the work, what happens to the verdicts if you strip that feature out, and what a defender of relative prioritarianism should say in reply -- the honest options, including biting the bullet and Temkin-style "pro tanto good but bad all things considered" moves. Also flag what the case can't show: I know it doesn't touch ordinary prioritarianism itself.

One thing I'll insist on: don't stipulate any of the verdicts. The numbers have to generate them.
