I keep catching myself giving inconsistent answers about when you're allowed to compare how good or bad things are for different people, and I want to know whether there's a principle under my gut verdicts or whether I'm just muddled. Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. A triage nurse has one dose of the strong painkiller and two burn patients. One is screaming and begging; the other, burned about the same, is quiet and just says it hurts. I think it's obviously fine for the nurse to give it to the one who's screaming. Ranking their pain seems completely legitimate here.
2. Me splitting the last slice of cake between my two kids, both swearing they want it more than anything in the world. I don't think either kid's testimony settles who'd enjoy it more. Coin flip or split it.
3. The same nurse later asks patients to rate their pain 0-10 and uses the numbers to decide who gets seen first. Fine by me — treating the numbers as comparable across patients seems right.
4. A cost-effectiveness analysis ranking charities: cataract surgeries vs. malaria nets, using something like QALYs. I accept this completely. If someone told me these comparisons were meaningless, I'd think they were confussed.
5. Willingness to pay: a rich guy would pay $10,000 for a concert ticket; a broke student would pay $500, which is actually a bigger sacrifice for him. I do NOT think the rich guy thereby wants to go more. Money doesn't measure intensity of desire across people, at all.
6. Comparing my friend's annoyance at a delayed flight to my other friend's grief over her mother's death. Trivially fine — the grief is worse, and I'd say so without hesitation.
7. A jury putting a dollar figure on a plaintiff's pain and suffering. This one makes me uneasy in a way the others don't, though I can't quite say it's wrong.

My economist friend is a Robbins-style skeptic — thinks all seven are meaningless because there's no common unit across minds — and on my bad days I half agree with him. But my verdicts above don't agree with him even on the bad days (look at 6). And some of them don't sit well with each other: 3 and 5 both turn numbers into rankings, and I like one and not the other; 4 and 7 both aggregate suffering into a figure and I'm fine with one and queasy about the other; and I can't decide whether 1 and 2 differ because of something real (the screaming isn't strategic, the kids are) or just because burns matter more than cake.

My tentative stab at a principle is something like: comparisons are OK when the states are similar in kind, and not OK when you're comparing across different kinds of states. But I can't get it to come out right on 5 vs 6, and I suspect it's wrong somewhere else too. Don't be gentle with it — if it's junk, say so.
