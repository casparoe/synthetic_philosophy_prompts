Last weekend a friend and I spent nearly four hours arguing about illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — the view, associated with writers like Keith Frankish and Daniel Dennett, that phenomenal properties as standardly conceived do not exist, and that consciousness science should explain why we are so strongly inclined to judge that they do. My friend's position eventually crystallized into three complaints: that the view is self-refuting (an illusion of experience would itself be an experience), that it achieves its plausibility only by quietly redefining consciousness in functional terms and then declaring victory, and that no empirical discovery could ever favor it over realism, since illusionism claims our introspective judgments are unreliable exactly where realism claims they are veridical. I ended the evening unconvinced by him but also unable to say precisely where he goes wrong.

In two weeks I will record a podcast episode with a philosopher who defends this strong illusionist position. Please draft eight to ten interview questions for me, in the order I might reasonably ask them. The questions should engage the actual content of the position rather than inviting restatement of it, and I want the following covered somewhere: (1) at least one question pressing the self-refutation objection my friend raised, with a follow-up keyed to the standard reply that the illusion consists in misdirected judgments or dispositions rather than in some further inner show; (2) at least one question on the "quasi-phenomenal" or illusionist reformulation of what introspection detects — in particular, whether that reformulation preserves anything worth calling consciousness, or whether illusionism collapses into eliminativism about consciousness altogether; and (3) at least one question on whether illusionism is a substantive, testable thesis or instead something closer to a reinterpretive stance one adopts in advance, and what it would take for the illusionist to regard the view as refuted. Beyond those three, include at least two further questions of your own devising that engage live pressure points in the literature, such as how the illusionist handles the knowledge argument or the conceivability of zombies, or why the intuition that there is something it is like to be us has proven so resistant to debunking. For every question, append a follow-up keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give, so that I can push past the first-line response. Where a question draws on my friend's objections, phrase it in a way that gives the guest a fair chance to answer rather than ambushing her.
