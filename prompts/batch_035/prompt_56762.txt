I teach a second-year course called Space and Time — physics and philosophy joint honours students, mostly — and every year when we get to the block universe I tell them my own view is "obviously fine" and then change the subject. This year I promised them a proper statement of where I actually stand, and writing it out I've started to worry it doesn't hold together. Here's the list:

1. Special relativity is literally true about the structure of spacetime — the relativity of simultaneity isn't an artefact of our notation, it's how the world is.
2. There's no fact of the matter about which distant events are simultaneous; simultaneity is at bottom a convention for carving up spacetime.
3. Time genuinely passes. The future is open — future events haven't happened yet, in the same straighforward sense that past ones have.
4. Reality isn't perspectival. There's one world, and if something is real it's real full stop, not "real relative to observer A". Observers differ in descriptions, not in facts.
5. The passage of time can't be an illusion, because an illusion of passage would itself have to unfold in time — a succession of experiences presupposes succession, not the other way round.
6. When we do the Rietdijk–Putnam setup (the two walkers passing each other in the street, Andromeda, the invasion fleet already "decided" for one of them and not the other), I tell my students the argument fails: the transitivity step only works if simultaneity carries ontological weight, and I've just spent a lecture telling them it doesn't.

Item 6 I'm fairly confident about. Items 1 and 2 look like they flatly contradict each other but I don't think they do — though check me on that. And then there's something I can't quite pin down around 3 and 4: my co-teacher says I can't keep objective becoming if there's no distinguished global "now" for it to happen along, and if becoming goes observer-relative instead, that seems to violate 4 — but I can't tell whether he's found a real problem or is just repeating the block-universe party line.

So: go through the pairs, tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only look that way, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one say what would have to give and what giving it would cost me. I keep being told the escape route is a hidden preferred foliation in the Lorentz style, which strikes me as an embarassing price to pay — but tell me straight if I'm wrong about that too. Also, please write the answer in British English (realise, colour, etc.).
