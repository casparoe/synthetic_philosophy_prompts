I'm a program officer at a small private foundation that funds math-heavy research across the sciences — mostly modeling projects, biology, climate, epidemiology, some economics, a bit of everything. We get roughly twenty proposals a cycle and I have to write a funding recomendation on each one in under half an hour, with written reasons the board can read, and I usually can't call up the proposer to ask what they mean. My actual problem: a huge fraction of these proposals are "let's take the math that worked great in domain X and port it to domain Y," and I have no principled way to tell the promising ports from the cargo cults.

Here's my half-baked theory, take it or leave it. I read Wigner's old essay years ago — the one about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences — and everyone remembers the mysticism, but what stuck with me is a worry in the opposite direction. The math that works tends to be math that grew up tangled with the phenomena it now describes: calculus came from people staring at moving bodies, probability from gamblers and insurers, and so on. And the corollary I've come to beleive: the "unreasonable effectiveness" is partly a selection effect. We keep the fields where the math worked and quietly drop the ones where it didn't, so of course the surviving success stories look miraculous. (Maybe I'm actually remembering some later response to Wigner rather than Wigner himself, but the point is mine now.) If that's roughly right, then the question for a proposal isn't "is math powerful?" but "was this particular piece of math developed under selective pressure from phenomena like this one, or is it being asked to work somewhere it has no track record?"

What I want from you: a decision procedure I can actually run in twenty minutes per proposal. The questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it should stop and say "don't fund on these grounds" (I can still fund it because I like the person or the question, but the procedure shouldn't pretend the math will carry it), and which questions are really about scientific taste that no procedure can settle, so it should flag them for me instead of answering them. It has to work when I can't consult the proposer, and it can't assume I know the math myself.

Then run it on three real-ish proposals from my pile: (1) someone wants to model collective decision-making in ant colonies using the stochastic machinery that's worked for particle systems, claiming the analogy is deep; (2) someone wants to port symplectic geometry — the machinery that organizes planetary orbits — onto ecosystem dynamics, on the grounds that both are systems with conserved quantities; (3) an economist who wants to model the spread of scientific fashions with the epidemiology of infectious diseases. I'll admit upfront I privately think the third one is fashion-math itself, so if the procedure green-lights it, that's the answer I won't like — you tell me whether the procedure is right anyway. And be honest about where this whole framing breaks down: if my selection-effect theory is wrong, what does the procedure get wrong with it?

Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I just want the procedure.
