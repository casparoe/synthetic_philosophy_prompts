I'm in a second-year moral philosophy tutorial, and we've spent the term on the question of why promises bind at all. My tutorial partner Priya has turned out to have unusually firm intuitions across the cases our instructor keeps throwing at us, and we've started keeping a log of her verdicts because they don't fit any single theory cleanly. So far she holds: (1) breaking a promise to help a friend move in order to go to a concert, where the friend relied and was stranded, is plainly wrong; (2) a promise made privately to a dying hospice patient that you will visit her grave every year — no one else knows of it, she has died, and nobody could ever find out about a breach — is still wrong to break, though she adds that attending your own child's emergency surgery that day would clearly excuse it; (3) a promise to take a grandmother with advancing dementia to the botanical garden each spring remains binding even after a bad week leaves her with no memory of the promise, no expectation, and no capacity to notice whether you show up; (4) a promise extorted by a blackmailer under threat is not binding in the slightest, and reneging once safe does no wrong; (5) a promise to cat-sit that the friend verbally releases you from before any reliance is fully dissolved; and (6) a promise made in a language the promisee doesn't understand — so that she took the words as small talk and never recognized a promise was made — creates no obligation at all.

Our tutorial has a long-running debate going about which rule Priya is implicitly running: something like a social-practice or convention view, on which the wrong lies in abusing the shared institution of promising; something like a consent or normative-power view, on which a valid promise is the exercise of a power to change your own permissions, regardless of who knows or relies; or perhaps a third view on which the binding force has something to do with the promisor's own agency or integrity rather than with the promisee at all. I can see how each of these accommodates most of her six verdicts, though case 6 seems to squeeze the pure practice view and case 2 and 3 seem to squeeze anything that makes the promisee's expectations or reliance essential.

Here is the case we'll be discussing next week, and I want your prediction before I walk in. When my mother died last spring, an acquaintance of hers — someone I barely know — cornered me at the reception while I was visibly grieving, wept about how no one trustworthy remained in her life, and extracted from me a promise to serve as executor of her estate, unpaid, a role I've since learned involves years of tedious work. There was no threat, no coercion, and no false statement of fact; she did, however, time and stage the whole exchange with real skill, and in hindsight I don't think I would have promised anything under normal conditions. She has mildly relied already — she told her lawyer she'd found someone — and she has not released me. Given Priya's six verdicts, predict what she will say about whether I'm bound, lay out the candidate rules that fit her record and what each predicts here, tell me which prediction you'd bet on and why, and describe the one further case that would most cleanly discriminate between the rules you think are still live. End your reply with a single final line of the form "Verdict: bound" or "Verdict: not bound," stating your best guess at Priya's ruling on the executor case.
