Before we read anything else on repugnant markets, I want the goalposts fixed. Write the desiderata: what an adequate theory of which things may not permissibly be bought and sold has to deliver. It must get verdicts right on the hard cases, not just name them — a legal, regulated kidney market as in Iran; vote selling; surge pricing of bottled water and generators after a hurricane; commercial surrogacy arranged across a steep wealth gap; paying schoolchildren to read books; and Florida's dwarf-tossing ban, where the state prohibited consenting adults from being paid for a stunt purely on grounds of dignity. It must also respect the distinctions that public debate collapses: wrongness located in the transaction's effects on the participants (exploitation, desperation, asymmetric information) versus wrongness located in what selling does to the good itself (corrupting its meaning); and condemning the sale versus condemning the background inequality that makes someone willing to sell in the first place. It should have something to say about what economists, following Alvin Roth, call repugnant transactions — exchanges third parties want prohibited even though no participant is harmed and nothing's meaning is obviously corrupted. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are prejudices of one camp, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that part I most want to see. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence: the handful of requirements that do most of the work. Then give the case behind each, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
