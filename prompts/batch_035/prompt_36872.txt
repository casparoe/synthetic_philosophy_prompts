I'm a retired physicist — spent most of my career in statistical mechanics, so I've spend a lot of time around divergent series and the tricks people use to tame them. Now that I'm retired I've gotten into decision theory, and I keep coming back to the Pasadena paradox: games whose expectation is conditionally convergent or worse, so the "expected value" depends on the order you sum the terms in, and the naive answer is undefined. Physicists ran into this kind of thing constantly and mostly shrugged — we regularized, renormalized, or restricted attention to the questions that were well-posed, and usually the infinity was a sign the theory needed more structure rather than that the answers were nonsense.

So here's what I want your view on: of the open problems around undefined expected values, which matters most, and for whom? For instance — is the key question whether there's a principled convergence condition that tells you when expectation maximization is licensed at all (and what to do when it fails), or whether there's a defensible ordering/summation rule for the bad cases, or whether the whole thing is handled by coarse-graining to a finite 'small world' where the sums converge? Which of those is upstream of the others? And separately, how much does any of this actually infect ordinary decision-making versus being a curiosity that only bites in the corner cases — St. Petersburg-ish philanthropy, Pascal's mugging, that sort of thing? I have the math background and maybe a few years of free time, so rank the questions by what's worth an ex-physicist actually working on, and defend the ordering — including which ones you'd tell me to drop because my physicist intuitions about "just regularize it" are exactly what will mislead me.
