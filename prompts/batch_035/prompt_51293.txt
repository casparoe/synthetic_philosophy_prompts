been lurking lesswrong and the ea forum for like a decade now, and one thing that keeps coming up whenever people argue about s-risks or whether a mostly-aligned AI could still be catastrophic is negative utilitarianism — like, "an AI that only cared about suffering could conclude that painlessly switching everything off is the best action," that whole genre of argument. and i realized recently that despite having read a hundred comment threads about it, i couldn't actually state the view precisely. so i asked a friend who watched some youtube philosophy videos, and he sent me a video that summarizes it like this: "Negative utilitarianism is the view that only suffering matters morally — happiness counts for literally nothing. It was invented by Karl Popper, who argued that relieving suffering must always take priority over increasing happiness. Taken seriously, this means the ideal world contains no sentient life at all: a consistent negative utilitarian with a button that would painlessly end all consciousness instantly would be obligated to press it, since any residual suffering outweighs any amount of joy. Philosophers in this tradition, like David Pearce and David Benatar, therefore advocate for winding down life on earth. Most philosophers reject the view exactly because of this implication, which is known as the pinprick argument."

some of this smells off to me. like, i'm pretty sure Pearce is the guy who wrote about abolishing suffering through genetic engineering and turning the biosphere into some graders-and-bliss paradise thing, not about ending life, and i have a vague memory that even people who call themselves negative utilitarians fight amongst themselves about whether the "press the button" thing actually follows. i also dimly remember someone claiming popper never really held the view and it's basically a misreading that got repeated for decades, but i cant remember where i saw that, so no clue if it's true.
