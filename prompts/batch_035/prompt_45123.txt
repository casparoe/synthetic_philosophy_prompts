I'm a backend engineer with zero background in this stuff who somehow got roped onto my city's planning commission (our neighborhood association needed a warm body and I made the mistake of attending a single meeting). Next month we vote on a proposal to tear down a corner laundromat and put up a six-story apartment building, ~90 units with a below-market-rate component. The neighborhood is mostly detached houses and the opposition writes itself: parking, shadows, "neighborhood character," the laundromat is "iconic" (it is not, I've been going there for nine years, it's fine at best). But what's actually bugging me isn't any specific objection — it's who gets to decide at all. The only people with a vote are people who already live here, including me. The nurse who'd live in one of those 90 units but can't afford a house in this city? No say, because she doesn't exist here yet. And a lot of the loudest opponents moved in 40 years ago when the city was building constantly and they bought cheap — they essentially got the benefit of a growing city and now say the neighborhood is "full." It feels almost rigged, like the current occupants of an invitation-only club voting on whether anyone new can join, and I can't shake the feeling that the whole input process (the hearings, the comment forms) is just this imbalance wearing a bureaucratic costume.

My fellow commissioners are mostly arguing traffic studies at each other, and honestly the hearing format rewards whoever has the most free time on a Tuesday afternoon. Draft me a memo I can circulate before the meeting. It needs to recommend an actual decision — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with concrete reasoning the other commissioners can respond to, not just vibes. But I also want it to engage the strongest version of the other side, which isn't "shadows": it's the view that of course current residents decide, it's their neighborhood, they bought in under a set of rules and expectations and it's not retroactively unfair to keep them. If you think that view has real force, say so — a few commissioners will dismiss anything that smells like abstract philosophizing or YIMBY advocacy, so the memo has to stay grounded and signable by a normal person. Maybe fold my "who gets a vote" worry in as one consideration among several rather than the whole argument, unless you think it can carry more weight without losing the room. Keep it short enough that people will actually read it before Thursday.
