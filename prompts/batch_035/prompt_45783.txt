I'm drafting an essay on the claim that Inside Out (Pixar, 2015) is the most philosophically committed picture of the mind that mainstream cinema has produced — and I want to stress-test the thesis before I commit to it. Read the film through the lens of Ned Block's distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, with Baars-style global workspace theory as the backdrop. Headquarters looks like a literal global workspace: specialist systems (the emotions, each with her own biases and console manners) compete for the bottleneck of Riley's attention and behavior, and whatever wins the competition gets broadcast to the whole system — long-term memory, the islands of personality, dream production. That this was deliberate is partly documented: Pete Docter brought in the psychologists Dacher Keltner and Paul Ekman as scientific consultants. So the film's commitments can't be waved off as accident.

Here is where I keep getting stuck, and where I want the reading to be honest rather than flattering to my thesis. In the workspace picture, the processors doing the competing are not supposed to be conscious; consciousness is supposed to be the broadcast, not the broadcasters. The film does the opposite. The competitors — Joy, Sadness, Fear — are the only characters with any interiority at all, and they have rich private experiences even when marooned in long-term memory, far from any workspace. Riley herself, the person whose consciousness this is all supposed to be, is filmed almost entirely from the outside, as though she were a very well-run executive system with no one home. So does the film dramatize access consciousness at the cost of precisely the phenomenal consciousness Block accuses workspace theory of leaving out? Or is the ending the film's answer: Sadness taking the console, the first mixed-feeling core memory, Riley weeping in front of her parents — as if to say that phenomenality just is what a sufficiently integrated workspace feels like from the inside, a one-level deflation of the homunculus that dissolves the regress rather than hiding it?

I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the Block/Baars/Dehaene debate seriously. Which scenes do genuine work for the workspace reading — the literalized train of thought, the dream studio as a factory of confabulation, the console getting a bigger ball and puberty-ready redesign in the closing shot? Which scenes resist it, and how hard — do Joy and Sadness's conversations in the memory dump, which no workspace is broadcasting, amount to sub-personal processes with phenomenal lives, or does the film have some way of reabsorbing them? And finally: does Inside Out end up saying something about the access/phenomenal gap that the philosophical arguments by themselves don't, or does it merely relocate the hard problem into the cuteness of the characters — making the mystery look solved by giving it a face? I would rather conclude the essay with the second verdict than the first, but only if it's earned. Please don't soften the reading to spare the film, and don't distort the philosophy to rescue my thesis either.
