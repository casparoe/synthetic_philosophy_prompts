I'm a third-year economics student in a seminar on comparative economic systems, and we've just finished a unit on the socialist calculation debate. I have a question about the "trial and error" solution that I can't fully resolve, and my professor's answer only deepened my puzzlement.

As I understand it, Lange's response to Mises is that central planners never actually need to solve the system of simultaneous equations describing general equilibrium. Instead, they can set initial prices more or less arbitrarily, observe where shortages and surpluses appear, and then raise prices in the sectors with shortages and lower them in the sectors with gluts, repeating until demand roughly matches supply. That is essentially the same iterative adjustment process that the Walrasian auctioneer runs in a competitive economy, so the "calculation" that Mises thought impossible reduces to a feedback loop that doesn't require enormous computing power at all. On this reading, Mises's impossibility claim was simply refuted, and the only surviving objection is Hayek's, namely that the information about supply conditions and consumer preferences is dispersed among millions of individual firms and households and so can't be conveyed to a planning board. But surely modern data infrastructure — transaction records, inventory systems, real-time sales data — goes a long way toward solving that problem too. My professor says both halves of my summary are contentious, but I honestly don't see why.

So my question is this: if iterative price adjustment finds equilibrium prices without anyone solving the equations, where exactly does the impossibility claim re-enter the argument? And if the knowledge problem is just a matter of the information being spread out, what is still missing once planners can observe shortages and gluts in real time from centrally collected data? I suspect I'm flattening something important about what the debate was actually over, but I can't put my finger on what it is.
