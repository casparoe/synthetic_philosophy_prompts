For years my brother-in-law and I have been arguing one question whenever the family gets together: were the big turns of history ever really avoidable, or were the major developments — industrialization, the world wars, the collapse of the Soviet system, the end of formal empires — going to happen roughly as they did no matter what any particular individual decided? My position is that history is dominated by contingency: if Gavrilo Princip had missed, if a handful of officials in East Germany had made different calls in 1989, if certain inventors had died young, I think the world would be genuinely different, not just the same world with different labels on it. His position is that the deep forces — geography, trade routes, population pressure, the incentives facing whoever happened to hold power — set the broad channels, and that individual choices mostly determine timing and local color. He says I write history as if it were a novel about characters; I say he writes it as a weather report about fronts.

We've been going in circles long enough that we've agreed to try something different: a single written statement we could both put our names to, setting out where we agree, where we disagree, and what would actually settle things between us. He's a retired engineer and I never studied history past high school — I've just read a lot on my own — so the document needs to be in plain language, but it must not be sloppy. In particular, we each want our own view stated so accurately that neither of us can complain about the wording after the fact.

Write that document for us. It should contain, in order:

1. The claims about history and about counterfactual reasoning that we can both honestly sign, including honest statements about how hard it is to test claims concerning events that never happened.

2. Our disagreements, each stated twice: once in words I would accept as a fair statement of my view, once in words he would accept as a fair statement of his. Where one of us holds a view in a qualified form, the qualification should appear; don't flatten either of us into a caricature for the sake of a clean contrast.

3. The cruxes — the specific sub-questions such that settling one of them would genuinely move at least one of us, rather than just giving us fresh things to argue about.

4. For each crux, at least one concrete historical case or line of reasoning that we both agree in advance bears on it, together with each of our predictions about what that case shows, recorded now so that neither of us can quietly move the goalposts later.

Two things I've noticed about our arguments that the document must address. First, I suspect part of our dispute is merely verbal: when he says an outcome was "inevitable," he seems to mean something like "overwhelmingly likely given the underlying forces," whereas when I deny inevitability, I mean "not necessary — it depended on choices that could have gone the other way." If part of our disagreement dissolves once we pin down our terms, say which part, and say so plainly rather than manufacturing a disagreement to keep the document symmetrical. Second, he has a standing complaint that my counterfactuals are uncheckable stories — that anyone can invent a plausible-sounding alternate world in which their favorite change matters, and that there is no way to grade these stories against each other. I think he uses that complaint as a permanent veto, a way to avoid ever engaging with a specific example. The document should put this methodological fight on the table as one of the explicit disagreements, with the strongest version of each of our positions on it, instead of leaving it as something he can invoke whenever a particular counterfactual becomes inconvenient to him.

Finally, where you cannot find a formulation that one of us would actually sign, say so rather than papering over it — the point of this exercise is to find out exactly what separates us, not to produce a diplomatic nothing.
