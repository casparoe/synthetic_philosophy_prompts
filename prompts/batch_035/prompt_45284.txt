I run a small reading group in philosophy of language that has spent the past month on conditionals, and for our final session I want to hand out a self-diagnostic quiz: "Which theory of conditionals do you actually hold?" — in the spirit of those personality quizzes that sort you by your answers to concrete cases rather than by which label you like. The trap I want to avoid is that everyone in the group says "oh, I'm basically a Stalnakerian" and then, when confronted with actual sentence pairs, gives answers that are flatly incompatible with that. I'd like the quiz to sort them by what they say about cases, not by which name they find fashionable, so no question should ever mention a philosopher or a theory's name, and every question should be answerable by a reflective person with no background at all.

You are a philosopher of language with deep expertise in conditional semantics; before writing the quiz, please brainstorm which concrete cases genuinely divide the theoretical landscape, and then build the quiz from those rather than from a checklist of doctrines. The quiz should have eight to ten questions, each built around a concrete pair or scenario — for instance the classic Oswald/Kennedy-style pair (an indicative like "if the shooter missed, someone else fired" paired with its counterfactual twin), backward-looking vs. forward-looking conditionals, Sobel-style sequences where a series of increasingly far-fetched antecedents seem to flip the truth value, conditionals with impossible antecedents, indicative conditionals whose antecedent the speaker knows to be false, "even if" and other strengtheners, and cases where the indicative seems fine but its counterfactual twin seems false or vice versa. Each question should offer two to four answer options phrased as direct verdicts about the case ("both of these seem true to me", "the first is true but the second isn't saying anything with a truth value", etc.), never as endorsements of a doctrine. Please think carefully about the option wording so that a reader answers based on their linguistic intuitions, not on a guess about which theory is being tested.

Then include a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the named positions: truth-functional orthodoxy (both indicative and counterfactual conditionals are material conditionals, with the apparent differences explained pragmatically), the assertibility or conventional-implicature refinements of that orthodoxy, a unitary possible-worlds selection-function semantics treating indicative and counterfactual conditionals as the same construction evaluated over different domains of accessible worlds, comparative-similarity semantics with and without the conditional excluded middle, strict-conditional views quantifying over a fixed domain, and the suppositional/no-truth-value family on which at least some conditionals lack truth values and are governed by conditional probability or assertibility conditions instead. After the key, add a short section noting (a) any two leaves the quiz cannot genuinely separate and why, (b) any combination of answers that corresponds to no standard position, with a sentence on whether that's because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and (c) the two or three questions that do the most diagnostic work, in case a shortened version is needed. Aim the whole thing at bright undergraduates: no jargon in the questions themselves, technical vocabulary only in the key, where each term should be glossed in a few words.
