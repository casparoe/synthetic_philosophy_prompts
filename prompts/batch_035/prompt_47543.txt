Population ethics seminar this term, and a grad student just walked me into an argument I can't find the exit to. It starts from the Benatar-style asymmetry between pleasure and pain, and by her lights it ends up showing that the empty universe is better than any universe with people in it, however well those lives go. She runs it like this. (1) Pain is bad and pleasure is good. (2) The absence of pain is good even when there is nobody around for whom that absence is good, while the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is somebody who is deprived of it. (3) So take any possible person whose life would contain any pain at all, and compare their existing with their never existing: on the existence side you get some pain (bad) and some pleasure (good); on the nonexistence side you get absent pain (good) and no absent pleasure worth counting (not bad, no one is deprived). Nonexistence wins. (4) Every possible life contains at least some pain — nobody gets out of life without stubbing a toe. (5) So it's always better that any given possible person never exists, and a world where nobody exists beats any populated one. That much is just the antinatalist position, which I disagree with but could live with as a consistent view.

The part I can't shake is what she does with the "good even if there's no subject" clause in (2). If absent pains are goods without subjects, then each non-created possible person is an extra helping of subjectless good, and there's no cap on how many possible people you can fail to create. So two people using contraception tonight have done a good deed if the foreclosed child would have had even one bad day, and the empty universe — which contains, as it were, the absent pains of all the possible people — comes out better than a universe holding a trillion long, blissful lives that each include a single pinprick. That last comparison is the one that smells wrong to me, but I can't tell whether the rot is in (2)'s first clause, in treating possible people as a currency you can mint unlimited amounts of, in (4), or in the slide somewhere between (3) and (5) from "better for the person" to "better, full stop." My gut says (3) is quietly equivocating between a person-affecting comparison and a world-comparison, and that (2) only ever made sense as a claim about harms to persons rather than about the values of outcomes — but I'd like that instinct checked rather than patted on the head.

Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. For what it's worth, I want to keep some version of the intuition behind (2)'s first clause: it really does seem good that the possible child who would have suffered some dreadful disease was never concieved, and I don't want to lose that by giving up the clause wholesale. But I also refuse to believe that the value of a world is a function of how many possible people I decline to create, which is where her reading seems to land. A colleague keeps invoking Narveson's old line about being in favor of making people happy rather than making happy people — if that slogan is where the real insight lives, explain why it can do the work (2) was pretending to do, and whether my student's argument is just what you get when you take (2) seriously as a claim about outcomes instead. Price the options for me like I'm a tenure committee.
