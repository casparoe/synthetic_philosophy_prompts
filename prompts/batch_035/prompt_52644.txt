I'm a postdoc in cognitive neuroscience, and for the last six months my grandmother has been living with us while her Alzheimer's advances; her most recent scan shows substantial cortical atrophy, so her brain is measurably much smaller than it was a few years ago. This has made an old seminar debate feel suddenly non-abstract: is brain size morally relevant, in any way at all? To find out what I actually believe, I've been writing down my gut verdicts on cases, and I'd like your help extracting a principle from them, if one exists.

Here are the verdicts. A sperm whale's brain is roughly six times the mass of a human's, and I'm completely confident the human matters more. A crow's brain is smaller than a cow's by more than an order of magnitude, but I think the crow may matter more — its neurons are packed tighter and it seems to do more with less. A person born with severe microcephaly, and an adult who has had an entire hemisphere removed, both have dramatically smaller brains than average, and I don't think their moral status diminishes in the slightest. An elephant matters a great deal to me — more than a dog — but still less than a human, though its brain is about three times the size of ours. And then the case I keep going back and forth on: my grandmother. I can't make myself think she matters less than she did, yet the capacities I'd cite to explain why she still matters — sentience, the persistence of some inner life — are also the ones I'd cite for the crow, and I'm not sure that story survives being used for both.

So: is there a single principle — total neuron count, cortical neuron count, density, capacity for consciousness, species-typical traits — that captures all or most of these verdicts? State it as precisely as you can, tell me which verdict motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for cases I haven't listed (a bee's small but concentrated nervous system, a hypothetical person engineered with twice the typical cortical neuron count, an AI with no brain at all). And please be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate. If you think the whole framing is confused — that brain size was never the operative variable and my grandmother's case is doing sentimental work the other cases aren't — say so plainly rather than constructing something gerrymandered to save every verdict; I'd rather overturn one of my own intuitions than keep a principle built to flatter them all.
