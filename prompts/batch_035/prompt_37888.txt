Longtime lurker here, finally posting because this has been bugging me for years and the decision theory threads never seem to touch it. So, Parfit's hitchhiker, rational irrationality, the whole deal — everyone knows the shape of it: the driver only picks you up if she's confident you'll pay once you're safely in town, so the winning move at the roadside is to genuinely be the kind of person who pays, precommitments or no. The sophisticated agents end up wishing they could be a bit simpler and more stubborn. I one-box, for the record, so I'm not coming at this as a CDT partisan.

Here's what I want and can't seem to construct cleanly: a thought experiment where the whole trick backfires — where being the kind of agent who can credibly bind themselves makes you do WORSE than an agent who genuinely can't. Constraints: (1) everyday setting — an office, a landlord dispute, a negotiation over a used car, that kind of thing. No deserts, no perfect predictors, everyone in the story is just a shrewd normal person reading other normal people. (2) The committed agent's disadvantage can't come from them having adopted a dumb commitment — the binding disposition has to be one that's genuinely optimal in the standard cases (deters free-riders, makes them a reliable counterparty, whatever), it just happens to be a liability here. (3) It should work at the level of dispositions, not at the level of any particular choice: the case still has to bite even if the agent never actually gets to exercise the commitment, so I'm not looking for "she followed through on a threat and it went badly."

I have a vague hunch the secret ingredient is that credible commitments make you *predictable*, and predictable resoluteness is itself something a clever counterpart can feed on — but if you can find a different mechanism, bonus points, and if my hunch mechanism secretly violates one of my constraints, tell me which one.

After the scenario, walk me through the decision theories: what plain causal decision theory says, what updateless/functional decision theory says, and what a genuinely impulsive or vengeful human does in the same spot — someone with no precommitment apparatus at all, whose follow-through is just real. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, and whether removing that feature collapses it back into ordinary hitchhiker-style territory where commitment wins. And finally: if the case works, doesn't it show there's a class of situations where the rational-irrationality crowd has it exactly backwards — where the winning agent's irrationality isn't strategic at all, it's just... actually irrational? Or is there some rescue move for the sophisticated agent, like committing to be selectively uncommittable in a defined domain, and if so does that look like a natural extension of the framework or a full-on reductio of it?

Context for why I care: I keep running into the claim that ideal rationality plus precommitment dominates everything, and I want a case to throw at the FDT enthusiasts that isn't just Newcomb with the numbers shuffled around — something where the sophisticated agent loses to a genuinely simple one. If my specs are unsatisfiable as stated, just tell me which constraint to drop and why; I'd much rather hear that than get a case that cheats by stipulating the verdicts instead of generating them from the theories.
