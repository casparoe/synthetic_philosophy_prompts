Write a poem on the St. Petersburg paradox in which the form plays the game rather than merely describing it. The speaker is a croupier making the offer: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, the payoff starting at two units and doubling with each preceding tails. Structure the poem as six stanzas of shrinking size — sixteen lines, then eight, then four, then two, then one, then a final fragment — so that the stanzas halve in length while the payoffs they announce double, the thinning of the poem's body tracking the halving probability of ever reaching each sum. Someone who knows the series should be able to reconstruct it from the form alone, and someone who doesn't should still find a good poem about being offered infinity. Do not resolve the paradox by fiat: no closing aphorism about diminishing marginal utility, finite bankrolls, or the unreality of infinitely deep pockets. Instead, let the poem break off mid-offer — the croupier waiting, the price still unnamed — so the reader is left holding the exact question the paradox poses: what is a fair price for an expectation without bound, and what does it cost us to walk away?
