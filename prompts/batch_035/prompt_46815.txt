I am an undergraduate in a seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and this week we are workshopping a set of short-answer problems anonymously: each student's response is graded by another student, and the instructor has asked us to assign a letter grade on the standard A–F scale together with specific, point-by-point comments that justify it, so the author can see exactly what was credited, what was not, and why. I would like you to act as an experienced grader for my assignment. Read the question and the response below, assign a grade, and then write the marginal comments you would give — organized point by point, quoting or locating the specific sentence or step you are commenting on, saying what is right as well as what is wrong, and never settling for a general impression of quality. Where the response makes a mistake, explain precisely what has gone wrong rather than just flagging it.

The question, from our problem set on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument:

1. Set out the Basic Argument against the possibility of true moral responsibility. Explain why Strawson thinks that being truly morally responsible would require being causa sui in some sense, and why he takes that to be impossible. Then raise the strongest objection you can to the argument, and say how Strawson would, or should, reply to it.

The response I have to grade:

The Basic Argument can be reconstructed in three steps. First, what you do is a consequence of the way you are — your character, values, and dispositions. Second, true moral responsibility for what you do requires responsibility for the way you are; you cannot be responsible, in the deep sense Strawson cares about, for an action that issues from a self you did not shape. Third, no one can be responsible for the way they are, because the way you are at any point is the product either of heredity and environment — genes, upbringing, things no one chose — or of earlier self-shaping choices, and those choices in turn flowed from an earlier way you were, and so on back. The regress cannot terminate anywhere you are responsible for, so true moral responsibility is impossible.

The key notion is causa sui, being self-caused. To be responsible for the way you are in the way premise two demands, you would have had to make yourself what you are, which would require existing, already equipped with a nature, prior to your own existence. Nothing can do that, so the demand is incoherent, and responsibility of the deep kind is impossible. Importantly, the whole argument rests on the claim that the way you are is fixed by factors outside your control, and that is just the thesis of determinism. So the Basic Argument is best read as a conditional: if determinism is true, moral responsibility is impossible. A libertarian who holds that a choice can originate in the agent without being fixed by prior character therefore has a direct reply to premise one. Perhaps for this reason, Strawson concedes that mere indeterminism would not help either: a choice that pops up uncaused, like a coin flip in the head, is not something you are responsible for. Still, the argument's real target is determinism, and its real lesson is that if the determinists are right, nothing is anyone's fault.

The strongest objection is that some choices are self-forming in a way that breaks the regress. When someone deliberates hard and resolves to change — the procrastinator who reorganizes his life, the convert who rewires her values — it seems they make themselves the way they subsequently are. Strawson's reply has to be that the way they were when they made those choices was itself not their doing: the resolve, the willpower, the capacity to deliberate well, all came from somewhere earlier. The regress restarts at every level, so the objection presupposes what it denies.

Finally, the argument's conclusion is practical as much as metaphysical. Following his father, P. F. Strawson, who introduced the framework of reactive attitudes, Galen Strawson holds that since no one is truly responsible, our reactive attitudes — gratitude, resentment, indignation — are unfitting and ought to be revised or given up, and that practices of blame and punishment rest on a mistake. On this view no one is responsible in any sense at all, and the difficult question is only how to live with that.

Please structure your grading as follows: first the letter grade; then a point-by-point assessment of each paragraph, crediting what deserves credit; then a short overall comment to the student. Keep in mind that our instructor cares most about whether the reconstruction of the argument is faithful and whether the student's own claims follow from it.
