I'm a retired materials scientist -- thirty-odd years of fracture toughness testing and failure analysis -- and since retiring I've gotten deep into the philosophy of dispositions, which turns out to be a whole war zone. In the lab we just said a sample was brittle if it shattered under impact, but philosophers have spent decades on cases like a fragile glass tended by a sorcerer who'd make it unbreakable the instant before any strike lands (so "if struck, it breaks" is false but it still seems fragile), or fragile glass shipped in styrofoam that stops the breaking without changing the glass itself at all. Could you write me a decision tree that sorts readers into positions on what makes "this glass is fragile" true? Build it from cases rather than school names: the sorcerer case, the styrofoam case, a glass that would break under only one very specific kind of blow, a vase that behaves exactly like a fragile one but for some unrelated internal reason, and whether the disposition lives in the glass alone or in glass-plus-situation (my old colleagues argued about precisely this with corrosion resistance, we just never called it metaphysics). Each node should be answerable by someone who's never heard of any of the positions, and the leaves should include at least the plain conditional view, some patched-up conditional, the view that fragility is an intrinsic structural property, the view that it's the structural property plus a "nothing would get in the way" clause, and a deflationary "fragile is just a label we use" option. Flag any two leaves the tree can't actually seperate, and note any combination of answers nobody seems to hold -- I'd like to know whether that's because the combo is incoherent or just unexplored.
