I'm a retired physical chemist -- about thirty five years in soft matter and phase transitions before I retired, so my native vocabulary is things like order parameters, symmetry breaking, universality classes, renormalization group flow, effective theories, coarse-graining, degenerate ground states. Since I retired I've been reading philosophy seriously for the first time, and I've gotten genuinely stuck on the problem of universals. As far as I can tell the classic positions are: some form of realism where universals exist independently (either off in a separate realm a la Plato, or instantiated in things a la Aristotle), nominalism where there are just particulars and everything else is a linguistic or mental add-on, resemblance nominalism where sameness of type is just resemblance between particulars, and trope theory where properties are themselves particular but can be exactly alike. An old colleague of mine (also retired, also reading philosophy, we argue over coffee on Thursdays) claims chemistry makes him a realist: the periodic table was out there waiting, and every copper atom in the universe has the same electronic ground state configuration regardless of who measures it. I half agree with him but I suspect we're equivocating between a few different things.

Here's what I want. Take each of those positions on universals and restate it in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. So, e.g., what would realism about universals say about universality classes -- the fact that fluids, magnets, and binary mixtures with completely different microphysics can share critical exponents? Is that a discovered universal, or just what a coarse-grained effective theory looks like when you integrate out the microscopic degrees of freedom? What would the nominalist say about the way we classify phase behavior, and does the resemblance-nominalist picture fit better with how two "the same" liquid crystals are actually identified in a lab? I want the translation to be honest -- don't quietly smuggle in the chemistry assumptions and call them translation, and tell me where you had to fudge.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto something I actually think about (the reality of chemical kinds, approximate vs exact symmetries, whether renormalized theories describe anything down there), which parts only translate with distortion, and which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all -- and I mean nothing, not even a strained analogy. Finally, help me adjudicate: does the residue mean my vocabulary is missing something the philosophers are genuinely onto, or does it mean their debate was less substantive than it looks? I'd especially like your view on whether my colleague's "the periodic table was out there waiting" argument survives translation into effective-theory language, since my instinct is that most of chemistry's apparent realism dissolves into scale-dependence when you push on it, but I can't tell if that's a point about universals or just a point about coarse-graining. Please be specific, I'm fluent enough in the physics that handwaving will bother me more than the philosophy does.
