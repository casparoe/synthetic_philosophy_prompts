I teach political philosophy, and for many years a senior colleague of mine has run our graduate seminar on famine, which she opens each year with Sen's Poverty and Famines. Her verdicts over the years have been remarkably stable. Bengal in 1943 she calls a political crime, leaning on Sen's point that food availability per head in Bengal that year was not much below the recent average, so that the million-plus deaths trace to wartime procurement, interprovincial trade barriers, and an administration with other priorities. The Irish famine of the late 1840s she also calls a political crime, stressing that substantial food exports continued throughout and that relief was shaped, and eventually curtailed, by doctrinal commitments about the moral hazard of assistance. The Great Leap Forward she calls a political crime in the strictest sense: policy created the deficit, and the apparatus suppressed the information that would have corrected it. She has natural-disaster verdicts too, though few: of a region-wide harvest collapse where the state had already spent its reserves, exhausted its credit, and imported everything its ports could land, she has said that nature, not the ministry, did the killing. Her seminar maxim, repeated often enough that our doctoral students can recite it, is that famine is never a question of how much food a country grew, only of who was allowed to eat it.

This term I want to press her with a new case, and I would like to be prepared. The case: a mid-sized country, not poor, holding substantial hard-currency reserves, is struck by a once-in-a-century drought that halves the harvest. World grain markets are well supplied, and prices are ordinary. But the governing party is committed, quite sincerely and with genuine popular support, to a doctrine that large-scale food imports breed dependency, wreck the balance of payments, and soften the national character; the discipline of scarcity, they hold, is instructive. The government accordingly imports nothing beyond routine levels. It exports nothing, seizes nothing, hoards nothing, and suppresses no one; the diminished harvest is distributed with scrupulous equality, the statistics are published honestly, and mortality proceeds over two seasons, falling hardest on the very young and the very old. Within the country, no one starves while another eats. The failure, if there is one, lies at the border rather than the table.

Before I raise the case in seminar, help me work through it. First, reconstruct the rules that fit her track record equally well — I can see at least two, one keyed to what the state did to the food supply (took, exported, diverted, repealed relief) and one keyed to what the state could have done with the means it had, act or omission alike — and check whether they genuinely diverge on my new case, as I suspect they do. Second, predict her verdict, with your reasons; I care less about the prediction itself than about which features of the case should move her, given her maxim about who was allowed to eat — since on my telling distribution inside the country was perfect, and it is not obvious whether her maxim reaches across the border. Third, propose the further case that would best discriminate between the rules she might be running. I have a candidate of my own — a famine in which policy caused severe shortage and panic, but timely relief meant that nobody actually died of hunger — though I am unsure whether it does the work I want, and I would like to hear yours, and an honest verdict on whether mine settles the question or merely relocates it.
