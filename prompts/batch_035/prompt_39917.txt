I'm putting together a seminar session for an undergraduate course on personal identity, and I want to teach Dennett's idea of the self as a "center of narrative gravity" — the notion that the self isn't a thing in the brain but an abstraction we posit, like a center of mass in physics, to organize and predict the narrative the organism spins about itself. For the screening that week I've chosen Christopher Nolan's Memento (2000), and I'd like a critical reading of the film through this Dennettian lens, one I can hand to students as a model of how to do philosophy with a film rather than merely gesture at a theme.

The obvious hook is Leonard's anterograde amnesia: he can't form new long-term memories, so he externalizes his narrative — the Polaroids, the notes, the tattoos, the Sammy Jankis story he retells to strangers. If the self is whatever organization a narrative imposes on a body and its traces, Leonard seems like a forced experiment, a person whose center of gravity has to be maintained by hand. But I want the reading to go past that first observation in at least two directions. First, the film's twist suggests Leonard actively edits the record: when Teddy tells him he already found and killed his wife's attacker, and Leonard responds by writing down the license plate number that will get Teddy killed, he seems to be choosing which facts his future self will get to know — self-authorship as self-deception. Does that strengthen the Dennettian picture (the self is constituted by the story, so curating the story is curating the self) or break it (if there's an editor behind the narrative, hasn't the theory smuggled in a self before the narrative starts)? Second, there's the structure of the film itself — the color sequences running backward, the black-and-white thread running forward, the "self" of the film only completed where they meet. I'd like to hear whether you think the form of the movie is doing genuine philosophical work here or is just an elegant gimmick.

Where I especially want care: a faithful reading has to notice what resists the theory. Leonard retains things that don't look narrative at all — his conditioning, his grief, his procedural habits, his reflexive moral vocabulary about what kind of man he is (or claims to be). And the Sammy Jankis storyline raises the possibility of a narrative whose center of gravity sits over the wrong data, a story Leonard may have grafted onto himself from someone else's life. So in addition to the reading, please tell me: where does Memento complicate or even embarrass Dennett's view rather than confirm it, and what do you think the film shows about the self that the argument in the philosophy of mind literature doesn't quite manage on its own? I don't need quotations from either the film or Dennett — I want the interpretive and argumentative work done well, in a form I can excerpt for my students.
