I work in insurance pricing, so I spend my days putting a monetary value on things people care about intensely, and I've been trying to work out where my actual line is on commodification -- not the line I'd defend in a meeting, the one I really have. Here are my verdicts. Paying plasma donors, as parts of the United States do: fine, and I'm mildly annoyed the UK doesn't. A regulated market in kidneys from living donors: not fine, though I can't see what distinguishes it from plasma except scarcity and irreversibility. Vote markets: obviously not fine. Surge pricing on generators after a hurricane: not fine. Surge pricing on hotel rooms during a festival: fine. Hiring a surrogate: fine with regulation. Selling one's place in a queue -- airport fast-track, Disney's paid line-skipping: fine. The people who ran bots to grab COVID vaccine appointments and then offered the slots to older strangers for a fee, back when slots were rationed and the desperate were ringing clinics at dawn: not fine, even though it is structurally identical to ticket resale, which I've decided is merely annoying. Life settlements -- investors buying a terminally ill stranger's life insurance policy and profiting if he dies early: fine, genuinely useful to the seller. An app that let patients pay to jump a public hospital's waiting list, with the money going to the hospital rather than the patients jumped: not fine, and I notice I'd feel differently if the payment went to the jumped patients, which unsettles me.

My tentative principle is something like: a market is legitimate when the good being sold was already being allocated by rough-and-ready impersonal means (queues, waiting lists, lotteries, price) and illegitimate when the transaction claims something that is constitutively not for sale -- a vote, a body part, a person's place in a civic scheme. Test that against my cases, tell me where it bends or breaks, and repair it or replace it. I want the principle stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I haven't thought of, and I want you to say explicitly which of my verdicts the best available principle cannot accommodate, since I'd rather overturn a gut reaction than bolt on an epicycle. Two new cases to test it on, if you have room: a futures market in which traders speculate on the value of life-insurance payouts after a pandemic, effectively betting on strangers' deaths en masse, and a scheme in which wealthy patients pay organ donors directly, with both parties consenting, intermediaries regulated, and the donor registry's queue untouched.

Please answer in British English, and keep it under 550 words -- clauses, verdicts, and the honest residue only, no scene-setting.
