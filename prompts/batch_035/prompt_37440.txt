In our department's pragmatism reading group, a colleague keeps invoking the line that truth is whatever works, most recently to wave off an objection that some very useful scientific models are strictly speaking false — convenient fictions, idealizations like frictionless planes. He seemed to think the slogan settles the matter: if a belief works, that's all "truth" can intelligibly mean, so asking whether the frictionless plane is really true is a confusion. I suspect the slogan is compressing several very different theses, only some of which would license his move, and that Peirce and James would even divide over which reading to accept. Please lay out the distinct precise claims the one-liner could be making — from innocuous readings (roughly: true beliefs tend to be useful, or utility is evidence of truth) to genuinely radical ones (roughly: utility constitutes truth, or whatever a community settles into believing is true by definition). For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false; where, if anywhere, it sits in the space between Peircean convergence-of-inquiry and Jamesian satisfaction-of-interests; and, crucially, whether it would actually support my colleague's dismissal of the idealization objection. Present the readings as a numbered list I can bring back to the group, with a short verdict on each.
