I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. By trade I work on systems that maintain probabilistic state estimates, and I've taught myself as much Bayesian statistics and formal epistemology as I can from textbooks and papers. Recently I've been reading about updating on uncertain evidence, and I've hit something I can't reconcile.

Standard Bayesian conditioning assumes your evidence is certain: you observe E, set its probability to 1, and redistribute everything else by the likelihood ratios. Richard Jeffrey's updating rule is supposed to relax this: instead of becoming certain of E, you directly revise your probabilities over some partition — say you become 0.7 confident that it's raining rather than 1 — and the rest of your beliefs are recomputed from the new partition probabilities. Every source I've read presents this as the principled way to handle observation under perceptual doubt, half-trusted testimony, unreliable instruments, and the like.

But those same sources all attach a condition: the rule only applies when the update is rigid, meaning the conditional probability of every other proposition, given each cell of the partition, is left unchanged by the observation. And that's where I lose the thread. If the evidence was genuinely uncertain and mixed — a blurry glimpse, a flaky sensor — then the very same experience should also be shifting my conditional beliefs given each hypothesis, since my opinions about how reliable the glimpse is are part of what the glimpse is evidence about. Rigidity seems to stipulate exactly the thing that uncertain evidence would disturb. So as far as I can tell, Jeffrey conditioning only covers cases where the observation, despite being advertised as uncertain, is somehow quarantined from everything else I believe — in which case the uncertainty is pure bookkeeping, the interesting cases fall outside the rule's scope, and the whole apparatus is just ordinary conditioning with an extra parameter. Is that right?

If not, I'd like an answer that spells out precisely what rigidity constrains and what it leaves free. Please work through one concrete example — say, glimpsing out the window in bad light and lowering your confidence that it's raining from 0.9 to 0.6 — and indicate, proposition by proposition, which probabilities move under the rule, which are held fixed, and why holding those fixed is an assumption about the causal structure of the situation rather than a covert reintroduction of the certainty we were trying to give up. I'd also like to know whether there's a principled way to tell, before updating, whether my case is one where rigidity is a reasonable idealization or one where it's simply false.
