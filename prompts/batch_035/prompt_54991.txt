I'm a software engineer with no training in philosophy or evolutionary biology beyond popular-science books, and I've ended up committed to a fairly strong version of the gene's-eye view of natural selection. Stated in my own terms: genes are the only genuine units of selection, because they're the only things that persist and get copied with high fidelity across generations. Organisms, populations, and species are build artifacts — compiled binaries that get thrown away while the source code carries on. On this view, classical group selection is basically incoherent: any altruistic tendency at the group level will be eroded by cheaters within the group, the way an unprotected shared resource always gets overrun. Apparent group-level adaptations should instead be re-described as strategies that pay off at the level of individual genes — most obviously via kin selection, where a gene favoring relatives can spread precisely because relatives are likely to carry copies of it. And the clincher, to my mind, is the existence of selfish genetic elements like transposons and meiotic-drive alleles: selection visibly operates directly on genes, even against the interests of the organism carrying them.

What would count against this? I want the observations, arguments, formal results, and kinds of cases that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be — fatal, serious, merely awkward — including things that have arguably already been demonstrated. More specifically:

(a) Have any cases actually been established where selection between groups drives a trait that within-group selection opposes, in a way that isn't just kin selection relabeled? What would such a case have to look like to count as a genuine refutation rather than another re-description?

(b) Is there anything a gene-level accounting demonstrably cannot describe? I'm thinking of things like clonal organisms, where the boundary between "one individual" and "a population of identical copies" blurs, or social-insect colonies, or tumors, where selection among cells during an organism's own lifetime produces adaptations (of the cancer lineage) that the gene's-eye view handles awkwardly at best. Do these cases threaten the view or just stretch it?

(c) Formal frameworks that partition selection across levels (e.g., the Price-equation approach of decomposing change into within-group and between-group components) — is this a genuine rival to gene-level selection, or just a re-parameterization of the same process? Does the "they're mathematically equivalent" result show that my position is safe, or that it's only safe in a way that makes the position contentless?

(d) What does the strong gene-centric view actually predict that its rivals don't? If it accommodates every possible observation by re-describing it at the gene level, has it been insulated into unfalsifiability — and is that a problem for a view of this kind, or an acceptable feature of a bookkeeping convention?

(e) What would the view's committed defenders themselves concede counts against it, as opposed to what critics say? I'd like that distinction made explicitly.

For what it's worth, I'd personally be most moved by either (i) an empirical case where gene-level and multilevel bookkeeping make different predictions and the multilevel one wins, or (ii) a formal demonstration that the two always make identical predictions, so the whole dispute is verbal. I'm genuinely unsure which of those I should be hoping for, and I'd like you to tell me whether that's a well-posed choice.
