I am at work on a novel whose central scene is a long dinner argument between two sisters: one has spent the last thirty years in Amsterdam and treats frankness as a basic form of respect, while the other has spent them in Kyoto and treats indirection the same way. The scene turns on white lies, and I want their positions to be genuinely contestable rather than a caricature in which one culture is simply right. To that end, I would like you to rank the following six white lies from most to least morally serious, where seriousness is measured by the wrong done to the person deceived rather than by the awkwardness avoided: a compliment about a dish the liar found inedible; concealing from a friend that her partner is flirting with others at a party; telling a dying relative that a negligent cousin has been phoning often; assuring a colleague that her disastrous presentation went fine; letting a child believe a scribbled drawing belongs in a museum; and falsely promising to attend an event one fully intends to skip. Then rank the same six a second time, this time by the damage each does to the liar's own character, and note where the two orderings come apart. For each entry, a sentence on what makes it as serious or as trivial as you judge it to be. Close with a paragraph on whether Dutch-style directness or Japanese-style indirection is the better instrument for handling whichever lie you ranked most serious, keeping in mind that the sister from Kyoto will object that bluntness can itself be a kind of aggression. Please write in American English, in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a letter.
