I'm a high school senior on the staff of our student newspaper, and every spring we publish a satirical edition written in the style of The Onion. I've been assigned the philosophy piece this year, and since you're presumably capable of acting as a professional epistemologist who also happens to have a decade of experience writing for a satirical news outlet, that's the role I'd like you to take on for this job.

The idea comes from the epistemology unit of our Theory of Knowledge class. We spent two weeks on what our teacher called the bootstrapping problem, sometimes called the problem of easy knowledge. The version that stuck with me: suppose you want to know whether your bathroom scale is accurate. The obvious move is to weigh an object of known weight and see whether the scale agrees. But how do you know the weight of the supposedly known object? If you weighed it earlier — on the same scale — then you have used the scale to certify the scale. And if you defend the scale by pointing out that it has been right every time you checked, the track record you're citing was compiled by the scale itself. One side of our class debate said this is just how perceptual knowledge legitimately works: each individual perception is justified until something goes wrong, and you don't need to prove your senses reliable before trusting them. The other side said that isn't knowledge at all, it's circularity smeared out over time — helping yourself to the reliability of the very source you were supposed to be checking, one seemingly innocent step at a time.

Here is the piece I'd like: an Onion-style news article — headline plus 400–500 words of deadpan wire-service prose — reporting that a bathroom scale has declared itself accurate after an exhaustive review of its own readings. The humor has to come from getting the epistemology right, so that our philosophy teacher nods instead of winces. Please work in at least three of the following, and pick the rest yourself: the scale citing its track record of past readings as evidence of its own accuracy; someone explaining that any individual reading is perfectly trustworthy as long as nothing has gone wrong so far; the complaint that there is no independent check anywhere in the process; something Moorean in flavor, such as the scale proving its accuracy from the fact that it just displayed 152.4 pounds; an escalation where the owner consults a second scale to verify the first, then slowly realizes what he's done; and the retort that ordinary people never infer reliability from track records anyway — they just keep stepping on the scale. Include at least one deadpan quote from a concerned skeptic, who is willing to grant that the scale exists but has concerns about literally everything else. Target the reasoning pattern, not any real philosopher; any quoted experts or spokespeople should be invented and clearly fictional.

A couple of process requests. Before writing the article, think carefully and brainstorm: list the four or five real features of the easy-knowledge debate you plan to satirize, with one sentence each on why the joke is fair to the actual position it exaggerates, and flag anything you decide to cut and why. Then write the piece. Keep the tone deadpan throughout — no winking at the reader, and the scale must be completely sincere, maybe even a little proud. The test I'd like it to pass: a philosophy graduate student should laugh at jokes that are technically correct, while my classmates who have never taken the class should still find it funny on a first read. Finally, append two or three sentences telling me which philosophical positions, if any, would reasonably find the piece unfair to them, so I can judge whether the satire is punching in the right direction.
