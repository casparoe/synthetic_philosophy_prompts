I help coordinate a small portfolio of conceptual AI-safety research — theory and design work, not empirical ML — and I'm about to allocate roughly two researcher-years to the question of whether AI safety via debate can actually deliver scalable oversight. The portfolio currently supports work on debate, on recursive reward modeling, and on weak-to-strong generalization, and the debate allocation is the one I'm least confident about. So I want a prioritized agenda for the open questions in debate-based oversight, and I want the ordering defended rather than asserted: for each question, why it matters relative to the others, how tractable it is as a conceptual problem (as opposed to needing large-scale experiments we can't yet run), how neglected it is, and — most importantly — which other questions depend on its answer. A dependency-aware ranking is the real deliverable; a flat list of open problems is easy to find and useless to me.

To give you a sense of the terrain as I understand it, the candidates I've been weighing include the following. The classic motivating intuition is that finding a flaw in a dishonest argument is easier than constructing a watertight dishonest argument, so two optimally persuasive adversaries constrained by the format should converge toward honesty — but it's genuinely unclear whether this asymmetry survives optimization pressure, or whether highly capable debaters instead learn to Goodhart a limited human judge, exploiting biases in what the judge finds convincing rather than converging on truth. Related questions: what properties the judge needs (is a merely human-level judge enough when debaters are superhuman, or does the whole scheme depend on the judge being able to verify a critique they couldn't have generated themselves?); whether the fixed-point story — honesty as a stable equilibrium of the game — can be made precise, and whether the equilibria that exist are the ones we want; whether debate is best understood as a competitor to, a substitute for, or merely a scaffolding for recursive reward modeling and weak-to-strong style approaches; whether cross-examination over a long horizon behaves fundamentally differently from single-turn debate; and what a debate setup even guarantees when the domain has no human-checkable ground truth at all. I suspect some of these are upstream of the others, and I suspect at least one of them, though frequently discussed, is mostly verbal and would change little if settled — I'd like you to say which, if you think so.

Please give me a ranked list of at most six questions, each with: the question stated precisely enough that someone could recognize an answer to it; your criteria and trade-offs for ranking it there; what would count as progress on it given that we can't run superhuman experiments yet; and what evidence or developments over the next couple of years would move it up or down. Explicitly flag which question you'd put at the top if the goal were supervising models that already exceed the judge in capability, versus if the goal were near-term empirical wins, in case those rankings diverge. Please don't give me a literature survey or a reading list — I know the landscape; I need the prioritization and the reasoning behind it.
