I am a retired comparative physiologist who, since leaving the lab, has been reading rather obsessively in the philosophy of biology and the philosophy of language. Every Thursday I have coffee with Dr. S, a developmental biologist I worked alongside for over twenty years, and we have settled into a standing argument about whether the word innate names one property or several that we lazily conflate. Over the years she has issued a settled series of verdicts, always crisply, and I have kept something like a ledger of them. On the white-crowned sparrow, which must hear adult song during a critical window to acquire the full song, but which will not learn another species' song even when that is the only song it ever hears, producing instead a crude isolate song: frame innate, details learned. On human language, where children extract a full grammar from thin, noisy, essentially uncorrected input: innate component, same structure — frame in the child, parameters in the environment. On the gosling that imprints on the first large moving object it sees, the ethologist himself in the famous photographs: disposition innate, object learned. On rats, which in a single trial will shun a taste that preceded nausea hours earlier, but will not associate the nausea with a tone or a light: learning, but on an innately pre-wired channel. On newborns, who within hours of birth track face-like patterns in preference to scrambled ones: innate. On reading, which is far too recent culturally for selection to have built, which most children need to be taught, and which the exceptions seem to extract from abundant exposure to print: not innate.

Next Thursday three new cases come up, and I would like to walk in with better than my usual intuitions. First, the common cuckoo: reared by foster parents of another species, it never hears a conspecific sing, yet as an adult it produces the species-typical cuckoo song. Second, the honeybee dance: different subspecies have measurably different dialects, in that the dance's calibration of distance differs across races, and I confess I cannot tell which side of her sparrow verdict this should fall on. Third, the case we will genuinely fight about: a large language model trained on nothing but raw text — no rules of grammar, no corrections, no explicit instruction — which nevertheless comes to produce fluent, well-formed English. Given our running argument about whether the child's input is really poor enough to require an inbuilt frame, she is bound to see the model as an acid test of the poverty of the stimulus, but I cannot predict which way she will call it.

What I would like from you is this. First, reconstruct the rule, or rules, Dr. S seems to be running from her ledger — my own suspicion, from the philosophy I have been reading, is that at least two or three fit her record equally well, one keyed to whether the structure is supplied by the organism rather than extracted from the input, another to whether the trait develops robustly across a range of environments, another to whether it is an adaptation shaped by selection. Second, say what each of those rules predicts for each of the three new cases. Third, give your best guess at her actual verdicts, with reasons. Finally, tell me which further case, added to the ledger, would best drive the candidate rules apart — and tell me plainly if you think her existing verdicts already force one rule over the others. I would rather concede the argument to her than win it on an equivocation.
