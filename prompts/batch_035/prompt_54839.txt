So, two people in my department have been going at each other for literal years about how old nations are, and I've finally bullied them into an adversarial collaboration. I need the joint statement they should produce.

One of them is a modernist — the Hobsbawm/Anderson type: nations are basically a product of the late 18th century onward, assembled out of invented traditions, print capitalism, mass literacy, state schooling, censuses, conscription. Before that, "the nation" in anything like our sense just wasn't there, and calling medieval kingdoms "nations" is back-projection. The other is an ethnosymbolist, closer to Anthony Smith: yes, modern nationalism is new, but it isn't built out of nothing — it grafts onto older ethnie (shared myths of descent, sacred homelands, golden ages, symbols), so national identity has real premodern roots, and the modern period is a transformation of that material rather than a creation from scratch.

What I want, in this order:

1. A numbered section of claims both of them could actually put their names to — not vacuous ones. They should be able to agree on things like: the world of nation-states as a normative order is recent; premodern polities had collective identities of some kind; and whether a given population counts as a "nation" can't be settled without first fixing what "nation" means. But push past the obvious — I want agreements that would actually surprise people who've watched them argue.

2. A table of the exact disagreements, each phrased so that the side holding it accepts the wording of its own view. My modernist friend will veto anything that smuggles in "real old nations," and the ethnosymbolist will veto anything that makes ethnie sound like mere raw material the modern state could take or leave.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them. I have guesses but I want theirs: one is probably whether mass national identity (as opposed to elite self-understanding) is even possible without print, literacy, and mass politics; another is whether continuity from an old ethnie to a modern nation makes the nation itself "old," or just makes it "rooted in something old" — that last one smells partly verbal to me. Somewhere in the document I want them to say which part of the whole arguement is verbal and why, because I suspect a chunk of it is two people using "nation" differently.

4. For each crux: a concrete kind of evidence or case both would agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction written down in advance so nobody can retro-fit later. Nothing exotic — think ordinary people's letters, diaries, court and inquisition records from before print, showing whether or how far down society any national self-label reached; or pairs of cases where the same kind of invented tradition took root in one country and flopped in the neigboring one, and what predicts which happens.

Don't give me a reading list — they've both read everything. And don't adjudicate who's right; the point is a document both could sign, with the residual disagreement made exact.
