I direct a graduate colloquium on philosophy of history, and our next session is built around a visiting historian who defends a position my students find seductive and I find slippery in equal measure. The visitor argues that non-human forces — pandemic pathogens, volcanic winters, long climate oscillations — should be treated as genuine historical actors rather than mere backdrop, and that this framing escapes the environmental determinism that discredited earlier geographic explanations of why some societies expanded and others collapsed. His emblematic cases are familiar ones: the plague years of the fourteenth century, the post-536 cold period, the Little Ice Age, the crop failures that followed Tambora. He insists his view is mediated, not deterministic: the shocks matter, but institutions and choices determine how societies absorb them.

I want you to draft nine to eleven interview questions for this session that go well beyond asking him to restate the thesis. At minimum I need one question on the trigger-versus-cause distinction — a volcanic winter may precede a famine, but what does the actor-framing add over calling it a mere trigger, and how would he answer the charge that calling it an "actor" is a rhetorical upgrade with no explanatory payload? I need one on divergent outcomes: the same climatic stress produced witch persecutions in one region, institutional innovation in another, and nothing much in a third, which looks like evidence that the human mediation is doing all the causal work while the climate merely supplies occasions. I need one on selection effects: if historians go looking for climate stress at moments of crisis, and crises are the moments historians write about, how does he rule out that the apparent importance of environmental shocks is an artifact of where the archives and the attention happen to sit — and what would a quiet, prosperous, climate-stressed century look like in his framework? And I need one on the specter of the older determinism: his view differs from grand geographic theories of Eurasian advantage in degree rather than in kind, so where exactly is the line, and who gets to draw it?

For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give — he is practiced at deflecting by conceding that climate is "only part of the story" and retreating to the claim that it is an underappreciated part. Please press on whether that retreat leaves him with a distinctive thesis at all, or just with a banal one everybody already accepts. I would also like at least one question that asks him to name a concrete historical episode where his framework gets the explanation noticeably better than a purely institutional or agentic account, and one that asks what future evidence — from ice cores, genetics, dendrochronology, or elsewhere — could count against his emphasis. The students will have read the usual popular syntheses, so assume broad familiarity with the major cases but no specialist knowledge; write the questions so that a historian of science and a philosopher of causation could both engage with them.
