Quick background: I've been reading LessWrong (and before that Overcoming Bias) since around 2010, mostly a lurker, occassional poster. I keep a small blog about decision theory stuff. Through a weird chain of events, an econ professor who works on repeated games read my post on acausal trade and invited me to present it at her department's game theory seminar next month. The audience is roughly: 15 economists who think about folk theorems, commitment devices, and mechanism design all day, one older philosopher who is a strict causal decision theorist in the Lewis tradition, and a CS postdoc doing multi-agent RL. Nobody there reads LessWrong and I'd like to keep it that way — I don't want the talk to read as internet folklore. My thesis, roughly: two agents with no causal interaction whatsoever — no signals, no shared light cone — can still rationally carry out a trade, if each can model the other well enough that their decisions are logically correlated and each has something to offer the other at some cost to itself. Each performs its side of the deal unilaterally, the way a one-boxer takes only box A. I want to claim this isn't just a cute puzzle but something a genuinely rational agent actually does, and — this is the part I've thought least carefully about — something that matters for how future AI systems deal with agents they can't communicate with.
