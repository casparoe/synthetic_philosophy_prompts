I've been following the evolutionary debunking debate in metaethics for a few years now and the core exchange basically never moves. You've got Street's Darwinian dilemma: realism about value either lets moral truth play no role in shaping our evaluative beliefs, in which case the correlation between those beliefs and moral truth is a coincidence that natural selection would not preserve, or it gives truth a hand in the process, which the realist owes us a story about and hasn't provided. Then the standard realist replies are all on the table and have been for a while: the third-factor story where something like the badness of pain is both fitness-relevant and truth-relevant, so selection ends up tracking truth indirectly (Enoch's move); the companions-in-guilt move that the same debunking reasoning would take down logic or mathematics, which nobody wants; and the no-defeater line, pushed by people like Wielenberg, that an explanation of reliability was never a condition on justified trust in the first place, so the demand for one is itself a substantive epistemological assumption, not a neutral premise. Street and friends reply that all of these just beg the question, and thats more or less where it has sat for fifteen years.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement isn't really about moral realism at all -- it's about one norm in general epistemology: whether justified trust in a domain requires an etiological explanation of why we track the truth in that domain, or whether trust is the default and debunking arguments have to supply a positive defeater. If I'm right, the debate can't move without settling a broader epistemological dispute that is itself stuck, and the 'moral' framing is just where it happens to surface. Is that diagnosis right, or is there something substantive hiding in here that I'm missing -- a genuine disagreement about the evolutionary facts, about what counts as a debunking explanation, or about which cases count as the data? Use what the sides actually say to make the call, and tell me what would even count as progress here: a distinction, a formal model of reliability, or nothing. One request: please be upfront about which parts of your diagnosis are more or less settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested, and don't present a contested verdict (that the dilemma is sound, that Enoch's move fails, whatever) as if it were consensus.
