The impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — that calibration and equalized odds cannot hold jointly across groups with different base rates, that you cannot balance both error types across such groups — have been settled mathematically for years, yet the argument over what they mean has not moved an inch: everyone concedes the theorems and the camps just talk past each other. On one side are those who read the results like Arrow's theorem, as proving that our fairness intuitions form an inconsistent set, so that any classifier deployed in a base-rate-divergent setting embodies an unavoidable value tradeoff that has to be made explicitly. On the other are those who think this is a category error: the results are statistical trivialities, artifacts of conditioning on group membership in populations with different prevalence — informative in the way Simpson's paradox is informative, but showing nothing deep about fairness, and dissolvable by fixing what we condition on or what we treat as ground truth. My working hypothesis is that the two camps are answering different questions under one heading — one asking which conditions can be jointly satisfied, the other asking which conditions are worth wanting — and so never actually collide. Is that right, or is there a genuine substantive disagreement hiding here, perhaps about whether base rates themselves are fair game or an injustice to be corrected? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a metric or intervention that dissolves the tradeoff, a conceptual distinction that splits the question, or an argument that the impasse should be expected to be permanent.
