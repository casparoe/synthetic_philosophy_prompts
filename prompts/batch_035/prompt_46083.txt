I sit on the animal welfare review panel at a marine biology institute in the UK, and we have a proposal I don't think our protocols were built for. A postdoc wants to run a three-phase study: pain-learning experiments on octopuses (mild electric shocks, no analgesia), a trade-off study on shore crabs where minor injuries are inflicted to see whether they'll give up shelter to avoid further harm, and a third phase on bees that's basically the crab design scaled down. Our protocols have always covered vertebrates; cephalopods got pulled in semi-formally after the sentience legislation a few years back, but nobody has ever had to decide about crustaceans or insects, and the law is famously unhelpful here — it recognises these animals as sentient without telling us what follows for research practice.

The panel is split three ways. One camp wants it approved as-is, on the grounds that the invertebrate evidence "isn't there yet" and we shouldn't make policy by vibes. One camp wants everything rejected pending anaesthesia protocols — which, awkwardly, nobody actually knows how to deliver to a crab. And a couple of us think the real job is to write down criteria we can reuse for future invertebrate proposals rather than just issuing a verdict on this one.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before thursday's meeting. I need a clear bottom line — approve, approve with conditions, or reject, per phase — plus a checklist of criterias the panel can apply going forward: what evidence about a taxon's nervous system and behaviour should matter, and what kinds of procedures should trigger what kinds of protections. The memo has to genuinely engage the underlying question, which is what grounds the distribution of consciousness across animals in the first place — is it nociception, is it centralised integration of information, is it the capacity for flexible trade-offs between avoiding harm and pursuing other goods, and which of these could we even detect from the outside? Where the honest answer is "we can't tell," the memo should say what we should do while we can't tell, not paper over it.

Two more things. Please take seriously the objection from the approve-as-is camp: precaution without limits isn't a standard, and if crabs are in scope then arguably bees are, and if bees then where does it stop — half of biology ends up needing an ethics licence. If our line just tracks "animals people feel bad about," that's vertebrate-plus-octopus chauvinism, and the memo should admit it if that's what we're doing. And write the whole thing in British English, kept short enough that the non-philosophers on the panel will actually read it.
