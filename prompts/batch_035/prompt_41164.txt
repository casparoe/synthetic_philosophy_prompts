I'm a science-and-ideas journalist at work on a long-form piece about how we weigh many small harms against one large one. The philosophical debate on this is usually staged with numbers no reader can picture — a momentary eye irritation for an unimaginably vast number of people versus decades of torture for one — and my thesis for the piece is that the same dilemma, with all its discomfort, survives intact at a scale ordinary readers can do arithmetic on. To that end, build me a thought experiment to the following specifications:

1. Human scale. Every number must be small enough for mental arithmetic, every burden must be one a reader has personally experienced, and the stakes must be the sort a city council or hospital administrator could actually face. No torture, no astronomical figures, no science fiction.
2. Same currency on both sides. The many small burdens and the single larger burden must be of the same kind, so that nobody can escape the arithmetic by claiming the two sides are incommensurable.
3. Engineered disagreement. The case must be built so that a linear aggregationist, who simply sums the burdens, is driven to the verdict readers will find cold, while a theorist who refuses to aggregate across persons lands on the verdict readers will find kind. The coldness must come from the arithmetic alone, not from making the single burden lurid or the many-side harm sneaky.

After the scenario itself (one paragraph), walk through what each of three positions commits to: (a) straightforward aggregation of burdens; (b) a threshold view on which burdens too small to register individually can never be added up into something that outweighs a serious harm; and (c) a contractualist view that holds burdens falling on different people cannot be summed against one another at all. Confirm that the case derives those verdicts rather than stipulating them. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, identify the crossover point at which the aggregationist's verdict would flip, and explain how a defender of the view that comes out looking cold would attack the case — and whether, at human scale, that defense is more or less persuasive than it is against the original. Please respond in American English.
