I just reread Ibsen's The Wild Duck for a seminar I'm teaching on self-deception, and I want to read the whole play through the debate about whether self-deception is even possible. The obvious frame is that Gregers is the philosopher who wants truth at any cost and Relling is the one who insists people need the lies they live by -- Relling even claims that stripping Hjalmar of his lie would be the real crime against him. But I want more than that. My real question is Hjalmar himself: by the end he seems to genuinly believe in his own invention -- the coming breakthrough, the great invention that will redeem the family -- while also half-knowing it's empty. So is Ibsen dramatizing the Sartrean picture, where self-deception is a weird state of sort of knowing and not knowing at once, or is it just motivated belief drift of the kind Alfred Mele says is enough, no contradictory beliefs required? And what do I do with old Ekdal up in the garret happily hunting rabbits that aren't much of a hunt, and with Hedvig, who might be the only character who never deceives herself and yet pays the worst price for everyone else's lying to themselves? I'd like a reading that says which view of self-deception the play actually supports, where the play resists being mapped onto that debate at all, and whether it ends up showing something neither the Sartre camp nor the Mele camp manages to say in plain argument.
