I teach an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of punishment, and last week's session on capital punishment ended with a colleague of mine in the law school shutting down our discussion of deterrence evidence by reciting Blackstone's line that it is better that ten guilty persons escape, than that one innocent suffer — delivered, as it usually is, as though it were a self-evident axiom. My students took it that way, too, and the conversation simply moved on. But the more I think about it, the less clear I am about what the maxim is actually claiming, or how it's supposed to do the work my colleague wanted it to do, namely, to show that the death penalty is impermissible. Blackstone himself, as I understand it, was making a point about the cautious admission of presumptive evidence, not about punishments at all, and the number has varied wildly across the tradition — Hale said five, Franklin said a hundred, Maimonides said a thousand. If the figure can be adjusted freely without anyone feeling they've changed the claim, then whatever the maxim is asserting, it presumably isn't a literal ratio of harms.

I'd like to open our next session by laying out the distinct precise theses that could be packed into that one sentence, so that we can see which of them my colleague was actually entitled to. Some readings I can gesture at: it might be a claim about the evidentiary standard for conviction (a rule of procedure about how much doubt suffices for acquittal); it might be a comparative claim about the relative badness of the two error types (false acquittal versus false conviction), perhaps even a cardinal one suitable for expected-utility reasoning; it might be an agent-relative claim about the special wrong done when the state, as our collective agent, kills an innocent person, as opposed to the diffusely distributed wrong of letting the guilty go free; or it might be a claim specifically about irreversibility, in which case it's worth pressing on whether that reading really distinguishes execution from a life sentence served by someone who dies in prison before exoneration. One of my students also proposed, rather cleverly I thought, that if the maxim is a genuine ratio, it should scale with the severity of the punishment — a higher tolerated rate of false conviction for parking fines than for decades of incarceration — and I confess I don't know whether that proposal honors the maxim or misses its point entirely.

Could you produce a disambiguation of the maxim, laying the candidate readings out as a menu running from the trivial to the substantive, with a verdict on each: whether it's plausible, and, crucially, whether it would actually entail the impermissibility of the death penalty or merely the need for a very high standard of proof before imposing it? For each reading, please say what work the number ten is doing — placeholder, rhetorical flourish, or genuine quantity — and where the reading leaves room for my student's scaling proposal. I want something I can bring back to the seminar as a map of the logical space rather than an argument for one position on it.
