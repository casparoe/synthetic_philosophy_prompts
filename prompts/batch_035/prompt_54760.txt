Here's the essay question we were given:

"In the paradox of deontology, a deontological constraint appears to forbid an action which, if performed, would reduce the total number of violations of that very same constraint. Explain why this is supposed to be a paradox, and assess the reply that an agent is simply not responsible for wrongdoing committed by other agents."

And here's what the student wrote:

"The paradox is that deontology forbids you from doing something bad even when doing it would prevent more of the same bad thing from happening. For example, you're not allowed to kill one person in order to stop five other people being killed by someone else. From a global point of view this seems mad: there ends up being more killing, which is exactly what the constraint was against. The consequentialist doesn't have this problem, because she just counts up outcomes and picks the option with fewer deaths.

The term was coined by Derek Parfit in Reasons and Persons. The point of the paradox is that if constraints are meant to protect people from being killed, then a rule that produces more killings protects people worse than a rule that produces fewer, so the constraint defeats its own purpose.

As for the reply that I'm not responsible for other people's killings — I think it works. Even a consequentialist like Scheffler accepts that agents can give extra weight to their own projects, so obviously they can also give extra weight to what they do with their own hands. If five deaths are caused by another agent and one is caused by me, the five are his fault and not mine, and morality is about what I do, not about the global scoreboard. So there's no paradox really: each person keeps their own hands clean, and that's all morality can ask of them."
