I've been testing my gut about causation on cases where the two big approaches come apart — the plain counterfactual view (C caused E iff had C not happened, E wouldn't have) and the interventionist view (C caused E iff wiggling C while holding the rest fixed wiggles E) — and my verdicts don't line up with either one, so now I want to know if there's a principle behind them or if my gut is just confused.

1. Suzy's rock shatters the bottle a moment before Billy's would have: Suzy caused it, Billy didn't. Even though had Suzy not thrown, the bottle still shatters — so no counterfactual dependence, and my verdict still feels rock solid.
2. Two arsonists independently set fires that merge and burn the house down, either fire sufficient on its own: I want to say each of them caused the burning, full stop — not just "contributed to" it.
3. The gardener who didn't water my plant caused it to die; the hundreds of neighbors who also didn't water it caused nothing.
4. A pilot shoots down a bomber seconds before it would've destroyed a bridge: I think the pilot caused the bridge's survival, even though that's a prevention of a prevention and I honestly can't say what you'd "hold fixed" to get it out of the formalism.
5. My parents meeting at that dance caused me to exist. Firm gut verdict. But any intervention on whether they meet seems to delete the very person whose cause we're asking about, so I don't know what the interventionist machinery is even evaluating here.

So: is there one principle that gets all five, or close to it? If the answer is some hybrid or something norm-sensitive, spell out exactly which of my verdicts it can't save and why. And then tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't made up my mind on: a safety regulator who omits to require a guardrail on a curve, where the car that crashed there would very likely have crashed somewhere else that same night anyway.
