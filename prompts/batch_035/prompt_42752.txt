On my product team we live by a rule our VP sums up as "telemetry over testimony": what users actually click in production is the ground truth about what they want, while what they tell us in interviews is unreliable self-report — the way a profiler's output is ground truth about which code actually runs, whatever the developer claims. A friend who did graduate work in economics recently told me that the phrase we borrow for this, "revealed preference," comes from a theory in which a person's preferences are just defined as whatever that person would choose, so that a choice cannot fail to match a preference. On that theory, she says, nothing is "revealed" at all: the choice is the preference, and calling it revealed is a relabeling. This bothers me, because our whole practice treats clicks as evidence about an independent state of users — their wants — which self-reports are also, more noisily, evidence about. If she's right, there is no independent state, and our ground-truth talk is empty.

Please audit the situation. First, give me the correspondence table: in our practice, what plays the role of the theory's choices, its preferences, and the underlying "want" we take ourselves to be measuring — and in the profiler analogy, what plays the role of the machine code that the profiler is checking against? Second, say where our usage of the idea holds and where it breaks: is the friend's version of the theory really as empty as she claims, or does it retain nontrivial content — for instance by constraining what counts as a single consistent preference, or by licensing claims about options the user never faced? Third, tell me whether the VP's conclusion survives: if preference is nothing over and above choice, does "users want X" reduce to "users clicked X," and what would we lose thereby — for example, the ability to say that users wanted a feature our interface hid from them? Finish by stating what would have to be true for clicks to genuinely reveal something beyond themselves, and whether the profiler analogy can be repaired or should be dropped.
