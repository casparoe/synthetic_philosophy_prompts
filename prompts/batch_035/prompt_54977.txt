I record a monthly interview series on philosophy of action, and our next guest is a philosopher whose recent book defends what she calls an intrapersonal constitutionalism about weakness of will. Her view, in brief: because we discount hyperbolically rather than exponentially, our preferences reverse as events draw near — the dieter who at breakfast sincerely prefers abstaining at dinner may, come dinner, sincerely prefer the cake — so the person is best modelled not as one agent failing to govern herself but as a succession of parties with misaligned interests, each doing tolerably well for itself given the others. Continent behaviour, on her account, is what this succession achieves when it builds internal institutions — binding arrangements, routines, deliberately engineered environments — rather as external institutions solve commons problems; akrasia is accordingly not a breakdown of practical reason at all, but a bargaining failure among selves, nothing more.

Draft around ten interview questions that engage the content of the position rather than asking her to restate it. At least one question each on the following, and for every question a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give: (i) whether her view genuinely answers Davidson's treatment of akrasia as acting against one's own all-things-considered judgement, or merely relocates the judgement to whichever self wins the bargain; (ii) whether precommitment devices — Ulysses ordering himself bound to the mast — amount to self-paternalism towards a future self that never consented, and how she would reply to someone who objects that treating the person as a crowd to be institutionally managed gets the ethics of self-binding exactly backwards; (iii) what becomes of responsibility if relapse is a coordination failure among selves — is the smoker who sincerely resolved to quit still blameworthy, and if so, whom exactly do we blame; and (iv) how much of the normative story survives if hyperbolic discounting turns out to be partly a laboratory artefact, given that measured discount curves vary with amounts, delays, domains and individuals, and some people some of the time fit an exponential model quite well. Please answer in American English.
