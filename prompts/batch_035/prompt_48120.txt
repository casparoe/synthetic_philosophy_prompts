My brother-in-law works at an AI lab and at thanksgiving he gave me his whole spiel on whether LLMs are agents, and I've been chewing on it since. His summary, as best I can reconstruct it: base models (the pre-RLHF ones) aren't agents at all — they're better described as simulators, programs that imitate the whole distribution of internet text, so whatever persona you seem to be talking to is just a character being simulated, like an actor reading lines. On his telling, the simulator is completely neutral, a screen that movies play on, and has no goals of its own in any sense. Then RLHF comes along and, he says, the simulator view itself predicts that this collapses the superposition into one single stable agent — the helpful assistant — so the chat models you actually use ARE agents, just very boring corporate ones. And the practical upshot he drew was that this basically dissolves the inner alignment worry about models developing their own misaligned goals: if there's nothing in there that's a persistent agent, there's nothing inside the model for a bad goal to attach to, and the whole "mesa-optimizer" concern is a category error. You just have to make sure you're talking to the right character.

I'm inclined to believe him since he does this for a living and I don't, but a couple of things nagged at me. He kept sliding between "simulating" in the sense of predicting text and "simulating" in the sense of imagining a little person in its head, and I couldn't tell if that mattered. Also the jump from "no persistent agent" to "nothing to be misaligned" felt fast — like, can't a computation do something bad even if it doesn't have a self? Anyway, I want an audit, not a lecture defending him. Go through his summary piece by piece: what would someone who actually holds the simulator view accept, what would they wince at, and where is he maybe taking a conclusion the view doesn't license. And tell me whether the "inner alignment is dissolved" upshot follows from the parts of his story that survive.
