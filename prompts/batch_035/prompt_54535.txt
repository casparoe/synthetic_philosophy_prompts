I paint signs and do lettering for a living, mostly storefronts and the occasional chalkboard menu. Lately I've been wrestling with something that happened on a job and I can't let it go, so I want to lay it out and hear what you think.

I did a big window for a coffee place where I painted the whole menu in this loose, gestural style — I basically wrote it the way you'd write a quick note to a friend, then fattened up the strokes. All the letters are technically "wrong" in the sense that no two of my a's look the same, some are barely closed, the crossbar of the t floats way off to the side, and my e is more of a loop with a gap than anything you'd find in a book. Customers walk up and read the menu fine, nobody has ever once asked what a letter says. But the owner's cousin, who does graphic design, told her the lettering "doesn't really function as typography" because the letterforms are undisciplined, and now the owner is hinting she wants it redone in something clean.

Here's what's bugging me. The cousin's complaint only makes sense if there's some real standard a letter is supposed to meet beyond being read. But I keep going back and forth on this. On one hand, sure, there's something an 'a' is — I can feel it when I'm drawing, some of my attempts land as 'a' and some just die into a squiggle, and I know the difference even when I couldn't tell you the rule. There's a line somewhere, and I've watched beginning students cross it constantly. On the other hand, if every single person reads my sloppy 'a' as an 'a' without hesitation, what work is the "real" standard doing? It feels weird to say millions of successful readings are all sort of lucky accidents while the cousin's textbook 'a' is the true one. And it's not just sloppiness — take the two lowercase shapes we both call 'a', the handwritten one-story kind and the printed double-story kind. They don't share anything, not one stroke, and yet somehow they're the same letter. That's before you even get to blackletter or the a's in old inscriptions, which my cousin-in-law the typography guy would insist are also 'a'.

So my actual questions: When the cousin says my letters don't function, is there a fact of the matter she's tracking, or just a preference for print conventions dressed up as a fact? Is the thing that makes an 'a' an 'a' something in the shape, something in us readers, or something in the whole system of the alphabet that no single letter carries by itself? And practically — if I want to keep pushing my loose style but stay on the right side of whatever line there is, what should I actually be paying attention to when I draw? I want to understand the issue, not just get told I'm right and she's wrong, so if she has a real point somewhere in there, tell me where.

Please write in American English. Also, tell me how confident you are in each part of what you say, and point to the single claim you'd be least sure of and why.
