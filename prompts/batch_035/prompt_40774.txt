Over the past year my friend has been arguing with me about who is owed what, and I have slowly realized her verdicts form a track record I can't decode. She spent a year on a local citizens' panel that heard appeals from people denied discretionary hardship grants, and though she has never studied philosophy, her rulings across the cases we've argued about at dinner are remarkably steady. Here is the record. A woman paralyzed from birth, unable to hold a job and needing daily care: owed substantial lifelong support. A man with the same income as everyone around him who is simply of gloomy temperament — no illness has ever been diagnosed, just a permanently low-set mood nobody has proposed treating: owed nothing beyond what anyone else gets. A deaf woman, content with her life, active in the Deaf community, who says she would refuse a cure if offered one, but who needs sign-language interpreters at public expense to do her job and take part in public life: owed the interpreters. A man who freely gave up a corporate career to be a potter, prefers that life, and consequently has less money and admits to being somewhat less satisfied than his former colleagues: owed nothing beyond the ordinary tax system, no special compensation for his modest welfare. And a man with a painful but treatable condition, who says he has cheerfully adjusted and that his life goes well: the state must fund the treatment regardless. The moods get nothing and the pain gets something, and I cannot yet say why.

Next month the three of us will be at dinner together, and I want to arrive with predictions rather than intuitions. The new cases I intend to put to her are these. First, a man who dreams of being a novelist, works hard at it, has no disability and a decent income, but simply lacks the talent — his welfare is low through no fault or choice of his own, in just the way the gloomy-temperament man's mood was not his choice: is he owed anything? Second, a disabled employee who could work fully if given costly accommodations, but who refuses them and instead demands their cash equivalent to spend as he pleases: does her approach entitle him to the accommodation, the money, or nothing? Third, a woman with adequate income and no illness whose life is simply lonely — she wants close relationships and cannot form them: is that like the gloomy temperament, so that nothing is owed, or like the pain case, so that something is owed, whatever something could even mean there?

What I would like from you is a careful reconstruction rather than answers. Lay out the candidate principles that fit her record so far — the standard answers to the old question of whether equality should be measured in welfare, in resources, or in capabilities, in whatever responsibility-sensitive variant it takes to accommodate the potter — and be explicit about the points where her verdicts leave these principles underdetermined, since on my reading at least two of them fit everything she has said. Then work through what each principle predicts about each of my three cases, so that I can see precisely where the principles come apart. Give me your best guess at her actual verdicts, with your reasons for preferring that reading. And finally, propose the further case — one I have not thought of — that would best discriminate between the principles that survive her record, since I would rather settle this over one dessert than over three.
