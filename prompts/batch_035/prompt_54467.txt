I write literary fiction and my editor keeps complaining that my characters "deliberate like spreadsheets" when they're at a big life crossroads. I've been reading about transformative experience theory, the L.A. Paul idea that for choices like having a kid you can't know what the post-choice self will even be like, so ordinary expected-value reasoning breaks down and the decision is made on a kind of jump. I want to turn that into my actual craft tool. Please write me a procedure I can run every time I'm drafting one of these decision scenes (there are three coming up in my current draft and my deadline is in six weeks): the questions to ask about the choice in order — is it epistemically transformative, personally transformative, both, or actually neither and I'm just being melodramatic — what the character can and can't plausibly know at the point of choice, how to render the deliberation so it feels real instead of either a coin flip or a pro/con list, and where the procedure has to stop and admit it's an artistic call I have to make myself. Then run it on three of my upcoming scenes: a woman deciding whether to have her first child at 41, a 19-year-old enlisting, and a man deciding whether to take a much better job in another country. Fair warning that I've planned a huge anguished setpiece around the job one — if your procedure says that choice isn't actually transformative in the relevant sense and I should write it as ordinary calculation, tell me, but know I'll be annoyed.
