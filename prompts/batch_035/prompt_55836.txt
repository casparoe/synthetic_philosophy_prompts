I've got a newsletter that goes out to the members of our formal epistemology reading group (mostly PhD students, a couple of stubborn faculty, and one maths postdoc who lurks) and this term we've been going through what happens when all your new information arrives as a conditional. We ended up living in the Judy Benjamin problem for a month: soldier dropped in a swamp, radio operator tells her that if she's in one territory the odds are 3:1 that she's near that side's headquarters, and if she's in the other territory the odds are 3:1 against, and the question is what her credence in which territory should be afterwards. Half the room thought the answer was obvious, and the other half agreed on a completely different obvious answer, which is when I realised nobody in the room could actually say which rule they were following as opposed to which answer they liked.

So the plan for the next issue: a "which update rule do you actually follow?" quiz, and I want you to write it. Eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the positions. The positions that have to show up as leaves: updating on the material conditional (treating the speaker as having told you not-p-or-q), minimum entropy or minimum relative entropy update, the van Fraassen-style nearest-prior approach, explanation-based updating where you also consider why the speaker said what they said, the view that a mere conditional probability report leaves your credence in the antecedent untouched, and the deflationary leaf where the question is ill-posed because conditionals aren't propositions with truth values at all. Don't just re-run Judy Benjamin every time — the cases should include the swamp scenario with my numbers (prior 50/50 on territories, 3/1 odds towards headquarters in each), an asymmetric variant where the two headquarters areas are different sizes, a case where the informant's deciding to speak is itself evidence (our radio operator is known to only call people she's located, say), and something from ordinary life like a colleague announcing that if the dean approves the department's budget request, they're 90% confident we get the seminar room. The key requirement: every question has to be answerable by someone who has never heard of any of the rules, so no question can just ask "do you believe in minimum entropy?" — it has to be a case where the rules genuinely come apart and the reader's gut answer places them.

Build the key from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions that split the space (does a conditional assertion carry information about the antecedent at all; when the constraints underdetermine the posterior, is the gap filled by minimal change, maximal entropy, explanation, or nothing; does it matter whether the information comes as a bare conditional probability versus a speaker's utterance you might model), then locate the positions in that space. Flag the combinations nobody holds, with your best guess at whether that's because the view is incoherent or just unattractive or merely unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves the quiz can't really seperate — I suspect minimum entropy and nearest-prior only come apart in the asymmetric cases, but check whether even those questions distinguish them, and if they don't, say so rather than pretending otherwise. One more thing: if you think the whole quiz rests on a confused premise — e.g. that there's no fact of the matter about what Judy should believe after a mere conditional, so the quiz would be sorting readers by reactions to cases rather than by a genuine disagreement between rules — please tell me that plainly instead of building it anyway; I'd rather know before it goes out. And write the whole thing in British English, favour, whilst, recognises, the lot. Also please keep the maths light enough that members can do the quiz on their phones over coffee — no question should require actual computation, the numbers are there to make the case vivid, not to be solved.
