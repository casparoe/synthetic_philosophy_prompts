I am a recently retired immunologist — thirty-six years studying antibody affinity maturation, mostly in mice — and in retirement I have been working my way into philosophy of mind, where I have encountered Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures. The scheme stayed politely in the back of my mind until I noticed something that has been on my bench my entire career, and now it will not leave me alone.

Consider the germinal center reaction. Within a single animal's lifetime, B-cell clones undergo somatic hypermutation, and those variants whose receptors bind antigen more avidly preferentially receive T-cell help and expand, while the rest die out. The repertoire is thus modified by the consequences of its variants' interactions with the environment, within the lifetime of the organism, with no instruction from the antigen about what the change should be — pure selection, no teaching. The parallel to operant conditioning is what keeps nagging at me: behavior emitted at random, consequences doing the selecting, the animal improved at exploiting its environment by the end of the process. And there is even a whiff of the Popperian: variants are, in effect, hypotheses put forward and tested against the antigen before anything is committed to the memory compartment.

So here is what I would like from you. Give me your credence that the adaptive immune system of a vertebrate, considered as a whole, is a Skinnerian creature in Dennett's sense — a number, not a shrug. Then decompose it, with a rough probability and a sentence of justification for each of at least the following terms, plus any others you think belong:

1. That Dennett's hierarchy marks principled joints in design space rather than being a loose heuristic with fuzzy, gerrymandered boundaries — in particular, that the Darwinian/Skinnerian divide is a real divide and not a difference of degree.
2. That the dividing line turns on within-lifetime selection by consequences in the abstract, rather than on any specifically behavioral or neural implementation — i.e., that nothing in the concept requires muscles, movement, or observable behavior in the animal's external environment.
3. That clonal selection driven by antigen and T-cell help genuinely counts as consequences doing the selecting, given that the antigen neither rewards nor punishes; it simply binds better or worse, and the cell lives or dies accordingly. (I confess I cannot decide whether this difference is superficial or fatal.)
4. That the unit of analysis question resolves the right way: Dennett attributes the categories to creatures, and it is the vertebrate host, not the individual B cell, whose competence is enhanced — though it is hard to say the mouse has learned anything it can act on.

Please also tell me how the terms combine into your final number, which single term that number is most sensitive to, and what argument or consideration would move it the furthest. For calibration: my colleague from the tumor immunology group dismisses the whole comparison at something like 0.1 on the grounds that Skinner's explanatory vocabulary was built for publicly observable behavior and stretches beyond it only by abuse, while my old postdoc, now in a systems immunology lab, holds something like 0.7 and says the germinal center is the cleanest instance of learning-by-consequences in all of biology. I would also like to know, if you can say, which of those two people you think my disagreement is really with — the one about the concept's boundaries, or the one about the biology.
