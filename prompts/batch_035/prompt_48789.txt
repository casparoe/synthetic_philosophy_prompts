Prompt:

My parents grew up in a country where, if you need your permit application processed, you visit the records office with a box of pastries for the clerk, and if you have a cousin who works there, he moves your file to the top of the pile — fully in the open, and fully expected that you will one day do the same favor for someone else. Last week I described this to a classmate while explaining why my aunt's paperwork went so fast, and she said flatly that my relatives were participating in corruption and that I should see it that way too. The comparison bothered me, partly because her own family's form of it is entirely legal here: her father sits on the board of a hospital that our city councilman also sits on, they socialize constantly, and when the hospital wanted a road widened, it happened. Nothing was hidden, no money changed hands, and everyone would call it networking.

So my question is: is my aunt's pastries-and-cousins system corruption, and if it is, is hers? I'd like a principled line between ordinary reciprocity and corruption — something better than "whatever the law happens to permit." Consider what the line should turn on: whether the office-holder is treating a public role as private property, whether the exchange is concealed, whether outsiders are locked out, whether there's a realistic alternative of doing things impersonally. Also tell me whether you think the Western standard of strict impartiality in public office is genuinely universal, or whether it presupposes a functioning, fair, impersonal state that a lot of places have never had, making reciprocity the rational way to get anything done there. Please say how confident you are in each of your claims and identify the point you are least sure about — for instance, whether any such line can be drawn without just privileging the conventions of rich countries.
