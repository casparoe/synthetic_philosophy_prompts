I'm a macroeconomist by training — comfortable with endogenous money, the monetary base, seigniorage, sectoral balances, the interaction of Treasury and central bank balance sheets, Cantillon effects, and the chartalist claim that taxes drive money — but with only a tourist's knowledge of the history of economic thought. I've been told that George Berkeley (the immaterialist bishop) wrote seriously about money in The Querist, published in Dublin in three parts between 1735 and 1737 and substantially revised in 1750, and that some historians read him as an early endogenous-money theorist, or even a proto-Keynesian. Rather than take their word for it, I want the position itself, translated into my vocabulary.

To save you guessing at what I might already know, here is my working sketch of the view as I understand it, which you should correct where I've got it wrong. Berkeley held that money is not wealth but something like a ticket or counter — a device for keeping accounts and transferring command over labor and goods between people — and that the wealth of a nation consists instead in the industry of its people, i.e., the quantity and employment of its labor. He took the mercantilist obsession with hoarding silver to rest on conflating the measure with the thing measured, argued that a country starved of coin could still sustain exchange among a trusting people by using paper credit, and proposed an Irish national bank issuing notes to fund public works and keep labor employed during the periodic slumps of an underdeveloped, specie-drained colony. He also seems to have thought the value of the unit could be stabilized by attention to the proportion between the credit issued and the industry it was meant to circulate.

Restate that position, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use. Specifically: map his ticket-conception of money onto the distinction between the unit of account and the means of settlement; say what his national bank proposal looks like described as a central bank with a development-finance mandate; decide whether his claim that credit should be proportional to industry is best rendered as something like a nominal-income-targeting rule, a loanable-funds claim, or neither; and place him relative to the chartalist versus commodity-money poles of the debate I know. Then give me the residue report. Which of Berkeley's claims survive translation into modern monetary economics more or less intact? Which survive only by being distorted — and say precisely what gets distorted? And which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all? I'm especially interested in his normative and theological framing: he seems to have thought unemployment and hoarded coin were moral disorders in a way that neither my welfare-theoretic nor my monetary-policy vocabulary comfortably captures, and I suspect his talk of money as a public trust rooted in mutual confidence among a people doesn't map onto anything I have. For each item in the residue, tell me whether you think the failure is a defect of my vocabulary — something Berkeley saw that modern monetary economics has dropped — or a sign that his formulation was less clear than it looked, resting on assumptions a colonized, pre-industrial economy let him make without noticing. Do not flatter him because he's famous, and do not assume the modern framework is better just because it's mine.
