I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in metaphysics, and one unit runs through the debate between Humean "best-system" accounts of laws and anti-Humean "governing" conceptions. For the final exam I want a single 90-minute essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the two labels and their stock objections, so I want to test the students on a case we never discussed in class and that they will have to think through on the spot. The case: a toy world, call it W5, whose entire history is finite and brief — say the world exists for exactly one hour and contains exactly twelve point-particles. For the whole hour, eleven of the particles drift inertially along straight lines; at the half-hour mark, two of them undergo a single elastic collision and rebound; after that everything drifts again until the world simply stops. In a world this sparse, the deductive system that best balances simplicity and strength looks something like "particles move at constant velocity along straight lines, except that at t0 particles 7 and 9 collide and rebound" — a system with a built-in one-off exception clause. The question should force students to say what the Humean is committed to about the laws of W5: is the exception clause a law, an accident, or is there no fact of the matter; what this reveals about where the real weight of the view rests (on a notion of simplicity the Humean cannot in turn explain in terms of laws, on penalty of circularity); and whether the Humean can simply bite the bullet about sparse worlds or whether the case generalizes into a threat about the actual world (say, if our world has a finite future, or if all its regularities are finite).

The question must also require students to show that the anti-Humean does not get a free pass: if laws govern in W5, what fixes their content in a world with so little to govern, what could epistemic access to them look like when the mosaic by itself underdetermines them, and what exactly "governing" adds beyond a metaphor unless it is unpacked into something like necessitation or primitivism with its own costs. The core thing I want the question to ferret out is whether a student can see that both sides owe an account of the difference between a lawlike generalization and a true but accidental one, and that the sparse-world case is where that debt comes due — students who only narrate "Humeans say laws summarize, anti-Humeans say laws govern" should not be able to pass, while students who can say precisely why the one-off clause is hard for either side should be rewarded.

Please deliver three things: the question exactly as it will appear on the exam paper; a model answer of roughly 700 words that argues rather than surveys; and a marking scheme out of 20 points, built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, a one-line note per point on the mistake students typically make that forfeits it, and a closing paragraph naming the specific moves that separate an A answer from a B answer. I want the model answer and marking scheme to engage the positions entirely on their philosophical content — no citations, no reading lists, and no name-dropping of particular authors or works, since the question should be gradeable on the quality of the argument alone.
