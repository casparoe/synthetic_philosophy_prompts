Write a piece of satirical software release notes, roughly 600–700 words, formatted as a version history for a hypothetical product called "Moral Language." The maintainers are a team of expressivists who originally shipped the product so that speakers could utter moral sentences by expressing noncognitive attitudes — the earliest versions handled standalone assertions like "murder is wrong" beautifully — but users then discovered what is now internally known as the embedding problem, going back to the Frege–Geach objection: once moral sentences are negated, put in conditionals, disjoined, or used as premises in mixed arguments with descriptive premises, the semantics stopped explaining how such sentences could have truth-apt content, figure in valid inferences, and mean the same thing embedded as unembedded, or why asserting a negation differs from merely denying the original sentence.

Each version entry should be a successive attempted fix in the spirit of the real moves expressivists have made — increasingly complex attitudes, higher-order attitudes toward whole patterns of attitudes, deflationary minimalist notions of truth and content — described entirely in changelog idiom: feature additions, deprecation notices, known issues, bug references like "disjunction bug #7," and a support forum thread in which users report that modus ponens with a moral major premise has stopped feeling binding. No philosopher, paper, or book may be cited by name, and the release notes must never step outside their genre to explain the philosophy.

Constraints on the piece:

- The humor must come from getting the semantics right, so that a metaethicist laughs rather than winces. Every joke should exaggerate a genuine feature of the expressivist's situation: the difficulty of distinguishing negation from denial, asserting a conditional without asserting its consequent, embedding the same sentence in attitude ascriptions, and preserving validity in mixed arguments — not invented bugs.
- The engineering dialectic should track the real one: every patch that restores compositional behavior in embedded contexts quietly reintroduces the truth-conditional machinery the team abandoned realism in order to escape, and the developers' own release notes should betray this pattern without anyone admitting it.
- The final version entry should report, deadpan, that the current release is behaviorally indistinguishable from its realist competitor, and that the difference between the two has been marked "working as intended."
- Stay deadpan and in genre throughout: no winking at the reader, no footnoted explanations. The piece should be funny to someone who knows the metaethics literature and still followable, as a story about a team shipping increasingly desperate patches, to someone who doesn't.
