The question (I invented a slur so students would argue about the concepts rather than get distracted):

"Consider the invented slur 'smick,' targeting people from the invented nation of Smidland; 'Smidlarian' is the neutral counterpart. Explain the difference between the view that a slur's pejorative content is part of its semantic content and the view that it arises through pragmatic implicature. What do embedded occurrences — under negation, in the antecedents of conditionals, in questions — and quoted or mentioned occurrences tell us? Does the fact that a slur and its neutral counterpart are co-extensional settle the question? Defend one view, and say what you take the strongest consideration against it to be."

The student's answer, verbatim:

"A slur and its neutral counterpart are co-extensional: 'smick' and 'Smidlarian' pick out exactly the same people. So 'Dirk is a smick' is true under exactly the same conditions as 'Dirk is a Smidlarian.' Since the two sentences have the same truth conditions, they say the same thing, and so the difference between them — the offense — can't be part of the semantics. It has to be pragmatic, an implicature.

The embedding data confirms this. In 'If Dirk is a smick, I'm not hiring him,' the speaker does not assert that Dirk is a smick, yet the contempt still comes through. Likewise 'Is Dirk a smick?' and 'Dirk isn't a smick' — the pejorative content survives even when the sentence is unasserted or outright denied. So the contempt is not part of what is asserted but travels alongside it, which is the classic behavior of an implicature.

More specifically it is a conventional implicature, since it is triggered by the word itself regardless of context, unlike a conversational implicature, which has to be worked out from the cooperative principle. And like all implicatures, conventional ones pass Grice's cancellability test: a speaker can say 'Dirk is a smick — not that there's anything wrong with that' and thereby cancel the offense, which proves it was never part of what was literally said. If the offense were semantic, cancellation would be impossible, like trying to cancel part of the meaning of 'bachelor.'

Quoted uses point the same way. In 'Smick has one syllable,' the word is mentioned rather than used, and no offense occurs. Quotation removes the speaker's assertoric force, and with it the implicature, exactly as the pragmatic view predicts.

The one wrinkle is reclamation: among Smidlarians themselves, 'smick' can be used without contempt. But that is just context fixing different implicatures, and implicatures are context-dependent by nature, so it is no problem for the view."
