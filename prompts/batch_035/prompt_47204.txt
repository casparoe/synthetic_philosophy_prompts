I've been trying to get straight on path dependence after a seminar discussion that left me more confused than when I started. The question I keep circling is when history is genuinely "locked in" versus when the same broad outcome would have arrived anyway through some other route, and my gut verdicts on cases don't seem to line up under any single rule. So here they are. QWERTY persists even though (as I understand it, though I've also read economists claim the Dvorak superiority was wildly overstated) it's at best no better than alternatives, and no individual typist or even a large employer has any incentive to switch alone: locked in, obviously. But nineteenth-century American railroads ran on several different track gauges, and rather than one of them winning by luck, the industry coordinated a consolidation to a single gauge within a generation: so sometimes a locked-in standard gets undone on purpose. Meanwhile, at the largest scale, I find myself thinking that even fairly drastic changes — the Persians winning at Salamis, Britain's coal sitting three hundred miles from its ironworks — would only have shuffled the details, and industrialization, state formation, and mass literacy would still have arrived somewhere, eventually. My own field adds small cases with the same flavor: the US never adopted the metric system despite a century of its being obviously more workable for everyone, yet I'd also say that had some particular laboratory or company not invented a given tool, an equivalent tool would have existed within a few years regardless.

My tentative principle is something like: path dependence governs implementations (which keyboard layout, which gauge, which measurement system), while convergence governs functions (typing gets done, rails carry trains, computation happens), with the size of stakes tracking the reliability of convergence. But it's in trouble immediately. I believe both that the gasoline car beat the electric car around 1900 partly through network luck — roads, refueling infrastructure, one pivotal cost curve — and that this was a small-scale, implementation-level case, so my principle says locked in, fine. Yet I also believe electric cars are now, a century later, being unseated... no, gasoline is being displaced by electric precisely because the underlying function (personal motorized transport) is what converges, and I can't tell whether that supports my principle or shows that the "locked in" verdict on 1900 was just wrong in the way the railroad gauge verdict would suggest. Worse, I hold that English as the global lingua franca is highly contingent — no World War II as it went, and we're all negotiating in German or, more plausibly, some other European language — yet that's an implementation-level fact about as structural as they come, which cuts against convergence applying to big things. And my convergence verdicts about industrialization sit oddly next to my conviction that the actual historical arc of, say, the twentieth century's wars could easily not have happened at all, which is a big structural outcome I treat as radically contingent.

So I'd like a verdict on my verdicts, and a principle, or a short list of candidate principles, precise enough to have clauses I can point to. Please say which case motivates each clause, and be explicit about which of my verdicts your favored principle simply cannot accommodate, so I know what I'm being asked to give up rather than getting a story that quietly reconciles everything. Then tell me what the principle predicts on cases I haven't listed — in particular whether some form of widespread written musical notation, or widespread decimal arithmetic, was a convergent function or a locked-in implementation, and whether the burning of the Library of Alexandria cost the world real centuries of science on my account or just changed the delivery route of the same ideas. Throughout, please say how confident you are in each of the main claims, and single out the one point in your answer you're least sure of — I'd rather know where the footing is soft than get a confident survey that reads better than it reasons.
