I co-lead a philosophy reading group that has spent this term on collective intentionality and joint action, and for our final session I want a diagnostic instrument rather than another paper summary: a decision tree that sorts each of us into the account of joint action we actually hold, as opposed to the one we think we like. The tree should start from a commitment everyone in the room already accepts, namely that there is a genuine difference between two people each mowing their own half of a lawn side by side and two people mowing the lawn together, and it should lead from that starting point, through a series of branch questions, to a named leaf: Bratman's interlocking-intentions account, Gilbert's joint commitment and plural subject view, Searle's appeal to we-intentions and collective intentionality in action, Tuomela's distinction between the I-mode and the we-mode, the reductive or summative view on which all apparent collectivity dissolves into overlapping individual intentions, and the group-agent realism associated with List and Pettit, on which some collectives are themselves intentional subjects over and above their members. If the logical space you construct leaves some combinations of answers with no occupant, say so and diagnose whether that is because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and if there are two positions your tree cannot genuinely pull apart, flag that honestly instead of inventing a question that pretends to separate them.

The diagnostic work should be carried by cases rather than labels, and there are five cases I want built into the branch questions, because our group has found that they do the real sorting. First, the fence case: every spring the four households on my street repaint the shared fence, with no agreement ever voiced; each person shows up because the others do, and nobody could honestly say we planned it, yet each would feel let down if the others stayed inside. Second, the dog case: two spouses each privately intend that we walk the dog tonight, each intending that the other do the walking, and by coincidence each intention refers to the other's, so the dog gets walked exactly once. Third, the duet case: two singers perform a duet, one of them silently mouthing the words while a recording does the work, with the audience none the wiser and the two performers indistinguishable from the outside. Fourth, the corporation case: a company buries an internal memo, where every individual employee acts from a private intention that happens to mesh with the others', and no single person intends the burying, though we ordinarily and without hesitation say the company decided to bury it. Fifth, the walking case: two people are walking together, and one of them wants to stop; the question is whether stopping requires anything more than her own decision, or whether she is in some sense answerable to the other for walking on. Every branch question should be answerable by someone who has never heard of any of the authors, phrased as a concrete claim about one of these cases or a comparable one, and each branch should be labeled by the claim that splits it from its sibling, so that the tree is genuinely built out of the logical space rather than out of the reading list.

As to format: please render the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write it in a long letter. No headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and no diagram. Describe the tree in sentences instead: state the root, then walk through each internal node as a question together with the claims that answer it in each direction, and say which leaf each path terminates in, referring to the nodes by number or by the case they turn on. If two paths converge on the same leaf, say so explicitly, and close with a short paragraph on which pairs of leaves the tree leaves adjacent, and on what further case, if any, would be needed to prise them apart. Please make the tree compact enough that a person could honestly answer it about themselves in fifteen minutes, which I would guess means somewhere between six and nine branch questions, but use your judgment about the exact number.
