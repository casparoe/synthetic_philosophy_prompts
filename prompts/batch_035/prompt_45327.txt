I teach a graduate seminar on self-reference and the limits of formal systems, and this term I have been building a unit around the conceptual toolkit that Douglas Hofstadter assembled in Gödel, Escher, Bach and later streamlined in I Am a Strange Loop: the strange loop, the tangled hierarchy, and what he calls the inviolate level. The canonical image is Escher's Drawing Hands, in which two hands sketch each other into existence, each the source of the other — and yet the sheet of paper on which all of this appears is drawn by no hand within the frame. The paper and ink constitute a level that nothing inside the picture can reach, alter, or draw; the tangle is real but bounded, and it is precisely the inviolate substrate that makes the tangle possible. In the mathematical case, the analogous trick is Gödel numbering: a formal system that becomes powerful enough to represent statements about itself acquires the capacity for apparent level-crossing, while the machinery that implements it remains untouched by the tangle it hosts. My students can recite this vocabulary, but I want to test whether they can actually put it to work rather than merely decorate an argument with it.

The episode I have in mind is the long-running controversy over the Gödelian argument against mechanism: J. R. Lucas's 1961 paper in Philosophy, Minds, Machines and Gödel, and Roger Penrose's later revival of the argument in The Emperor's New Mind and Shadows of the Mind. The core claim, in brief, is that if a human mathematician's mind were equivalent to some formal system T, then Gödel's construction would yield a sentence G(T) that T can neither prove nor refute, while the mathematician, contemplating T from outside, can see that G(T) is true — so the mind outstrips any machine. For the analysis, assume the standard rejoinders as background: that what the human actually sees is only the conditional — if T is consistent, G(T) is true — and that by the second incompleteness theorem T cannot certify its own consistency, nor can the human certify it without begging the question; that the mind might itself be an inconsistent machine whose errors we would be unable to distinguish from insight; and that for any machine proposed, there is another whose Gödel sentence the human is then in no position to see. Do not re-litigate these from scratch; treat them as the known dialectical terrain.

What I want from you is a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions. First, map the episode onto the lens with care: identify what plays the role of the tangled hierarchy, what plays the inviolate level, and what exactly Lucas and Penrose are claiming — on the face of it, a genuine level-crossing, a mind that stands outside the tangle on no level at all. Then say what the lens genuinely illuminates: for instance, whether the standard rejoinder that the human's seeing is just another move inside some larger tangle, rather than a view from nowhere, is best expressed in this vocabulary, and whether that rejoinder gains anything by being so expressed. Finally, and most importantly, say what the episode shows about the lens itself: does the tangled-hierarchy picture do real explanatory work here, or does it quietly settle the mechanist question by stipulation — defining level-crossings as always bounded by an inviolate substrate — so that the analysis cannot be neutral between Hofstadter and Penrose? If you think it begs the question, say so plainly, and then try to state whatever non-question-begging content the lens retains in a form that a committed Penrosian could accept as a fair rendering of the dispute. Where the lens is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
