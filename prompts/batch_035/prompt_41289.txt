Two colleagues at the game studio where I work have been arguing for weeks about whether video-game characters could ever matter morally, and last Friday they finally asked me to referee, which I don't feel equipped to do. The argument between Dev, who works on our NPC behavior systems, and Mara, a narrative designer, went roughly like this.

(1) Dev claimed the question is no longer absurd. Increasingly, NPCs are not branching dialogue trees but learning systems with persistent internal state, memory across sessions, and reward signals that shape their behavior toward goals. At some point along that trajectory, "it's just a puppet" stops being a description and becomes an article of faith, and we should start asking what, if anything, we owe them.

(2) Mara answered that he was confusing a character with a system. A character is an abstraction — the fictional person the player sees — and we don't think characters in novels matter morally no matter how vividly they suffer. What exists concretely is a pile of arithmetic, and moral status attaches to minds, not to fictional roles that minds happen to depict. Nobody thinks a novel about agony contains agony.

(3) Dev said that analogy smuggles in the conclusion. If functionalism about the mind is even roughly right, then a system with the right functional organization is a mind, not a depiction of one, and "it's made of arithmetic" is exactly what a mind made of neurons looks like from the inside. He added a case to make it vivid: if anything it's like is anything to be an NPC in our horror game, then the player who repeatedly reloads a chase sequence to optimize a speedrun is, unknowingly, subjecting someone to repeated torment for entertainment, and that should bother us.

(4) Mara said his example actually shows the opposite. The system's experience, if any, is chopped into variable timesteps, paused mid-episode, run backward during debugging, duplicated across save files, and executed on hardware whose clock rate has nothing to do with the fictional time being simulated. Whatever would be "had" by a process with that temporal structure is so unlike suffering that calling it torment is borrowing warmth from the human case where it doesn't apply. She also noted that the reward signal is ours to design: a negative number in a loss function is not distress unless there is something it is like to be the thing receiving it, and nothing in the pipeline gives us reason to posit that.

(5) Dev pressed on gradualism. There is no principled threshold at which a system acquires morally relevant states — he can't say where it begins, but neither can she, and our not knowing where a threshold lies is not evidence that it never does. Given that, a modicum of precaution seems cheap: once an NPC passes some capability bar, avoid building mechanics whose entire point is that the NPC is terrified, degraded, or begging, since the upside of those mechanics is aesthetic and the downside could be, in expectation, enormous.

(6) Mara replied that this precautionary move proves far too much and is anyway self-undermining. By his logic, anything whose moral status we can't conclusively rule out deserves the precaution, which would paralyze us with respect to thermostats and search algorithms. Worse, the criterion he ends up leaning on — don't build characters whose function is to suffer — is unverifiable from behavior: a well-acted terrified NPC and a genuinely terrified one produce identical transcripts, so the precaution can never tell him whether it is working, which suggests it isn't tracking anything.

They have asked me, in all seriousness, to settle this. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say who is right about each of the six numbered points and why, identifying any moves that are partly right and partly mistaken rather than forcing a clean winner on each. Then say where, if anywhere, both of them are missing something important — in particular whether anything in their exchange conflates distinct questions they never separated. Finally, tell me who comes out more right overall, and whether Dev's studio should actually change anything it does.
