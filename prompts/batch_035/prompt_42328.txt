I am a philosophy professor who came to the field late: before graduate school I spent over a decade in veterinary practice, mixed small and large animal, and I confess that I still think in the vocabulary of the clinic — presenting complaints, signalment, reference intervals, differential lists, herd health status, quality-of-life scoring, and the practical distinction between a sick animal and an animal that is merely old, or merely inconvenient. I now teach philosophy of medicine, and I have grown dissatisfied with how the naturalism-versus-normativism debate about health and disease plays out in my seminar, where both sides tacitly treat reflective adult human patients as the paradigm case. My hunch is that veterinary medicine, where the patient almost never has articulate views about its own flourishing, is the better stress test — but I want this done carefully rather than polemically.

So: restate the main positions in that debate — Boorse's biostatistical theory, Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis, and a representative normativism in the vein of Nordenfelt — in the vocabulary of clinical veterinary practice, as faithfully as you can. For each position, tell me what translates cleanly, what must be distorted to fit the clinic's terms, and what cannot be expressed in those terms at all. I am particularly interested in whether the translation holds up for cases the human-medicine literature rarely foregrounds: subclinical dysfunction with no presenting complaint, selectively bred populations whose pathological tendencies are statistically typical precisely because we designed them that way, and the boundary between disease and mere deviation from a breed standard. Then give me the residue report: does anything in the philosophical debate survive only once we leave the clinic's vocabulary behind, and if so, is that because the clinic's vocabulary is missing something, or because the debate was never as clear as it looked when human self-report was doing unnoticed work throughout? I want to know whether my suspicion is right that naturalism looks strongest precisely where we never ask the patient.
