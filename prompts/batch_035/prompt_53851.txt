Here are the notes:

Working title: "In Defence of the Concorde: three cases where honouring sunk costs isn't obviously irrational"

Part 1 — Sunk costs as evidence, not reasons. The classic argument says sunk costs are causally inert: the money's gone whether I continue or not. But there's a Bayesian wrinkle. My past self's decision to invest heavily in a project is itself a piece of evidence about the project's value — evidence I may no longer have direct access to (I've forgotten the details of the original analysis, the people I consulted have moved on, etc.). In that case "I've already put so much in" functions less like a reason and more like a summary statistic of forgotten deliberation. Continuing isn't honouring a sunk cost, it's trusting an earlier computation whose input I've lost. I think this is the strongest of my three, and it's basically the observation that in real agents, the past decision isn't transparent to the present one.

Part 2 — Self-signaling / internal reputation. This is the one I worry is hand-wavy. The idea: if I abandon projects whenever the going gets tough, my future self knows this, and that knowledge weakens the force of my current commitments. Deciding to continue on the basis of what I've already invested is like a precommitment device — it's me telling my future selves "when I say I'll see something through, I mean it". This only works if the future self can't tell the difference between "quitting because the project is genuinely bad" and "quitting because quitting is cheap", which is sort of the whole problem. I got this from thinking about how people describe Ulysses-style commitments generally, not from anywhere specific.

Part 3 — the one I'll probably cut: something about how "fallacy" talk smuggles in a model of the agent as a single unified utility maximiser, and that once you see the person as a sequence of partially cooperating agents (past self, present self, future self), the notion of a "sunk" cost gets murky — costs sunk for the present self may still be live for the system as a whole. Honestly I suspect this is just part 2 restated with more words.
