At a recent city council hearing on legalizing fourplexes in my city's single-family zones, "housing is a human right" was invoked by both sides: by the YIMBYs, to argue that zoning which forbids apartments on most of the city's land violates that right, and by a tenants'-union speaker, to argue that the right obliges the city to build social housing itself, so that permitting market-rate construction merely hands a right's fulfillment to developers for profit. I left unsure what the slogan actually claims, and I would like the distinct precise theses it could be expressing laid out as a list I can work from. A few candidate readings of my own: a policy aspiration that housing be universal; an enforceable claim-right that the state house anyone who cannot house themselves; a liberty-right that others not prevent you from providing housing for yourself, which condemns exclusionary zoning but is silent on who builds; a thesis that tenure security against eviction, not quantity of units, is the core of the right; and a thesis about the illegitimacy of any property regime that prices people out of shelter. Please say for each reading whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, or false; which reading each side at the hearing was most plausibly relying on; and, crucially, whether the tenants'-union inference — from the right's existence to the conclusion that market-rate construction does nothing to fulfill it — follows from any of the readings or instead requires an independent premise about profit or commodification.
