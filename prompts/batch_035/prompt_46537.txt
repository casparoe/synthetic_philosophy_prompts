The student's answer, as submitted:

---

The morphological difference between the two conditionals is real but superficial: the "would" of the counterfactual is just the past morphology of "will," so the contrast cannot be a contrast of tense. What the contrast tracks is the speaker's epistemic position: an indicative requires the antecedent to be live for the speaker, while a counterfactual is used when the speaker takes the antecedent to be settled false. On the restrictor view, this makes the two conditionals instances of a single construction: the if-clause restricts a modal operator, and the difference between the two lies only in the flavor of the modal — epistemic for the indicative, circumstantial or metaphysical for the counterfactual. So the underlying syntax-semantics mapping is uniform.

For counterfactuals, the standard semantics is the Stalnaker–Lewis possible-worlds account: a counterfactual is true at a world iff the closest world or worlds where the antecedent holds are worlds where the consequent holds, closeness being determined by a similarity ordering. This immediately explains why antecedent strengthening fails: Sobel sequences like "if the match had been struck, it would have lit; if the match had been struck and had been wet, it would not have lit" are perfectly coherent, because the closest antecedent-worlds change when the antecedent is elaborated.

On the indicative side, the central datum is the Equation: the probability of an indicative conditional equals the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent. But as Adams showed in his triviality theorem, no proposition can obey the Equation under the classical laws of probability, so indicative conditionals cannot be truth-conditional at all; they have assertability conditions in place of truth conditions. And the Equation generalizes beyond the indicative: the same reasoning that supports it for indicatives supports it for counterfactuals too, so the probability of a counterfactual like "if Bizet and Verdi had been compatriots, Bizet would have been Italian" is simply the conditional probability that Bizet was Italian given that they were compatriots, i.e. one half.

With respect to inferences, modus ponens is valid for both conditionals. Modus tollens is equally valid for counterfactuals: if the closest world where the match was struck is a world where it lit, and the match in fact did not light, then the match was not struck — for if it had been struck, the closest struck-world would have to be a world where it lit, contradicting the fact that it did not. The invalid pattern, as noted, is antecedent strengthening.

The conclusion is that unification is available and correct: both conditionals are restricted modals differing only in flavor, and the apparent probability asymmetry between them is a pragmatic effect rather than a semantic one.
