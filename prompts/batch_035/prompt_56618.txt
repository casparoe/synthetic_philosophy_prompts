I've been grading my own behavior in a bunch of situations where I either flat-out lied or said something true but engineered, and I have gut verdicts on each one, but no principle behind them. Here's the list:

- Murderer at the door asking where my friend is. I say "he's not here" — he's in the basement. Fine, obviously.
- Same guy, but instead I say "I haven't seen him since Tuesday." Also true — he came by Wednesday and is in the basement. Also fine, but it feels cleaner than the lie, and I suspect it shouldn't.
- Aunt asks if I like the sweater. I say "I love it." I dont. Fine.
- Same aunt, but I go with "it's so colorful," which is true, and she takes it as enthusiasm. Fine too, but it feels slightly cheap — cheaper than the lie, weirdly.
- Interviewer asks why I left my last job. I say "my role was eliminated in the restructuring." The paperwork really does say that — it's just how HR codes being fired — and I know he'll conclude I was laid off. This one feels wrong to me, nearly as wrong as saying "I quit" would.
- Kid asks if the dog ran away. I say "he went to live on a farm upstate." Wrong, but I'd do it. The misleading version — "he's somwhere with lots of room to run" — somehow feels more dishonest than the lie, which I can't explain.

So my verdicts seem to say: sometimes a lie and a misleading truth are morally identical (the murderer cases), sometimes the misleading truth is worse (the interview, the dog), and sometimes it's better (the sweater, maybe?). Is there one principle about lying vs. misleading that captures all of this, or do I have to give some verdict up? Which one, and what does it cost? And what does the principle predict for a case I keep going back and forth on: a dying relative asks "did my son call?", and I say the true "someone from his office called yesterday to confirm arrangements" — his assistant did call, but the son himself hasn't called in months, and I know she'll conclude it was him personally checking in. Is that a kindness or a theft, on your principle?
