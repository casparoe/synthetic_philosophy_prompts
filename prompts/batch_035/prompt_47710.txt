My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether everything of value can be traded off against everything else. She held that any two good things can always be compared and weighed; I kept objecting, but in the cold light of morning my own gut verdicts look less principled than hers. Here is my honest ledger. If a developer offered me well above market price for my grandmother's house — the one she and my grandfather built — I would sell, though with an ache. No sum, however large, would get me to sell her wedding ring. If the local hospital could fund either one identified child's rare and expensive surgery or a screening program that would statistically save more lives, I would fund the child without feeling the pull of the alternative at all. Yet I fully endorse the vaccination program that each year causes a handful of identified severe reactions in order to prevent many more statistical deaths. I would choose the senior meals program over restoring the town's historic theater without hesitation, and yet I do not think someone who chooses the theater is making the kind of mistake made by someone who chose the theater over hospital equipment — and I cannot articulate what makes those two choices feel so different. I would not sell my vote at any price, even one large enough to fund the child's surgery outright, and a bigger offer makes the proposal more insulting, not more tempting. Finally, when two friends' weddings fall on the same day, deciding by coin flip seems acceptable to me, while deciding by ranking how much each friendship is worth feels somehow wrong — even though I could rank either friend against a stranger in a heartbeat.

Is there one principle that captures all of this? The ring versus the house looks like constitutive versus replaceable attachments; the surgery and vaccination cases look like they turn on the identified-versus-statistical distinction, except my verdicts run in opposite directions on that axis, which worries me considerably. I want you to take a definite position: commit to the best single principle (or a principled small set if you genuinely think no single one survives), state plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why it is the verdict rather than the principle that should go, and rule definitively on the case I keep circling: a health board deciding whether to stop covering one identified, named patient's very expensive ongoing treatment in order to fund prevention that will save unnamed others. Do not leave me with a menu of options and the observation that it is difficult.
