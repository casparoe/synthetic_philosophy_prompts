A popular book on collective intelligence that I've been reading explains the Condorcet jury theorem to its readers roughly like this:

"In 1785, the Marquis de Condorcet proved a remarkable theorem about groups. Suppose a committee must choose between two options, exactly one of which is correct, and suppose every member is even slightly more likely than not to pick the correct one — 51 percent rather than 50. If the members decide independently, then as the committee grows, the probability that the majority picks the correct option approaches certainty. With a million voters each right just 51 percent of the time, the majority is wrong less than once in a million such decisions. Condorcet showed that a majority is not merely a way of registering the collective will but an instrument for discovering the truth; this is why large electorates tend to get things right, and why the simplest way to improve a group's decisions is to make the group bigger. The theorem is named for juries, and for good reason: twelve jurors, each only modestly reliable, convict and acquit with near-perfect accuracy. It is the same logic that lets a crowd at a county fair guess the weight of an ox almost exactly. And it generalizes: in an election with many candidates, the Condorcet winner — the candidate who beats each rival in a head-to-head vote — is the one the group, made large enough, will converge on as the right choice. This suggests we should trust referendums over expert panels and, wherever feasible, put questions directly to the whole electorate."

I found this persuasive, but a friend doing a PhD in political theory says the passage "glosses over exactly the parts that are interesting." I don't have the background to know where the book is cutting corners. Would you go through the passage claim by claim and tell me which statements are accurate as mathematics, which hold only under assumptions the book fails to flag, and which conclusions do not survive once those assumptions are made explicit? In particular, I would like to know whether the claims about juries and about the ox-weighing crowd really follow from the theorem, whether the extension to elections with many candidates is as straightforward as the book suggests, and whether the final recommendation about referendums follows from anything Condorcet actually proved. Please also tell me whether my friend's skepticism is warranted, or whether she is just objecting to a harmless simplification.
