I'm a retired fluid dynamicist -- about thirty years working on ocean circulation and boundary layers, then a stretch doing climate modelling -- who got seriously into philosophy of science after retiring. What pulled me in was Friedman's old essay from the 50s about methodology in economics, the whole line that unrealistic assumptions are fine so long as the predictions come out right. I gather people call this the F-twist. My first reaction as a physicist was that it was obviously nuts: if your derivation of ocean heat transport assumed the ocean was a bathtub, nobody would care that it fit three data points. But the more I read the responses to Friedman, the more I suspect the debate never got settled, it just acquired a more sophisticated vocabulary.

So here's my situation. I have maybe ten good working years left, a decent pension, a physics background, no formal training in economics or philosophy beyond reading, and I want to spend a chunk of those years writing seriously about idealization in science instead of just being an opinionated old man at dinner parties. Which open questions in this area are actually worth those years, and in what order? I'm less interested in a syllabus than in the reasoning: for each question, why it's more worth working on than the alternatives, whether a physicist-turned-amateur-philosopher is even the right person for it or whether it needs somebody trained differently, and what would make you bump it down the list.

To give you a sense of the candidates sitting half-formed in my notes:

- The prediction/explanation gap. Friedman's test seems to be predictive performance. But half of what we did with idealized models in fluid dynamics was explanation -- showing, say, that some turbulence effect came from the walls and not the bulk, even when the idealized version couldn't predict anything quantitatively. So "when can a knowingly false model explain a real phenomenon" -- is that a live open question, or did someone settle it while I was debugging code? I also gather there's a distinction people draw between assumptions that treat something as negligible and assumptions you know are wrong but hope don't matter much. That seems relevant to whether the F-twist is even coherent, but I can't remember who pushed it -- some paper from the 80s, I think.

- Model pluralism. For the ocean we never had one model, we had a toolbox: different idealizations for different questions, sometimes giving conflicting answers to the same question. Nobody in the lab found this scandalous. Is the philosophical question of how mutually inconsistent models can all be accurate enough (or however you want to put it) actually going anywhere, or is it philosophers slowly rediscovering something practicioners shrug at?

- De-idealization and when it's a mistake. The folklore among modellers is that adding "realism" -- finer resolution, more processes, more parameters -- sometimes makes predictions worse. Overfitting, parameterizations tuned to the coarse model, whatever. Is there a principled account of when a more realistic model is a worse model? Because that seems to cut against the naive reading of the anti-Friedman side, and it would be embarrassing to spend a decade writing against a position that's right in the cases I actually know.

- Whether any of this generalizes. Does one theory of idealization cover the frictionless pendulum, the representative agent in macro, the Ising model, and parametrized clouds in a climate model? Or is "idealization" a grab-bag word covering unrelated sins, and the real open question is cutting it into kinds? And if that's the open question, how would you even know you'd made progress on it?

Rank these, or replace them with better ones I haven't found, but defend the ordering -- I want to know what makes a question worth a decade, not just that it's unresolved. And be honest with me about the last part: would three careful case studies of idealization inside actual fluid dynamics practice be worth more to this literature than a conceptual treatise would be? If the answer is yes, tell me early, before I waste the good years.
