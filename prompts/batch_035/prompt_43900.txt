I've been reading about the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation, and I want to check that the practical lesson I'm drawing from it is the right one, because at the moment it strikes me as fairly dramatic.

As I understand the phenomenon: when a body votes proposition by proposition, a majority can accept each premise while a different majority rejects the conclusion that those premises jointly entail, so the group's official position comes out formally inconsistent even though every individual member is perfectly coherent. My first thought was that this is just Condorcet cycling transplanted from candidates to propositions — both seem to be cases where majority rule takes consistent individual inputs and produces an incoherent collective output. If that's right, the fix seems obvious: groups should stop voting on premises entirely and take a single vote only on the final conclusion. A direct vote on the conclusion can never leave the group holding an inconsistent set of judgments, so the inconsistency disappears by construction.

Is this the solution the field has settled on? If so, I'm puzzled about why courts and committees still bother recording votes on the individual issues at all — wouldn't recording them only invite the inconsistency we're trying to avoid? And if it isn't the settled solution, I'd like to know what I'm missing: is my identification of the discursive dilemma with majority cycling itself too quick, and is there some cost to conclusion-only voting that I haven't considered?
