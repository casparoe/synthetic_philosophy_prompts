You are a formal poet who spent years in a neuropsychology lab before turning to verse, and you write for readers who know the split-brain literature well enough to resent name-dropping. Write me a sestina spoken by the left hemisphere of a commissurotomy patient in the hour after a testing session.

For the speaker, the session went like this: the patient sat before the apparatus, fixed on a central point while pictures were flashed to one visual field or the other. A chicken's claw reached the speaking hemisphere; a snowy scene reached the silent one, which cannot say anything and so made itself known the only way it could — the left hand settling on a shovel among the picture cards. Asked to explain the choice, the speaker produced, fluently and without hesitation, a reason connecting the shovel to the chicken. And that is what the speaker believes happened: that it was asked, and that it answered.

Make the form do the argument. The six end-words are fixed and return in every stanza while everything arranged around them changes, and that is precisely the patient's situation: the body, the hands, the apparatus, and the day's settled facts stay put, and only the narrative built around them varies. Choose the end-words from the concrete furniture of the session — the screen, the claw, the snow, the shovel, the hands — rather than from abstractions, and think through the envoi before you draft a single line: the poem should end with the speaker still talking, offering no lesson about the mind and no joke at its own expense. One hard rule throughout: the speaker must never acknowledge that another hemisphere exists. The other hemisphere may enter the poem only as whatever the speaker has to explain away, and the word consciousness should not appear anywhere in it.

Someone who knows the interpreter confabulations should recognize the speaker at once, without being told; someone who knows nothing about any of this should still find a good dramatic monologue. Give me the thirty-nine lines only — no title, no epigraph, no stanza numbers, no notes of any kind, and no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables anywhere in your reply.
