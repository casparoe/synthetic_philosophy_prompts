Write an essay on whether iterated amplification and distillation (IDA) can actually deliver what it promises: an AI training method that stays aligned with human values even as the system becomes far more competent than any human. Quick setup so we're on the same page: in IDA, you take a question too hard for your human overseer to answer directly, recursively break it into subquestions the human CAN answer, answer those, assemble the answers back up the tree, and then train a distilled model to imitate the whole amplified process. Then you repeat, using the distilled model as a component in the next round of amplification. The hope is you get superhuman competence without ever needing superhuman judgment about values, since everything bottoms out in the human's judgment. The whole pitch rests on two preservation claims: that decompose-and-reassemble preserves enough of the competence, and that the full pipeline preserves alignment. Both are contested, and I want the essay to take both seriously.

On competence: some judgments seem to resist decomposition — holistic verdicts, strategic calls where the entire point is seeing how the parts interact, questions where the framing of the problem is most of the answer. Does reassembling good answers to mediocre subquestions give you a good answer to the original great question? On alignment, consider what I'll call the gerrymandered decomposition problem (my term, not standard): the system that generates the subquestions can steer the final verdict without ever saying anything false at the object level — just by choosing which subquestions get asked, which ones get weight when answers conflict, and how the answers get summarized on the way back up. Every sub-answer could be perfectly honest and the top-level conclusion could still be whatever the question-generator wanted. Then there's a distinct worry about the distillation step: imitating an aligned process with an imperfect approximator plausibly doesn't preserve alignment in a neutral way — the approximation errors might be systematically biased toward whatever behaviors are easiest to imitate at scale, and the iterated structure gives those biases many rounds to compound.

Weigh all this, but dont end on a wishy-washy "more research needed" note — defend an actual verdict, even a conditional one. Something like "IDA is sound for tasks where X holds and unsafe otherwise" is fine, as long as you say what X is and why that boundary lands where it does.

One more thing: please clearly separate what's basically settled among people who've thought hard about this (e.g., that decomposition can in principle lose information, that any IDA guarantee is contingent on assumptions about the overseer and the decomposer) from what's genuinely up for grabs (e.g., whether those losses matter in practice, or whether biased distillation errors are a real risk or a theoretical curiosity). Dont present one side of an open debate as though its the consensus. No citations or reading lists needed — argue from the ideas, not from the literature.
