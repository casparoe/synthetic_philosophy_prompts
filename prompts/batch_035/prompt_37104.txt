My friend and I got into a two-hour argument last night about judging the past, and she kept ending every round the same way: quoting L.P. Hartley's line from The Go-Between (1953) — "The past is a foreign country: they do things differently there." The occasion was a book club row over a biography that flatly calls a 19th-century mill owner cruel for working children 12-hour days, and also for the way he wrote about his wife, which reads pretty controlling to us. My friend's line was: different country, different standards, so the biographer is basically a tourist calling the locals barbarians — both the cruelty charge and the sexism charge are "presentism" and the book should have just described the guy. I pushed back that description itself smuggles in standards, but honestly I got muddled about whether that's true or whether I'm just refusing to accept where her slogan leads.

Can you sort out what that one sentence could actually be claiming? I want the full menu laid out — I suspect it runs from something trivially true (the past differs from the present in lots of ways) through something descriptive-methodological (understand actors in their own terms before judging), to something pretty radical (past norms are so incommensurable with ours that no cross-temporal evaluation is possible, maybe even our evaluating vocabulary — cruel, controlling — misfires on the past the way "bachelor" misfires on the Pope). And don't leave me with a shrug at the end: tell me which reading my friend was actually leaning on in each of the two cases (the child labor charge vs. the way he treated his wife, which I think aren't the same kind of case), and give a definite verdict on whether that reading holds up. If you think one of the readings is just false, say so plainly. I want to walk into our next argument with a position, not another question.
