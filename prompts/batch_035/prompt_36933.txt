Here's the twist I want built in, a variant I made up so they'll actually have to reason. Call it the escrow deal. Standard case first, so we're on the same page: you're stranded in the desert, a passing driver is a very reliable predictor who only stops for people she predicts will pay her $1000 once delivered to the city, and the standard CDT verdict is that at the city gates not paying dominates. Now the variant: this driver offers stranded motorists a choice of two deals. The plain deal is just the promise to pay $1000 in the city. The escrow deal is that, at the moment she picks you up, you hand her a $1500 deposit, which she refunds the moment you pay her $1000 in the city — so if you pay, the ride nets out to $1000, and if you don't, she keeps the deposit. Notice that with the escrow deal in place, paying at the city gates is actually the locally dominant move, which is exactly what I want them to chew on. The exam question should make them (a) explain why a consistent CDT agent takes the escrow deal and then pays, while the very same agent, offered only the plain deal, dies in the desert; (b) say whether this rescues CDT — rational agents just buy commitment tech when they need it — or exposes something wrong with it, given that the paradigm case is precisely one where commitment tech isn't available and the agent can foresee its own future refusal; and (c) evaluate whether the standard fixes (resolute choice, constrained maximization, updatelessness) can do internally what the deposit does externally, or whether "choosing now what you'll choose later" collapses back into either CDT or plain irrationality at the later node. They're welcome to bring in the toxin puzzle as a contrast if it helps them, but again, the question itself shouldn't require remembering any of this.
