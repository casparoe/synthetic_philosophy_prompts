I hold the whole-brain criterion of death: a human being has died when and only when all functions of the entire brain, brainstem included, have irreversibly ceased, and I regard the circulatory criterion as a mere stopgap for contexts where neurological testing is unavailable. Tell me what would count against my view. I am aware of the standard pressure points, and I want you to assess how damaging each really is: patients who satisfy the criterion but retain hypothalamic activity, and so continue to grow, regulate temperature, and in rare cases even gestate a fetus, which seems to contradict the claim that the brain is the integrator of the organism; controlled donation after circulatory death, where organs are procured after only a few minutes without a pulse, on the reasoning that circulation would never resume on its own — an apparent slide from irreversible to permanent that, if legitimate, looks like it hands my opponent the victory; and the widely reported case of a young girl declared brain-dead whose family sustained her bodily functions for years and who was later found to respond to commands. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: which of them would a serious defender of the whole-brain criterion concede as genuine evidence against it, which would be dismissed as diagnostic error or as confusing the criterion with its application, and at what point does that distinction stop being honest? I also want your judgment on whether the view, once qualified in the usual ways — whole brain versus higher brain, functions versus capacity for function, irreversible versus permanent — has been insulated against anything short of a patient who meets the criterion and then wakes up, and whether that insulation is a defect in a view of this kind or just what maturity looks like for a medical-ethical criterion.
