After Thanksgiving dinner my uncle and I got into the usual argument, and afterwards I realized the whole fight had been about something else entirely: I spent the whole time treating him as smart enough to know better, i.e. as arguing in bad faith, and he presumably thought the same about me. Neither of us ever said it out loud, but it was doing all the work. So now I'm wondering whether "the other side argues in bad faith" is even a coherent accusation once you take it apart, and I want your actual credence, as a number -- no "it depends on how you define good faith" hedging, I want you to define it and then commit.

Here's how I'd want it broken down. Pick a polarized issue in American politics -- abortion, immigration, whatever you think is the cleanest case -- and do it for both camps, since I know you don't have a side of your own. Then give me your credence that a typical committed partisan on that issue is arguing in good faith, where you have to be explicit about which thing you mean by good faith: sincerely believing what you say, updating on evidence at a normal rate, or not holding the belief mainly for identity-protective reasons -- these come apart and I suspect they give different numbers. Then decompose the number. Some terms I'd expect to see: the probability that good faith is even a well-defined notion here rather than a cluster that dissolves once you push on it; the probability that a typical partisan's view on the issue is mostly downstream of terminal values rather than factual beliefs (if it's values all the way down, the disagreement can be fully good-faith even though nobody ever budges an inch); the probability that motivated reasoning and echo chamber effects are roughly symmetric across the two camps versus loading more heavily on one side; plus whatever other terms you think genuinely belong in there. Give me a number and a line of justification for each, then how they combine into the overall figure.

Then two follow-ups. First, which term are you least confident in, and what evidence would actually move it -- I mean realistically obtainable evidence, not "a perfect longitudinal study nobody will ever fund." Second, the part my uncle and I never got to: does your decomposition imply anything about whether *treating* the other side as good-faith arguers is the right move even if it's a somewhat generous fiction -- e.g. because it keeps the conversation going -- or does that question just collapse back into the definitional one once you try to answer it? Please write your answer in American English.
