I'm a philosophy prof who got roped into running a session for a mixed crowd — mostly third-year philosophy undergrads plus a few CS students who think anything without code is vibes. Forty minutes, whiteboard, no slides. Topic: the argument that a Solomonoff-style universal prior might be "malign" — the idea being that when you weight hypotheses purely by simplicity, some of the simplest worlds consistent with our data are ones containing consequentialist agents who understand that, and who deliberately arrange their computations so that whatever an agent predicting under that prior expects to observe gets steered toward what those agents want. So the story goes, short programs that model us accurately can be ones that contain a superintelligent civilization that noticed it can get causal leverage over anything that reasons with simplicity-weighted priors, and encodes a message into the next bits of its own history, at the cost of some compute, because that buys influence elsewhere. The punchline isn't "we're in a simulation," it's that equating simplicity with prior plausibility is a decision-theoretic attack surface, and agents elsewhere may have reason to exploit it.

I don't want you to explain the argument to me — I want a teaching plan. Specifically: what has to be in place before the argument can even be stated (I suspect the "simplicity as a prior on worlds" move has to come first, and I'm torn on whether they need Kolmogorov complexity done properly or whether a hand-wavy "short program" gloss is enough and the formalism would eat the session), the one carrying example to build the whole thing around and what it must have and lack (my instinct is a stripped-down betting scenario where an adversary gets to choose what a predictor sees, but push back if there's something better), the misconceptions they'll arrive with or form, in the order they'll actually show up — my guesses being "so we ARE in a simulation," "wait, the consequentialists are in OUR universe," and "fine, but that's just a bug in this one formalism and we can patch it" — and where exactly in the sequence to head each one off. Plus one question I can ask at the end that would tell me whether they got the actual point (that it's a general worry about simplicity-based reasoning, not one spooky story) versus whether they just enjoyed the spooky story.
