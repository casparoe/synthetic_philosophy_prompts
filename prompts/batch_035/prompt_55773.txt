I'm a sociology graduate student presenting to my department's economic history workshop a moderate version of Weber's Protestant ethic thesis: that in nineteenth-century Europe, regions with ascetic Protestant majorities showed persistent economic advantages, and that while much of this ran through literacy and schooling, a residual share is best explained by the values themselves — the treatment of disciplined work, thrift, and reinvestment as religious duties rather than as mere instruments. The workshop is dominated by cliometricians who regard cultural explanations as unidentified until they've been reduced to institutions or human capital, plus two historical sociologists who quote Weber fondly but will not go out on a limb for me. Prepare my Q&A: list the objections the economic historians will actually raise, phrased in their vocabulary (identification, mechanism, falsifiability, "what's in your error term"), ordered by how much damage each would do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Flag in advance what I should concede about the literacy channel, and what I must hold to keep the thesis alive at all. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — the one about whether my residual "values" term is anything more than a name for whatever my regressions fail to model.
