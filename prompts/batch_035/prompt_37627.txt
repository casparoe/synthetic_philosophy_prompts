My roommate is doing a math econ PhD and she's had me stuck for a week on a version of the two-envelope thing, and honestly I cant tell anymore whether it's a real paradox or a party trick. The setup: someone puts money in two envelopes, one holding twice the other, I pick one at random, and I can keep it or swap for the other. Her argument goes like this. (1) Call the amount in my envelope X. (2) Whatever X turns out to be, the other envelope contains 2X or X/2, and since the envelopes were filled symmetrically, each of those is probability one half. (3) So conditional on X = x, swapping has expected value (1/2)(2x) + (1/2)(x/2) = 5x/4, which beats x. (4) If one random variable beats another conditional on every possible value, it beats it outright. (5) So even with my envelope still sealed, I should swap. (6) But whoever holds the other envelope can run the identical argument for hers, so we should each be dying to swap, and after we swap we can run it again, forever — and worse, (5) seems to say I should be willing to pay a fee of up to a quarter of the expected amount just for the privilege of swapping, which we could then keep paying over and over, bleeding out to whoever runs the envelopes. (7) Her conclusion is that expected-value reasoning just breaks whenever you don't know the process that generated the amounts, and that for that kind of unmodeled uncertainty you need a different decision theory altogether.

I tried the standard pushback — look, if the amounts are $10 and $20 and I open mine and see $20, obviously I stay — and she says that's exactly her point: in that case I know the distribution, and her argument is about what happens when I *don't*. Which I grant is most real situations. The step I'd most like to keep is (3), honestly, since conditional expectation is just bookkeeping, and I also like (2) because "the setup is symmetric between the envelopes" seems undeniable — I genuinely feel like, whatever number I'd find in there, the other one is equally likely to be double or half. But clearly not every step from (2) to (7) can be fine, since (6) is insane and (7) would have us abandoning expected value over a sealed envelope.

To make it worse, she has a follow-up she claims is airtight: she says she can describe a perfectly ordinary, explicitly specified way of choosing the two amounts — a real probability distribution, no tricks, finite amounts — such that after you open your envelope, whatever number you actually see, swapping has positive expected value, and in *that* version she insists the advice to swap is simply correct and our horror at the infinite-swap conclusion is the thing that needs explaining. She might be bluffing about being able to construct it; I wouldn't know how to check. So: which step do I give up, why did it look so fine, and what does each alternative way out have to pay? Please actually commit to one answer rather than walking me through all the exits — if you think the guilty step is different in the sealed-envelope case and the open-envelope case, say so and tell me why that's not just changing the subject, and tell me whether her construction in the last paragraph is possible or whether that's where the bluff is.
