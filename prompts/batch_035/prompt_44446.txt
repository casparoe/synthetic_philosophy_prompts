I co-run a reading group in formal epistemology for graduate students, and this term we have been working through expected-utility theory alongside Lara Buchak's Risk and Rationality (2013) and some of the surrounding literature on risk. For our final session I want a quiz tentatively titled "Which decision theory do you actually hold?", to be handed out before we discuss the papers, so that people discover their positions rather than defending ones they already know they are supposed to have.

The quiz should contain eight to ten questions. Each question must be a concrete choice between two or three fully specified gambles — exact dollar amounts and exact probabilities, since the audience is fluent in probability — and each option must be a choice, never a calculation or a statement of a principle. Hard constraint: no question may name a position, a theorist, or an axiom. No question may ask whether the respondent "accepts" anything, and no question may be a disguised version of such a question. The instrument should sort people by what they choose, not by what they can recite.

The quiz must include the following cases, in whatever form you judge best:

1. Both stages of an Allais-style common-consequence pair, presented as two separate, non-adjacent questions so the respondent does not see them side by side.
2. A Rabin-style calibration sequence: a small-stakes bet the respondent is asked whether they would refuse at each of several stated wealth levels, followed by a very favorable large-stakes bet that an expected-utility maximizer with the utility curvature implied by those refusals would also have to refuse.
3. An Ellsberg-style case with unknown probabilities. This one is deliberately a trap: the scoring key must explain that the answers do not sort risk-weighted expected utility from expected utility at all, and must say what, if anything, they do sort.
4. A case where one gamble first-order stochastically dominates another, to catch patterns unrepresentable by any of the target positions.
5. At least one case on which a risk-weighted expected-utility maximizer with a cautious risk function and an expected-utility maximizer with a sufficiently concave utility function give the same answer — the point being that the key must indicate which questions do no separating work.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto these leaves: strict expected-utility maximizer (risk attitudes only via the utility function); risk-weighted expected-utility maximizer with a globally cautious risk function; risk-weighted maximizer with a globally daring risk function; risk-weighted maximizer with a mixed or locally varying risk function; and a category for patterns no one of these can represent. For each leaf, give one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where a pattern is unoccupied in practice, say whether you think that is because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.

Finally, be honest about the limits of the instrument. If the answer patterns cannot distinguish a cautious risk-weighted maximizer from, say, someone fitting a rank-dependent weighting function or a prospect-theoretic value function to the same choices, the key must say so plainly and label the leaf "cautious non-EU, mechanism underdetermined" rather than pretending to a diagnosis the choices cannot support. I would rather the quiz visibly fail to separate two positions than separate them by smuggling in a question that only works because someone has read the literature.
