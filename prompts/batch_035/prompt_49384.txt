You're a professional philosopher who works on cultural property ethics and also has hands-on museum experience, so please think carefully about this before writing -- maybe start with a quick brainstorm of the principles involved, then build the procedure. Here's my situation: I'm the registrar at a mid-sized university museum, and we hold something like 4,000 objects acquired between roughly 1890 and 1940 through channels that range from documented field excavations with permits to, honestly, a lot of stuff that arrived via missionary societies, colonial administrators' estates, and auction purchases whose earlier history is a total blank. We now get four or five restitution or repatriation requests a year, they're getting more frequent, and right now we handle them ad hoc: whichever curator knows the region drafts a memo, the director has a feeling, and the board rubber-stamps whatever protects us legally. That obviously can't continue.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my team can actually run on a claim: an ordered set of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers stop the procedure with a clear no, and -- this part matters -- the places where the procedure should halt and hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, with some guidance on how that person should frame the value question instead of just "use judgment." Ground it in the actual philosophy: the tension between cultural internationalism and cultural nationalism, the gap between legal title and moral claim, the universal-museum argument and its critics, and whether restitution demands are best understood as property claims or as something more like apology and repair. My own working instinct is that the crucial line is between objects taken in some discrete violent event (a punitive expedition, a battlefield) and objects that trickled out through markets that were deeply distorted by colonial power but where no one could point to a specific theft -- but I can't tell whether that distinction does real moral work or just tracks whatever happens to be easy to document, so stress-test it rather than adopting it.

Constraints: two weeks per claim at most, run by me (art history MA, no legal training) plus one curator, legal counsel only for actual liability questions, and the board has to sign off on anything we return because of deaccessioning rules. Then run the procedure on three cases: a set of brass plaques looted in a punitive expedition and bought at auction by our founder in 1912; a marble frieze fragment purchased in good faith from a dealer in 1926 in what was then a colonial possession, whose national museum now wants it back; and ancestor remains donated by a collector's family in the 1950s that a descendant community wants returned for reburial but which our anthropology wing considers research-essential. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire, and flag any case-type where I suspect it hands the board a respectable excuse to say no to something that really should go back -- I'd rather see that failure mode now than discover it in practice.
