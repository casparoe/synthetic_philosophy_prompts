I'm a high school junior and one of the two student representatives on my school's Dining and Sustainability Committee, which is made up of administrators, the head chef, two teachers, and us students. At our next meeting we vote on two proposals, and I've been asked to circulate a written recommendation beforehand. Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in collective action and ethics, and draft that recommendation memo for me. Before writing it, think through the problem carefully — maybe sketch the possible positions and the strongest version of each before committing to one — because the committee is tired of vague statements of values and wants a document it can actually act on.

The two proposals:

1. Two meatless days per week in the cafeteria (the vegetarian line becomes the only hot line on those days; a cold meat sandwich remains available).
2. A default swap with no restrictions: the vegetarian entrée becomes the standard hot line, and students who want the meat entrée have to ask for it at a separate station. Meat stays fully available every day.

The obstacle is our senior representative, who plans to vote both down on what he calls the "rounding error" argument, and honestly his version is better than the usual one. He concedes that if everyone changed it would matter. His claim is narrower: that neither proposal, as decided by us and complied with by students, makes any difference. The school's total emissions are a rounding error on the global problem, so even full compliance changes nothing that anyone will ever detect. And more specifically — this is the part I can't answer — he got our chef to confirm that meat is ordered from the supplier in fixed case sizes, roughly enough for two weeks of meals, and the kitchen adjusts orders based on average consumption, not on any individual lunch. So, he says, no single student's choice of the vegetarian line brings a single animal into or out of existence or prevents any measurable amount of emissions; each lunch is like one drop in an already-flowing river, and the memo shouldn't pretend otherwise. He also points out that the same reasoning shows his vote in national elections is pointless, and that he accepts that conclusion, so he's at least consistent.

The memo should give a clear bottom-line recommendation on each proposal — approve, reject, or approve with conditions (and if conditions, name them concretely, e.g., a pilot term and a review criterion) — with reasoning that engages his argument on its merits rather than dismissing it as cynicism. Two things I'd especially like handled honestly: whether the fact that a harm is dispersed across millions of contributors really means each contribution does nothing, or means each does a tiny or threshold-dependent amount, and what follows if the honest answer is that some individual acts really are inefficacious but the collective policy still matters — because that would treat the two proposals differently (the policy is collective by construction; the compliance is individual). Also address the difference between the proposals: the second one works (if it works) entirely through shifting defaults for individuals, which is exactly the level his argument targets, while the first is a collective rule.

End the memo with a short section stating what would change the recommendation — which empirical assumption about how the kitchen and supplier adjust orders, or which step in the moral argument about dispersed contributions, would have to fail for the committee to reach the opposite verdict — so the other members can see where the reasoning is most likely to go wrong rather than just receiving a conclusion. Keep the whole thing under about 700 words, at a level the teachers and administrators on the committee will take seriously.
