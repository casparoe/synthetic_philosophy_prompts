Two members of my philosophy reading group got into a protracted disagreement about Molyneux's question — whether someone blind from birth who is suddenly granted sight could, by sight alone and before touching anything, tell a cube from a sphere. Ravi maintained that the question has effectively been settled empirically: surgeons have occasionally restored sight to congenitally blind patients, and such patients reportedly could not at first distinguish shapes by sight alone, which answers 'no'. Jasmin replied that this misses the point entirely, because the question concerns an ideally functioning visual system, whereas the patients' eyes and brains developed without vision, so their initial failures show nothing about what sight, properly developed, could do with concepts acquired by touch. Ravi said that reading renders the question unfalsifiable — if every empirical outcome can be explained away by appeal to the patient's abnormal history, nothing would ever count as evidence, and a question no evidence could bear on is not a substantive question. Jasmin countered that some questions are conceptual rather than empirical, and Molyneux's is one of them: it asks whether the tactile concept of a cube and the visual presentation of a cube share a common structure that reason can recognise without tuition, which is settled (if at all) by reflection. Ravi's final move was that this construal quietly substitutes 'could the person work it out' for 'does sight alone suffice', which is not what Molyneux asked, and that Jasmin's conceptual question is answerable only by pretending we can survey the structures of experience from the armchair — something we patently cannot do, as the disagreement itself demonstrates. They finally asked me to referee, and I could not. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: state who is right at each step, whether one of them is more right overall, and what, if anything, both are missing. Please answer in British English.
