Two researchers have spent two years arguing past each other about whether language models have beliefs, and have finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration. One — call her the attribution realist — holds that when intentional-state vocabulary delivers systematically successful prediction and explanation of a language model's behavior, that success is defeasible evidence of belief-like states in the model: real patterns with somewhat determinate contents, discoverable rather than stipulated. The other — the deflationist — holds that the success of the vocabulary reflects only facts about the interpretability of the model's outputs plus the projection and pattern-completion skills of human stance-takers, so that attributions are useful bookkeeping whose correctness is a matter of convention, not discovery. Draft the joint statement they should produce. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both can sign — including about the predictive and practical value of intentional vocabulary for these systems, about what mechanistic interpretability does and does not show, about the fact that attribution is licensed by observed behavior rather than by knowledge of the substrate, and about the difference between attributing belief-like states to a language model and attributing them to a thermostat or a chess engine; (2) a table of their remaining disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording — I expect the main fault lines to be whether predictive indispensability at some grain of description constitutes evidence of genuine states, whether attributions can be true or only apt, whether determinacy of content can be undermined by the interpretive latitude that stance-takers enjoy, and whether it matters for the question that the model acquired its dispositions by ingesting human-produced text rather than by acting in the world; (3) the cruxes — the subquestions such that settling them would move at least one side — with, for each, a concrete test, intervention, or argument both agree would bear on it (for instance: whether independent competent stance-takers converge on the same attributions when given the same behavioral evidence and incentive structure; whether intentional-level predictions survive perturbations — paraphrase, temperature changes, targeted fine-tuning — that the mechanistic story says should disrupt them if the attributions were merely bookkeeping; whether mechanistic accounts eventually displace intentional ones at the grain where the two currently agree); and (4) each side's prediction about each test, recorded in advance. Finally, explicitly flag any part of the residual disagreement that you judge to be verbal — e.g., a dispute about the word "belief" rather than about the model — and say which part, and why. Keep the scope fixed to present-day language models rather than hypothetical successors.
