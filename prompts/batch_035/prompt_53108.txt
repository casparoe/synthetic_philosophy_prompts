I'm on the five-person release review board at a mid-size AI lab, and I need a decision procedure for whether things get made public, because our current rule is a mess. Anything an employee wants to release — weights, datasets, blog posts with capability details, demos — lands with us if it's flagged, and the rule right now is: it goes out after 72 hours unless somebody objects, and one objection blocks. So one optimist can push something out over four skeptics if nobody's watching their inbox, and one pessimist can sit indefinitely on something four people think is fine. I don't have a better rule, I just know this one is doing both bad things at once.

The thing I can't stop thinking about is the unilateralist's curse, which someone once explained to me as: if n people each have the power to do a risky thing, and each person's estimate of whether it's good is noisy around the truth, then "do it if anyone wants to" effectively hands the decision to whoever got the most favorable error in their estimate. On average the group's action tracks its most optimistic member, not the group's average opinion. That part seems like it's just math, given the assumptions. What I'm much less sure about is the fixes people propose — norms of conforming to the majority, giving dissenters a veto, prediction markets on outcomes, quorums — and I suspect that's where it gets genuinely contested, so don't just pick one and call it settled.

What I want is a procedure the board could actually adopt and run in, say, a 90-minute weekly meeting: the questions to ask about a release in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it stops with a yes, and — this is the part I care most about — where it has to admit the question is a values call and hand it back to a human rather than pretending the procedure answered it. Be honest about failure modes too, because the visibility asymmetry keeps me up at night: if we block something and we were wrong, nobody ever sees the good thing we prevented, but if we release and we're wrong, everyone sees the harm. That asymetric error visibility probably explains half our board dynamics, and I want the procedure to actually account for it instead of treating the two kinds of mistakes as symetric.

Then run the procedure on three real-ish cases we've had: (1) a scraped medical imaging dataset where the "anonymized" patient notes still mention ages and hospital names; (2) a chemistry assistant demo that will give plausible synthesis routes for controlled substances if you jailbreak it, which we know because we jailbroke it; (3) a technical report that describes, in passing, a jailbreak that also works on several competitor models — patched for us, not for them.

One request on how you write it up: keep separate what's actually settled (the pick-the-most-optimistic-estimate logic, if I've even got that right) from what people who know this area genuinely disagree about — like whether veto rules are the right fix at all, or whether the whole framework assumes away the fact that board members talk to each other and to the authors before deciding, which changes the noise story entirely. Don't hand me a contested fix dressed up as the consensus answer, and definately flag the places where you're choosing between fixes on judgment rather than on grounds.
