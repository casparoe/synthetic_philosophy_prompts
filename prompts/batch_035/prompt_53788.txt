I'm a software engineer -- fifteen years in backend and infrastructure work, no philosophy background beyond a bit of pop reading -- and I've noticed an argumentative move I keep bumping into that I can't find a name for. It comes up whenever someone shows a system doing something competent that nobody, inside or outside the system, understands the way we ordinarily assume understanding works: an LLM passing a professional exam, a protein-folding model, a chess engine's move that grandmasters spent decades deriving the point of after the fact. The skeptic in these conversations is usually willing to grant that the competence is real. But they insist it can't have originated where it's observed. When you ask where it did originate, they always locate it further up some causal chain: the competence is really in the training corpus, which humans wrote; or in the engineers who set up the learning process; or in the labelers; or diffused across civilization at large. The system is at best a conduit. The striking thing, to me, is that the direction of the search never varies: competence observed without a local comprehender is treated as impossible as a violation of conservation of energy, and the fix is always to trace it back to some mind upstream. I've started calling this, privately, the conservation of comprehension -- the assumption that understanding can be transmitted but never created, so every instance of competence must have a chain of custody terminating in somebody who genuinely gets it.

What convinces me this is a recognizable pattern rather than my imagination is that I've seen the same move applied outside of machine learning. In arguments about evolution, opponents of design-free accounts will concede that the eye is exquisitely competent and then insist the competence must be on loan -- from a designer, from the environment "as designer," from information injected at the origin of life. And there's an older, mirror-image version inside cognitive science that people do have names for: explaining understanding by hiding a little understander inside the head, which just pushes the problem down a level. That one is at least an attempt to locate comprehension inside the system. The move I'm interested in is the opposite direction -- comprehension is stipulated not to be in the system, and then found by walking backwards through the causal history until a human shows up. Yet there's also a strange selectivity to it. Nobody runs this argument about the legacy codebase at my company, which no single person has understood end to end since about 2013 and which nonetheless reliably bills customers; nobody demands a chain of custody for the competence of a market setting prices, or of a coral reef maintaining a stable ecosystem. The move seems to switch on only when the artifact's competence is of a kind we associate with minds.

So, my actual questions. Is there an established term or literature for the specific inference I'm describing -- the move of granting competence while refusing to grant that it can originate, and discharging the debt by finding a comprehender upstream? If there is, I'd like to know how the canonical statement frames it and what it says about the cases where the move misfires. If not, I'd like help naming and defining it precisely enough to distinguish it from its neighbors: from the little-man-inside-the-head fallacy, which explains comprehension by positing an inner comprehender rather than denying one; from the routine where people redefine a task as not requiring intelligence once a machine does it; and from ordinary, correct claims about provenance (it's obviously sometimes true that a system is a conduit for human competence -- a library book is). What are the boundary cases, and what would make the inference legitimate rather than reflexive? I'd rather not have a label than have a label that swallows everything; I want to know where the edges of this thing are.
