I've followed the decision-theory discussions on rationalist forums for years — the ones about Schelling points, updatelessness, and the pathology of agents that threaten and counter-threaten their way into mutual destruction — and I've generally accepted the received view that in bargaining situations, the ability to bind oneself early is a strategic asset. A friend from those circles has now turned that received view into an argument I can't bring myself to accept, though I also can't locate the flaw. Since you can't consult the threads we're drawing on, I'll reconstruct the reasoning from scratch.

His argument, as best I can lay it out:

(1) In a strategic interaction, an agent capable of making a binding, verifiable commitment does strictly better than an otherwise identical agent incapable of commitment: binding yourself to walk away rather than accept a bad deal changes the other side's incentives, since a threat that is genuinely irrevocable cannot be called as a bluff.

(2) A commitment gets its force from being made before the other party's corresponding decision and from being immune to revision once made. A "commitment" I can quietly reconsider when the stakes get high is strategically just a preference statement, and the other side will treat it as one.

(3) If commitments confer advantage, then earlier is better. An agent that commits first presents the other with an accomplished fact; an agent that commits second must either submit to the first agent's terms or trigger the mutually disastrous outcome the commitment was meant to avoid. So there is pressure to commit as early as possible.

(4) Determining which commitment to make requires deliberation, and every unit of deliberation delays the commitment.

(5) Therefore sophisticated agents face a race: whoever binds themselves first captures the advantage, and each agent has an incentive to cut deliberation shorter than its rival, with the limit being a commitment made with essentially no deliberation at all.

(6) A commitment made with no deliberation is simply fixed behavior. An agent that has bound all of its future conduct to policies fixed in advance has no remaining room in which deciding well could make a difference.

(7) So among sufficiently sophisticated agents, rationality confers no advantage over being a simple fixed program; the strategic ideal is indistinguishable from a rock with a threat painted on it.

(8) That conclusion is hard to swallow, and not only aesthetically: real strategic actors — litigants, states, poker players — do deliberate and then commit, and they seem to do better than either the perpetually deliberating or the truly rigid.

I want to say the failure is somewhere between (5) and (7), but I can't make the charge stick, and I notice I'm choosing that location mainly because it lets me keep (1) through (3), which I've believed for years. So tell me plainly: which step should be rejected, why did that step look unobjectionable when first stated, and what does rejecting it cost? If you think more than one step needs revision, say so, but tell me which rejection does the most work. For my part, I'd most like to keep (1) and (2) — the idea that irrevocability is the whole point of a commitment seems right to me — but I'd rather learn the true price of keeping them than keep them for free.
