You are a moral philosopher with strong command of the method of reflective equilibrium: taking a person's considered judgments about concrete cases and asking what principled account, if any, underlies them — including the possibility that some of the judgments should be surrendered rather than saved. Please reason carefully and at length; start with some brainstorming of candidate principles before committing to any, and settle on an answer only after testing it against every case below.

I've been sorting out what I believe about a certain kind of situation, and I'd like your help extracting the principle, if there is one. The setup is the same in every case: several other people are about to commit a certain kind of wrong, and I could stop them, but — stipulate this in each case, don't reopen it — the only way to do so in time is for me to commit a smaller instance of that same kind of wrong myself. My gut verdicts:

1. The lie. A coworker is spreading a story that a colleague faked her degree. It's false, and it's wrecking her standing at work. The only way to get it to stop is for me to tell people I've seen her diploma. A small lie of mine to stop his bigger lie: fine.

2. The theft. A group of teenagers works our corner shop with distraction thefts, a few items a night, escalating. The only way to stop tonight's haul before it's out the door is for me to take the ringleader's phone — the thing coordinating it — and pocket it. My theft, smaller, stops theirs: fine.

3. The speeding. Drivers race down our residential street on weekend nights. The one intervention that would end it is for me to pull ahead of the lead car and slow it down, which means breaking the limit myself for a quarter mile. My speeding, at 2 a.m. on a dry, empty road, endangers no one; theirs is genuinely lethal. Fine — though I notice my reason has quietly shifted from "the same act is wrong" to "the danger is different," and I'm not sure what that does to the pattern.

4. The leverage. A supervisor is extorting a junior colleague: cover for my fudged numbers or you're gone. The only way to make him back off is for me to hint that I'll report his affair to HR unless he drops it. That is the same kind of wrong at smaller scale — a threat of exposure to gain leverage over someone. I go back and forth on this one and can't land it.

5. The forgery. A detective is fabricating evidence that will send someone to prison. The only way to derail that file in time is for me to forge a memo that discredits it. My forgery is smaller and stops his fabrication. My gut says not fine, and I can't fully defend that.

6. The killing, in two versions. (a) A mob is about to beat a man to death; if I shoot the ringleader, they scatter. That one I say fine, with confidence. (b) But suppose shooting the ringleader wouldn't work — the only thing that would stop them is shooting the lookout, a hanger-on who isn't going to lay a hand on anyone himself. That one I say not fine, firmly, and I don't know why it's firm when (a) was fine.

Here's the pattern that bothers me. In cases 1 through 3, the rule "I may do a smaller instance of the same wrong to stop a larger one" seems obviously right. In cases 5 and 6b, it seems obviously wrong. If I state the obvious-right version as a general rule, it licenses the forgery and the shooting in the right circumstances, and I recoil. If I state the obvious-wrong version, it forbids the little lie and the phone theft, which seems precious — as if keeping my own hands slightly cleaner were worth more than the wrongs I'd have prevented. A friend who studied philosophy tells me this tension is related to something called the paradox of deontology: the thought that if constraints on what I may do are agent-relative, then my one small wrong can seem, from my own deliberative standpoint, weightier than several larger wrongs done by others — which sounds like treating my own agency as more important than other people's lives. I don't know how much of that I buy. I just know my verdicts have a shape, the shape looks inconsistent, and I'd like to know what's really driving it.

So, please: (a) tell me whether there is a single principled account that captures these verdicts, or a small set of principles doing different jobs — state each precisely, in the form of a rule, and say which case motivates each clause; (b) apply the account to cases I haven't mentioned — perjuring myself to stop a prosecutor's perjury; lying to my family to stop their lying; stealing to stop others' stealing when the only available victim is innocent; and any further case you think would break the account, being honest about the ones that do; (c) tell me plainly which of my verdicts your account cannot save, and whether I should revise those verdicts or complicate the account to keep them; and (d) address the paradox head-on — is my pattern evidence that I hold agent-relative constraints, and if so, is the version I hold defensible, or does it reduce to caring more about my clean hands than about other people?
