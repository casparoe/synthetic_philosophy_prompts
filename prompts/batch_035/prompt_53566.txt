I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I've been chasing a pattern in how people rank and choose that I can't find a name for. The cleanest version of it shows up in choices that trade off two dimensions. Take a very mild headache lasting two days against one that is barely more intense but lasts only one day: most people would confidently pick the shorter, slightly sharper one, since the added intensity is trivial and the saved day is not. Now repeat that same trade a few dozen times — a bit more intensity each round, in exchange for a shorter duration — where each individual step still looks like the same obvious bargain. The endpoint of the chain pairs a trivial afternoon ache with a fraction of a second of the worst agony imaginable, and at that point nearly everyone reverses the verdict. So we get a run of pairwise comparisons, each of which seems clearly right, whose conjunction says a moment of agony is better than two days of mild discomfort — which is also clearly right to deny. I've since noticed the same shape outside medicine: critics' ordinal rankings of wines or cameras in which every adjacent pair differs "negligibly" while the top and bottom of the list are worlds apart, and job candidates who take a slightly worse role for a $500 bump, over and over, until the accumulated payoffs obviously don't cover the accumulated costs. A mathematician friend tells me the technical issue is called the failure of transitivity, and I take her word for it, but that label alone doesn't help me. What I want to know is whether this specific configuration — a chain of individually confident verdicts colliding with another confident verdict — has an established name of its own, and whether there is a serious literature that uses such chains to argue that "better than" simply isn't transitive rather than treating them as a trick to be explained away. I can see that it is adjacent to the sorites paradox, but in the sorites the steps involve indistinguishability — grains of sand, shades of colour — whereas here each step is a clear, decisive judgment, and the conflict is between judgments, not between a judgment and a description; I'd like to know precisely where the boundary runs, because casual discussion constantly runs the two together. Finally, since economists treat transitivity of preference as close to a rationality requirement, I'd like to understand what the defenders of transitivity say about cases like mine: do they reject the individual verdicts, reject chaining them together, or bite the bullet and accept the cycle? Please write your answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write a long letter to a colleague.
