I teach a second-year undergraduate course on thermodynamics and the philosophy of physics, with about sixteen students drawn roughly half from physics and half from philosophy, and I have a 90-minute seminar coming up on Maxwell's demon and the physics of information. The students can already recite the story: the demon sorts fast from slow molecules, the entropy of the box seems to fall, the second law seems threatened, and everything is patched up by Landauer's principle that erasing one bit of memory costs at least kT ln 2 of dissipated heat, plus Bennett's point that the demon's real sin is resetting its memory to a blank state rather than the measurement or the gating. The trouble is that they treat the resolution as a bookkeeping footnote. They have never felt, in their bones, that the information is not free — that the sorting looks like it generates extractable work for nothing, and that the bill arrives only later, and only when the memory must be cleared for reuse.

I want you to design a single in-class game that produces that feeling before I say a word about Landauer or Bennett. The ideal shape is something like: the class or a subset of it constitutes the gas in a two-chambered box, one or more students play the demon at a gate between the chambers, observations get recorded on physical tokens of some kind (cards, counters, whatever you think works), and there is a resource — tokens, points, some proxy for extractable work — that the demon's side visibly accumulates as the sorting proceeds. Somewhere in the mechanics there must be a moment where the memory fills up and has to be reused, and clearing it costs something, so the participants run a profitable-seeming enterprise, get comfortable, and then discover the debt only when the reset is forced on them. Give me the full rules, the materials list, the timeline across the 90 minutes, a payoff scheme that requires no real money, and enough detail that I could run it cold. Include one genuinely tricky wrinkle: I'd like the students to be able to attempt a 'cheat' in which they clear their memory by simply standardising the tokens — shuffling the cards into a canonical order, say — so that in the debrief we can discuss whether that escape is real or whether it just relocates the erasure, which is exactly Bennett's move against the old objection that one can always forget for free.

Then give me three further things. First, a debrief script that maps each moment of the game onto the formal story: which mechanic corresponds to measurement, which to the gate, which to memory, which to the reset, and where the Szilard one-molecule engine and the kT ln 2 bound live in it. Second, an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students free-riding, the pacing collapsing, someone grasping the point too early and announcing it — and how to handle each. Third, a candid note on which features of the actual physics the activity cannot reproduce and I must simply assert: my molecules will not have a genuinely thermal velocity distribution, the gate in the room will not admit the possibility of a fluctuation undoing the demon's work, and measurement in the game is error-free and free in a way no measurement is. Please commit to one definite design with definite rules and definite payoffs — do not offer me a menu of variants to choose between — and defend the choices you make rather than flagging them as provisional. Write everything in British English.
