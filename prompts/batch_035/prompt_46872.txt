my department's reading group has spent basically the whole semester on vagueness and we've gotten stuck in a genuinelly annoying way: everyone knows the moves between epistemicism and supervaluationism and nobody budges an inch. The Williamson side says there's a sharp cutoff between bald and not-bald, we just can't ever know where it is; the supervaluationist side says meaning simply doesn't settle the penumbral cases, and the "one true cutoff hiding out there" picture gets the whole thing backwards. We've each got the other side's arguements memorized, including the responses to the responses.

My own hypothesis, which I'd rather you test than agree with: the disagreement isn't really about cutoffs at all, it's about what a semantic theory is supposed to *do*. Epistemicism treats meaning facts like any other external-world facts — they can outrun every speaker's ability to recover them, and that's fine, the world is full of facts like that. Supervaluationism seems to bake in the thought that meaning is somehow constituted by use, so where use runs out, there's simply nothing left for meaning to be. If that's right, the sorites arguments are almost theater — both sides' verdicts on them were fixed in advance by a decision about the function of semantics that nobody ever argues for directly. Does that hold up?

There are also two sticking points our group keeps circling that I want folded into the diagnosis:

1. The supertruth thing. Supervaluationists can admit that under every admissible precisification there's a cutoff, so "there is a sharp boundary" comes out supertrue — but they can't say where it is, and they visibly don't want to assert the sentence. One person in the group claimed this makes the whole dispute nearly verbal: "supertrue but not assertible" versus "true and knowable by no one" are supposed to be rival theories of reality? Someone else thought that was too cute. Settle it.

2. Higher-order vagueness. The supervaluationist needs admissible precisifications, but vagueness about which precisifications are admissible seems to send the machinery into regress, while the epistemicist just shrugs and says yes, there are infinitely many levels of sharp but unknowable boundaries, deal with it. Is *this* the real crux, or does it only bite one side?

So: is this a substantive disagreement, or two projects — the metaphysics of meaning versus the codification of competent use — sharing a word? Is it bedrock intuitions about what facts are even available in the penumbra, intuitions nobody can argue for without circularity? And would anything actually count as progress — a formal result, psycholinguistic data on how speakers really treat borderline cases, some distinction nobody's drawn yet — or should we expect this exact stalemate forever, and if so, is that a sign the question is ill-posed or just hard? Don't give me a survey of the views, I have that already. I want a diagnosis with reasons, and an honest answer about whether the debate can move.
