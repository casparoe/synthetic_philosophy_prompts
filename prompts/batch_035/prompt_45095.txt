I'm teaching an upper-level undergraduate course on personal identity, and we just finished a two-week unit on Parfit's Reasons and Persons, covering the teletransporter cases, the Branch Line case, fission, and the arguments that personal identity is not what matters in survival. For the midterm, I asked students to answer the following question: "Present Parfit's teletransporter thought experiment and explain the distinction he draws between personal identity and what matters in survival. Does Parfit think the person who steps into the scanner survives? Should the answer depend on whether the scanning process destroys the original body, and what do branching cases suggest about whether identity is what matters?"

One of my students submitted the answer below. I'd like a second opinion before I finalize grades, partly because I read a stack of forty of these and worry I'm glossing over details. Please give the answer a letter grade and then walk me through it point by point: what the student gets right, and where the answer goes wrong. I'm especially interested in subtle issues — places where the answer sounds correct on a quick read but conflates distinctions Parfit works hard to keep apart, misstates his position, or draws a conclusion that doesn't quite follow from what came before. Please be specific about which claims in the answer you're basing each part of your assessment on, so I can lift your comments more or less directly onto the grading rubric.

Here is the student's answer:

"Parfit asks us to imagine a teletransporter that scans your body, destroys it on Earth, and then creates an exact replica of you on Mars, complete with all your memories, beliefs, and personality traits. Because the replica is identical to you in every respect, the replica simply is you — this is what Parfit calls numerical identity. Parfit's view is that personal identity consists in psychological continuity, which he calls the Relation R, so when the scanner preserves your psychology, you survive the trip; what is destroyed is merely your body, which is not what you are.

The interesting case is when the machine malfunctions. In the Branch Line case, the scan goes through and the replica on Mars is created, but the original on Earth is not destroyed right away — the Earth person survives for a day or so before dying. Now we have two psychologically continuous people, and it seems arbitrary to say that only one of them is you. Parfit concludes from this that personal identity comes in degrees: since psychological continuity is a matter of degree, identity must be too. On his view, the person on Mars in the Branch Line case is, say, ninety percent you.

This is why Parfit says identity is not what matters. What matters is the Relation R itself, and this holds between you and the replica whether or not the original is destroyed. Since R comes in degrees, survival is not the all-or-nothing thing we ordinarily take it to be. Parfit illustrates the same point with his Combined Spectrum, where he argues that there must always be a further fact about whether you survive a given transition, over and above the degree of psychological continuity. In the end, Parfit's view is comforting: even if the teletransporter destroys you, what matters in survival is fully preserved."
