write a sestina about the ship of theseus where the six end-words are the ship's parts (keel, plank, mast, rope, sail, oar -- or whatever set you like), and let the form itself do the philosophical work. the whole point is that in a sestina the same six words keep returning in a fixed rotation, so the poem preserves its identity through nothing but the ordering of the same components -- which is one possible answer to what the ship's identity consists in. but note the rotation is a strict rule, not random repair, so i'd like the poem to at least gesture at whether that matters (is a ship replaced plank-by-plank in a fixed schedule the same ship as one that just happens to get repaired ad hoc?). somewhere in the middle stanzas work in hobbes's wrinkle -- the original planks being saved and reassembled into a second ship while the repaired one sails on -- and the envoi should handle the two ships somehow, not dodge them. if you want to get cheeky, the envoi can ask whether a sestina with all six end-words swapped for synonyms would still be the same poem. someone who knows the paradox should be able to see that the form is arguing a position, not just decorating one, and someone who doesn't should still enjoy the boat.
