I write literary fiction, mostly in close third person, and I've been struck by an odd asymmetry in the conventions of my trade. When my narrator recounts a character's most humiliating private thought, readers experience this as intimacy — the reward of the form. Were a real person to obtain and publish that same access to my inner life, we would all call it a violation. Yet the license the novel grants is not merely permitted but prized: we speak of interiority as the genre's great achievement. At the same time, the moment real private material enters the book — a friend's confession repurposed as a scene, a family's catastrophe given a new name — a different set of customs kicks in: I change the identifying details, I worry over the acknowledgments, my publisher prints a boilerplate disclaimer about resemblance to actual persons that all parties know is not strictly true. So the profession seems to treat fictional interiority as harmless and real interiority as dangerous, while using the one as camouflage for the other.

What theory of privacy does this pattern of practices presuppose? Perhaps privacy is informational control, in which case the novel is a standing scandal it has somehow laundered; perhaps it is closer to the view some philosophers call contextual integrity, on which the wrongness of a disclosure depends on the context into which the information moves, so that the same fact can be intimate in a friendship and inert on the page. Reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can from these customs, tell me which rival theories of privacy's value the novel's conventions quietly rule out and which they are merely compatible with, and then say what would actually have to change in the craft — in narrative technique, in my obligations to the people I mine for material — if we took a strict control-based view of privacy seriously. I would particularly like to know whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several theories of privacy would license it unchanged, or whether it commits the novelist to something definite about what the inner life is for.
