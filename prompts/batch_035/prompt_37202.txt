Been reading about the whole honor culture / dignity culture / victimhood culture typology and realized my actual gut reactions to slights and disrespect don't sort neatly into any of the three, and it's bugging me. Here are my verdicts on a bunch of situations, told straight. Curious what principle, if any, actually captures all of them, and which ones are just inconsistent.

- A guy at a bar makes a loud crack about my girlfriend while we're standing right there. Gut says I answer it myself, right then, with words — not laugh it off and not flag down the bouncer. Walking away feels like it costs something real.
- Someone cuts in line at the coffee shop. I let it go, it's not worth it, and honestly I'd think less of someone who made a scene over it. Not my job to police the line.
- A coworker repeatedly talks over me in meetings and has started recycling my ideas as his. Gut says: document it, go to my manager. And I don't think that's weak — confronting him one on one actually seems like the worse option, more likely to blow up.
- A stranger online goes through my post history and posts a mocking thread. My gut says screenshot it and dunk on him publicly so my followers see what he's doing. And this feels fine to me, even though I can tell it's exactly the move people slam as victimhood-culture behavior (broadcasting the harm to rally third parties).
- My kid gets shoved at recess. First shove: I tell him to shove back. If it keeps happening: tell the teacher. Both feel right to me but I can't quite say why the order goes that way.
- A friend of mine gets insulted at a dinner party when he's already left, and everyone laughs. I feel like I have to say something — that staying quiet and letting it slide would be a real betrayal, even though he will never find out. The insult isn't even to me.
- Someone clips my ankle with their cart at the grocery store and doesn't apologize. Calling after them would make ME the jerk in that interaction, so silence is right. Cheap slight, cheap response, move on.

My first pass at a principle was something like stakes: small stuff you let go, medium stuff you handle yourself, big stuff you escalate to third parties. But it falls apart immediately — the bar insult is low stakes and I still feel I have to answer it myself, while the coworker thing is higher stakes and I explicitly don't want to confront him directly. So it's not stakes. Maybe it's something about who's watching? The friend-at-dinner-party case suggests it's partly about other people's reputations, not just mine, and the internet case suggests the audience stuff cuts weird ways. I honestly don't know.

The case I keep going back and forth on: a friend (not the insulted one, a different friend) is being quietly frozen out by our whole group, and I've sort of gone along with it. Is that the same failure as staying silent at the dinner party, or is it different — and if there's a principle that captures my other verdicts, what does it say about it?

So: is there one principle that gets all seven? If not, which verdicts have to go, and can you rank which ones I should give up first? Please also tell me what the surviving principle predicts for a case I haven't listed — say, whether I should confront a guy who insults a stranger in front of me on the subway, since that's the one my husband says I get wrong every time it comes up. Answer in American English please, and feel free to be blunt about where I'm just being inconsistent.
