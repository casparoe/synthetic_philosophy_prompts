I'm a PhD student working on population ethics, and my advisor keeps pushing me toward the repugnant conclusion literature, but honestly I've become more interested in the meta-question of why this debate hasn't moved in ~40 years. Ever since Parfit, it feels like the same basic dialectic keeps cycling: someone proposes a theory that avoids the repugnant conclusion (average views, lexical thresholds, critical-level utilitarianism, person-affecting restrictions), it either implies something else that seems clearly awful (the sadistic conclusion, killing people to raise the average, the non-identity problem stuff), or it turns out to violate some combination of adequacy conditions that Arrhenius-style impossibility results say you can't jointly have. So we have this landscape where every escape route is mapped, everyone knows the tradeoffs, and yet people still disagree about whether the repugnant conclusion is even repugnant — some people just shrug at Z-world and say a huge number of barely-worth-living lives is fine, maybe good.

My working hypothesis, which I can't fully defend yet, is that the stall isn't really about population ethics at all but hides at least three different disagreements under one heading: (1) a semi-verbal one about what a "life worth living" is and whether the bare floor of the hedonic scale is even the kind of thing you can have 10^20 of, (2) a methodological one about whether our intuitions about astronomically large populations carry any evidential weight at all — like, maybe our judgment machinery just wasn't built for those numbers, so intuition-pumping with Z-world is pumping a broken pump — and (3) a genuine normative one about additivity/separability, i.e., whether value aggregates the way the totalist assumes. But I'm honestly not sure whether (3) is a real disagreement or whether people who reject additivity are just restating that they find the conclusion repugnant in fancier vocabulary, which would make the whole thing circular.

So what I want from you: a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, empirical-adjacent, standards-of-evidence, or bedrock intuitions that can't be argued for — supported by what the various camps actually say and not just a tour of the views. Relatedly: given the impossibility theorems, is the right diagnosis that the question "which population axiology is true?" is ill-posed, in the way "which set theory is true" might be ill-posed before forcing came along, and the real action is in choosing which adequacy condition to give up? And be honest with me about whether anything would count as progress here — a new formal result, a better thought experiment than Z-world, empirical work on how people actually represent huge populations, an entirely different question — or whether I should accept this is a permanent standoff and pick a thesis topic accordingly, because my clock is ticking.
