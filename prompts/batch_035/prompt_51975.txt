I'm a PhD student in political theory, and our program runs a prospectus workshop in which each student circulates a one-page statement of their working commitments before the committee takes aim at it. Mine concerns the ethics of public office, and before I send it around I would like an audit of whether the package hangs together. Here is the statement, roughly as it will go out:

1. Dirty hands are real. In some circumstances a political leader both must do something and wrongs someone in doing it. Walzer's example of the official who authorizes the torture of a captured rebel in order to find where the bombs are planted is, I think, exactly such a case: the act is obligatory and wrong.
2. What distinguishes dirty hands from an ordinary tragic choice is the moral remainder. The official ought to feel guilt, and I accept the principle that guilt is fitting only toward wrong one has done.
3. Office converts deontic status. The very act that would be impermissible for a private citizen in identical circumstances can be required of an official, because role obligations genuinely change what one may and must do.
4. In private morality I am a threshold deontologist: near-absolute constraints against killing, torturing, and deceiving, which give way when catastrophe looms — and when they give way, the act is simply permissible, with no residue attaching to it.
5. On institutional design, I oppose ex ante legal authorization of the torture case, torture warrants and the like: formal authorization would normalize and multiply the practice, so the official must act outside the law and try to clean his hands afterward.
6. At the same time, I hold that democratic accountability demands legal answerability: officials who violate rights must be prepared to stand trial, and the torturing official should in fact be indicted and tried — even though I also believe he must ultimately be acquitted or pardoned, since he did what he ought.
7. I think that official should also resign, and that his resignation would be morally required rather than supererogatory — though I cannot settle whether, on my view, it is required as penance for a wrong, as a duty of democratic responsiveness, or as something else entirely.

Please go through these and tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what each genuine tension would cost to resolve. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad; for each, say what the cheapest fix would be and what that fix would cost elsewhere in the set; and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I would rather hear it from you than from my committee, so please be unsparing.
