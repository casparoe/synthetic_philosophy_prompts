I am a graduate teaching assistant who has just been asked to take over the discussion sections of our department's mid-level philosophy of mind course — the regular instructor went on unexpected medical leave mid-semester, and I inherited about 18 students and an 80-minute block per section. Next week's topic is the hard problem, but at a recent departmental seminar I became preoccupied with what David Chalmers has called the meta-problem of consciousness: not the problem of explaining consciousness, but the problem of explaining why we judge and report that there is a hard problem at all — why creatures like us, upon having an experience, go around insisting that no amount of physical information seems to capture what the experience is like. What grips me is the tension this generates: the meta-problem is supposed to be an ordinary functional and mechanistic problem, solvable in principle by standard cognitive science, and yet a complete explanation of why we produce these reports seems simultaneously to leave the hard problem untouched and to threaten to debunk it — as though the science of why we complain about the gap could do the work the science of the gap was supposed to do. I want my students to run into that tension from the inside rather than be told about it.

Please take on the role of an experienced instructor of philosophy of mind with a strong track record of designing active-learning exercises, and think the design through carefully — brainstorm a couple of possible structures first, then commit to the one you judge best and defend that choice briefly. Design an in-class activity in which the students generate the meta-problem themselves. One structure I can imagine, though you should feel free to improve on or discard it: one group of students must produce the "phenomenal reports" under a constraint — describing an experience to another group using only information that a neural or functional duplicate of them, lacking the experience itself, could in principle possess — while a second group plays the cognitive scientists trying to give a mechanistic explanation of why those reports occur. The students should feel the point where the explanation of the reporting seems to explain everything about what was said and yet to leave something out, and then feel the further twist: that this "something left out" is itself available to them only as another report, which the scientists could equally explain.

Include, in whatever structure you choose: concrete rules and roles; simple materials; a timing plan that fits 80 minutes including the debrief; an incentive or payoff scheme that does not require real money; and a debrief that maps specific, foreseeable moments in the activity onto the components of the meta-problem — the reports, the mechanistic explanation of the reports, and the residue the explanation appears to leave. Then tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle each case (students refusing the constraint, students theatrically overcomplying, a clever student short-circuiting the whole thing on day one). Finally, be frank about the feature of the issue that no such activity can in principle reproduce — my students, unlike the hypothetical duplicates, really are conscious, and their conviction that something is left out will not have been produced by the constraints — and tell me exactly what I must supply in the debrief to compensate.
