I write fiction with a lot of nonhuman viewpoints — hive minds, uplifted animals, machine intelligences — and I've come to believe that my failures with these characters are taxonomic: I keep giving every alien a full-blown reflective mind and then papering over the difference with stylistic choices. A friend who reads philosophy pointed me to Dennett's ladder of creature types, the one that runs from Darwinian creatures (fixed strategies, selection acting only across generations) through Skinnerian (learning by overt trial and costly error), Popperian (running candidate actions through an internal model so mistakes die in the head rather than in the world), and Gregorian (importing ready-made mental tools — labels, concepts, others' designs — that restructure the very model doing the simulating). I want to internalize this ladder in my hands rather than my head, and I want the same for the members of a small genre writers' retreat I convene twice a year: fourteen of us, mostly novelists and a couple of game writers, one ninety-minute session in a rented library meeting room. The goal is not literacy in the philosophy but felt discrimination — before the session ends, each of us should have bodily experienced what it is to be stuck at each rung, so that afterward we can ask of any character: what rung is this mind on, and what does the prose at that rung have to conceal?

I'd like you to design the full session as a staged game or set of escalating exercises rather than a discussion. My half-formed idea is some task — navigation, foraging, or a cooperation problem — that the same participants attempt repeatedly under deliberately different information regimes: one pass where the environment punishes every wrong move irreversibly (no learning within a life, only across teams), one where overt trial-and-error is allowed but only post hoc, one where private rehearsal or lookahead is permitted before commitment, and one where participants can receive tools, names, or hints from another player before acting. But treat that sketch as provisional; you may restructure entirely if you see a better route to the felt distinctions. The debrief should map concrete moments from the play onto the rungs of the hierarchy and, crucially for a room of novelists, translate each rung into a narrative-craft question. Please also flag what will predictably go wrong on a first run — I expect one hard problem is making the Popperian and Skinnerian passes feel genuinely different, since the difference is internal and a game can only regulate behavior — and be frank about which feature of the real hierarchy no ninety-minute exercise can reproduce, since Darwinian selection operates over generational time that a game session can't honestly simulate.

One request about the tenor of your reply: if you think my framing is confused — if, for instance, the ladder's rungs blur together once you try to operationalize them behaviorally, or if you think the exercise will inevitably smuggle Gregorian scaffolding into every condition because rule-following itself is a kind of culturally transmitted tool — say so directly and tell me what you'd do instead, rather than delivering a polished plan that quietly cheats. I'd rather learn the taxonomy's soft spots now than discover them mid-session.
