I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a good while now, and the Dennettian slogan "there is no Cartesian theater" has lately become one of those lines people deploy as a full argument in itself. The problem is that it seems to be doing quite different work depending on who says it, and I'd like a careful disambiguation before I start using it myself.

Three recent encounters, to fix the uses I care about:

1. In a thread about whether language models have any kind of experience, one commenter shut down the discussion by invoking the slogan — as though the absence of a Cartesian theater settled the question of whether an LLM's processing involves anything worth calling experience.
2. A discussion of global workspace theory got sidetracked when someone objected that the global workspace, with its spotlight of attention broadcasting to the rest of the system, is just the Cartesian theater rebuilt in neural furniture — stage, audience, and all — and that Dennett disposed of that picture decades ago. (Bernard Baars, notably, was never embarrassed about the theater metaphor; he embraced it.)
3. Someone described a meditation retreat where they seemed to notice that the observing self is constructed moment by moment, and another commenter waved this off with the slogan plus something about postdictive editing — referencing experiments like the color phi phenomenon, where the brain seems to fill in an illusory intermediate state after the fact, so that the "stream" of experience looks stitched together retrospectively.

What I want is a menu of the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing — from the near-trivial (there is no literal little screen in the brain where everything comes together for a homunculus) to the genuinely radical (there is no unified stream of consciousness at all, no fact of the matter about when a content becomes conscious, and perhaps no coherent notion of a subject of experience). For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive but dubious, or plainly false. Then tell me which readings, if any, would actually support each of the three uses above: does any reading of the slogan settle anything about machine experience? Does any reading show global workspace theory to be fatally circular, or is the objection a confusion between a metaphor and a mechanism? And does any reading support dismissing first-person meditative reports about the constructed self — or is that precisely where the slogan overreaches?

If it helps, note where Dennett's own positive picture (the multiple drafts model, and his later replacement of the drafts metaphor with something like fame in the brain — content becoming conscious in the way a rumor becomes famous, without a single moment of becoming) sits relative to your readings: it is worth flagging which of your readings the multiple drafts model actually requires, versus which it merely permits. Present the readings as a numbered list with a one-line verdict each, followed by the assessment of the three uses.
