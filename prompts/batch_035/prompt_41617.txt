so I'm a novelist, currently mid-draft on a book that follows a great power through what I intend to be its decline and fall. my narrator is a historian character who's fond of saying the inside view is for suckers: empires are a well-populated reference class, the base rates of collapse within a few centuries are brutal, and any particular empire's "unique" troubles — overextension, fiscal strain, elites turning on each other — are just the standard checklist arriving on schedule. so her bet is basically always "decline, on roughly the historical timetable," and she dismisses anyone who argues from this empire's particular virtues or reforms as doing motivated reasoning.

I've basically adopted her view as my own working position (for the book, but honestly also for thinking about real great powers). but I don't want to write her as a strawman or as unassailably wise, and I'd like to stress-test the view itself. so: what would actually count against the outside-view position on imperial decline? not just "every empire is unique so reference classes are meaningless" as a slogan — I want the specific things: what would a good outside-viewer themselves concede as a problem (like reference class arbitrariness — why is the unit "empires" and not "maritime trading states" or "nuclear-armed bureaucratic states"? what about the observation that no empire has ever collapsed after acquiring nuclear weapons — does that break the class?), what would genuinely be decisive vs merely awkward, and whether the view can be qualified into something that can never lose (if she can always redraw the reference class after the fact, is she actually predicting anything?). rank the defeaters for me, fatal to merely inconvenient, and tell me which ones a committed believer would wave off and which they'd have to eat. if it turns out the strongest counter is that base rates only tell you the long run and my novel takes place over 30 years where the inside view dominates, say that too. typo-ridden but urgent, cheers.
