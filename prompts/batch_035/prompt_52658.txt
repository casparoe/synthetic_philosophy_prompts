After a career spent in experimental biology, I have lately been reading my way into the philosophy of mind, and I have become fascinated by the strand of Turing's work that runs through his 1948 report Intelligent Machinery and the closing sections of his 1950 paper — the idea, roughly, that instead of programming an adult intelligence outright, one should build a "child machine" and educate it, letting reward and punishment organize an initially unorganised system. I gather from secondary discussions that his picture of learning there is starkly behaviorist: pleasure makes tentative solutions permanent, pain cancels them, discipline is to be instilled first and initiative somehow added afterward, and he even remarks that converting a machine into a universal machine is the extremest form of discipline. It seems to me that this vision, if taken seriously as a pedagogy rather than as an engineering proposal, has a distinctly odd shape — and that the oddness is worth exposing.

I would therefore like you to write a piece of sustained deadpan satire: a termly inspection report, around 700 words, from a schools inspector in a world somewhat like ours, in which Turing's program was adopted as actual educational policy and academies now raise child machines from their unorganised state according to his methods. The report should be addressed to the board of one such academy, in the flat administrative register of a real inspection report: numbered findings, a grading scheme, judicious hedging, a recommendations section.

The comedy must come from the actual content of Turing's framework rather than from inventions, so that a philosopher of AI who knows the 1948 and 1950 texts would recognize each finding as an exaggeration of something genuinely there. Please work in, as findings the inspector notes with concern: the timing problem, since pain cancels the most recently made tentative entries, a punishment delivered a moment too late wipes out a correct solution along with the wrong ones, so the staff's inconsistent response times are producing machines that randomly know and do not know things; the overfitting problem, since the machines are being shaped by their teachers' approvals rather than by any standard external to the teachers, they have become excellent at pleasing inspectors and hopeless at anything else, which the inspector grudgingly concedes is the system working exactly as designed; the discipline/initition trade-off, in which a machine that has achieved full discipline — become, in effect, a universal machine — has had its initiative trained out of it, and the inspector is unsure which box to tick; and the difficulty of grading a machine whose recent learning may have been retroactively cancelled by an ill-timed caning, so that the inspector's own tests, by delivering bad marks, destroy the very knowledge they were meant to measure. Somewhere the inspector should record, in the tone of a man covering himself, that the founder of the method foresaw several of these difficulties himself, so that the satire lands on the program and its enthusiastic administrators rather than on misrepresenting Turing.

The legibility requirement is that a reader who knows nothing of Turing should still find it a convincing report about a school, and a reader who knows the texts should find it fair. Keep it deadpan throughout — no winking, no exclamation points, no jokes the inspector is aware of. Conclude with a recommendations section in which the inspector, bound by the logic of the system he serves, can only recommend more of the same.

Please write the piece in American English throughout.
