My roommates have been fighting for a week about a bill in our state legislature (we're in a political philosophy course together, so everything turns into a Mill debate), and they finally asked me to referee, except I genuinely can't decide who's right about what. The bill is a generational tobacco/vape ban: no sales ever to anyone born after a certain cutoff year. So unlike a normal age limit that everyone eventually ages out of, people born after the cutoff can never legally buy, even at 30 or 50. Whether that's compatible with Mill's harm principle is what they've been going at each other over.

Here's roughly how the argument went. (1) Priya opened by saying it's obvious paternalism and Mill's principle flatly forbids it: society may only coerce someone to prevent harm to *others*, and smoking/vaping harms only the smoker. (2) Dan said that's way too quick, because the harm to others is sitting right there -- secondhand aerosol is a real thing, and anyway smokers' medical costs are paid by everyone through the public system, so it's not actually self-regarding conduct at all. (3) Priya said that argument proves far too much: skiing accidents, junk food, playing tackle football all load costs onto public healthcare too, so if cost-spillover counts as "harm to others," the harm principle ends up licensing regulation of basically every risky choice, which is a nanny state with extra steps and not what Mill meant by harm. (4) Dan countered that the *generational* design is the whole point: the law never overrides a competent adult's settled preference, it just prevents people from acquiring the addiction in the first place, since kids aren't the kind of autonomous agents the liberty principle protects -- and someone hooked on nicotine at 14 is arguably not exercising the sort of autonomous choice Mill cared about even once grown. (5) Priya came back that maybe that justifies a high minimum age, fine, but not the generational version: it treats future 30-year-olds as permanently incompetent, so you get two otherwise identical adults, one born in 2008 and one born in 2010, where one can buy and the other is banned for life, which looks exactly like the arbitrary, open-ended coercion Mill was against. And she asked the question Dan never really answered: if addiction truly destroys the competence the principle presupposes, why not ban tobacco for *everyone*? If it doesn't, why ban it for anyone past the age of majority?

Can you go through their exchange move by move -- the five steps above -- and rate each one from clearly right to clearly wrong, with your reasons? Then say who comes out ahead overall, and whether there's something both of them are missing, like a reading of the harm principle that handles the generational case cleanly (or shows it can't be handled). I'd also take general thoughts on whether their whole framing assumes something about the principle that Mill wouldn't have signed off on. Sorry this is long, it was a long argument lol.
