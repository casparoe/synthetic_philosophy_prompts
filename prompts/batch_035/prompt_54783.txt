I recently started my first full-time job, and the experience has pushed me to read up on Marx, whose ideas I last encountered in an undergraduate elective. I would like your help thinking through a puzzle that my reading has not resolved.

My situation: I work at a worker-owned cooperative. I have an equal vote in company decisions, I receive a share of the surplus rather than a fixed wage, and by every account I can find I am not exploited in anything like Marx's technical sense. And yet I find the work itself deadening in a way I did not anticipate: the tasks are repetitive, I feel no connection between what I do all day and anything I care about, and by Friday I notice my engagement with the rest of my life has thinned out. My housemate is in the mirror-image position. She works enormous hours at a game studio owned by a parent conglomerate; the surplus she generates plainly flows to shareholders. But she is genuinely absorbed by the work, takes pride in the product, and says the long hours feel chosen rather than imposed.

Comparing our two situations has made me suspect that exploitation and alienation, which my old course syllabus treated as a single package, are actually two separable theses that can come apart in both directions. I would like you to compare the two concepts as carefully as you can. Specifically:

1. Reconstruct each concept in its own terms. What exactly does exploitation consist in for Marx (the transfer of surplus value, the unfairness claim, or something else), and what exactly are the distinct components usually bundled under alienation (from the product, from the activity, from species-being, from other workers)?
2. Assess whether the two concepts are logically independent. Does either entail the other? Could my case really be a case of alienation without exploitation, and my housemate's exploitation without alienation, or do these cases only appear that way because we are misdescribing them?
3. Consider what the separation implies for Marx's overall critique of capitalism. If a socialist arrangement like my cooperative can still be alienating, is alienation a complaint about capitalism at all, or about industrial production, or about labor as such? Conversely, if someone finds wage labor absorbing and self-expressive, does that undercut the exploitation claim, or does it merely explain why it is tolerated?

Please answer in American English, in a formal register. I am more interested in careful conceptual analysis than in historical commentary or appeals to authority; where the concepts are ambiguous, say so and work through the readings rather than settling for a textbook definition.
