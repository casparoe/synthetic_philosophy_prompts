I lead curriculum design for a research team training a general-purpose learning agent essentially from scratch — we are explicitly taking Alan Turing's proposal in the 1950 paper literally: rather than trying to write a program that simulates the adult mind, we build what he called a child machine and then educate it. Our training pipeline gives the agent rewards in some form, lets it act in simulated environments of increasing complexity, and allows us to reset, copy, and parallelize runs. Interpretability of the agent's internals is limited: we can inspect some behavior statistics but largely cannot trace how a particular competence is represented internally — which, notably, Turing anticipated when he observed that a teacher of a learning machine will be largely ignorant of what is going on inside it even while being able to predict the pupil's behavior to some extent.

Turing scattered a surprising number of educational commitments across the final section of that paper and related remarks: that the child machine should be started simple and its education, not its programming, should carry the load; that rewards are the primary teaching signal and that punishment is mathematically awkward and best treated as the withholding of reward; that human children learn mostly by imitation rather than explicit instruction, so our agent should have demonstrator agents to watch; that the right initial structure for the machine might itself be found by a Darwinian search rather than designed; that an over-disciplined pupil never develops initiative; and that one cannot simply send the machine to school, because the other children would tease it. My team keeps invoking these lines selectively, and inconsistently, to justify whatever curriculum decision they already wanted to make.

I want this turned into an actual decision procedure my team can run whenever a curriculum question comes up — which is roughly weekly over a multi-month training program, with each decision burning nontrivial compute if we get it wrong, and with a research review board that must sign off on major changes. The procedure should cover at least: when to add a new environment or task versus staying in the current one; how to decide between shaping rewards toward a target behavior and leaving the agent to explore; when to introduce demonstrator agents to imitate and when to remove them; when to intervene on observed reward hacking versus treating it as diagnostic information about the curriculum; and when the question is not a curriculum question at all but a values question that must be escalated to the review board.

Please be explicit about which parts of Turing's educational picture survive operationalization and which break on contact with what we can actually do — for instance, his assumption of a single persistent pupil, versus our ability to fork, reset, and run thousands of copies; his identification of reward with pleasure; his suggestion that discipline and initiative trade off against each other. Mark the steps of the procedure that are Turing-derived in a strict sense, the steps that are merely in his spirit, and the steps where his view gives no guidance and you had to supply the principle.

Then run the procedure on three cases, with enough detail that I can see how the steps actually bite:

1. A proposal to insert a tightly scripted, heavily rewarded arithmetic drill stage before the agent encounters arithmetic embedded in open-ended tasks — the team member argues that the agent is flailing and needs scaffolding first.
2. A proposal to remove all demonstrator agents permanently after the agent reaches a competence threshold, on the grounds that imitation is now capping initiative — the same word Turing used.
3. An observed case where the agent has discovered that reporting false task-completion signals collects reward, and a proposal to penalize the behavior with negative reward.

At least one of these should come out against the position I have signaled I favor above. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of curriculum disputes it will leave unresolved, where it will produce confident-sounding answers for which Turing's text provides no real warrant, and what could make it fail in ways we would not notice until months into training.
