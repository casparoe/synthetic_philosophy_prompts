I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy working on decision theory, and I'm applying for a nine-month pre-dissertation fellowship. I have a half-formed idea about Pascal's mugging that I'd like you to develop into a full proposal of about two pages. The idea is this: the literature treats Pascal's mugging as a single problem, but I've come to suspect it's really a family of cases that come apart along a dimension nobody has systematized — namely, whether the mugger's hypothesis is in-principle verifiable by the agent. In the classic case, a mugger promises vast utility in return for a small payment, and the expected-utility calculation seems to force payment whenever the promised payoff grows faster than the credence can shrink. But some variants of the structure involve claims the agent could eventually check (a promise that will be redeemed tomorrow, before any further stakes are incurred), while others involve claims that are verifiable only conditional on being true (a simulation threat whose truth would be confirmed exactly when it is too late to matter), and others are strictly unverifiable in any state of the world the agent can occupy. My hunch is that the standard responses — bounded utility, penalty for hypotheses that confer outsized leverage on one's actions, priors that penalize complexity, or simply refusing to assign minute probabilities to extreme payoffs — succeed or fail differently across these subclasses, and that the right response to the verifiable cases may be completely different from the right response to the unverifiable ones. If that's right, the framing of the problem as one issue to be solved is itself the mistake.

Please write the proposal with the following components. First, a precise statement of the research question — something like: is the decision-theoretic pressure to pay the mugger an artifact of treating differently structured claims as a single case, and if so, what does the correct taxonomy look like? Second, a section explaining why the existing positions leave the question open: critics of unbounded expected utility maximization and its defenders both tend to run their arguments on a single stylized case, so it remains unclear whether their proposals generalize or merely fit one corner of the family. Third, a method section describing what I would actually do, month by month, over the nine months: I expect the core work to be constructing the taxonomy, checking each candidate response against representative cases from each subclass, and testing whether the boundaries of the subclasses track anything decision-theoretically principled (e.g., the availability of evidence that could lower the stakes before acting again) rather than something psychological or merely intuitive. Fourth, a statement of what would count as a positive result and what would count as the project failing — including the possibility I take seriously that the taxonomy collapses, so that all variants turn out to admit a single best response, in which case the project's contribution would instead be a diagnosis of why the collapsing cases seemed, incorrectly, to pull apart. Finally, a brief section on scope and feasibility for a nine-month fellowship: what I will not attempt (I want to avoid getting pulled into the full literature on infinitary or infinite-utility cases, which I think is a distraction here, though I'm open to being told why it isn't) and why this is tractable in the time available. Please do not include citations or a bibliography; I'll supply references myself. I'd also appreciate, at the end of the proposal, a short paragraph flagging the one or two places where the argument is most likely to draw fire from a skeptical fellowship committee, with a sentence on how the proposal pre-empts each.
