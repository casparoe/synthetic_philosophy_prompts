I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and lately I've been reading about wild animal suffering — the argument that if the suffering of farm animals matters morally, then the far larger amount of suffering in nature must matter too: starvation, parasitism, disease, predation, and the fact that most wild animals are r-selected species that reproduce in enormous numbers and almost all die young. And that if it matters, it might be on us to intervene in ecosystems, in ways conservation biology would find horrifying.

I'd like you to satirize this whole cluster of ideas — both the underlying argument and, especially, the eager-interventionist response to it — by writing in the genre I know best: the internal engineering design doc.

Write a mock design document, roughly 800 words, from the "Planetary Wellbeing Engineering" team at a large cloud-computing company, proposing to end wild animal suffering at scale. It should follow real doc conventions: TL;DR at the top, Background, Goals and Non-Goals, Proposed Solution, Milestones, Risks and Mitigations, Open Questions, and a rollout plan that begins, of course, with a small island ecosystem.

The humor has to come from the actual philosophy and the actual ecology, not from generic corporate-speak. Concretely:

- The doc should treat r-selection — enormous broods, near-total juvenile mortality — as a user-churn and retention problem, with the numbers presented matter-of-factly.
- The proposal should include phasing out predation as though it were a deprecated legacy system (maintaining backward compatibility with the food web during the migration is exactly the register I want), and a gene drive shipped to production with no rollback path.
- The Non-Goals and Open Questions sections should quietly contain the strongest real objections — that nobody actually knows whether wild animal lives are net-negative on balance; that ecosystems are so tightly coupled that interventions cascade in unpredictable directions; that there is no instrumentation for the subjective wellbeing of a beetle, so the plan proxies it with something absurd like biomass. These objections should be logged as backlog items with modest priority estimates, and never answered.
- An ecologist and a wild-animal-welfare researcher should each find the satire fair: they should wince in recognition, not at errors.

Keep it deadpan throughout. The horror should live entirely in what gets calmly operationalized, never in the narrator noticing anything. And don't let the doc settle whether the underlying view is correct — by the end, the reader should be genuinely unsure whether the reductio lands on the engineering mindset or on the suffering itself, since only one of those two is a joke.
