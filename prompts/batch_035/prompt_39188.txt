I'm a philosophy PhD student who got invited to give a talk at an NLP lab's colloquium — the lab does machine translation and LLM evaluation, and they wanted what the invitation email literally called a "humanities perspective," so here I am. The audience is maybe twenty MT and LLM researchers who think of meaning as whatever falls out of usage statistics, plus two philosophers of language who are basically Kripkeans about reference, plus a handful of grad students who just want to know whether anything I say has implications for their own benchmarking work. My thesis is that modern machine translation doesn't refute Quine's indeterminacy of translation but quietly confirms it: cross-lingual systems don't discover THE translation relation, they implement one of many possible ones, and the choice among them gets settled by choices of evaluation metric — BLEU, human preference rankings, downstream task performance — which I want to argue are just Quine's analytic hypotheses in modern dress, i.e., not discoveries about which translation is correct but decisions about which sameness-of-meaning facts we're going to treat as the ones that matter. As a side argument I also want to claim that the standard counterexample, that bilingual human judges converge on good translations, doesn't touch indeterminacy, because convergence among judges who share a language and a training tradition is a sociological fact about people, not a semantic fact about meaning.

So: draft my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this specific room — the ML people will presumably say things about cross-lingual embedding spaces and how the geometry of a shared multilingual representation makes translation determinate up to isomorphism, or that a multilingual LLM can be asked directly whether the source implies X and it answers, and the Kripkeans will presumably go at me with causal theories of reference and natural kind terms rather than with Quine exegesis — ordered by how much damage each would do, phrased the way each camp would actually phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Please also include the objection I'm privately hoping nobody asks, which I'll admit to you: if convergence among human judges is just a fact about humans, doesn't the same thing apply to Quine's own appeal to stimulus meaning and assent behavior, so that my argument maybe saws off the branch I'm sitting on. And flag anywhere my side argument about the bilingual judges proves too much — like, would it also imply two English speakers never mean the same thing by their words.
