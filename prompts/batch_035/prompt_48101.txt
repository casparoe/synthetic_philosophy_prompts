Here's roughly my credence-related credo, and I'd like someone to audit it for consistency, because I suspect I'm being selective about which Dutch book arguments I take seriously:

1. The static Dutch book argument works: if your credences violate the probability axioms, you're irrational, because a bookie can construct bets you'd accept at each one which jointly guarantee you a loss.
2. The diachronic Dutch book argument is just as good as the static one, and it shows conditionalizing on your evidence is a genuine requirement of rationality, not just a nice habit.
3. I reject van Fraassen's Reflection principle, even though the Dutch book argument for Reflection is structurally parallel to the one for conditionalization. Something about it feels wrong — my future self might be drunk or sloppy, and that shouldn't constrain what I'm entitled to beleive now — but I confess I don't have a principled line for why the parallel breaks.
4. Credences aren't dispositions to bet. Betting is just one way of eliciting or illustrating them; a hermit with no bookie in sight can still have irrational credences. So to the extent the Dutch book argument is any good, its real force must be that it reveals an inconsistency in your state of mind itself, not that you could literally be drained of money — the depragmatized reading.
5. Imprecise credences are sometimes rational: in the face of ambiguous evidence, the right state can be a set of probability functions rather than a single one.

Which of these hang together, which are in real tension, and which only look like it? For each genuine tension tell me what has to give, how severly it costs, and what the cheapest fix is — and don't list everything as equally bad, order them and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. If a tension just dissolves on inspection, put it in the other bucket and explain why.

Please give me the whole thing as a JSON object with this schema: a field "verdict" holding a one-sentence overall judgement; a field "tensions" holding an array, ordered most severe first, of objects with fields "commitments" (the numbers involved, e.g. "2 and 4"), "severity" ("high", "medium", or "low"), "explanation", "cheapest_fix", and "cost_of_fix"; and a field "apparent_tensions" holding an array of objects with fields "commitments" and "why_it_dissolves". Keep the JSON valid — no trailing commas, no commentary outside the object.
