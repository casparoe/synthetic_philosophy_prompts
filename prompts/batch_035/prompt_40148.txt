i'm a phd student in political theory and this keeps coming up in my thesis work on civic obligations, so i want to actually get it straight. australia (and belgium, and a bunch of others) has compulsory voting: you're legally required to turn up at the polls on election day and you get fined if you don't — though the fine is tiny, closer to a parking ticket than a real penalty, and turnout still sits above 90%, so the fine obviously isnt doing all the work. the wrinkle that interests me is that the law compels attendance, not a vote: the ballot is secret, and you can and some do hand it back blank or deliberately spoil it. so the state drags you to the booth and then doesn't care at all what you do there.

what i want to know is what this practice presupposes about what voting IS. like, does compulsory turnout only make sense on a picture where voting is a duty on par with jury service and taxes — something you owe the polity whether you feel like it or not? but that seems to presuppose a civic republican view that the rest of the surrounding system doesn't obviously share. or is the valued thing just the showing-up itself, which would fit a picture of voting as an affirmation of membership rather than a choice among options? and there's something that looks like an inconsistency to me: the same legal orders that compel this treat compelled expression as anathema everywhere else — forced speech is a paradigm rights violation — and dragging every citizen to the polls on election day at least looks adjacent to compelled political expression. is it? and notice we dont compel anything else in the neighborhood: no law makes you write a letter to your representative or attend a town hall, and no democracy that i know of compels voting where its optional elsewhere. can a duty-reading and an expression-reading both license the australian design, or does the actual shape of the law — compel the body, leave the mind free — point at exactly one of them?

dont give me a survey of positions and leave the picking to me, i can get that from the literature. take a definite stand on what the practice as designed actually presupposes, say which rival readings it rules out and which it tolerates, and defend the call. and if your honest view is that the practice is genuinely underdetermined, that's fine too — but then commit to that and tell me what feature of the practice, if it were different (say, a law that also punished blank ballots, or one that let you discharge the duty by mail), would settle it one way or the other.
