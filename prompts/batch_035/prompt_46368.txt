I chair the Committee on Academic Standards at a large state university, and I teach philosophy, so my colleagues expect me to be able to say what our grading and honors rules actually mean. The problem is that our current regime is an inheritance rather than a design, and its parts point in different directions. In the engineering college, core courses are curved to a mandated distribution: no more than a fixed share of any section may receive an A-range grade, regardless of the absolute quality of the work submitted — which means a student's grade depends in part on who happened to enroll in the same section that semester. Across campus, election to our senior honors society is capped at a fixed percentage of the graduating class, so in an unusually strong year we decline students whose records would have earned election in a weaker one. Meanwhile, Dean's List membership and nearly all scholarship retention rules are purely threshold-based: clear an absolute GPA cutoff and you qualify, no matter how many others also do. And our accreditation self-study states, in so many words, that our grades certify mastery of defined standards of competence rather than relative standing among peers.

The committee must decide this year whether to harmonize these rules or leave the mixture intact, and I want to understand what we are implicitly committed to before we touch anything. So: what theory of desert, if any, does each practice presuppose? Does curving presuppose that desert is comparative — that what a student deserves is a function of rank within a reference class — while threshold rules presuppose that desert attaches to absolute performance, and the accreditation language to something like genuine achievement of standards? Are these genuinely rival views, or can a single account of desert license all three at once, for instance by letting the reference class vary with the purpose of the grade (signaling to employers versus certifying competence)? Where is the system underdetermined, such that several theories of desert would accept the mixture as is, and where does it flatly presuppose something?

I would also like you to take seriously the possibility that the whole framework is rotten. If one accepts the Rawlsian thought that even the disposition to work hard is itself the product of natural endowment and family circumstance for which the student merits no credit, does anything in our system have to change — would capped honors, for example, have to become a lottery among the clearly qualified — and would that be a change of desert theory or an abandonment of desert talk altogether? And please address the reference-class problem directly, since it seems to me the sharpest issue: if a curved grade is deserved relative to a section, the same performance would merit different grades had the student enrolled elsewhere, which looks like exactly the arbitrariness that desert is supposed to exclude. Can any comparative account of desert survive that, or does the arbitrariness infect only some comparative practices?

What I need from you is a reconstruction that distinguishes what our practices strictly presuppose from what they merely sit comfortably with, an assessment of whether the mixture is coherent or has to be revised, and a statement of which parts would have to change under each of the two or three most plausible unified theories of meritocratic desert — not a defense of grading reform as such, which is a separate and more empirical fight.
