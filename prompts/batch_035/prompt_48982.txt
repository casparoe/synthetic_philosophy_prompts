I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm at work on a long-form feature about what happens to our decision-making when we honestly confront how little we can foresee. The occasion is familiar enough from my reporting: effective altruists worrying about the far future, epidemiologists weighing lockdowns against knock-on effects nobody can model, climate modellers conceding that the tail risks run in both directions. Philosophers call the underlying problem consequentialist cluelessness, and in my interviews the word keeps doing three different jobs — sometimes meaning we can't trace the causal chains, sometimes meaning we can't even assign probabilities to the outcomes, and sometimes meaning that whatever we do, history re-balances in ways that dwarf the intended effect. Before I commit to a line in the piece, I want a specification rather than a survey: what would an adequate account of how to act under cluelessness actually have to deliver? I'd like the desiderata set out one by one, with the motivating case behind each — for instance, it should presumably tell us why it can be rational to keep smallish charitable donations on a par with ordinary prudence while not licensing paralysis over every crossing of the street, and it should say what separates the situation where foreseeable good and bad effects plausibly cancel from the situation where we know only that the effects are large and unquantifiable, if those situations really do come apart.

Two further things I want from the specification, since they're where I suspect the philosophical action is. First, please flag which of the desiderata are non-negotiable and which are prejudices of one camp — I'm aware, for instance, that some philosophers treat the demand that a theory preserve the intuition that we're often obligated to act despite massive uncertainty as a bedrock requirement, while others suspect it's a hangover from ordinary ethics that ought to be discarded; I don't know how to tell genuine desiderata from inherited bias, so any principled way of separating them would be gold for the piece. Second, tell me whether you think some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — in particular whether the demands that a theory neither collapse into doing nothing, nor slide into permitting anything whatsoever on the ground that anything might be best, nor rest on probabilities that the clueless agent by hypothesis lacks, can all be honoured at once. If they can't, that itself is a result, and arguably the most newsworthy one in the field: it would mean the moral deadlock my sources keep talking around is structural rather than a failure of analytical nerve. I don't need an encyclopaedia of who has said what; I need the standard an account must meet, and where you think it breaks.
