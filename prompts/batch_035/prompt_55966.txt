For the past two years I've been the maintainer of our company's internal forecasting platform — a side project that grew into a real service around 350 engineers now use to make calibrated predictions about release dates, incident counts, and whether migrations will land before the quarter closes. It started because I was tired of sprint-planning estimates that were wrong by a factor of three, and I'd read enough about prediction markets and calibration training to think we could do better, which we mostly have. Somewhere along the way I stumbled across the term "futarchy" — the idea, associated with the economist Robin Hanson, that organizations or polities should decide on goals through normal deliberation but delegate belief-questions about how to achieve those goals to betting markets — and while I have no interest in the political-theory version, I've become convinced a modest corporate variant is worth piloting: for a defined class of prioritization decisions, we open subsidized internal markets on the outcomes conditional on each option, and commit in advance to going with whichever option the market prices as best on the metric the council itself chose.

Next month I present exactly this proposal to our monthly architecture council, and I need to be ready for the question period, which is not friendly territory. The room is about a dozen people: several staff engineers who are allergic to anything that smells like a process fad and will default to "we already have opinions, that's what tech leads are for"; two data scientists who know prediction markets, market scoring rules, and the mechanics of manipulation attacks far better than I do; a director who lost a prioritization fight last year and will be looking for ammunition; and our VP of Engineering, who genuinely likes measured things but visibly resents anything that looks like a scheme to outsource decisions she was hired to make. Nobody in the room has any philosophy background, myself included — the closest thing to a shared ethical framework is a pragmatic, roughly consequentialist "did it work in production" mentality, and any reply that leans on jargon or on appeals to how elegant the mechanism is will go over badly.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they would do if I fumble them — I expect things like market manipulation by people with a stake in the outcome, thin markets and what the subsidies will actually cost, Goodharting whatever metric we pick, insiders who know something simply declining to trade rather than moving the price, self-fulfilling or self-defeating prophecies once traders know the market drives the decision, and some version of the VP's authority question — each phrased the way a staff engineer or data scientist at this company would actually put it, with a two-minute reply I can give under pressure and an honest note on where that reply is soft or where I should concede and propose a smaller pilot instead. Also flag which objections have real force that I should acknowledge up front rather than try to beat, and include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks — I have my suspicions about what it is, but I'd rather you tell me, along with the least bad answer to it.
