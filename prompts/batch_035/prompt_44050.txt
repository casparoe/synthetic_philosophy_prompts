I'm a philosophy MA student writing the "where I stand" section of a term paper on whether consequentialism can actually guide action, and my supervisor's only written comment so far is that my commitments seem to pull against each other in ways I haven't noticed. Rather than guess where she means, I want to lay them all out and get a proper audit. For background: the paper works through the cluelessness problem, and I've ended up somewhere that feels right to me but that I can't quite make stable on paper.

Here is the package. (1) I'm an act consequentialist about the criterion of rightness: an act is wrong just in case its actual consequences are worse than those of some available alternative. (2) I think the criterion of rightness and the decision procedure come apart, and that the right decision procedure for a consequentialist is to maximize subjective expected value given one's best credences. (3) I take cluelessness to be thoroughgoing rather than a fringe worry: for most real choices, the foreseeable near-term effects are swamped by indefinitely ramifying downstream effects, and I don't think we can even assign non-fabricated probabilities over total consequences — any precise credence we offer there is a number we made up to feel decisive. (4) Despite that, I donate on the basis of measured near-term outcomes, things like malaria nets and vitamin A supplementation, and I'm genuinely confident that's better than funding my local opera house; I also think people who cite radical uncertainty as a license to fund whatever they were going to fund anyway are rationalizing. (5) I firmly reject the move, when calculation fails, of falling back on common-sense morality or deferring to settled intuitions — that strikes me as abandoning consequentialism for rule consequentialism dressed up, or as plain status quo bias. (6) I hold that a moral theory which cannot actually guide action, one that leaves every real choice in the dark, fails a basic adequacy test, because ought implies can in the epistemic sense too. (7) Finally, I'm strongly drawn to longtermist reasoning that expected value favors working on existential risks, but I refuse to accept that a fantastically tiny probability of an astronomically vast payoff should dominate my practical choices — I won't bite that bullet however the arithmetic comes out.

What I want from you is not a lecture on consequentialism but a consistency audit of this set, since I honestly can't tell which parts are load-bearing and which are just habits. Go through the commitments pairwise and tell me which are in genuine tension, which only look like they conflict and dissolve on closer inspection, and how severe each real tension is. For each genuine one, say what would have to give and what abandoning that commitment would cost me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether there's a stable position in the neighborhood I've described or whether the whole package points toward something I haven't listed. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and be specific about which commitment you think is doing the most damage — I have my own suspicions, but I'd rather not prime you with them.
