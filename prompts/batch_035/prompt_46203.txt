I've been rereading Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, the 1874 essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and I keep wanting to run a concrete case through his tripartite scheme, because I suspect the scheme is both more illuminating and less adjudicating than it first appears. The case I have in mind is the post-2015 wave of debates over Confederate monuments in American cities — removals in New Orleans and Richmond, the fights over what to do with Stone Mountain, the "keep it but add context" compromise that several cities settled on. It's the rare public controversy where the participants themselves talk like Nietzsche without knowing it: one side defends the statues as heritage and local memory, one side demands that a community destroy or at least demote what it can no longer honor, and both sides conspicuously tell stories about heroic ancestors for present purposes — the lost-cause nostalgia on one side, the civil-rights lineage on the other. That looks superficially like a clean instance of antiquarian history versus critical history, with monumental history running on both sides at once.

But I want a disciplined mapping, not a clever one. So, first, lay out what each of the three modes actually is in the essay — what need of life each serves, and what characteristic distortion or injustice Nietzsche attaches to each (his claim that critical history is itself always a kind of cruelty toward the past, since we can only destroy the past from the surplus of a new strength and can never judge it cleanly from the outside; the claim that antiquarian piety cherishes what is small and local but degenerates into mist when it tries to see everything historically; the claim that monumental history draws motivating analogies across eras at the price of falsifying difference). Then map the actors onto the modes: are the preservationists really antiquarians, or are they monumentalists whose antiquarian rhetoric is doing monumental work? Is removal genuinely critical history in Nietzsche's sense — a living generation judging and, where it must, burying the past — or is it closer to a competing monumentalism that wants different heroes on the pedestals? And where do the intermediate policies fit: contextualizing plaques, recasting statues as artifact rather than honor (the pedestal emptied, the figure kept), museum relocation of the sort I've seen at the Budapest statue park, where the Soviet-era monuments were gathered into one curated place? Is museumification a stable synthesis of the three modes, or does Nietzsche's essay give us reason to think such syntheses are unstable?

Second, and this is the part I actually care about: does the essay's governing norm — history is justified only insofar as it serves life — adjudicate the dispute at all? Both sides claim their relation to the past serves life: one side civic continuity and reconciliation, the other the vitality of a community that cannot act while honoring what it cannot honor. I have my suspicions that the criterion is doing no work here, or worse, that Nietzsche's particular conception of life — the life of the rare individual and of a culture that produces such individuals — quietly rules out the egalitarian premise of the whole debate, in which case the essay isn't neutral between the camps but indifferent to the question everyone is fighting about. So tell me where the schema explains the controversy well, where it misdescribes it (is anything in the essay about communities at all, or only about individuals?), where it is simply silent, and whether the episode should count as a confirmation of Nietzsche's typology, an anomaly for it, or a case it never anticipated. I don't need an answer about the monuments themselves — I need to know how much analytic mileage the framework honestly has.
