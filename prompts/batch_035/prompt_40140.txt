I'm a novelist, and last week's dinner party gave me better material than anything I've managed to write this year. Two of my friends — Maya, who's been vegan forever and gives what she can to animal-welfare groups, and Theo, who's devout and gives to a crisis pregnancy center — got into it over something one of them had read about called "moral trade." Maya floated it as a plan: she'd give $500 a year to Theo's pregnancy center and Theo would give $500 a year to her animal charities. Here's roughly how it went. (1) Maya called it an obvious win-win: by her lights the animals get money they'd never see otherwise, and by his lights the center does. (2) Theo said it's a wash — the same thousand dollars gets donated either way, you've just switched the names on the checks. (3) Maya said that's only true if he was donating to the center anyway, and he wasn't, his budget's tight — so each of them is converting a non-donor into a donor for their cause, and by both of their own moral standards the deal beats no deal. (4) Theo said fine, but it's still off — it amounts to paying him to fund something he believes is wrong, his conscience isn't for sale, and he said there's a word for buying people off like that. (5) Maya said nobody's conscience is being sold, because by his own views the trade makes the world better than no trade, so refusing means fewer of the things he thinks matter get funded — all he's protecting is the way it looks. (6) Theo came back that this is where she's missing something real: trading works for preferences because satisfaction is all there is to them, but moral beliefs are supposed to be beliefs — he might be wrong about abortion, sure, but he acts on his best guess, and his best guess says his marginal $500 does more good at the center, so that's where it goes, full stop; knowingly settling for what you believe is second-best isn't integrity, it's being bought. (7) Maya's last move was the one I honestly lost the thread on: she said whoever's right about the underlying question — her or him — the trade still produces more of the right thing than no trade, so it's not even about exchanging moral views, it's just each of them getting more of what one of them is right about. Theo just said she was treating the possibility that he's wrong as if it were a bargaining position. I want to use this fight in the book almost verbatim, but I need to know who actually comes out on top. Can you go through the seven moves, say who's right on each specific point, and tell me what (if anything) both of them are missing? And please be honest about whether move 7 is as clever as Maya clearly thought it was.
