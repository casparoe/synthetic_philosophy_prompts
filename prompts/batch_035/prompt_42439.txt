I'm a second-year law student taking a seminar on the philosophy of death (weirdest elective I've picked, no regrests), and I've gotten slightly obsessed with an asymmetry in tort law that I want to write my paper on. If you injure me, I can recover for everything you did to me: the pain, the disability, and — at least in lots of jurisdictions — the loss of enjoyment of life itself. If you kill me, the only claims that survive are the ones I accrued before dying. My estate can sue for my pain and suffering in the hospital, my medical bills, my lost wages up to the moment of death, and then it stops. The harm of being dead — the lost decades, the experiences I would have had — isn't part of my claim at all. Wrongful death statutes hand a claim to my family instead, and it's their loss that gets compensated: companionship, guidance, funeral costs. So the single worst thing you can do to me is, legally speaking, the one thing I have no claim for. Meanwhile the criminal law treats intentional killing as the gravest offense of all, far above assaults that leave the victim in permanent pain, which suggests the law does take death to be an enormous harm *to the victim*. I can't tell if these pieces add up to a theory or just to institutional drift.

I want to argue in my paper that this pattern quietly presupposes an answer to the Epicurean question — whether death can be bad for the person who dies — and I genuinely don't know which answer. Help me reconstruct the implicit view: does the structure of survival actions plus wrongful death fit better with a deprivation view (death harms you by taking away goods you would otherwise have had), with something Epicurean (death isn't bad for the dead person, so there's nothing to compensate them *for*, and it's only the survivors' loss that's a real loss), or is the whole thing just a pragmatic mess about standing — dead plaintiffs can't hold rights or feel compensated — dressed up as a philosophical position? I'd like you to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, say where it's underdetermined enough that several theories of the badness of death would license the same rules, and tell me what damages rules would look like if a legislature took the deprivation view seriously and let the estate recover for the lost years. And please tell me whether the criminal law point really cuts against the tort pattern, or whether punishing murder more harshly than maiming is compatible with the tort rules once you notice that punishment and compensation might be answering different questions.

Since I have to defend this to a professor who pokes at everything: be upfront about how confident you are in each step of the reconstruction, and flag the single claim in your answer you'd be least sure of — I'd rather know where the soft spot is before she finds it. Also, if the practice as I've described it is off somewhere — say, in how survival damages actually work, or whether some places do let estates recover for lost years — correct me on the structure, but keep it at the level of doctrine a philosophy paper can use rather than case citations, I'll do that part myself.
