I write as a retired experimental physicist: for thirty years I worked in low-temperature physics, measuring heat capacities and thermal conductivities at millikelvin temperatures, and thereby learning in my bones how nature charges for every movement of heat. In retirement I have taken up the philosophy of physics, and I have become captivated by the long argument over Maxwell's intelligent doorkeeper — not least because it smuggles a question that looks like engineering into a question that looks like metaphysics: whether information is a physical thing, with a physical price.

I would like you to write a piece of satire on my behalf, and I ask you to approach it as follows.

First, take on the role of a professional satirist who also holds a doctorate in statistical mechanics — someone for whom the Szilárd engine, Landauer's principle, and Charles Bennett's resolution of the paradox are working tools rather than pieces of trivia. Before writing anything, think carefully and set down a brief brainstorm: which features of the actual history are absurd enough to be worth exaggerating, and which popular misconceptions you must be careful not to bake into the jokes. Only after that brainstorm should you draft the piece.

The piece itself should be a termination letter and severance agreement, roughly 700 words, written in the flat, euphemistic register of a corporate human-resources department. The employer is Universal Carnot Utilities, a venerable heat-engine concern; the employee being dismissed is the Demon, recruited in the 1860s on a physicist's recommendation to stand at a tiny door and sort fast molecules from slow. The occasion for dismissal: the company has finally run a proper audit, and under the accounting introduced by Rolf Landauer — every bit of memory the Demon must erase to do his job costs at least kT ln 2 in heat dumped to the surroundings — his celebrated sorting operation has been running at a loss for over a century without anyone noticing.

The letter should include numbered clauses along these lines: an audit report, citing Leo Szilárd's one-molecule engine as the internal study that first flagged the problem; a severance paid at exactly kT ln 2 per bit of memory surrendered, arranged so that the Demon leaves the company precisely as solvent as he found it, a result the letter should present as uncommon generosity; a requirement that he return his registers in a blank state, with the cost of that blanking quietly charged against his severance — this is the central joke, since the erasure is the very point at which the second law finally collects its fee; and an attached formal grievance, filed by the Demon's union representative, one C. H. Bennett, arguing that the accounting is sloppy because reversible operations need not dissipate anything at all, and that the company has simply chosen to book the loss wherever it was most convenient. The company's written reply to the grievance should be magisterial, and wrong in a precise, identifiable way.

A few constraints I ask you to observe. The physics inside the jokes must be correct: the Demon is never to be shown actually beating the second law, because the humor lies in the fact that he never can, and in the way the reason keeps migrating — first the measurement was supposed to be what paid, then the erasure — so that the company's accountants are always one insight behind the theorem. A reader who knows the Szilárd–Landauer–Bennett literature should laugh rather than wince; a reader who knows none of it should still enjoy the letter as a portrait of any firm that belatedly discovers a revered old employee has been quietly unprofitable. The satire has two targets: the corporate genre of the dignified, dishonest termination; and the persistent popular temptation to treat information as a free resource — as if observing, remembering, and forgetting cost nothing. Please keep the register deadpan throughout; no winking at the reader.

Finally, after the letter, append a short paragraph — three or four sentences — assessing whether the satire is fair to the physics, and whether it dramatizes anything about the status of information that the standard textbook treatments leave inert. That last question is what carried me from the laboratory into philosophy, and I confess it is what I most want to see addressed.
