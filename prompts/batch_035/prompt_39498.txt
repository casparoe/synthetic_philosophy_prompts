I teach a small undergrad seminar on coordination failures and collective action, and next week I want to spend the first half hour on something fun: Dr. Seuss's The Butter Battle Book as a case study in Moloch / multipolar traps, the stuff from the Scott Alexander essay (races to the bottom, everyone trapped by incentives nobody chose, lose the whole values-space to Moloch, etc.). The book is obviously about the arms race, but I want a real reading of it through the multipolar-trap framework, not just "Seuss predicted nuclear war."

Here's the tension I want the reading to dig into. On one hand the book is a nearly perfect dramatization of the trap: the Yooks and the Zooks are divided by something trivially arbitrary (which side the bread is buttered on), the wall and the escalation follow from that arbitrary difference, every weapons upgrade on one side forces the other to match, and each individual step in the arms race looks locally rational to the people taking it. Nobody has to be a monster for the whole thing to converge on two old men standing on a wall each holding a bomb. That final page, with the standoff and the "we'll see" ending aimed at a child, is doing a lot of work.

But on the other hand I think the book kind of resists the Moloch reading, and I want you to take that seriously rather than paper over it. Seuss's explanation for all this is vanity, herd-mindedness, and childish pettiness — his narrators seem like fools, and the implicit story is that the arms race is folly, a failure of character or sanity. Whereas the whole point of the multipolar-trap analysis is that it doesn't need foolish agents: everyone can be perfectly rational and the outcome is still catastrophic, because the incentives do the work. So does the book actually dramatize a multipolar trap, or does it dramatize a moralized version of one where the problem is that the participants are ridiculous? Is the arbitrariness of the butter difference doing the same work the game theorist's payoff matrix does, or something different?

A few specific things I'd like covered: (1) where the book genuinely adds something the game-theory discussion doesn't — I'm thinking the arbitrariness of the initial difference is felt in a way no payoff matrix makes you feel it, and the fact that the fable is told by a grandpa to a grandson makes the intergenerational stakes literal, but tell me if you see more; (2) whether the unresolved ending is a cop-out or actually the honest ending — Schelling-style deterrence depends on a willingness that can never be tested without ending the world, and a picture book that can't resolve the standoff might be the only truthful way to end it; and (3) where the analogy strains — e.g. the arms race in the book seems driven more by prestige and one-upmanship than by any actual security dilemma, so maybe it's a better picture of a status race or a tragedy of the commons than of a true multipolar trap, and the seminar should notice that.

Dont reduce the book to an illustration of the essay, and dont twist the essay to fit the book. If Seuss is actually doing something different from Moloch-world, say so plainly and tell me how to frame it for the students. Point me to which scenes/beats to actually discuss in the half hour, since I obviously can't do the whole thing. And close with your honest take: what does the fable understand about escalation that the rational-choice analysis misses, or is that feeling of understanding an illusion the pictures create?
