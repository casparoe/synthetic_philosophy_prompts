I facilitate a small death-and-dying discussion group that grew out of my volunteering at a hospice, and the question of whether death is bad for the one who dies has recently stopped being academic for me: three months ago I was diagnosed with a progressive cardiomyopathy that will very likely be fatal within a decade. I've noticed that our group's discussions keep collapsing because people use the same labels to mean different things — someone says they "agree with Epicurus" but then grants that dying tonight would deprive them of everything they were looking forward to, and someone else says death is obviously bad without being able to say for whom or when. I'd like a decision tree that sorts participants by what they actually believe about the cases, so we can argue about substance rather than nomenclature.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names. The branching questions should be ones a thoughtful participant with no philosophical training can answer without knowing where their answers lead — things like: whether an instant, painless, unanticipated death tonight involves anything bad happening *to you* as opposed to merely being bad for the people who love you; whether an indefinite but reversible suspension of all experience (no deprivation of any particular good, just no goods and no harms) would be bad for the person suspended; whether the eons of nonexistence before your birth are a comparable misfortune to the eons after your death, and if not, what breaks the symmetry; whether, for something to be bad *for you*, there must be some time at which it is bad for you (before, during, or after your death) or whether that demand can be resisted; and whether the badness of death depends on whether an unending continuation of your life, of the sort you could actually have, would be good for you (the long-lived-opera-singer worry about immortality and boredom). The leaves should include at least the following positions, plus whatever else the space generates: the orthodox Epicurean view that death is nothing to the one who dies; the view that death is bad only for survivors; deprivation accounts of the comparative badness of death; desire-based variants on which the badness consists in desires of yours that are permanently thwarted; responses to the timing problem on which death is bad before it occurs or at no time in particular; conditional views on which death is bad for you only if further life would have been good for you; and views on which the finitude death imposes is partly constitutive of, rather than merely a limit on, life's value. Aim for eight to twelve leaves. Where the tree cannot genuinely separate two positions — say, two variants of deprivationism that agree on every case you can construct — flag that rather than inventing a fake branch, and note any combination of answers nobody seems to occupy, with a sentence on whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Please deliver the tree as structured data so I can hand it to a colleague to render as a handout: a single JSON object with fields "nodes", "leaves", "inseparable_pairs", and "unoccupied". Each element of "nodes" should have the fields "id", "question" (the branching question in plain language), "separates" (one sentence on which positions genuinely come apart here), "yes_next" and "no_next" (ids of the next node or leaf), and "yes_label" and "no_label" (a short plain-language gloss of what each answer commits one to). Each element of "leaves" should have "id", "position_name" (a standard label where one exists, otherwise "unnamed: <short description>"), "one_line_summary", "awkward_commitment" (the consequence a defender most has to swallow), and "telltale" (the answer pattern that routes a participant here). "inseparable_pairs" should be an array of objects with "positions" and "why", and "unoccupied" an array of objects with "combination" and "diagnosis". I'll fill in citations myself, so none are needed — but don't invent labels for positions that don't have them.
