I'm in an upper-level ethics seminar on utilitarianism (15 students, one 75-minute session) and I have to run next week's activity. I want it to make the class feel the force of the separateness of persons objection rather than just hear about it — and also to feel why the utilitarian doesn't think it's obviously fatal. My idea: students should first endorse, for a single life, exactly the kind of trade-offs utilitarianism makes between lives, and only then discover that they balk when the very same arithmetic crosses persons.

Here's roughly the arc I'm imagining, which you should refine rather than obey: the group designs a life-plan for one fictional person — deciding, with concrete numbers, how much of her youth to sacrifice for her old age, whether to undergo a painful procedure for decades of later benefit, that sort of thing. Then, without warning, the same numbers get reassigned: the costs fall on one group of people and the benefits on a different group, alive at the same time. The facilitator should be neutral throughout; the discomfort has to be produced by the students' own earlier commitments, not by anything said out loud. Afterwards, the debrief should introduce the objection properly, and also stage the standard utilitarian counter — that "one person across time" is itself just a aggregation of experiential states held together by memory and anticipation, so if temporal trade-offs are fine, the burden is on the objector to say what cross-person trade-offs lack — letting students locate exactly where they think the disanalogy bites.

Please give me the full design: concrete materials and numbers (indexed utilities or comparable welfare units, no real money), a round-by-round timeline, the scripted switchover between the intra-personal and inter-personal versions, and a debrief script that maps specific moments in the activity onto parts of the objection and the reply. Include what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the philosophical dispute no classroom activity can reproduce and I'll have to supply by talking. Keep the whole thing under 600 words.
