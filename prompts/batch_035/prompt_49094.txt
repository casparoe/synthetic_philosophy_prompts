In my bioethics course we have spent three weeks on the doing/allowing distinction, and by now my classmate Marta is something of a local institution: she issues verdicts on hypotheticals instantly, never revises, and so far no case anyone has brought to section has knocked her off her position. Our study group has taken to collecting her rulings the way lawyers collect precedents. Here is the record so far, each case as precisely as I can state it:

1. A runaway trolley is heading toward five people. She could flip a switch diverting it onto a side track, where it will kill one. Verdict: permissible.
2. Same trolley; the only way to stop it is to shove a heavy stranger off a footbridge into its path. Verdict: impermissible.
3. Same as 2, but with no shoving: a lever she controls opens a trapdoor beneath the same man, dropping him in front of the trolley, which his body stops. Verdict: impermissible.
4. Same as 1, except the side track loops around and rejoins the main line just short of the five, so the diversion saves them only if the one's body stops the trolley. Verdict: impermissible.
5. A torrent of floodwater is surging toward five people. She can open a sluice that channels it into a runoff channel where one person is standing and will drown. Verdict: permissible.
6. She wakes up attached to an ailing violinist whose circulation is filtering her blood; unplugging him kills him. Verdict: permissible to unplug.
7. She could drug a healthy camper and harvest his organs to save five dying patients. Verdict: impermissible.
8. She can switch off the ventilator of a patient who will die within days regardless, in order to reassign the machine to another patient who will otherwise die. Verdict: permissible.

Two new cases have come up, and I want to know what her precedents commit her to before I spring them on her at section.

Case A (mine, adapted from a homework problem): a runaway trolley is heading toward five people and this time cannot be diverted. The only way to save them is to release a second trolley parked on an adjacent siding; it will roll down, sideswipe the runaway at a junction, and derail it. On its way to the junction it will inevitably strike and kill a stranded track worker, although his death contributes nothing to stopping the runaway — the collision does that — and he was in no danger otherwise. She would be setting a new moving mass in motion that foreseeably kills someone.

Case B (my housemate's): a slow cloud of toxic gas is drifting toward five people at one end of a rail yard and will kill all of them. The only intervention available is to switch on a large fan that will push the cloud toward the far end of the yard, where one person is standing and will die instead. The gas is the pre-existing threat; the fan is a force she creates.

What I would like from you:

First, lay out the candidate rules that fit her track record — I suspect more than one does, and I'd like to know which of her rulings does the work of eliminating which famous rule, since some of the obvious candidates seem to me to die on specific items above. Second, predict her verdict on Case A and Case B under each surviving rule, and tell me which prediction you would actually bet on and why. Third, describe the single further case I should put to her that would most cleanly separate the surviving candidates — describe it fully enough to run, because I intend to.

I should say that I care less about which answer is morally correct than about the rule she is implicitly running. But two cautions: if you think I've described either new case too loosely for any verdict to be determinate — the worker's escape options, the mechanics of the fan, whatever — say so plainly and tell me what detail you'd need rather than guessing. And if you think her rulings cannot be captured by any rule that isn't hopelessly ad hoc, tell me that directly instead of manufacturing a fit.
