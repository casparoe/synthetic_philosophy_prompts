Retired biochemist here, spending my pension on economic history podcasts and the philosophy shelf. Something keeps nagging at me about the old Weber thesis -- the one where Calvinist anxiety about salvation made people work hard, save, and stop enjoying themselves, and that ascetic habit of accumulation eventually hardened into capitalism. People still fight about it, and I've noticed the pro-Weber side almost always leans on a picture nobody spells out: ideas as germs. The doctrine spreads from preacher to congregation to children, it infects behavior, and the behavior has downstream consequences the infected never intended -- smallpox didn't mean to kill the Aztecs either, it just rode along. My wife, a retired epidemiologist, uses the same move when people talk about behaviors spreading through friend groups.

The anti-Weber side says that's not a real cause: if you ask why the idea took hold in Holland but not in Catholic France, you end up citing ports, wool prices, guild structures, whatever -- so the material conditions were doing the work all along, and the idea is at best a middleman.

So audit the germ analogy for me, because it's doing alot of load-bearing work and nobody checks it. What maps onto what -- pathogen, host, route of transmission, incubation period, virulence, immunity, and which part is the fever (the behavior? the capital?) -- and where does the mapping break in ways that actually matter for whether Weber's causal claim stands? I can already see cracks: you don't catch a cold because it suits your interests, but you often adopt a belief because it does; germs don't get edited and "improved" every time they pass to a new host the way doctrines do; and a pathogen isn't itself the kind of thing you still owe a further causal explanation of, while a value system apparently is -- which seems to be the whole fight. But I want someone to sort out which of those disanalogies is fatal and which is cosmetic.

For contrast: my own instinct, after forty years with enzymes, is that culture is less like a pathogen and more like a catalyst -- it supplies no energy of its own, it just lowers the activation barriers for some social forms rather than others and channels reactions that were thermodynamically possible all along. If that's the better picture, does the causal thesis survive in a weaker but still real form, or does the regress objection kill the germ version and leave the catalyst doing no causal work either? Give me the correspondence table for both analogies, the places each quietly imports something the target lacks, and a verdict on whether the "culture can be a cause" claim survives once the disanalogies are on the table. And if neither of my analogies is any good, what's the right one? Don't just tell me whether Weber was right -- I want to know what kind of cause, if any, a value is.
