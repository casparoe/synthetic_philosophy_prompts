I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a column on the question of how old nations really are. A new popular history of nationalism I've been sent, in its opening chapter, sums up the classic "modernist" position as follows:

"Two of the most influential historians of the twentieth century, Benedict Anderson and Eric Hobsbawm, showed that nations are fabrications. Hobsbawm demonstrated that the traditions nations cherish — anthems, costumes, supposedly ancient ceremonies — were largely invented in the nineteenth century by ruling elites and then imposed on populations hungry for a past, while Anderson proved that nations themselves are a kind of collective hallucination: communities too large for their members ever to meet, held together by nothing more than the printing press. Both men drew the same conclusion: because nations are constructed rather than natural, they are fraudulent, and like all frauds they will be exposed and dissolve as people become better educated."

I'm broadly sympathetic to the modernist picture myself, but the final sentences make me uneasy — the slide from "constructed" to "fraudulent," and the confident prediction at the end. Before I build a column on this paragraph, I'd like an accuracy audit. Please go through it claim by claim: which parts would a historian of nationalism accept, which are conflations or overstatements (I notice "invented" and "imagined" seem to be treated as if they meant the same thing — should they be?), and does the conclusion about nations being doomed to dissolve follow from anything these two authors actually argued?
