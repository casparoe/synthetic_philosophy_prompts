Two philosophers have spent a decade arguing about whether death is bad for the person who dies. The first holds an Epicurean position: death cannot be a misfortune for its subject, because a harm requires a subject for whom things go badly, and death removes that very subject; moreover, where death is, the one who died is not. The second is a deprivationist: death is bad for its subject when, and in proportion to, the extent to which the life it cut off was better than what the person's future would otherwise have contained; the badness of death is the absence of further goods, not the presence of anything.

Draft the joint statement that a well-run adversarial collaboration between these two should produce. The document should contain the following parts:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. These should include at least: that death is not bad for its subject in the way pain or humiliation are bad, since it has no phenomenal character; that the question is about the subject of the misfortune, not about the badness of a world containing a death; that the mirror-image symmetry argument (our nonexistence before birth compared with our nonexistence after death) is at least initially puzzling for the deprivationist and cannot be waved away; and that the "timing problem" — pinning down when death is bad for the one who dies, if it is — is a genuine difficulty that neither side has dissolved.

2. A disagreements table stating their residual differences in wording each side would accept for its own view. Pay special attention to the ambiguity in "bad for": whether it means that a life containing the death is worse, on the whole, than the relevant counterfactual continuation (the comparative reading), or that some state of affairs is bad for, or a misfortune of, the person (the predicative reading). State explicitly which parts of the residual disagreement survive once this ambiguity is resolved, and which parts turn out to have been verbal.

3. A cruxes section. Each crux should be a subquestion such that settling it would move at least one party, and for each crux you should record an argument, thought experiment, or test case that both sides agree bears on it, along with each side's prediction about it. Suitable material includes: a person who would have lived exactly one further blissful day; a person whose continuation would have contained a hundred years of steadily worsening misery; whether harms require a bearer who exists when the harm obtains; and whether posthumous events, such as the betrayal of a promise made to the dead, can harm the person who died.

4. A closing stipulation both parties accept: that the entire statement is framed within eternalism, the view that all times are equally real, since this makes the deprivationist's counterfactual comparisons most defensible. Note in a final paragraph, in wording both would sign, which of the cruxes each side thinks would shift if the background view were presentism instead, and whether either side believes the metaphysics of time is load-bearing for its position or merely convenient.

Aim for formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted: an outside reader should come away knowing exactly what would settle this dispute, and why it has not been settled yet.
