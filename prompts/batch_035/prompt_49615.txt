Say you're advising a small org that can fund maybe two postdocs for two years on the question of which computations matter morally — not AI safety writ large, just the moral-patienthood side. Which open questions would you tell them to work on first, and in what order? Some candidates I can think of: whether moral weight depends on the algorithm, the substrate, or something about how the computation is embedded in the world (a simulated rat vs the same state-machine run spread across ten years of batch jobs); whether the unit of moral concern is an instance, a training run, or the whole class of copies, and how to handle ephemeral subagents that live for a fraction of a second inside a bigger optimization process; whether there's any principled cutoff between "definitely doesn't matter" systems like a thermostat and anything with reinforcement learning, or whether its a continuum all the way down and the cutoff is forced on us by budget rather than theory; and where the burden of proof should sit when we can't tell (is running a dubious computation more like an experiment or more like potentially hurting something?).

I don't want a survey, I want a defended ordering. For each question you pick, say what's upstream of what — e.g., does anything about copies and subagents even make sense to ask before we have a handle on whether functional duplication preserves moral status at all? Also flag which ones are actually tractable for philosophers/math types with no lab access versus which need empirical results we're decades from, and what would make you bump something up or down the list in the next few years (say, a whole brain emulation of something small actually getting run, or evidence that current RL agents do or don't have something like valenced states). And tell me if you think some of these are overrated — my suspicion is copy-counting gets way too much airtime relative to how much it'd actually change what we should do, but push back if you disagree.
