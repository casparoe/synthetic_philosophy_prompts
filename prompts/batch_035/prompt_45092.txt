I'm a philosopher who teaches a social science seminar, and for about a decade now I've been arguing with Dana, a colleague in economic history, about whether culture can be a cause. She never argues back, exactly — she just issues verdicts on proposed explanations, like a one-woman court. Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it: Weber's original claim, that thisworldly asceticism among Puritans helped cause capitalist development in seventeenth-century Holland and England, she accepts — it's her favorite example of a real cultural cause. But she rejects the companion claim from Weber's China book, that Confucian values blocked rational capitalism there, even though that's her hero's own view. She also rejects Banfield's amoral familism story for the Mezzogiorno, and she rejects the popular claim that postwar Japanese corporate culture — lifetime loyalty, work devotion — caused the high-growth miracle. She almost never gives reasons, though she mutters things occasionally: on the Italy case she once said "cause or consequence?", and on Japan she said that if you show her a loyal worker she'll show you a seniority pay scale. On different days those remarks seem to point in different directions, which is part of why I can never predict her.

The new case came up because my PhD student wants to write a thesis chapter arguing that Quaker culture caused the Quakers' wildly disproportionate commercial success in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Britain — the iron industry, banking in particular (think Barclays, Lloyds), and later confectionery (Cadbury, Rowntree). The student's mechanism is the one you'd expect: plain dealing, fixed prices, refusal to haggle, honesty in credit — values that came straight out of religion, decades or centuries before the commercial payoff, and which, incidentally, carried real costs at first, since refusing to swear oaths kept Quakers out of universities and public office. On the surface this looks like the Puritan case again, her one clear yes, so my first instinct is that she'll say yes. But I have a suspicion the verdicts she'd accept rest on more than one possible rule, and that the Quaker case, unlike the ones she's already ruled on, is exactly where those rules come apart — because if you look closely, the honesty only did commercial work in a world of weak contract enforcement, where being a visibly honest Quaker functioned like a brand, and where the Quaker meetings could discipline members who cheated. That channel looks less like conscience and more like an enforcement institution with a religious logo.

So here's what I want from you. Lay out the candidate rules that fit Dana's record on the four settled cases — I'd like at least two, and I suspect there are three — and say for each what it predicts about the Quaker case and why it fits the earlier verdicts just as well as its rivals. Then give me your best guess at her verdict, with your reasons for preferring that reading over the others, and tell me honestly whether the closest analog among her precedents is really the Puritan case or whether that's a trap. Finally, propose the further case — some other cultural explanation she hasn't ruled on — that would best discriminate between the rules you've identified, and say what each rule predicts there. One formatting request: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; just continuous prose, the way you'd write a long email to a colleague. It doesn't need to be short, and typos are fine, but please don't invent quotations from Weber or anyone else — paraphrase in your own words if you need to bring him in.
