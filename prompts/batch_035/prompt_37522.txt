I teach a 300-level epistemology course and I'm writing the final. My students keep treating pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism as two names for one view, and the standard secondary summaries let them get away with it, so I want one essay question they can't answer by reciting labels. Build it around a pair of cases I'll specify here: in the first, the subject's practical stakes go way up while the conversational setting of the people attributing knowledge stays fixed; in the second, the attributors' setting shifts (a casual chat vs. a deposition, say) while the subject's stakes don't change at all. The question should force them to say, for each case, what a contextualist, an interest-relative invariantist, and a classical invariantist each predict about third-party attributions and about two attributors who disagree with each others' verdicts — first-person assertions are exactly where the views blur together, so keep the focus off of those. Then write a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme worth 20 marks total, where the A/B line is whether the student can say cleanly what happens when the subject's context and the attributor's context come apart — that's the exact spot where the label-memorizers merge the two views, so make the scheme hit it hard, and add a one-line note on how students typically mess up each must-hit point. One practical thing: our exam software chokes on formatting, so write the question, the model answer, and the scheme all as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
