My graduate seminar on rational choice this term has been animated by a running quarrel between a decision theorist of the expected-utility orthodox persuasion and a committed convert to what its proponents call ergodicity economics, and the two have spent the semester talking past each other about the Kelly criterion and what is now sometimes called geometric rationality. The former holds that risk attitudes belong in the curvature of a utility function over wealth, so that the Kelly criterion is simply what logarithmic utility prescribes — a device known since Bernoulli, repurposed by Kelly for a gambling-on-a-noisy-channel setting, and long since domesticated by expected-utility theory as one special case among many. The latter holds that for multiplicatively compounding processes the ordinary expectation is an ensemble average across parallel possible worlds, that a single agent lives along just one trajectory, and that maximizing the time-average growth rate — which amounts to maximizing the expected logarithm — is what rationality consists in, with no utility function required; on this view expected-utility theory accommodates the same behavior only by fitting a concave curve after the fact. Before the semester closes I intend to convene them for a proper adversarial collaboration, and I want the joint statement they should come out with. Draft it for me with the following structure. First, a numbered section of claims both sides could sign: about the mathematics (that maximizing expected log wealth maximizes the almost-sure long-run growth rate; that staking one's entire fortune on a repeated favorable-but-volatile flip leads to ruin almost surely; that in the standard repeated-gamble setting the two frameworks prescribe the same bets), about what each framework was originally invented to explain, and about the respects in which each has since been stretched. Second, a table of the residual disagreements, each stated twice — once in wording the expected-utility theorist would accept as a fair statement of her own view, once in wording the ergodicity proponent would accept as a fair statement of his. I have in mind at least the unfalsifiability charge, which cuts both ways: is logarithmic utility a free parameter fitted post hoc to whatever behavior is observed, or is the other side's choice of the wealth observable, together with the assumption of multiplicative dynamics, an equally free parameter doing the same work in reverse? And does their agreement on prescriptions show that the dispute is only about representation, or can two theories that always agree about bets still disagree about something that matters? Third, the cruxes — the subquestions such that settling one would move at least one side — and, for each, a case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. The cases I would expect to see include the boundary between multiplicative and additive dynamics (a salaried bettor risking fixed sums out of income rather than out of a compounding bankroll; a syndicate that pools many independent draws and thereby lets one decision effectively ensemble-average; ordinary insurance, where the ergodicity camp says pooling converts a non-ergodic gamble into an ergodic one and the utility camp says risk aversion was doing that work all along), and settings where resets, replacement, or inheritance blur the single-trajectory picture the time-average view depends on. Fourth, a closing verdict on which residual disagreements are substantive and which are merely verbal — in particular, whether the charge that geometric rationality has merely rediscovered Kelly and Bernoulli is a dispute about historical priority, about the source of normative authority, or about nothing at all. The drafting is the hard part: every agreed claim must be one a reasonable member of each camp would actually sign, every statement of disagreement must be non-slanted in both directions, and the verbal/substantive classification must be argued rather than asserted.
