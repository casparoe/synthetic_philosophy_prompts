For the last meeting of my seminar on personal identity, which ends with Dennett's picture of the self as a center of narrative gravity — a posit rather than a thing in the head, an abstractum like a center of mass, introduced to organize the many narratives a brain is always spinning rather than located inside them the way a walnut is located in its shell — I'd like a sestina to close the term.

I want the form to carry the content. The six end-words should be six ordinary, concrete words from a single life — objects, places, days of the week — with nothing abstract among them: neither "self" nor "mind" nor "story" may appear as an end-word. They recur in the traditional rotation through all six stanzas, so that each stanza is a further draft of the same life: the same materials re-narrated in a new order, with no stanza established as the canonical telling. One stanza in the middle should do the work of the analogy: the center of mass is nowhere in the body, is not a little pellet or a ghost, and yet is not nothing — it is a point we posit because the positing pays — and the self is to be found in the same way. The word "I" is forbidden in the six stanzas; the drafts are not yet anyone's. It first appears in the envoi (three lines, all six end-words distributed two to a line), and only there, and only as what the six recurring words turn out to have been organized around all along — not an extra character introduced in the final lines, but the point the stanzas already had in common.

After the poem, add a short prose note that takes a definite position rather than laying out the options. I want the poem to back the reading on which the self is real but abstract — like a center of mass, which no one has ever seen and no one doubts — rather than the reading on which the self is an illusion to be debunked; the note should say which reading the poem has committed to and why. It should also name one place where the poem takes a liberty with the ideas or leans on a contested reading — for instance, on whether a life needs narrative unity at all to be someone's life, which not everyone grants. A philosopher of mind who knows Dennett should find the poem right rather than name-dropping; the students who never warmed to him should find it a good poem anyway. The verse itself should quote nothing and cite no one.
