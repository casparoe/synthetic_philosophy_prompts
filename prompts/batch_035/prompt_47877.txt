My book club has been working through a popular book that mixes cognitive science with philosophy — I'd rather not name it, since I don't want to be unfair to the author — and one chapter is devoted to experimental philosophy. The author summarizes the Knobe effect roughly like this: "At the start of this century, a few philosophers stopped sitting in armchairs and started running surveys, and they quickly found something embarrassing: the ordinary concept of intentional action doesn't really exist. In the famous chairman case, most people say a businessman who pursues profit while knowingly wrecking the environment harmed it intentionally — but that he didn't help it intentionally when the very same program happens to help. The only thing that changed is whether the outcome was bad. So the folk concept of intention turns out to be nothing but a disguised moral verdict: saying someone did something intentionally is just a way of saying they deserve blame for it. The effect has been replicated across many cultures and even in young children, which means philosophers who spent centuries analyzing the concept of intention were analyzing an illusion, and the armchair method is finished."

I took one intro ethics course years ago, so I can't audit this myself, but I have two reasons to be suspicious. First, the conclusion that there is no stable concept of intention at all feels like it overshoots whatever the surveys showed — surely finding that moral judgments influence intentionality ascriptions is weaker than finding that the concept is nothing but blame. Second, a friend who majored in philosophy tells me the book makes a classic mistake: it conflates ascribing intention with assigning blame, and it ignores the possibility that the moral information in the vignette changes how people interpret the question being asked, rather than showing that moral appraisal is part of the concept itself. But my friend is confident about a great many things, so I'd rather have an independent check.

So here is what I'd like: go through the summary and tell me which parts a working experimental philosopher would accept as accurate, which are contested within the field, and which are misrepresentations or non sequiturs. In particular, does the chairman study (and the wave of follow-up work) actually support the claim that intentionality ascription is just blame in disguise, or are the friend's alternative explanations — pragmatic interpretations, or something about how the vignette's details are understood — genuinely live options that the book papers over? How much should I trust the claims about replication across cultures and in children? And finally, setting aside whether the author's description of the evidence is fair, does anything in the summary actually license the sweeping conclusion that the concept of intentional action is an illusion and that armchair methods are obsolete, or is that rhetoric bolted onto a much more modest finding?
