Quick bit of context, then the ask. I sit on the clinical guidelines committee for a mid-sized regional health system. Part of my job is that three or four times a year we get proposals to adopt or drop diagnostic and screening categories — things that came down from the ICD or DSM people, or things our own psychiatrists dream up. When gaming disorder landed in the ICD a few years back we had to decide whether to screen for it in adolescent well visits, and we basically argued about it for two meetings with no framework at all and then voted on vibes.

A philosopher friend of mine got me reading about Ian Hacking's looping effects — the idea that once you name a kind of person, the people classified start responding to the classification, changing themselves, reading about themselves, performing or resisting the label, and then the category itself drifts because it was tracking people who are now different. He called it "making up people," and whether or not everyone on the committee buys it, it seems obviously relevant to us: our decisions determine what gets screened, what gets a billing code, what shows up in a patient's portal and then in their self-image.

What I want from you is a procedure the committee can actually run in a 90-minute meeting when a proposal comes in. Something like an ordered list of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a "no," and where it explicitly hands off to a human because the question is one of values, not facts — probably our patient advisory board, since we have one and use it too rarely. Assume no one on the committee is a philosopher; the procedure has to carry the philosophy inside it without anyone needing to know the theory. It also has to be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat.

Then run it on three proposals, two real-ish and one hypothetical:

1. Universal screening for gaming disorder at adolescent well visits (this is basically the fight we already had, badly).
2. A proposal from two of our psychiatrists to add a workplace burnout code so we can bill employers for counselling sessions — the pitch is purely that it funds care people need.
3. A proposal to retire our adult ADHD screening instrument on the grounds that it's "saturated" — everyone self-diagnoses off TikTok now, so a positive screen means nothing.

Two things I specifically want flagged. First, one of these should come out of the procedure with an answer I won't like — I can tell it'll probably be the ADHD one, since I like that screening program — and I want you to just say so rather than sanding it down. Second, Hacking himself never seemed to think looping effects were simply bad; some people get communities, names for what's wrong with them, identities they're glad to have. So the procedure can't just treat "this category will change people" as a knockdown no, and I'd like to see where it accounts for loops that help. Finally, be blunt about what a checklist genuinely cannot do here — where two committees running the same procedure in good faith would still come out differently.

(ps — our medical director signs off on everything, and the insurers watch what codes we add, if that matters for how you design it)
