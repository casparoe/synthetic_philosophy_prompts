I'm a software engineer by training — CS degrees, no philosophy background — and I own the model-evaluation infrastructure for the fraud-detection systems at my company. I've come to suspect that our everyday measurement practices quietly assume a particular view of what probability is, and I want to know which one, and whether our assumptions are consistent. I'm not looking for a survey of the options; I want you to take a position.

Here is the workflow. Our classifiers output a score between 0 and 1 for each transaction, and we treat that score as a probability claim: a transaction scored 0.73 is supposed to carry a 73% chance of being fraudulent. To check this, we take a large holdout set of past transactions, group them by score band, and verify that roughly 73% of the transactions in the 0.7 band were actually fraudulent. We report calibration curves, expected calibration error, and Brier scores, and when a model is miscalibrated we correct it with Platt scaling or isotonic regression. Downstream, risk teams set decision thresholds on these numbers: everything above 0.9 is auto-declined, everything below 0.5 is approved, and the band in between goes to human reviewers.

Two features of this have started to bother me. First, the heterogeneous bucket problem: the transactions in the 0.7 band have nothing in common except the model's score — different merchants, countries, times of day. Saying that 73% of them were fraudulent seems to be a fact about a pile of past cases plus a sorting decision we made, not a fact about any one transaction. Second, we also calibrate per segment, not just overall, and the numbers move depending on how we segment: a 0.73 in one market gets re-checked against that market's data, and different defensible groupings assign the same transaction different calibrated probabilities. Yet the downstream decision is made per transaction — this transaction is declined because its number crossed 0.9 — and our legal team says regulators increasingly expect a justification for the individual automated decision, which we provide by citing that same number.

So my question is: what view of probability does this practice presuppose? Is the 0.73 a long-run frequency for a class of cases that we ourselves constructed, a degree of belief about one transaction, or something else entirely? And can one conception license both halves of the workflow — the calibration over constructed buckets and the per-transaction threshold decisions — or are we quietly running two incompatible conceptions and getting away with it because the buckets are large?

Please structure your answer with the following sections, in this order, with rough lengths as indicated: "What we are actually measuring" (~150 words); "The implicit theory of probability" (~400 words); "Where the workflow is consistent and where it strains" (~350 words); "My verdict" (~250 words); and "What would change under the rival view" (~200 words). In the verdict, commit to a position: tell me which conception of probability our pipeline actually needs, whether the segmentation practice and the single-decision use can be reconciled under it, and, if you think the tension is real, which half of the workflow should change and how.
