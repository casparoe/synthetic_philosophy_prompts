I'm an undergraduate enrolled in a seminar on distributive issues in bioethics, and we've spent the past two weeks on rescue cases: a triage officer with a fixed supply of blood products who can save either one patient or five, a fire crew that can reach either a single stranded climber or a family of five, and so on. The anchor text was Taurek's 1977 article "Should the Numbers Count?" in Philosophy and Public Affairs, where he considers a case involving a scarce drug that can go either to a single patient, David, or to five others, and entertains the idea of flipping a coin between the two groups rather than simply saving the five. Since then, the one-liner "the numbers don't count" has become a running refrain in our discussion sections, and last week a classmate invoked it to shut down a debate: she claimed it settles that the triage officer does wrong if she prefers the five, since even in that institutional role numbers should play no role.

I doubt the slogan means just one thing, and I'd like to be able to press her on which reading she needs for her conclusion. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that "the numbers don't count" could be expressing, ordered from the most trivial to the most radical? I'd particularly like you to consider at least the following candidates, though please add any others you find: (1) a near-tautological reading about pairwise comparison, on which no individual on either side has a claim that outweighs any individual on the other side, so there is nothing that tips the scales person-by-person; (2) a stronger anti-aggregation reading, on which there is no such thing as the sum of benefits across distinct persons, so "five deaths is worse than one death" is either false or not the kind of truth that can justify action; (3) a fairness reading, on which numbers can count in evaluating outcomes but equal concern for persons requires giving each person an equal chance of rescue, so the right move is a lottery among all six rather than saving the greater number; and (4) a permissions reading, on which numbers count in what's best but do not generate stronger duties to the many, so preferring the five is permissible but not required.

For each reading, please say whether it is plausible, whether it is substantive or nearly analytic, and — most importantly — whether it actually supports what my classmate wanted it to support: that a triage officer would act wrongly in saving the five. Two further complications I'd like your thoughts on. First, on the fairness reading: a coin flip between the one and the five seems to give each of the six individuals an unequal chance of survival, which looks like unequal concern rather than equal concern; does that reading instead mandate a flat one-in-six lottery, or is there a principled way to defend the coin flip? Second, one of my classmates floated what struck me as the most interesting reading of all: that numbers can count within a single life but never across lives, so it matters whether one person suffers five separate misfortunes rather than five people suffering one each, yet there is nothing to be said for counting misfortunes that fall on different people. Call this the aggregation-boundary reading. It seems to explain why we recoil from sacrificing one to harvest organs for five, while still permitting the triage officer to save the five. Does it actually succeed there, and does it generalize plausibly, or does it collapse into one of the other readings under pressure?

Please present the readings as a clearly numbered menu with a verdict on each, and conclude by saying which reading people who repeat the slogan in ethics seminars most likely intend, and whether that reading is the same one Taurek himself would have needed. Please write your answer in American English.
