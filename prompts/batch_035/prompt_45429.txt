I teach an introductory ethics course, and for a unit on impartiality I'd like a diagnostic quiz titled something like "what do you actually stand on care?" — for students with no philosophy background, so nothing in it can presuppose terminology. Please build it as a decision tree: each internal node is a concrete case a student can answer from intuition alone, and each leaf is a named position. The cases must be chosen to pull apart three things students routinely conflate: (i) whether favoring intimates is merely permitted, or whether ongoing relationships themselves generate obligations prior to any promise made; (ii) whether attentiveness to a particular person's needs is an epistemic skill for figuring out what a general principle requires, or a normative consideration in its own right; and (iii) whether large-scale need among strangers — famine relief, say — falls within the theory at all or is someone else's department. At a minimum, use these cases: keeping a promise to a stranger rather than helping a close friend with a minor request; helping someone you will never see again and with whom you will therefore have no ongoing relationship; being torn between two people you love whose urgent needs conflict; and persisting in care that its recipient emphatically rejects, as when an adult child takes over an aging parent's affairs over her objections. The leaves should include at least an impartialist position, a justice-first contractualist position, care ethics proper, a virtue-ethical view of partiality, and a deflationary "care is one consideration among many" option. Construct the tree from the logical space of answers first and only then locate the positions in it; flag any combination of answers nobody holds, with a sentence on whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and note explicitly any pair of positions your cases cannot cleanly separate rather than pretending the tree does.
