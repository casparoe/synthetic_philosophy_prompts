I have been listening to a popular history podcast that ran an episode on where nations come from, and at one point the presenter offered this capsule account of what she described as the two classic studies on the subject:

"Nations are a modern con, and two historians proved it in the 1980s. Benedict Anderson showed that nations are imagined communities — that is, communities that exist only in the heads of their members, with no objective existence at all. Eric Hobsbawm showed that the traditions that make a nation feel ancient, its costumes, ceremonies, and rituals, were almost all invented within the last two hundred years and deliberately passed off as old. Once you see this, the conclusion is unavoidable: national identity is a fabrication, so patriotism rests on a falsehood, and nationalism as a whole is best understood as a scam that states run on their own citizens. The older a nation claims to be, the more certainly it is lying about its age, and by exposing the con, Anderson and Hobsbawm effectively refuted nationalism as such."

I have no background in the historiography of nationalism, so I cannot locate the errors, if there are any. Something about the passage felt off — the presenter spoke as though two careful academic historians had produced a straightforward debunking, and the step from invented to fraudulent seemed suspiciously fast — but for all I know that is indeed what these authors argued. I am also fairly sure I have read that at least some famous national costumes really are only a couple of centuries old, which supports part of what she said.

Please audit the passage claim by claim. Tell me which parts a scholar of nationalism would accept, which they would treat as an acceptable simplification, and which they would reject outright. I am particularly interested in whether imagined, in Anderson's sense, means what the presenter takes it to mean; whether Hobsbawm's thesis about invented tradition is really that nearly all national traditions are recent fabrications, or something narrower; and whether either author drew anything like the normative conclusion that nationalism was thereby refuted, as opposed to something more historical or descriptive. Then tell me which of the presenter's own conclusions, if any, survive once her claims are corrected.
