I am drafting, for a graduate reading group on animal minds, a specification of what any adequate theory of the distribution of consciousness across the animal kingdom must deliver — before we turn to the actual contenders (behavioral or reflex-based criteria, neural-substrate theories in the style of integrated-information or global-workspace views, biological-function accounts, and higher-order theories). The standard of adequacy itself is the deliverable; the scorecard comes later and elsewhere.

By a theory of distribution I mean a principle that takes a description of an organism — its nervous system, its behavior, its evolutionary history — and yields answers to three questions: whether it is phenomenally conscious at all, in what degree if degrees are possible, and of what kinds. I would like the requirements list built around the following cases and worries, though you may add others you think genuinely belong:

1. The octopus, with roughly two-thirds of its neurons in the arms and no centralization comparable to the vertebrate brain — a test of whether the theory can handle decentralized and distributed nervous systems.
2. Convergent evolution: birds achieve complex cognition with a pallium organized on a fundamentally different plan than the mammalian neocortex — a test of whether any criterion tied to a particular neural architecture smuggles in mammalian chauvinism.
3. C. elegans, with exactly 302 neurons and a fully mapped connectome — a test of whether the theory yields any verdict at all in the one case where nearly everything structural is known, or whether it turns out to be empty precisely where neural information is maximal.
4. Decapod crustaceans and insects, where the behavioral evidence is mixed — trade-offs between injury and valuable resources, analgesic self-administration in fish, play-like behavior in bumblebees — a test of how the theory weights behavior relative to architecture.
5. The boundary with nociception: the theory must keep the question of phenomenal feel apart both from the mere presence of nociceptors and withdrawal reflexes and from moral status, and say which of these separations it is drawing.

Beyond the cases, I want the list to include — or argue against including — requirements of roughly this shape: that the theory be applicable in principle from third-person evidence alone, since no animal can report; that it state whether consciousness admits of degrees or is threshold-like, and what follows for organisms near the threshold; that it not use phylogenetic proximity to humans as even a tacit tiebreaker; and that it extend in a principled way to organisms unlike any we have observed, since a theory that only interpolates among familiar animals is a field guide, not a theory.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, mark it non-negotiable or negotiable, and say honestly which marks reflect the prejudices of a particular camp rather than genuine demands of adequacy. Then two analyses I most want: identify any subset of the requirements that is jointly unsatisfiable — if the full set cannot be met, say which requirement should be dropped and what it costs to drop it — and identify the single requirement that excludes the most contenders among the four theory families above, with the argument for why it does.

One constraint on framing: the purpose of the theory, for us, is practical — deciding where welfare and research protections should extend. Negotiability judgments should therefore be made relative to that purpose and not to the purpose of solving the mind–body problem itself. A theory that is silent on the metaphysics but usable for boundary-drawing is acceptable to us; the reverse is not.
