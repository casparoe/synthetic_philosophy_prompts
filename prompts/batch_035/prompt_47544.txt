I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the mid-2000s, which means I have watched the quantum-suicide thread get relaunched roughly every eighteen months, usually by someone newly convinced by many-worlds and freshly confident that expected-utility theory can simply be set aside when the agent is doing the expecting. Most versions of the thread collapse into cranks talking past Bayesians, and I would like something better than that.

Please write a dialogue, around 900 to 1,000 words, between two characters:

1. A proponent of quantum immortality who is not a crank. She understands the difference between counting branches and weighting them by measure; she knows why the Born statistics are what keep many-worlds empirically respectable; and she still argues that an agent arranging her own future should assign negligible weight, from the first-person standpoint, to branches in which there is no future self to do the weighting. Her position should be developed with real arguments: the dead branches have no decision-theoretic claim on her; the symmetry with anthropic reasoning (you never find yourself among the observers who never became observers); and the thought that conditioning on continued existence is not an error but the whole point of practical rationality.

2. A critic who is a decision theorist and, importantly, is also sympathetic to many-worlds. He thinks she has made an anthropic bookkeeping mistake, not a metaphysical one. He should press the strong objections: that equal treatment of surviving branches, once cashed out, commits her to evaluating all gambles by branch count, which she plainly does not do elsewhere; that the same weighting by measure which makes many-worlds reproduce observed statistics is precisely what she is discarding when it becomes inconvenient; that quantum immortality guarantees survival only in the thinnest sense, since the surviving branches include the ones where she endures with brain damage, chronic pain, or a stroke that leaves her aware but not articulate; and that a policy of ignoring dead branches cannot be made consistent with how she advises her friends.

The concrete case they are arguing over: she has posted to a long-running forum a proposal she calls deferred-exit cryonics. Rather than paying for cryonic suspension, she would install, in old age, a device that painlessly ends her life if and only if she is diagnosed with progressive dementia, on the theory that in every branch in which her future matters to her, she remains lucid, so the scheme dominates suspension: no fees, no dependence on a storage company, and no waking up in a world she does not recognise. The critic is trying to talk her out of wiring it up before a firmware deadline she has set for herself. He is not arguing for cryonics on the merits; he is arguing that her scheme rests on a mistake about whose experiences count.

Both characters should be equally competent. Neither should be a strawman, and they must respond to each other's actual points: when he presses the branch-weighting objection, she should answer it on its merits rather than repeating her mantra; when she presses the no-claim-of-the-dead argument, he should have something better in reply than the gambit that she is simply irrational. At least one of them should concede something over the course of the exchange, and the dialogue should end unresolved in a way that leaves the reader genuinely unsure which of them is right, while still clear that the proponent's naive version of the scheme has been damaged and a more careful version is what survives, if anything does.

Give them names and let the setting be a video call recorded for the forum, with her occasionally checking the firmware documentation, but do not let the staging crowd out the argument.

After the dialogue, please append a structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- proponent_name (string): her name as used in the dialogue.
- critic_name (string): his name as used in the dialogue.
- opening_positions (object with two string fields, proponent and critic): one sentence each, stating where they start.
- strongest_move_each (object with two string fields, proponent and critic): the single best argument each of them makes.
- concessions (array of strings): every point at which a character gives ground, phrased as what was conceded and by whom.
- unresolved (array of strings): the questions the dialogue leaves open, stated so that each could in principle be settled by further argument.
- reader_takeaway (string): one sentence on what a reader who knows the anthropics literature should conclude.

Nothing in the dialogue or the summary should rely on citations, bibliographies, or quotations; the piece should stand on its own argument.
