My wife Mara died three weeks ago, after nine years with early-onset Alzheimer's. She taught philosophy of mind in our department for twenty-six years; I'm the ethics guy down the hall, so for all that time she used me as her test audience and complained I kept changing the subject to blame. Anyway, she was a completely sincere and very public friend of the extended mind thesis -- Otto and his notebook in every intro lecture, the parity principle on the final, the whole bit. And here's the part I've avoided thinking about for nine years: for the last five of them, I basically *was* her notebook. I answered questions for her, carried the stories, told her who was on the phone, finished her sentances until there weren't sentence-starts left. We built routines that functioned, honestly, as memory. The question of whether that means her mind extended into me -- on her own theory, the one she defended at conferences for two decades -- is exactly the kind of question she would have relished, and I can't get near it without the grief knocking me over. So I'm outsourcing it, sort of.

I'm giving the eulogy. The memorial is at the university chapel, and the room splits badly: half is our colleagues from philosophy and cog sci, who will catch every allusion and knew her work, and half is her family, who spent nine years watching what they understandably call the loss of her mind, and who will hear any claim that she was "still all there" as a cruel comfort. I can't do the conventional thing -- she didn't believe in an afterlife, so the usual consolations were, as she would have put it, not available. But I also can't stand up and lecture a room of grieving siblings about coupled cognitive systems.

So please write the eulogy, and make real choices -- don't lay out options and hand them back to me. In particular I want you to commit on the central one: either invoke her thesis openly, including what it implies about what she lost and what she never lost, and about what exactly it means that I'm the one now holding all the memories; or keep it as a structural undertone and let the shape of the speech carry it. Pick one and defend the choice in a sentence or two after the speech. Then, in that same closing note, tell me plainly where the eulogy leans on a contested reading rather than a safe one -- the parity-style stuff about notebooks and phones is respectable enough, but the claim that one person's memory can live in another person, let alone that it survives there after death, is not something Clark and Chalmers ever signed up for, and I want to know which sentences rest on that stretch and why I should stand behind them anyway. Five to seven minutes spoken. And she was funny -- she would have hated a solemn one, so it needs at least one real laugh.
