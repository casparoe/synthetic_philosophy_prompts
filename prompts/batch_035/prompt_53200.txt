Here is my opening move:

I don't think you get to be a moderate about this, and that's your problem. Your argument doesn't just show that creating a child is morally risky, the way any act affecting another person is. It purports to show that a life with even one episode of mild pain is worse than no life at all, because that pain's absence would have been a genuine good while the lost pleasures are not a genuine bad. But that first clause does all the work, and it's the one nobody can independently verify. Consider: we have a person who exists, and we can ask whether their life goes well or badly for them. There is no person in the never-existing case. So when you call the absence of pain there 'good', the good you're pointing to has no subject — no one it's good for — and no actual world in which it's realized; it's good only by comparison with a possible person's suffering. And if it's only comparatively good, then symmetrical reasoning makes the absence of pleasure comparatively bad, and your asymmetry collapses into a flat assertion that pain's absence counts and pleasure's absence doesn't. Either your 'good' is non-comparative, in which case you owe me an account of welfare goods with no bearer, or it's comparative, in which case you've begged the question. Which is it?
