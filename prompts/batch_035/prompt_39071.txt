I teach an applied ethics seminar, and every year the nonidentity problem eats a week: someone proposes a person-affecting restriction, someone proposes impersonal values, someone says the moral intuitions in play are confused, and we never get to the question I actually want the students to confront first — namely, what would even count as a solution. I am drafting the framing handout for that week, and I would like you to write the core of it: the desiderata.

Fix the standard of adequacy relative to a concrete purpose: a solution good enough to guide real institutions — health ministries deliberating about genetic counseling, environmental agencies setting decades-long emissions policy — and not merely good enough to systematize the intuitions of philosophers.

Then give the requirements, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. The list should include at least the following, though you may add or reformulate:

- It must save the judgment that the reckless procreator — the teenager who chooses to conceive now, knowing the child will have a predictably worse life than any child she could have later, though still a life worth living — acts wrongly, without identifying any person who is worse off than they otherwise would have been; or else it must explain, clearly and without hand-waving, why that judgment deserves to be abandoned.
- It must respect what we might call the preference fact: the resulting child, once adult, can rationally endorse the choice that was made, since the alternative open to the parents was, from that child's point of view, never existing at all.
- It must extend to the aggregate cases, in which no single decision fixes who will exist — a sustained carbon policy shapes identities across generations — without the wrong evaporating the moment the collective act is decomposed into individually harmless contributions.
- It must deliver usable guidance under moral uncertainty: an agency that is unsure whether impersonal reasons even exist should still be told what to do, rather than being told to wait for metaethics to settle.
- It must not purchase the solution with commitments that independently generate worse problems. If the impersonal principle that handles the teenager also entails the Repugnant Conclusion, the handout has to say so plainly.

For each requirement, mark whether it is non-negotiable or reflects the prejudices of a camp — person-affecting theorists will reject parts of my list, totalists other parts — and say which camp, so the students can see where the philosophy ends and the faction begins. Finally, identify the pair of desiderata you believe cannot be jointly satisfied; that tension is what the seminar discussion is meant to build toward. Do not score the existing proposed solutions against the list — the students will do that in class, and I want them to do it with the criteria they have just watched you struggle over.
