THE PLANNER'S NEW BRAIN (working title, bad, I know)

When Mises published his calculation essay in 1920, the argument everyone remembers him making is: without private property in the means of production, there are no genuine prices for capital goods, and without genuine prices there is no rational way to compare the cost of alternative production plans. Socialism therefore cannot allocate, it can only grope. Lange's famous reply, and the whole "market socialist" family that grew out of it, conceded the point about prices and then proposed to keep them: the state owns the firms, but firms buy and sell at market prices, and a central board adjusts the price of consumer goods by trial and error until supply meets demand, just Walras's auctioneer with a Politburo letterhead.

I want to argue that both sides spent decades fighting over the wrong half of the problem. The interesting claim was never about whether prices exist under socialism. It was Hayek's, and it's an epistemological claim dressed as an economic one: the knowledge that prices aggregate is dispersed, unarticulated, partly inarticulable ("knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place" is how he puts it, though I'm paraphrasing, not quoting -- check this later), and it therefore cannot be fed into any calculation, because it never exists in a form that can be fed. The planner is not bad at arithmetic. The planner is missing his inputs, and no improvement in hardware fixes a missing input.

Now, here's the move I want to make and I'm not sure it's honest. There's a line of argument, going back before Mises but sharpened since, that says: fine, the full optimization problem is monstrous, but nobody needs the optimum. Corporations internally plan economies the size of mid-tier nations and don't collapse; why can't the whole economy be one corporation? Hayek's answer to that (and Lange's, oddly, from the other side) is that the firm only works because it floats in a sea of market prices it can read off rather than compute. The firm is a local planner, parasitic on a global market. That's the real "knowledge" argument, I think, and it's much better than the incentives stuff people usually teach.

So here's my two-halves split. Half one: the aggregation claim. Prices compress distributed information into single numbers, and this compression is what makes decentralized coordination possible at all. Half two: the inarticulability claim. Some of the knowledge never gets compressed even by markets -- it's tacit, embodied, in the heads of people who couldn't write it down, and it's the source of genuine novelty, so any system that can only process what's written down is parasitic on the unwritten.

My thesis: modern ML puts real pressure on half one and none on half two. On half one -- and this is where the essay gets its hook -- a language model is a machine for compressing dispersed, unarticulated, time-and-place knowledge into usable form. It is trained on exactly the sediment of what people know but never formally stated: forum posts, bug reports, the way a mechanic describes a noise, the way a supplier complains about a part. The claim "the planner can never get his inputs" is an empirical claim about what inputs exist in collectible form, and it's a claim that gets weaker basically every year. Hayek, writing in 1945, could assume that if a piece of knowledge wasn't in a book or a ledger, it was functionally nowhere. That assumption now looks like a period detail.

On half two, though, I think Hayek holds. There's a difference between interpolating over the space of things people have already written down and generating the new thing that gets written down next. The first is a planner. The second is an entrepreneur. And the essay's other half -- I've only got notes here -- is that the standard debate runs these two together constantly: people slide from "the planner can't compute the optimum" (increasingly false) to "the planner can't produce the new" (unaffected), and back, as needed. Cockshott and Cottrell made the computational point decades ago, that with linear programming and modern hardware the input-output problem is tractable at scale, and everyone yawned, and I think the reason they got ignored is that the yawners were answering the entrepreneurship question while the arguers were making the computation one. That's the confusion I want to name and kill.

Where I think I'm probably wrong, and would like your honest read: (a) is the interpolation/generation distinction doing more work than it can bear, given that we can't actually define it very well? (b) I may be strawmanning Hayek's 1945 essay by treating it as purely informational when there's a motivation/incentive strand in it I'm not engaging. (c) The whole thing risks being a hot take about LLMs stapled to a history-of-thought paper, and if that's what it is, better I find out now.
