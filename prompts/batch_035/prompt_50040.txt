Quick bit of background on my job first, because it's the whole reason I'm asking. I work at a local benefits office and one of the things I have to decide is whether two claimants count as "a couple" for payment purposes — the guidance says things like whether they live together "as husband and wife" or are in a relationship "akin to" marriage, and it stays exactly that vague all the way down. Real examples from my desk: two people who keep separate flats but stay at one of them five nights a week, do the grocery shopping together, and everyone including their families treats them as a couple. Another pair are both on the lease, split every bill to the penny, but haven't been romantic in two years and never got around to formalising a split. We get maybe fifteen of these a week, I have about twenty minutes per case, every decision moves a few hundred pounds a month for someone, and because they can appeal, I have to write down reasons that will survive an appeal panel reading them cold. At the moment it's basically vibes plus whatever we did on the last case that looked similar, which I'm not proud of.

I ended up down a rabbit hole on this after a colleague mentioned the heap paradox (take grains away one at a time and no single grain seems to make the difference, and yet). Since then I've been chewing on two ideas and honestly can't pick. Idea one: in every one of my cases there IS a fact of the matter — there's some last fact that tips it from not-a-couple to couple, and the rule picks it out, it's just that no human could ever know where that line sits. We're ignorant, not the rule. Idea two: the rule itself is just blurry, and any precise version of it (counting nights, looking at shared finances, asking about intentions for the future) is just one legitimate way of sharpening it, none of them the "real" rule — so a call I make is only really solid when every sensible sharpening gives the same answer, and the cases where they disagree are, in some sense, calls I'm making rather than discoveries. Both of these feel right to me on different days and I can't tell if that's because they genuinely disagree about what I should do at my desk or because they're just two moods.

So here's the ask: write me a decision procedure my team could actually run in twenty minutes. Give me the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which cases the procedure settles firmly and which it should punt to a supervisor because what's left is a values judgement, not a fact. Then run it on three cases: the separate-flats couple, the lease-but-cold pair, and one obvious flatmate case everyone agrees on — I want the last one because I'm curious what the procedure says about a case that's only one step back from the borderline. And please be straight with me about which of my two ideas, if either, actually changes anything at the desk — if the honest answer is that "there's an unknowable fact of the matter" and "it's true only when every sharpening agrees" lead to the same checkboxes, say so. Also include at least one case where the procedure's answer annoys me — I'm braced for the criterion I like (shared finances) to lose to one I don't, and I'd rather see that coming than have it happen on a live case. Finally tell me what the procedure can't do at all — where an appeal panel is going to see through it no matter how well I follow it.
