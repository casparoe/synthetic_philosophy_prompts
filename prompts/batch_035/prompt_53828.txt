In my philosophy of death reading group we have spent two terms on the Epicurean argument, and we have reached the peculiar situation where everyone can recite every move on both boards and nobody has budged an inch. The dialectic as we run it: the Epicurean holds that death cannot be bad for the person who dies, because a misfortune must be located somewhere — some time at which things are going badly for its subject — and while you exist your death has not yet occurred, while once it has occurred there is no subject left to bear the badness. The deprivationist replies that death is bad for you comparatively: it deprives you of the goods a continued life would have contained, and a harm need not be an event that happens to you at a time; it can be a loss constituted by the absence of further goods. To this the Epicurean side returns three standing objections, each of which our deprivationists answers in a standing way: the timing problem (if death is bad for you by deprivation, when is it bad for you — before you die, when you do not yet lack those goods, or after, when there is no you?), answered variously by holding that the misfortune is bad for you timelessly, or bad for you during your life in virtue of what it will cost you, or that the demand for a time is a confused demand in the first place; the Lucretian symmetry (being deprived of future goods by dying young seems no worse than being deprived of past goods by being born late, yet nobody counts a late birth as a misfortune), answered by appeals to the metaphysics of persistence, to the rational asymmetry between our attitudes toward past and future, or by biting the bullet; and the charge that deprivationism owes an account of the for in bad-for — what makes something bad for a subject who is in no state at all.

My working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than endorsed, is that the impasse is not a substantive disagreement about death at all but a standoff over the logical grammar of the predicate bad-for: the Epicurean is operating with an event-requiring reading on which every misfortune must be datable from the subject's point of view, the deprivationist with a counterfactual-comparative reading on which it need not be, and each side hears the other as committing an obvious error rather than adopting a different stipulation. On this hypothesis the debate should dissolve, or at least relocate, once the two readings are separated explicitly; and what continues to look like argument about death would really be negotiation over which reading better deserves the phrase — which would make the disagreement partly verbal, though perhaps not merely verbal, since one could argue that the event-requiring reading is itself grounded in something substantive about what welfare is.

I would like a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the positions. Please tell me: (1) whether my hypothesis is right in whole, in part, or wrong — and if only in part, say exactly where it breaks; (2) if the disagreement is verbal, what the two sides are really disagreeing about underneath the words — is it, for instance, a substantive disagreement about the temporality of welfare, or about whether value is subject-relative at all, with the death case merely its sharpest test; (3) if it is substantive, identify the load-bearing premise on each side and say whether any of those premises can be argued for without assuming the conclusion; and (4) what, concretely, would count as progress — a distinction, a case, a formal model of welfare both sides could accept — and whether anything could actually end the debate, or whether it should be expected to persist, and why.

Most importantly for me: state explicitly what would change your mind about your diagnosis — which argument or consideration, if it emerged from either side, would move you to a different verdict — and identify the step in your reasoning where you think you are most likely to have gone wrong. If the evidence strikes you as genuinely balanced between two diagnoses, say so rather than manufacturing confidence, and tell me what further information would tip the balance.
