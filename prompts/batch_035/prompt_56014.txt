For a seminar on political epistemology, I've been asked to circulate a short statement of my actual positions so the group can probe them, and I'd like an audit before I send it. Below is the set I hold. Please tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and rank the tensions by severity rather than treating all as equally bad; for each, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost. If the set fails as a whole in a way that no pairwise comparison catches, say that too.

1. On the diagnosis: I accept the distinction, now common in social epistemology, between an epistemic bubble, in which contrary voices are merely absent, and an echo chamber, in which those voices are heard but pre-emptively discredited, so that members are trained to discount outside correction before it arrives. On this basis I hold that the deeper problem in contemporary politics is not underexposure to the other side but corrupted trust in sources, and that this is why fact-checking and corrections do so little.

2. On remedies: I nonetheless believe the most promising response is structured cross-partisan deliberation — bringing committed opponents into one room under good conditions, with facilitation and equal time — since the consistent finding from deliberative forums is that participants come out rating the other side as more reasonable and more sincere than they had expected.

3. On good faith: I hold that good faith is not symmetrically distributed across the political landscape. On at least some issues, a substantial faction on one side knowingly advances claims it recognizes to be false, and I regard the reflex to model the two sides as mirror images as itself a form of false balance that misdescribes the situation.

4. On the other side's mirror move: I think it is epistemically vicious when the other side pre-emptively discounts our claims as astroturf or bad faith, and I hold, as a methodological principle I try to teach, that one must engage the strongest version of an opponent's claim before passing judgment on the arguer.

5. On the engine of polarization: I think the evidence points to identity-protective cognition rather than misinformation: people reason toward the conclusions their group identity requires, not outward from false facts — which is why supplying accurate information changes so few minds.

Does this package hang together? If some of these commitments undermine the motivation I'd ordinarily give for others, point at the exact seam, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please don't soften the reply into general lessons about the value of listening to one another; I want the specific inconsistencies, if there are any, named and ranked.
