I'm taking a seminar this term on nineteenth-century thought, and we just spent two weeks working through Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, "On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life." On the train home after our last meeting I listened to a philosophy podcast whose host gave a brisk five-minute summary of the essay, and while most of it sounded like what we'd discussed, a couple of things felt off in a way I can't quite pin down. Here is roughly what he said: "Nietzsche argues that too much history is bad for you. He distinguishes three ways of dealing with the past — monumental, antiquarian, and critical — and complains that modern Germany suffers from an excess of history: everyone is soaked in scholarly knowledge of the past, which makes people feel like belated epigones incapable of original action. He illustrates the alternative with the grazing animal, which forgets everything the moment it happens and is therefore happy, so the prescription is essentially to live the way animals do, unhistorically. By 'life,' the standard everything is supposed to serve, he means Darwinian survival and adaptation — the test of good historiography is whether it helps a people win the struggle for existence. And of the three modes, critical history is the one he endorses, since judging and condemning the past is the most scientific and objective of the three." He then drew the conclusion that the essay is basically a manifesto against historical study as such, and that Nietzsche would happily have cut history from the school curriculum.

Some of this matched my seminar notes — the three modes, the excess of history, the belatedness complaint — but I have a feeling the podcast is conflating things my instructor worked hard to keep apart, and possibly turning an ambivalent or conditional point into Nietzsche's settled recommendation. Could you audit this summary claim by claim? Please tell me which parts a careful reader of the essay would accept as accurate, which are defensible simplifications, which are genuine misreadings that the text itself warns against, and whether the host's closing conclusion follows from whatever survives the audit. I'd especially like to know whether Nietzsche really holds up the animal's forgetting as an ideal to be imitated, whether "life" in this essay can plausibly be read as Darwinian fitness, and whether he ranks the three modes at all. Please keep your whole answer under 550 words.
