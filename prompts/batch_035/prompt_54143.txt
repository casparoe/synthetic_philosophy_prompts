I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, at work on an essay about whether a sense that life is worth living is something for which there can be evidence at all — or whether belief in life's meaningfulness must forever outrun its grounds. My editor pushed me toward William James's 1895 essay Is Life Worth Living?, collected in The Will to Believe, and Other Essays in Popular Philosophy (1897), and while I can follow it sentence by sentence, I don't trust my reading of its closing move, which is the part my piece depends on. I'll give you my paraphrase of the reasoning there, stripped of James's own words, and I'd like you to tell me whether it is faithful, and then to explain its logic.

As I understand him, James argues roughly this: when a hoped-for outcome depends partly on your own sustained effort, your confidence that the outcome is attainable is itself one of the causal conditions of attaining it; therefore, if you have reached a point where abandoning hope guarantees failure, then believing that a way exists is the only remaining route to finding out whether one does — trust in an outcome can, in that position, help bring the outcome about; and so, he concludes, believing that life is worth living can be reasonable precisely because that belief will help create the fact that life is worth living.

Three questions about this. First: James seems to address the argument only to a person whose vitality is already ebbing — someone near collapse, not someone contentedly indifferent to the question of life's worth. Is that restriction doing real work in the argument, and if so, what — or is it just a rhetorical softening for a general license to believe? Second: is the closing move a justification of belief or merely a causal claim about what belief does? I can't tell whether James is entitled to pass from 'believing it will make it so' to 'you may rationally believe it', or whether he thinks the first already suffices, and I'd like the step-by-step structure that makes his entitlement (or lack of it) clear. Third: I keep catching myself reading him as offering a psychological technique — a trick for staying in the game — yet he insists he is making a philosophical point about the ethics of belief. What am I missing if I take the deflationary reading? Please note wherever my paraphrase distorts him, since I've reconstructed the passage from memory rather than quoting it.
