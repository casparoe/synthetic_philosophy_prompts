I chair the promotions and reappointment committee in a mid-sized humanities faculty, and I've run into a problem with our voting procedure that a colleague in philosophy tells me is a recognized issue in a body of work on something called judgment aggregation, though she couldn't tell me how to solve it in practice. Our university rules require the committee to submit a recorded vote on a set of named criteria for each candidate — "the research record meets the standard for the rank," "teaching meets the standard," "service meets the standard" — and then a recorded overall recommendation, and the dean's office accepts only packages in which the overall recommendation follows from the criteria votes. Last month we had a case where this fell apart. On a five-member committee (I vote only to break ties), three members judged that the research record was sufficient and three judged that teaching was sufficient, so by our rules both criteria were met — yet a different three-member majority had privately concluded that the candidate should not be promoted, and when we voted on the recommendation directly, we produced a package the dean's office rejected: criteria all met, recommendation negative. We ended up re-voting with some members switching out of embarrassment, which felt both arbitrary and unfair to the candidate. My philosopher colleague says this pattern is sometimes called the discursive dilemma and that there is apparently a theorem showing no voting rule avoids it in general, which is comforting in a way but doesn't help me run a meeting.

Here is what I need from you, and I want to be clear that I need a usable procedure, not a survey of the literature. We handle roughly fifteen candidates a year, each case must be finished within a single two-hour meeting, the votes become part of the record and can be examined in an appeal, and I cannot afford to spend more than a few minutes per candidate on procedural meta-discussion — the committee members are impatient and one has already suggested we just "vote on the bottom line and skip the criteria," which would get us in trouble with the dean's office. Please write a decision procedure I can actually follow in the chair's seat: the questions to ask and in what order; a rule for when the committee should vote premise-by-premise on the criteria versus directly on the recommendation; a way to detect the inconsistent situation before it's on the record, and a default for what to do when we detect it; and clear marking of the points where the procedure has to stop and hand the question to the committee's own judgment, because those are choices of value rather than steps of logic. Then run the procedure on three cases: a candidate where the numbers reproduce the dilemma exactly and the procedure resolves it in a direction I personally dislike; a case where a member votes against her own sincere judgment on a criterion purely to keep the package consistent, and I want to know whether the procedure should tolerate that; and a case where one criterion is borderline in the way people say "meets the standard" is a matter of degree rather than a fact. Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do — where it will misfire, and what I should say in the meeting when it does, given that I have to sign my name to the outcome either way.
