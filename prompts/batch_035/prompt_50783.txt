A colleague and I sat in on a reading group discussion that ended in a genuine stalemate, and I'm still unsure who had the better of it. The topic was Daniel Dennett's suggestion that Darwin's and Turing's strange inversions of reasoning are, at bottom, the same discovery: that competence can be generated without comprehension, and that the comprehension we instinctively posit as a cause of competence is really a late-arriving product of it. One participant, Maya, defended the sameness claim. Darwin showed how the appearance of design in living things could be produced without any designer; Turing showed how the appearance of understanding could be produced by a machine that follows formal rules while understanding nothing. In both cases, she said, competence comes first, and the intelligence we thought was needed to explain it turns out to be one of its downstream effects — comprehension is itself just a particularly rich kind of competence, so the inversion applies to minds the way it applies to eyes. The other participant, Dev, argued the two cases come apart. In Darwin's story nothing anywhere in the system comprehends; selection is mindless all the way down. But Turing's machines only run because a comprehending engineer wrote the code, so Turing didn't eliminate comprehension — he relocated it from the machine to the programmer, which is a far weaker and less interesting claim. Turing inverted a question about how we might recognize thinking; Darwin inverted the explanation of design itself. Maya replied that Dev's objection proves too much, since the same objection would indict Darwin: if you insist that every upstream source of order counts as a hidden comprehender, then the environment's role in shaping adaptations makes it a designer too, and the word has lost its point. What the sameness claim really requires, she said, is only that comprehension be erodible — and modern computing shows exactly that erosion: compilers compiling compilers, evolved and machine-learned code that no human understands, software layered until the comprehending engineer is a marginal figure in the process. Dev answered that this reply only works because Maya has quietly assumed her own theory of comprehension, identifying it with flexible predictive competence, which is precisely what a defender of the inversion needs to prove rather than stipulate; if comprehension is anything over and above competence, however hard that surplus is to specify, then Turing's machines have, so far as the argument shows, no more of it than a thermostat, and Darwin's inversion — where there genuinely was no comprehension to relocate — remains a distinct and stronger result. Neither would concede anything, and the group dispersed without a verdict. Please adjudicate their exchange round by round: say who is right about which specific points, where each argument is weak or equivocal, and whether the sameness of the two inversions can be secured by anything other than a contestable theory of what comprehension is. Please answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
