I've become pretty convinced that the Born rule problem in Everettian quantum mechanics has been basically solved by the Deutsch-Wallace decision-theoretic program, and I want to stress-test that conviction. The position, as I hold it: in a branching universe there's no room for primitive single-case chances, so asking "why is probability given by amplitude-squared?" is a confused question -- the real question is what a rational agent inside the multiverse should care about, and the answer is that the only coherent set of preferences over branching outcomes weights each branch by its squared amplitude. The Born rule drops out as a theorem of rationality rather than an extra postulate bolted onto the formalism.

So tell me what would count against this. I don't just want a list of objections critics have raised -- I want a map of the view's actual exposure. Specifically I'd like you to address: (1) the circularity charge, that the rationality axioms in the derivation (equivalence, dominance, additivity and so on) are only compelling because they've quietly built amplitude-weighting in, so the theorem is a rigged game -- what would show this charge is right or wrong, and would the Everettians themselves concede any axiom as doing suspicious work? (2) the branch-counting problem: a skeptic says a rational agent facing 100 branches should care equally about each branch, not weight by amplitude. The standard reply is that branch number isn't well-defined, branches split continuously, so counting is a non-starter. Does that reply rescue the derivation, or does it just mean the derivation wins because the alternative has been defined out of existence -- and is that a legitimate win? (3) the retreat problem: it seems like the view has moved from "this is what quantum probability IS" to "this is why you should act as if the Born rule is true" to "this is how an ideally rational agent who's already been persuaded of Everett would behave." Has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so is that damning for it or just the normal fate of positions in foundations of physics? Also worth covering: whether anything from the self-locating uncertainty approach (Sebens-Carroll style) or from rivals like Bohmian mechanics or objective collapse counts as genuine evidence for or against the decision-theoretic story, and whether there are any conceivable observations, theorems, or formal results that would count as decisive against it rather than merely awkward.

One thing I personally find persuasive, so tell me honestly if I'm overrating it: the point that a rational agent's preferences over a large number of tiny-amplitude branches have to be insensitive to how you slice up the branches, and that this more or less forces continuity, which forces the Born weights. Also, where would a committed defender of the program just dig in and refuse to treat something as a defeater at all -- and where does that refusal start to look like dogmatism rather than reasonable confidence? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Please answer in American English.
