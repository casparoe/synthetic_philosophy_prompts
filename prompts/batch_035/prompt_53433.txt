I hold a strengthened version of Dennett's real-patterns view of mental states, and I want a ranked map of what would count against it — its actual exposure, not a recitation of the standard objections.

Here is the position as I hold it. On Dennett's picture, we can adopt three stances toward a system: the physical stance (predict from its constitution plus the laws of physics), the design stance (predict from what it was designed, or selected by evolution, to do), and the intentional stance (treat it as an agent with beliefs and desires, and predict it will act rationally given them). Beliefs and desires, so conceived, are real patterns: attributing them compresses and predicts the system's behavior better than the available alternatives. My strengthening has two parts. The first is ineliminability: I claim that for systems like us, no predictor working purely from the physical stance — with unlimited computation and complete microphysical data — could match the intentional stance's accuracy per unit of description, except by implicitly reconstructing the intentional vocabulary; the pattern is in the world, not in our interests. The second is exclusivity: where the intentional stance yields a real pattern, that pattern is what the belief is — there is no deeper level at which the attribution could turn out to have been wrong all along, so cases of indeterminate or gerrymandered attribution are features of the pattern rather than gaps beneath it.

Tell me what would count against each part, ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and including developments that have arguably already occurred. Among the candidates I want evaluated: the confabulation findings, in which subjects' reports of their own reasons appear to be post-hoc inventions, and whether that shows the intentional pattern is shallower than it introspectively seems or instead vindicates it, since the observer may attribute better than the agent; the existence of systems that pass every behavioral test for intentional attribution while everything we wanted the belief to do is done by mechanisms with no place for it; domains in which the best predictors of human action are population-level variables that make no reference to what anyone believes, which would pressure the claim that the intentional pattern is the predictive one rather than merely a predictive one; and — what I would personally find most persuasive — any demonstration that the same behavioral data support incompatible but equally compressive intentional descriptions with nothing in the world deciding between them, which would strike at exclusivity directly.

Two further things. First, distinguish what a committed real-patterns theorist would concede as counting against the view from what they would dismiss, and say where that line is principled rather than defensive: Dennett's habit of replying to every objection that the pattern is real but only from a stance invites the charge that the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and I want to know whether that charge lands and, if it does, whether it is a problem for a metaphysical claim of this kind or whether stance-relativity is simply what being real amounts to here. Second, tell me what my strengthened view predicts that Dennett's original does not, so I can judge whether I have made it more falsifiable or merely more fragile.
