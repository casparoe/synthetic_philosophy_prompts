I spent most of my career as a developmental biologist before retiring, and I have lately been reading philosophy of mind as a hobby, working through the literature on functionalism and multiple realizability. I keep noticing what seems to me a strong parallel with something I know well from biology, and I would like help thinking through whether the parallel is deep or merely verbal.

In philosophy of mind, as I understand it, multiple realizability is the claim that a given mental state or kind — pain, belief, memory — can be physically instantiated in wildly different substrates: mammalian brains, octopus brains, perhaps silicon, perhaps alien biochemistry. This is standardly taken to undermine type-identity theory (the view that pain just is, say, C-fiber firing) and to support the autonomy of psychology as a special science.

In biology, we have two familiar phenomena that look, on the surface, like the same thing. One is convergent evolution: wings arose independently in insects, pterosaurs, birds, and bats, from utterly different anatomical starting points, with the same function arriving via different structures. The other is what my field calls degeneracy: within a single organism, structurally different mechanisms can stand in for one another functionally — duplicate metabolic pathways, different antibody molecules binding the same antigen, gene knockouts that are rescued by unrelated compensatory changes. Degeneracy is not just a fact about evolution across lineages; it is a design feature of robust systems, and it is usually invisible until you perturb the system and the function persists anyway.

Here is my question: should philosophers treat these two biological notions as a single analogy for multiple realizability, or as two importantly different analogies with different philosophical payloads? It seems to me that convergent evolution is the closer match to the classic thought experiments (octopuses and mammals), while degeneracy is the closer match to what cognitive scientists actually find in neural systems — distributed, redundant, plastic implementations where removing parts leaves the function intact. But I suspect the two pull in opposite directions: convergence is a claim about how independently evolved systems happen to solve the same problem, which fits multiple realizability as an argument against type identity; degeneracy, by contrast, suggests that within a given system the "same" function may be realized differently at different times or in different individuals, which is arguably a stronger and stranger claim than anything Putnam-style arguments require.

So please compare multiple realizability with convergence and degeneracy as two separate analogies. Specifically: (1) In what respects does each analogy capture, and in what respects distort, the philosopher's thesis? (2) Does the degeneracy analogy create a problem that the convergence analogy avoids — for instance, if the realizer of a mental state can shift within one organism over time, what happens to the idea that psychological kinds get their explanatory value from being realized at a coarser grain than neural ones? (3) As a scientist, I confess I find it natural to say that degeneracy shows "the function is the explanatorily real thing and the structure is implementation detail." Is that actually functionalism, or am I smuggling in an assumption philosophers would contest — for instance, about whether functions can themselves be multiply realized at yet higher levels, which threatens an infinite regress of realizer levels? (4) Finally, is there any way to make the analogy do real work rather than mere illustration — for example, could a philosopher legitimately infer from the prevalence of degeneracy in evolved systems anything about whether minds are likely to be substrate-independent in practice rather than merely in principle?

I would appreciate a careful, critical comparison rather than an endorsement of my framing; if the analogy is superficial, I would genuinely like to know where it breaks.
