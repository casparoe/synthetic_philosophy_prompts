I am a PhD student in behavior genetics working on the genetics of cognitive and personality traits, with a long-standing sideline in philosophy of science, and there is a feature of my field's everyday practice that I keep circling without being able to state its commitments plainly. I would like help making them explicit.

The practice is variance decomposition. Nearly every study in my field reports, as its headline quantitative result, a partition of phenotypic variance for some trait — educational attainment, neuroticism, height, what have you — into components: from the differing similarity of monozygotic and dizygotic twins, or from genomic relatedness among unrelated individuals, we estimate additive genetic, shared environmental, and nonshared environmental shares (the familiar ACE model), or the fraction of variance tagged by measured SNPs. These numbers come with standard errors, get compared across studies and cohorts, are tracked over historical time, and increasingly get regressed on environmental variables: the literature on whether the heritability of certain traits is lower in low-SES households is precisely this — the heritability coefficient treated as a dependent variable that environments can move. Gene-environment interactions, when found, are reported as moderators, a coefficient layered on top of main effects. And no one in the field treats these decompositions as mere descriptive bookkeeping; the numbers are used to answer causal questions, to frame policy debates, and to justify the next round of genome-wide association studies.

Everyone in my orbit knows Lewontin's fifty-year-old complaint — his 1974 argument that the analysis of variance is not the analysis of causes, since variance shares speak to the origins of differences within a particular population, not to the developmental machinery that produces the trait. The field has absorbed that complaint as a ritual caveat and gone on to build an entire research program on the numbers anyway. So the interesting question is not whether Lewontin was right; it is what the ongoing practice must presuppose for the numbers to bear the weight the field actually puts on them.

Here is what I want. Reconstruct the implicit theory of causation that routine variance decomposition presupposes. Does the practice only make sense if genetic and environmental causes are conceived as additive, roughly context-independent difference-makers whose contributions remain stable enough across contexts to be summed — and if so, what exactly is an interaction term supposed to be: a perturbation of the true contributions, or a symptom that the decomposition never tracked causes in the first place? Is the practice internally consistent: the field insists, in one breath, that heritability is a property of a population and not of the trait, and in the next treats a shift in heritability across environments as a discovery about the trait's genetics — or can these two stances be reconciled, and how? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and tell me where it is philosophically underdetermined — where rival theories of causation would license exactly the same statistical routines, so that no experiment within the practice could adjudicate between them. Finally, tell me what would concretely have to change in the day-to-day practice of my field — not in its rhetoric, but in what we compute, report, and treat as a finding — if it took on board a fully interventionist view of causation, on which a variance share is not a cause but a description of a population's structure. I am not asking for a literature survey or a reading list; I can find the references myself. I want the commitments, not the bibliography.
