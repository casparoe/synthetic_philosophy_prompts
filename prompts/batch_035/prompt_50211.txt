I'm a PhD candidate in philosophy finishing a dissertation in normative ethics, and as part of my clinical ethics training I serve as the theory consultant for a hospital's ethics consultation service. About once a month we get a case that turns on the line between harm that is intended and harm that is merely foreseen: terminal sedation for intractable suffering, opioid escalation in a dying patient, ventilator withdrawal, and once, the separation of conjoined infants in which the weaker twin's death was certain. The tempo is unforgiving — the consult team gets roughly twenty minutes with a chart and a resident, and everyone else in the room is a clinician with no philosophical training. At present, committee members invoke the doctrine of double effect by name whenever they want a frightening option to be permissible, so the phrase has come to function as little more than a certificate of good motives, and trolley-problem intuitions get cited as though they settled the intended/foreseen question rather than illustrating it.

Please write a procedure the committee can actually run. It should be an ordered checklist in language a clinician can apply to a live case, with each step keyed to what the doctrine genuinely requires: that the act not be independently wrongful, that the good effect not be produced by means of the bad one, that the bad effect, if foreseen, not be intended, and that there be a proportionally serious reason for permitting it. Do not paper over the hard part, which is operationalizing the intended/foreseen line for people who have never read the literature: specify what question to ask the clinician, what sort of answer indicates that the harm is a means rather than a side effect, and what to do when the clinician's self-report of intention is unreliable, self-serving, or simply unavailable in an emergency. Include a short battery of calibration cases, modeled on the contrasts the trolley literature is actually good at — diverting a threat versus using a person as an obstacle to it — that members can work through in training so the distinction isn't applied by feel.

Then run the procedure on four cases: high-dose opioids in a ventilated patient expected to die within days, where the dose needed for comfort may suppress respiration; withdrawal of a ventilator from a patient who will die of the underlying disease within minutes, over a family's objection that the withdrawal is the killing; the conjoined-twin separation, where the stronger twin requires the shared organ and the weaker will die on the table; and the old craniotomy-style case, in which the mechanism of the intervention is the fetal death itself, contrasted with removing a cancerous uterus in a pregnant patient knowing the fetus cannot survive — a pair the Catholic tradition separates and most secular committees do not, which makes it a genuine stress test of whether the procedure's distinctions do any work.

Two further demands. First, some philosophers have argued that the intended/foreseen distinction carries no intrinsic moral weight, and there are revisionist variants that relocate the line — for instance, between harms a person is used to produce and harms a person suffers while being acted on. Do not give me a menu of options: choose whether the procedure commits to the classical formulation, adopts the revision, or treats the distinction as a rebuttable presumption, and defend the choice, noting where it changes the verdict in the four cases. Second, mark explicitly which steps are facts the checklist can settle, which are judgment calls it cannot eliminate (proportionality above all), and what the procedure cannot do even when perfectly followed. Include at least one case where it returns a verdict the committee — including me — will dislike, and be candid about it rather than adjusting the procedure to reach a comfortable answer.
