My older brother is a palliative-care physician, and after our grandfather's family meeting — at which Grandfather, still lucid, refused a feeding tube that might have bought him several years at what the clinicians described as a quality of life just above the line — my brother argued that we had collectively chosen the worse world, and that anyone who declines life-extending treatment in such a case does the same. His argument runs as follows. (1) A period of life that is good for the person living it and bad for no one never makes an outcome worse, and makes it better if it adds to the total. (2) The added months would have been just above the line: positive, though barely. (3) There is no axiologically relevant difference between appending such a period to an existing life and composing it as a new life — in both cases the extra time is positive for whoever lives it and changes no one else's welfare. (4) So the world containing the extra months is the better one, and choosing against it requires a reason. (5) The candidate reasons — Grandfather's stated preference, the shape his life had as a whole — are reasons a person can have only by already existing, so if they cannot block the creation of new people whose lives run against preferences they never got to form, they cannot block extension either. (6) Therefore the comfort-only choice was the worse choice; by parity, so is every comparable refusal of life-prolonging treatment; every person now living well should rationally want barely-worth-living decades warehoused onto the end of it; and by the same parity anything that adds further positive periods dominates — which, he says, is just the repugnant conclusion arriving at the bedside, and he is entirely fine with that.

I am not fine with it. Step (6) seems to imply that my grandfather was mistaken about the manner of his own death, and I do not believe that; yet each of (1) through (5) sounded plausible on its own when he said it. My suspicion is that something happens to the phrase 'worth living' between (2) and (5) — as if 'positive for those months' and 'good for him' quietly trade places — but I cannot name the maneuver. Tell me which step I should reject and what rejecting it costs, both in this case and in the population-ethics setting my brother is importing it from. If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) than (3), since (1) looks like little more than 'worsen no one and count welfare when it rises,' which I need for other judgments; tell me whether that instinct is right, or whether the trouble has already entered at (1).
