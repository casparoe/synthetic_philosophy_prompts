I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of mind course, and we have just spent three weeks on the meta-problem of consciousness — the question of why we report and judge that consciousness presents an explanatory problem in the first place, i.e., what to say about the fact that normal humans, contemplating the brain, insist that something about experience seems left out of the physical story. My professor suggested that instead of another paper on the usual thought experiments, I analyze a working piece of clinical practice that quietly takes a stand on exactly the relation the meta-problem makes puzzling: the relation between conscious experience and the mechanisms by which we report it. I want to write that paper, and I'm hoping you can help me think it through.

The practice is the standard approach to detecting intraoperative awareness — the scenario in which a patient under general anesthesia receives too little anesthetic, is conscious (sometimes in pain) during surgery, and, because muscle relaxants prevent all movement, can signal nothing at the time. Anesthesiology has developed three characteristic safeguards, and the interesting thing is that they seem to presuppose different things. First, in some hospitals the anesthesiologist can use an isolated forearm technique: before the muscle relaxant is given, a blood-pressure cuff on the upper arm is inflated so the drug never reaches that arm, and during the operation the patient is periodically asked to squeeze the anesthesiologist's hand if awake. Second, many operating rooms use depth-of-anesthesia monitors — machines that process the raw EEG into a single number, with a threshold below which the patient is judged very unlikely to be conscious, no report required. Third, after every general anesthetic, patients are interviewed with a standard set of questions about the last thing they remember before losing consciousness, the first thing after waking, anything experienced in between, and any dreams — and this retrospective interview is treated as the gold standard for determining whether awareness actually occurred, with machine readings counted as merely fallible correlates.

Here is what I want to work out, and where I keep getting stuck. It looks like the practice treats a positive report as sufficient for consciousness: a squeeze during surgery, or a patient's later statement of having heard the surgeon, is taken as proof of awareness. But the practice clearly does not treat report as necessary — otherwise the EEG monitors and the whole apparatus for detecting aware-but-paralyzed patients would be pointless, since by construction such patients cannot report. So the implicit picture seems to be: report is sufficient but not necessary for consciousness. Is that combination even a coherent implicit theory, or is it just a pragmatic patchwork? A second puzzle: studies of the isolated forearm technique apparently find that some patients squeeze the hand when asked during surgery but, interviewed afterward, report no memory of the operation at all — and such patients are typically not counted as having been aware, because the field's official standard is based on recall. When the contemporaneous report and the retrospective report conflict, the practice sides with memory. Does that mean the implicit criterion of consciousness is not experience at all but rather memorable experience? And a third wrinkle: when a patient reports vague floating sensations, the interview is designed to sort these into dreams rather than awareness, a distinction drawn entirely from the character of the report itself. What does it presuppose that we can trust a subject's classification of their own episode as dream rather than awareness — that introspection is reliable, or just that there is nothing else to go on?

The questions I would like you to address, roughly in this order: Reconstruct the implicit theory of the relation between consciousness, report, memory, and neural measurement that this three-part practice presupposes, if any single theory can be reconstructed at all. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with — for instance, could a hard-problem realist, who thinks experience can outrun all report and measurement, and an illusionist or deflationist, who thinks consciousness just is whatever the reporting and monitoring mechanisms track, both endorse the practice unchanged, or would each have to revise some part of it? Where exactly is the practice philosophically underdetermined, and where, if anywhere, do its parts presuppose inconsistent things? Finally, tell me concretely what would have to change in the operating room if the implicit view were replaced by a thoroughgoing deflationism on which consciousness is exhausted by reportability or monitorability — for example, would the retrospective interview lose its gold-standard status, and would the dream-versus-awareness distinction become meaningless or instead become trivially easy?

I'd also like the analysis to connect back to the meta-problem explicitly, since that is the unit it is for. If the practice can be shown to be neutral between realism and deflationism about consciousness, does that suggest the meta-problem is in some sense a practical non-problem for medicine — something clinicians can run on either fuel? Or conversely, does the fact that the whole practice only matters because patients later report awareness as dreadful presuppose that the badness of an experience is itself something reports reliably convey — a substantive commitment about the very report-experience link whose apparent looseness is what generates the meta-problem in the first place? Please be as precise as you can about which claims are mine, which are standard in the philosophical literature, and where you think I have misdescribed the clinical practice itself.
