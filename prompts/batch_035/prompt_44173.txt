Here's the exam question (worth 25% of the final):

"Fritz Fischer's 1961 book Germany's Aims in the First World War touched off one of the great historiographical controversies of the twentieth century. Explain what Fischer actually claimed, distinguishing carefully between (a) the claim that Germany bore primary responsibility for the outbreak of war in July 1914, (b) the claim that Germany deliberately planned or sought a general war well in advance, and (c) the claim embodied in the Versailles treaty's war-guilt clause. Which of these claims did critics of Fischer think his evidence failed to support? Finally, does it make sense today to hold a nation responsible for a war a century ago — and if so, in what sense: the responsibility of a government for its policies, of a population for its support or acquiescence, or of later generations for something inherited?"

And here's the student's answer, more or less as written:

"Fischer was a German historian at Hamburg who in 1961 became the first person to work through the Imperial German foreign policy archives, which had basically been closed to researchers since 1918, and what he found in them destroyed the comfortable story that WWI was an accident that all the powers stumbled into together. His book Germany's Aims in the First World War argued that Germany deliberately instigated a world war in order to become a world power: the ruling elite, backed by pressure groups that wanted expansion in Eastern Europe and Africa, used the July Crisis after Sarajevo as an opportunity to act on plans for a war against France and Russia that would create a German-dominated Mitteleuropa and Mittelafrika. The centrepiece of his evidence was the September Programme of 1914, drafted by Bethmann Hollweg's staff, which called for massive annexations in Europe and Africa, showing that the war aims existed from the very beginning. Fischer had actually been making this case since 1959 in an article, but the book, which argued that Germany had been systematically planning and preparing the war since 1911, is what caused the scandal. The strongest evidence that the war was premeditated is the Schlieffen Plan: Germany had a detailed plan for a two-front war ready years in advance, so the decision for war clearly came long before 1914.

His critics, led by Gerhard Ritter, complained that Fischer ignored the other powers' room for manoeuvre, especially Russia's mobilisation, and that the September Programme was drafted after the war had already begun, so it shows what Germany wanted once at war, not that it wanted war in the first place. This last objection was always a bit weak since Fischer showed the leadership consciously ran the risk of a general war. Ritter fought him for years but eventually conceded the substance of Fischer's case.

On responsibility: the Versailles war-guilt clause declared Germany morally guilty for starting the war, which is essentially the same claim Fischer made, except Fischer grounded it in documents rather than victor's justice. So yes, it makes sense to hold the nation responsible — the nation is its people, and the crowds cheering in Berlin in August 1914 show the population wanted the war as much as its leaders did. That Germany no longer exists as a state doesn't matter; nations are real moral agents over and above their governments, and the cheering crowds are the proof."

That's the whole thing. Again: a grade out of 25 and the point-by-point comments, including anything I might be under- or over-crediting.
