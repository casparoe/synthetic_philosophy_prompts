I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm at work on a long-form feature about what happens to the philosophical debate over moral character when it is taken out of the laboratory and set down in the middle of history. The debate I have in mind is the situationist critique of virtue ethics: the line of work running from Hartshorne and May's honesty studies of the 1920s, through Milgram's obedience experiments and the Darley and Batson study in which seminary students hurrying to give a lecture on the Good Samaritan parable stepped around a slumped stranger, to John Doris's book-length case that behavior is governed far less by stable cross-situational traits like compassion or honesty than by seemingly minor features of the immediate situation. Virtue ethics, on that view, posits a kind of person the evidence says barely exists, and the standard philosophical replies — that traits are fine-grained, or situation-specific, or socially scaffolded — are what my piece is really about.

The episode I want to run through this machinery is the rescue work on the plateau around Le Chambon-sur-Lignon in south-central France between 1940 and 1944. In brief: Le Chambon and its neighboring villages, a few thousand people in all, were led by the pacifist Protestant pastor André Trocmé and his colleague Édouard Theis; drawing on a Huguenot memory of persecution, the community sheltered several thousand refugees, most of them Jews and many of them children housed in the plateau's boarding houses, using forged papers, warnings of raids, and escorts toward Switzerland; Vichy officials repeatedly demanded that the pastors hand over the names of those being hidden, and were refused at genuine risk, with the pastors at one point arrested and held; the great majority of those sheltered survived the war. Two features of the case strike me as doing unusual work. First, conduct was remarkably uniform across individuals in one place — which sounds situationist — and yet neighboring communities in the same region, under the same occupation and the same laws, did not do this, so the "situation" itself demands an explanation. Second, the behavior was sustained for four years across worsening and shifting conditions — roundups escalating, direct police pressure, personal danger mounting — which looks like exactly the temporal extension and cross-situational stability the situationists claim is largely absent from moral behavior.

What I'd like from you is a disciplined case analysis, not an essay on the general debate, which I know well enough. Treat the episode as a test that runs in both directions. Map the case onto the situationist framework carefully: who or what is the unit of analysis here — the individual villager, the household, or the plateau as a whole; what counts as the "situation" and what counts as the "person"; which features of the rescue the situationist apparatus genuinely explains, and where it is silent rather than merely strained. Then tell me what the case does to the theory: is the Chambonnais story an anomaly for situationism, a confirmation of it, or a category the whole debate never anticipated — cases in which the situation that produces the behavior had to be built, over decades, by something that looks uncomfortably like a community-level character trait? Where you can, give me your honest verdict rather than the balanced-sounding one, and where the framework has nothing to say, say that instead of stretching it. And if you think the episode cannot bear this weight at all — that a magazine-length framing is forcing a laboratory-sized question onto history, or that organized, life-risking rescue under occupation is simply the wrong kind of case for either side of the argument — please tell me so plainly instead of writing the tidy version I might prefer.
