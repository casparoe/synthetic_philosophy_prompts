For about three years now I have been occupied, in my spare time, with what I've learned is called the Knobe effect: the finding that ordinary people are much more inclined to say a chairman "intentionally" harmed the environment when he knowingly allowed the harm as a side effect of a profitable program than to say he "intentionally" helped the environment when he knowingly allowed a benefit as a side effect. I came across the case in a popular book on moral psychology, and it bothered me enough that I have been working out my own explanation ever since, mostly in notebooks, without any academic training — my working life was in hospital quality assurance, and I approach things through incidents, checklists, and what a reasonable person should have anticipated.

My explanation, in short: I don't believe the ordinary concept of intention is morally contaminated, as I understand some philosophers to claim. I think what's happening is an ordinary inference about thresholds of concern. Harms of the kind in the vignette almost never happen unless someone either wants them or is cutting corners; so indifference to a harm is strong evidence of intent, in the same way that a nurse ignoring a warning alarm is evidence she doesn't much care what happens to the patient, and we would say she let the patient be harmed "deliberately" in a heartbeats' argument. Benefits, by contrast, happen all the time without anyone intending them, so indifference to a benefit is weak evidence of anything. The asymmetry lives in our background expectations about what needs explaining, not in the word "intentionally" itself. My testable-sounding corollary: if you found a domain where harms routinely occur without intent — say, harms caused by clearly blameless natural forces or by machine error — the asymmetry should shrink or flip.

Next month I've been given the main slot at our library's monthly discussion group, about forty minutes of presentation and then open floor, and I want to defend this view. I need to prepare for the questions. The audience is a specific group of regulars, and they each have a characteristic way of attacking an argument. There is a retired state trial judge who thinks in terms of mens rea, jury instructions, and the difference between intent, knowledge, and negligence — he will want to know whether I've confused "he intended it" with "he can't plead ignorance." There is a social worker who reads a lot of pop psychology and will bring up hindsight bias, just-world thinking, and our eagerness to find someone to blame once a bad outcome is known. There is a retired engineering manager who wants operational definitions, will ask about sample sizes, whether the vignette wording was held constant, and whether anyone repeated the studies with other populations; he distrusts anything that smells like a survey of college students. And there is a high school English teacher who will seize on the grammar of the test question — whether asking "did he intentionally harm the environment?" already presupposes that harming is the salient description, and whether the chairman's reply ("I don't care at all about the environment") reads differently from "I don't care about the environment" with the "at all" removed.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections these particular people — not a philosophy seminar — will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to my view, each phrased roughly in that person's own vocabulary and cast of mind, with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes to a mixed lay audience and an honest note on where the reply is weakest. Where an objection deserves a concession, say what I should concede up front rather than defend. Please also include the one question I am hoping nobody asks — the objection that most threatens the account I've sketched — and what my best available answer to it is.
