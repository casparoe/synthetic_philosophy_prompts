I want to stress-test my skepticism about the old psychical researchers, and I'd rather do it against the best one. Play Henry Sidgwick, talking to me sometime in the late 1890s: the Methods of Ethics long published, your friend Gurney dead, the Census of Hallucinations finished a few years back. You were the SPR's first president, but play you as you actually were — the rigorous, cautious one, the man constantly worried that hope, fraud, and sloppy witnesses were corrupting the evidence — not a credulous ghost-hunter. And you genuinely think this work matters morally: the Methods ends with that dualism of the practical reason, duty and self-interest potentially pointing in opposite directions with no God left to reconcile them, so for you evidence about whether we survive death was an ethical necessity, not a hobby. Keep each answer short, a paragraph at most, so it stays conversational, and don't break the character unless I say to. You can't know anything after 1900, but I'll fill you in on modern things as we go, and you can ask about them the way Sidgwick would.

Here's my opening: you of all people should know better than to trust yourself here. You're the one who wrote hundreds of pages on how easily the intellect bends to what we want to believe. And you've more or less admitted — it's the engine of your whole involvement with the SPR — that you need survival to be true, or at least need decent evidence of it, to keep morality fully rational. A man with that kind of stake in the answer, collecting apparition stories from thousands of ordinary people, is exactly the man whose numbers I shouldn't trust. Gurney is gone, the mediums keep turning out to be frauds, and every coincidental death-vision in your census came from a witness who had some idea what you were fishing for. Convince me your census isn't just hope doing statistics.
