You are a professional moral philosopher with a gift for pedagogical design. I teach an advanced undergraduate seminar on moral luck, and I want a one-page decision-tree handout that starts every student at the same innocent-looking starting commitment — "a person's degree of blameworthiness can only depend on factors within their control" — and walks them, node by node, to the position on moral luck they actually hold. Before drafting, brainstorm carefully about what the genuinely load-bearing questions are; the tree must be built from the logical space, not from a memorized list of labels.

Requirements on the nodes. Each node must be a concrete case or a claim about one, phrased so a student can answer it without knowing which position it leads to. The tree must route through at least these cases, which I consider non-negotiable: (a) the equally reckless drunk drivers, one of whom kills a child and one of whom arrives home unnoticed; (b) the painter who abandons his family for his art, where the question is whether the eventual success or failure of the paintings bears on how his choice is assessed; (c) the German who emigrates in 1932 and later reflects that had he stayed he would very likely have become a Nazi functionary, a man of no worse character than himself; and (d) a person of innately vicious temperament who struggles daily to behave decently, contrasted with a naturally gentle person who behaves equally decently with no effort — the question of whether character itself is luck in a morally relevant sense. The nodes should force students to distinguish blame from regret, from admiration, and from how a life retrospectively adds up, since much of the literature's apparent disagreement trades on running these together.

Requirements on the leaves. At least five distinct end-points, including: the strict Kantian who holds the control principle without exception and accepts whatever counterintuitive results follow; the position that concedes luck pervades moral assessment so thoroughly that the everyday practice of blame is largely incoherent, and says what should replace it; the position that what one's life retrospectively amounts to can depend on luck even though blame cannot, and that this is the interesting phenomenon all along; and any principled mixed view — e.g., someone who denies that outcomes matter while conceding that circumstances or character do. Give each leaf a name, a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment, and, where a region of the logical space is unoccupied, say so and hazard a diagnosis: is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is simply unexplored?

Finally, flag honestly any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate — I suspect at least two of the positions differ mainly in tone or emphasis rather than in any case they would decide differently — and tell me where the tree would need another discriminating case to pull them apart. Keep the whole thing compact enough to hand out and work through in twenty minutes of discussion.
