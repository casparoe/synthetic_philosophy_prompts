I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mathematics, and I run the discussion section (about 18 students, 75 minutes) for an intermediate logic course that has just finished classical first-order logic and the completeness theorem. For next week, I want a one-session activity that makes mathematical constructivism feel like a live constraint rather than a museum piece: the students should run into, in their own behavior, the difference between asserting that a statement is true and being able to produce a witness or a method for it — and feel the pinch when a proof by contradiction delivers only it is not the case that no counterexample exists, with no example to point at. Ideally the rules of the activity themselves enforce a BHK-style discipline (you may assert P only when you can exhibit a construction establishing P), so that excluded middle fails in practice: the students should discover that some concrete statement — say, whether a run of a hundred consecutive 7s occurs somewhere in the decimal expansion of π, which as far as I know is open — cannot be asserted in either direction, while nearby decidable statements divide cleanly. Please design the game: rules, materials, a payoff scheme that doesn't involve real money, roles if useful, and a timeline for the session. Then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the formal notions — the witness condition, the constructive reading of disjunction and existence, and the failure of double-negation elimination — with intuitionism named only at the end. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which feature of the constructivist position no staging of this activity can reproduce and I will simply have to supply in the debrief.
