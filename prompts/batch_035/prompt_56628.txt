Last weekend I had a long argument with my friend Tom, a robotics engineer, about whether autonomous systems create genuine responsibility gaps. Then, a few days later, I recounted the whole thing to my colleague Priya, who used to practise product liability law, and a second, longer argument broke out between them in my kitchen while I mostly stayed out of it. I have been turning it over since, and I still cannot decide who is right about what.

Tom's opening position, as he restated it for Priya: the systems his firm ships into warehouses learn from operation and adapt their behaviour in ways no engineer can foresee. When one of them eventually causes a serious injury, there will be nobody who controlled the outcome, nobody who intended it, and nobody who could have foreseen the specific chain of events. Since the traditional conditions for holding someone responsible — control, intention, foreseeability — fail for every human in the loop, the harm will sit in a gap: bad things will happen and no one will be answerable for them.

Priya's reply was that this gap, if it exists, is ancient. Corporations and government bureaucracies have produced it for as long as they have existed; scholars of administration have a name for it, the problem of many hands, where everyone contributes a fragment of a decision and no one's fragment suffices for blame. Yet we never insisted on an identifiable blameworthy individual before allowing these institutions to act. We spread responsibility across designers, deployers, insurers and regulators, and victims are compensated. Autonomous systems, on her telling, are a new instance of a very old shape.

Tom said this changed the subject entirely. Liability is not responsibility. A firm writing a cheque for damages is not the same as anyone being answerable — apologising, being the fitting target of resentment, having to justify what was done. Forward-looking duties to design safer systems likewise do not fill the backward-looking gap; they say who ought to have done what before the harm, not who is on the hook after it. Distributing money is bookkeeping, he said, not the practice of holding one another responsible.

Priya answered that the presupposition doing Tom's work for him — that every significant harm must have an owner of blame — was always optional, and we quietly abandoned it long ago. We already tolerate blameless harm: acts of God, no-fault accidents, victims of disease. Nobody searches for someone to blame when lightning strikes a rambler. She even noted that Roman law made an owner answerable, in the sense of paying, for damage done by his animals and slaves — a category of answering-for without fault — and suggested we treat malfunctioning learning systems rather like weather: compensate the victims out of a pooled fund and give up on blame altogether.

Tom's counterattack: lightning does not make anyone a profit. These systems are deployed precisely because removing the human from the loop saves money, and if deployers are allowed to treat the resulting harms the way we treat weather, they get to privatise the gains and socialise the risk. Priya's fund, he said, would amount to a state subsidy for laundering blame through machine opacity — the more inscrutable you make your system, the more completely you escape answerability.

Priya's rejoinder was that the blame then lands on the decision to deploy, which was made freely and with knowledge of the statistics. You do not need to foresee the particular incident; you need only foresee the class of risk, and that is how we treat everything from pharmaceuticals to hiring decisions. The engineer who could not predict this injury in this warehouse still knew, when the system was signed off, that systems of this kind injure people at some rate.

Tom's last word was that this merely relocated the mystery, because the grain of description was now doing all the work. At the level of risk classes, virtually everything is foreseeable — even a drunk driver foresees that drink-driving causes crashes at some rate — while at the level of individual events almost nothing is. If foreseeability at the class level suffices for blame, we blame people for outcomes that were one in a million; if it does not, Priya's rejoinder collapses and the gap reopens. Neither of them moved after that, and they left it to me to referee.

Please go through their exchange move by move and tell me who is right at each step — not simply who wins overall — and say what, if anything, both of them are missing.
