I'm putting together an exercise for my first-year seminar on free will and agency, in which students audit a piece of popular science writing rather than a primary text. The paragraph below, from a popular article a colleague passed on, will be one of the test cases, and I'd like a worked model answer from you first. The paragraph reads: "When the sphex wasp drags her paralysed prey back to the burrow, she leaves it at the threshold and goes inside to check that all is well before pulling it in. Move the prey a few inches while she's inside, and she will repeat the entire routine without hesitation — and she will keep repeating it for as long as you care to keep moving the prey, never once registering the displacement. The wasp is running a fixed program; she isn't really seeing the cricket, just triggering on it. This is what Hofstadter called sphexishness, and it shows that the wasp, like all insects, has no intelligence of its own, only blind instinct. The lesson reaches further than wasps: the same kind of loop runs in us whenever we act from habit, which is why free will is an illusion — our so-called choices are just longer subroutines." Please assess this claim by claim, separating what an ethologist or philosopher of mind would accept, what they would wince at, and what they would simply reject — and tell me explicitly whether the closing inference about free will follows from the parts of the paragraph that survive scrutiny. I'd also like you to state how confident you are in each of your main judgements and to single out the point you are least sure of. After your analysis, append a JSON object with the following schema: "overall_verdict" (a string: "fair", "mostly fair", "mostly unfair", or "unfair"), "claim_assessments" (an array of objects, each with "claim" — a short paraphrase of one claim from the paragraph — "verdict" — "accept", "wince", or "reject" — and "comment" — one or two sentences of justification), "free_will_inference" (a string stating whether the final inference follows and why), "confidence" (a string summarising your overall confidence), and "least_sure_point" (a string identifying the judgement you are least certain of).
