My flatmate came back from a value theory seminar with this distinction between "right kind" and "wrong kind" of reasons, and now she issues verdicts on whether some consideration is a *genuine* reason for an attitude — by which she seems to mean a reason that counts in favour of the attitude itself, rather than a reason to just engineer the attitude into existence because having it would suit you. Her track record so far: a 90% rain forecast is a genuine reason to beleive it'll rain tomorrow; that believing your side will win would keep morale up is not a genuine reason to believe they'll win; a friend's kindness is a genuine reason to admire her; that a dealer will pay you handsomely to admire a truly dreadful portrait is not a genuine reason to admire it; the bear's growling and its size are genuine reasons to fear it; that a demon will torture you unless you come to desire your rival's flourishing is not a genuin reason to desire it; that you actually dropped the ball on the group project is a genuine reason to feel guilty; and that feeling ashamed of your body would nudge you to exercise is not a genuine reason to feel the shame. She's remarkably consistant, but I can't for the life of me work out the rule she's implicitly running — or whether there are several rules that would fit her record just as well, and she mumbles something about the fitting-attitudes view of value being in trouble if it can't sort out which kind of reasons make for value, which I honestly don't follow.
