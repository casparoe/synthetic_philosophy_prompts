I teach an upper-level seminar on ethical theory — 15 students, one 90-minute session — and I want a classroom activity that makes the demandingness objection to consequentialism something they feel rather than read. The design I have in mind: students are given a pool of the seminar's real discretionary budget (a few hundred dollars, actual money I would genuinely spend on the end-of-term party) and a one-page price list from an effective charity, with bednets at a stated cost per net and a lives-affected figure, plus a nonlinearity thrown in — a matched-funding threshold and a quantity discount — so that marginal returns are not flat and students have to reason about where the curve bends. They first allocate privately, then in small committees, and then each committee's cutoff is audited in front of the room by a rotating auditor whose only permitted move is asking some version of why stop there, backed by the marginal item on the price list at the margin they chose. I want them to hit, themselves and in this order: the initial comfortable cutoff, the discovery that every defense of a cutoff appeals to something the price list cannot see (the party matters to us, we're not saints, the money is ours), and the nagging sense that none of those defenses would convince someone actually under the net. Please design the full activity — rules, materials, roles, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and how the matched-funding wrinkle is supposed to complicate the obvious every-dollar-saves-a-life reasoning — plus a debrief that maps the positions students will actually have taken onto the standard responses (satisficing, threshold views, agent-centered prerogatives, integrity objections, the reply that costs to the agent count in the calculus too), without lecturing: I'd rather the debrief consist of questions that make students name their own stopping place. Also tell me the two or three things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect diffusion of responsibility once the committees form, at least one student going full saint, and someone challenging the reality of the price list to escape the stakes — and how to handle each. Finally, be honest about which feature of demandingness the activity cannot reproduce: my sense is that the objection's real force is diachronic — the demands recur at every choice for a whole life — and a one-off allocation decision, however staged, can't deliver that. If you see a way to gesture at the repetition (carryover rules, a 'next term' card, anything workable), include it, but tell me plainly if it's only a gesture.
