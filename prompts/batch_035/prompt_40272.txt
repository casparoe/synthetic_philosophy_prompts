You are an expert in value theory, the logic of preference, and practical decision analysis. Think carefully and systematically before you write; begin by brainstorming the different ways pairwise "better than" verdicts can fail to fit together before you settle on your procedure.

I am the editor of a publication that reviews consumer products. Our bread and butter is the head-to-head verdict: we test two products and publish a sentence of the form "A is better than B for most buyers." Each comparison is written by one tester in about an hour, and I sign off on every verdict. Recently a reader wrote in pointing out that across three of our running-shoe comparisons we had declared model A better than B, B better than C, and C better than A — with the same criteria cited each time. When I asked the testers involved to defend the cycle, each individually insisted their verdict was right. This made me realize we have no framework at all, and I suspect the problem is not sloppiness but something philosophers of value have worried about for a long time: whether "better than" is even transitive, and whether it is exhaustive — whether any two comparable options must be better, worse, or exactly equal, or whether there is room for a verdict of "on a par," neither better nor worse nor equal.

Please write a decision procedure my testers can actually run on a proposed head-to-head comparison. It should specify, in order: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, when the procedure licenses the verdict "A is better than B," when it forces us to publish something other than a winner (a tie, a parity verdict, or a "different buyers should pick differently" split), and where the procedure stops and hands the decision to me because the question is one of values rather than facts. The procedure must grapple with three specific hazards. First, chained small differences: testers often judge that a one-gram difference in weight or a barely perceptible difference in cushioning makes no difference to the verdict, but a chain of such differences can lead from a verdict any tester would endorse to one nobody would. Decide explicitly how the procedure should treat imperceptible or negligible differences, and be honest about the cost of each option — treating them as real differences risks false precision, treating them as no difference risks absurd conclusions. Second, parity: when two products are dissimilar in strengths and weaknesses such that neither is better but they are not a tie either, the procedure needs a way to say so that is still useful to readers. Third, cycles: specify what the team should do if, after following the procedure honestly, the verdicts around a set of products still form a loop — whether that is a bug in the procedure, a fact about value, or a signal that the verdict format itself is wrong for that product category, and what I should publish in the meantime.

Then run the procedure on three cases. Case one: two running shoes where one is measurably lighter and the other measurably better cushioned, and our testers split on which matters more. Case two: a chain of five mattress models, each differing from the next by one incremental improvement in coil count that no sleeper could feel, ranging from a budget model to a luxury model that is clearly better overall. Case three: two laptops where one wins on every measured specification, yet all three testers independently preferred using the other and struggled to articulate why. In the third case, I expect the procedure to give me an answer I will not like, since our traffic depends on clean winners; say plainly what that answer is.

Finally, say what the procedure cannot do — which kinds of comparisons it will settle badly no matter how faithfully it is followed, and where "better than" talk is the wrong tool altogether.
