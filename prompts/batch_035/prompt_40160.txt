At a reading group on moral psychology, a colleague offered the following argument against virtue ethics, and I have not been able to locate the mistake, though I am confident there is one.

(1) A virtue, as virtue ethics conceives of one, is a stable disposition to behave well across situations: the honest person is honest at work, among strangers, under time pressure, and when lying would be cheaper.
(2) The situationist experiments show that whether ordinary people behave well in a given moment is dominated by morally trivial situational factors — a coin found in a phone slot, a few minutes of haste on the way to giving a talk, an experimenter in a lab coat issuing instructions — and that measures of individual character, where such measures were taken at all, predicted little.
(3) In those studies, behavior was controlled by the situation rather than by character.
(4) A disposition that is overridden whenever a trivial situational factor pushes hard enough is not stable across situations.
(5) The subjects were ordinary adults, and the situations were within the ordinary range — hurry, mild authority, distraction — the sort each of us encounters regularly.
(6) So the dispositions of ordinary adults are regularly overridden by trivial factors.
(7) A disposition that is regularly overridden by trivial factors is not a virtue.
(8) So ordinary adults possess no virtues.
(9) The extraordinary cases do not help: the obedience data suggest that nearly everyone has some situation that overrides them, and we cannot tell from a lifetime of good behavior whether someone possesses a virtue or has simply never met the situation that would have exposed its absence.
(10) Therefore almost nobody possesses virtues, and virtue ethics, as a picture of actual human beings — habituation, the practically wise agent as a genuine possibility — is empirically refuted.

He is willing to let virtue ethics survive as an ideal for moral education, but he insists that as a description of ordinary moral life it is finished. I would very much like to keep (2), and (1) strikes me as close to definitional, which is why the argument seemed watertight: every time I try to break the chain I find myself denying something I believe. Yet the conclusion — that almost nobody possesses any virtue at all, rather than merely that virtue is rarer than the tradition pretended — seems to me to prove too much.

Tell me which step I should reject, why that step looked irresistible, and what rejecting it costs, including what my colleague gains by insisting on it. Please keep your reply to at most 450 words.
