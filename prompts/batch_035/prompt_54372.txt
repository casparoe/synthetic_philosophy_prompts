Every spring my department lets the grad students vote on which four or five speakers to fly in for a colloquium series, and the process gets relitigated constantly. My friend Priya has become the de facto judge of every voting proposal anyone raises, and over the years she's built up a real track record. Plurality with a long list of candidates: unacceptable, she says, because of the wasted vote problem — people desert their favorite to stop their least favorite. Borda count: unacceptable, because you can bury a rival under your own fringe candidates. Instant runoff: acceptable, this is actually what we use now. A Condorcet-style method (she's endorsed one when it came up, electing whoever beats everyone head to head): acceptable, though she grumbled it was clunky. A plain dictatorship and also a silly proposal where the alphabetically first nominee always wins regardless of ballots: both obviously unacceptable. Simple majority between exactly two options: fine, no complaints.

Next month someone is proposing we switch to approval voting — check every speaker you'd be happy with, most checks wins. I have to try to predict Priya's verdict before the meeting, and I'm realizing her past rulings don't pin it down. I can see at least two rules she might be running. One is about spoilers: a rule is fine if ranking your favorite sincerely doesn't backfire, which is roughly why she hates plurality and tolerates instant runoff (approval voting does pretty well by that standard, I think?). The other is about majority legitimacy: the winner ought to be somebody's genuine favorite, or at least be backed by a majority over the alternatives — which is why Borda's "compromise candidate nobody ranked first" bothered her. Approval voting looks fine under the first rule and terrible under the second, especially since under approval the winner can easily be everyone's second choice. And there's a third thing I keep wanting to say she must care about — that the rule can't be gamed — but I took a social choice course once and I remember the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem basically says every non-dictatorial rule with at least three outcomes that can pick any of them is manipulable somehow, including instant runoff, so that can't actually be her criterion either. Which maybe tells us something about what her criterion secretly is.

So: predict her verdict on approval voting, lay out the candidate rules that fit her record and where they diverge on this case, and tell me which rule you'd bet on and why. Bonus if you can name a further rule or scenario that would cleanly separate the candidate rules — I've been wondering about a top-two runoff, since it guarantees a majority winner but is wide open to spoilers, so it seems like it would split her intuitions. I'm less attached to the bottom line than to the reasoning, so please also flag where you'd most expect your prediction to fail, and what would make you flip to the other rule if it came up. (Also, if you think her past verdicts are actually inconsistent with each other and she's just been winging it, I'd genuinely want to know that too — it wouldn't shock me.)
