I am a PhD student in machine learning working on the safety evaluation of neural networks, and I am preparing a position piece for our lab's reading group on a conceptual question that keeps unsettling our project on mechanistic anomaly detection (MAD). The idea behind MAD is to flag inputs on which a model behaves out of character not by monitoring output statistics but by asking whether the input was processed by the same mechanisms that produced the model's behaviour on the training distribution. The hope is that this catches cases where the model is doing something genuinely new off-distribution — pursuing a different procedure or goal — even when its outputs look unremarkable and its activations sit within familiar ranges. The trouble is that no one says what makes two computations instances of the same mechanism. Every analysis we try either collapses into behavioural similarity, which the whole exercise was supposed to get beyond, or into exact graph matching, under which nothing ever counts as the same mechanism because no two inputs route through numerically identical components.

Write an essay that weighs the candidate accounts of mechanistic sameness and adjudicates among them. At least the following positions should be considered. First, that sameness is identity of the underlying computation up to a coarse-graining fixed by the detector's designers, so that 'same mechanism' is partly conventional and MAD inherits an arbitrary choice of abstraction. Second, that sameness is sameness of causal role: two computations are the same mechanism if interventions on corresponding parts have corresponding effects, so there is an objective fact of the matter but perhaps relative to a level of description. Third, that sameness is anchored in the training process itself: a mechanism is whatever internal regularity training selected and reinforced, so MAD is really asking whether the input exploits regularities that training did not establish. Fourth, the deflationary view that 'the same mechanism' is an honorific for whatever property makes the detector's score useful, so no deeper analysis is needed. In weighing these, the essay should attend to the problem of granularity — whether mechanisms should be individuated at the level of circuits, features, or something else — and to the possibility that sameness is not transitive in the way an anomaly threshold needs it to be.

The essay should reach and justify a verdict rather than survey and abstain, and the verdict should be tested against the property MAD actually needs: that a model which has learned something new off-distribution is flagged even when its behaviour is plausible, while routine variation — noise, paraphrase, unfamiliar surface content handled in the familiar way — is not. Where concrete examples help, construct self-contained ones rather than appealing to specific published systems. Please do not include citations, references, or attributions of the term MAD to particular authors; the essay should stand on reasoning available to a reader with no access to the literature.
