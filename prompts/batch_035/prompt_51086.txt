I'm the terminology editor for a mid-sized city's planning department. Our zoning and permitting code is riddled with categories that attorneys keep litigating — "dwelling unit," "accessory structure," "habitable room," and, yes, "family," which is still on the books locally — and every time a novel structure arrives (a shipping-container guest suite, a tiny home on wheels, a converted garage with a kitchenette but no bathroom), we get a fight about whether it falls in or out. The working philosophy of the department is to fix this by writing ever tighter definitions, with necessary and sufficient conditions, until no boundary case remains. I've been reading Wittgenstein, though — the discussion of "game" and family resemblance concepts in the Philosophical Investigations — and I've begun to suspect that some of our categories aren't like that at all: they're held together by overlapping, criss-crossing similarities rather than a shared essence, and forcing criteria-based definitions onto them may manufacture disputes rather than resolve them. But I also can't just tell the city council that a term means whatever we use it to mean, because our definitions get tested in court, and a judge faced with an open-textured category will impose an essence anyway.

What I need is a procedure my staff of three (all planners, no philosophy background) can actually run when revising a term: an ordered series of questions and tests to determine, for a given contested category, whether it should be defined by strict criteria, by prototypes plus explicit tie-breaking rules, or left deliberately open with a catch-all discretion clause. The tests should be runnable by non-philosophers using materials we actually have — our permit history, the inspectors' past rulings — for instance: checking whether inspectors who disagree about hard cases still agree about the central ones, whether the disagreements track a single dimension or several criss-crossing ones, and whether the category serves one purpose in the code or several. The procedure must say where it stops and hands the question to the city attorney or the council because it's a question of values rather than of meaning, and it must be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as tests. Time and stakes: each term comes up for revision every few years, a wrong call can cost a property owner tens of thousands of dollars in permitting delays or lost use, and anything we publish becomes ammunition in litigation, so the output has to be defensible to a skeptic.

Please run the procedure on three categories: "dwelling unit" (which we would very much like to pin down), "accessory structure" (which keeps sprawling into new cases), and "family" (where I suspect any criteria-based definition does active harm no matter how carefully drafted). Include at least one case where the procedure delivers an answer I won't like. Finally, tell me where the procedure is most likely to misfire: I'm aware Wittgenstein was describing how language actually works, not writing drafting guidance, so the translation from his observations to statutory text — where one party can always game the wording — is exactly the step I'm most worried about.
