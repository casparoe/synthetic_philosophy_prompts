The Condorcet jury theorem is constantly glossed as the claim that adding voters makes the majority more likely to be right, and the gloss gets applied: expand the panel, widen the committee, add one more reviewer. I want a thought experiment aimed at that gloss rather than at the theorem itself. Set the case somewhere realistic and institutional — a hospital tribunal voting on whether to keep a device's approval, a hiring committee, a parole board — not an abstract electorate. The specifications are as follows. The panel begins with at least three members, all sincere, statistically independent, sharing the aim of the true verdict, facing a single binary question, each individually right more often than not (nobody below 51%, and the incumbent members at least 55%). Then one further member joins, meeting every one of those same conditions. The panel decides by simple majority, and whatever mechanism it uses for an even split must itself be unbiased — drawing straws, say, not a chair with opinions. Yet the probability that the enlarged panel reaches the correct verdict must come out strictly lower than before the newcomer arrived, by at least a full percentage point. The degradation may not come from correlated errors, a shared biased source, strategic voting, or any other violation of the theorem's assumptions — it has to emerge from the mathematics of finite panels operating under the tribunal's own voting rules, with every assumption of the theorem intact.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specs. State each member's competence and, briefly, how it is known inside the fiction (track records, calibration testing). Spell out the voting rule including the split-resolution mechanism, and compute the probability of a correct verdict before and after the newcomer joins, showing the enumeration rather than only the totals; two decimal places is enough. Then identify the single feature of your construction that is doing the work, and perform the removal test: delete that feature and show the effect disappears — if it does not, the case is leaning on something I forbade. Give the Condorcet defender's reply: does this threaten the theorem, or only its popular finite-sample reading, and how would the defender redesign the tribunal — odd panel sizes, competence-weighted votes — so that adding a member can never make the verdict less reliable? Also check whether the enlarged panel is still more accurate than its average member, and say honestly what that comparison shows about how much damage has really been done. Finally, explore how large the drop can be made while every constraint holds, and say whether the figure you reach is a bound you can argue for or merely the best construction you found.

Close with a table, one row for each panel configuration you consider (original, enlarged, and each repaired design), with the following columns: Panel size; Individual competences; Tie-breaking rule; Probability of a correct verdict; What drives the change.
