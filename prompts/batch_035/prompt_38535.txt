My colleague Deb has thought way harder than the rest of us about the Taurek material — do the numbers count when you can save the many or the few — and after years of teaching it her verdicts have gotten pretty consistant, so here's her track record. One stranger drowning on one side, five on the other, she can reach either: flip a coin, and she says she'd say the same for one versus five hundred — group size makes no difference at all. One versus one: coin, obviously. One versus five, but the one is her son: she saves her son, no apologies, though she insists a rescuer with no ties would be free to flip there too. Save one person from death or five from broken legs: save the one — so severity does enter somewhere. Two versus three: coin again. And she gets visibly annoyed when anyone says the five together have more at stake — her line is that the five are five someones, not one bigger someone.

Next week the department runs a case session and I want to walk in with predictions. Case A: Deb can press one button that gives each of the six (the one on one side, the five on the other) an equal one-in-six chance at rescue, or another button that just flips the coin between the sides. Which does she press, and why would it matter to her which? Case B: Deb is driving, the brakes fail, and she must swerve onto one pedestrian or five — does her numbers-don't-count thing carry over from rescuing to harming, or was it always a claim about what a rescuer may do rather than about aggregation itself?

Give me your best guess on A and B, the rule you think she's actually running, the other rules that fit her track record about as well, and — the part I actualy care about — the next case I should pose in the session that would best split those rules apart. And if her coin-flipping in the one-versus-five already commits her one way on A, say so, since then the interesting question is whether she'll bite that bullet or start revising.
