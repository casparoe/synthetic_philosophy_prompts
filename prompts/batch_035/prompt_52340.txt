My oldest friend and I have argued about well-being for years, and at some point I started keeping a written record of her verdicts on particular cases, because I suspect there's a hidden theory in them. Her track record so far: (1) a life spent entirely inside Nozick's experience machine goes worse than an ordinary real life with somewhat less pleasant experience, and she holds this even when the person is told beforehand, accurately and in detail, exactly what the machine is and what it is not; she does not treat the informed consent as softening the verdict at all. (2) A woman who believes her monthly donations have been sustaining a village school for twenty years, while the charity's director has actually pocketed every cent — the woman never finds out and is serenely happy — has a life that goes worse than she thinks it does, and worse than an equally pleasant life in which the donations really do the work. (3) On Mill's old comparison, she sides without hesitation with the dissatisfied thinking person against the contented pig: the thinking life is better even when it feels worse. (4) A mountaineer who spends two years in misery, risk, and estrangement to complete a first ascent has, in my friend's judgment, been made better off by the project — and not merely by the later satisfaction of pride, but by the ascent itself counting for something. And yet (5) she sees nothing at all wrong with a burned-out person taking a two-week machine holiday, and even thinks it prudent.

Now I have two new cases to put to her, and I'd like a prediction before I do, since I suspect the answer will finally expose her theory. Case A: a man whose single deep, stable, reflectively endorsed desire — formed with full knowledge of everything the machine is and is not — is to spend his life in maximally pleasant experience, with no interest in love, achievement, or truth; he plugs in, is delighted, and never regrets it. Case B: a reclusive composer whose music is genuinely great, who knows her art deeply and works with real insight, but whose central desire in life is to be recognized as a great composer; she dies unknown, believing herself a failure, and her work is never heard.

Please structure your answer as follows, in American English. First, a section called "The pattern, and its rivals" (roughly 200 words): identify at least two theories of well-being that each fit her verdicts on cases 1 through 4, and note whether verdict 5 is genuinely in tension with verdict 1 or only apparently so. Second, "Predictions" (roughly 350 words): say what verdict each of the candidate theories delivers on Case A and on Case B, making clear exactly where the theories diverge on these two cases. Third, "My best guess" (roughly 150 words): commit to the verdict she will most likely give on each case, with your reasons for preferring that reading of her record over the others. Fourth, "The next case" (roughly 100 words): describe one further case — one I haven't mentioned — that would best discriminate between the candidate theories, ideally including a twist (about timing, posthumous outcomes, or reshaped desires) that my cases leave untouched.
