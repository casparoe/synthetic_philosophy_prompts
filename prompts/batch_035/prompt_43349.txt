A friend of mine — the sort of Jamesian who keeps a copy of The Will to Believe within arm's reach — has produced an argument that appears to rescue James's permission to believe from every counterexample I know how to raise. I arrived at our coffee meetings armed with the usual Cliffordian objections, and she met them all with a single line of reasoning that now strikes me as proving far too much. I cannot find the false step, and I would like help locating it and pricing the alternatives.

Her argument, as best I can reconstruct it in numbered steps:

(1) Where we face a genuine option that present evidence cannot settle, we are entitled to let our passional nature decide the belief rather than wait for proof that will never arrive. She presents this as nothing more than the fair statement of James: the will gets a say only where evidence is silent and the choice cannot be deferred.

(2) Whether an option is genuine — whether it is live, forced, or momentous — and whether evidence can settle it, are matters judged from where the believer stands. There is no further tribunal: the hypothesis is live if she can seriously entertain it; the option is forced if, as her situation presents itself to her, she must either act on it or go without it; it is momentous if the stakes so strike her; and it is undecidable if the evidence leaves it open as far as she can tell — and no one, she insists, is better placed than she is to tell.

(3) It follows that for any hypothesis H that a person finds attractive and that cannot be conclusively refuted, she can bring her belief under the rule: if she can imagine H, must either act on it or forgo its benefits, and finds the evidence inconclusive, then believing H on passional grounds is permitted.

(4) So the following believers are all within their rights: the patient who believes her illness will remit against every statistic, because hope is what sustains the fight; the partner who believes in faithfulness whatever the pattern of the last three years, because trust is what her marriage asks of her; the investor who believes the venture will pay whatever the prospectus says, because conviction is what keeps her from selling at the bottom.

When I objected that (4) licenses wishful thinking wholesale, she agreed — cheerfully. On her view, our contrary intuitions are just Cliffordism leaking in: the permission really is that broad, and anyone who accepts James's rule should accept its consequences. This is where I get off the bus, but I also cannot see which step to reject.

If I reject (1), I am pushed back toward Clifford's rule that believing on insufficient evidence is wrong always and everywhere, and I have never been comfortable there: it seems to condemn not only the patient but anyone who has ever trusted a friend ahead of the evidence. If I reject (2), I need there to be facts about which options are genuinely forced and genuinely undecidable — facts that can obtain even when the believer is wrong about them — and when she presses me on how such facts could ever be established, I notice with some alarm that my demand for an external tribunal is the very thing Clifford's critics held against him. Step (3) is where I keep staring, but as far as I can tell nothing moves from (2) to (3); (3) simply follows, which is what frightens me.

So, my questions: which step should be rejected, why did it look so harmless when first stated, and what does rejecting it cost — for her Jamesianism, for my residual Cliffordianism, and for the ordinary cases neither of us wants to lose? In particular, can the rule be repaired so that it saves the patient without saving the investor, or does every repair of that kind smuggle evidence-talk back in through a side door? And if she is right that nothing short of consequences distinguishes the two cases, I want to know exactly what I am conceding when I admit it.
