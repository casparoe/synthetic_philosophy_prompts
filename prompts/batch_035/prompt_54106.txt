I'm running a discussion section for a first-year undergraduate course in intermediate microeconomics, and next week I'm responsible for revealed preference — the idea, going back to Samuelson, that if a consumer chooses bundle A when bundle B was affordable at the same prices and income, they've revealed that they prefer A to B, and that in principle we can build up the entire preference ordering from observed choices, without ever mentioning utility as an inner quantity. My students will have just come from a block on utility functions, and most of them think of utility the way the textbook trained them to: a number inside your head that choices are designed to make big. The section is fifty minutes, chalkboard only, about twenty students, and I've been told not to re-derive anything formally — no axioms, no proofs, just the concept.

Plan the explanation rather than giving it to me. What has to be established first so that the point isn't heard as merely bookkeeping? What single concrete example should carry the whole thing — I've considered using a cafeteria tray with changing daily prices, but I'm open to something better, and I'd like the example chosen so that it shows both what revealed preference can recover and what it structurally cannot (why choices alone stay silent about the strength of preferences or about what someone would have done in situations that never arose). The misconception I most expect is the circularity one — students concluding that "utility is whatever you choose" and "you choose whatever maximizes your utility" is a pointless closed loop — and I want to know exactly where in the sequence to confront it, not as an objection at the end but as a fork in the road: either the theory is a tautology dressed up, or the weak axiom has actual bite, and where exactly that bite shows up. I'd also like to know what to deliberately refuse to cover — I suspect something should be said about where this all breaks when preferences shift over time or when the consumer isn't one person but a household, but tell me if that's a bridge too far for this audience, and what to say when someone raises it anyway. Finally, give me one question I can pose in the last five minutes whose answer would tell me whether they've genuinely grasped revealed preference as a constraint on explanations rather than a description of an inner number, as opposed to having just learned to parrot the cafeteria example back at me.
