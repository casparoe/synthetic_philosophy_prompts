I write novels for a living, mostly the character-driven sort, and a postgrad at my book club who is deep into Sartre has just told me my whole trade is a monument to bad faith. She laid it out like this and I cant find the hole in it, though there must be one because (spoiler) I dont intend to stop writing.

(1) Bad faith, in Sartre, is fleeing your freedom: treating yourself as a finished thing whose nature settles what you'll do — his famous waiter who plays at being nothing-but-a-waiter.
(2) Radical freedom means your facticity — your past, your body, your role, your 'character' — never settles what you do next. You could always do otherwise, and you know it.
(3) Knowing that is anguish, and anguish is unbearable, so we flee it, and the most common flight is character: 'that's just who I am', 'I couldn't do that, I'm not the sort'.
(4) To invoke character like that is to treat the pattern of your past choices as a cause of your future ones.
(5) But an explanation by character just is a claim that character settled the action — otherwise it isn't an explanation, it's a coincidence that the person happened to do the thing consistent with their character.
(6) So whenever I make a character vivid and then have them act as that character would act, I am modelling the flight from freedom — and the better I do my job, the better I model it.
(7) Therefore psychological fiction as such is a manual of bad faith, and Sartre should have condemned the form. Instead he wrote Nausea and the Roads to Freedom books, which (she says) are the most character-explained fiction going — I havent read them yet, which she never lets me forget.

For what it's worth, I'd rather keep (6) — I dont want to write characters as coin flips — but I'd also like to keep (5), because when I explain Mathilde's betrayal by her vanity I do think I'm explaining something, not reporting a coincidence. So I suppose I'm hoping the rot is somewhere in (3) or (4)? Honestly cant tell. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me as a novelist — does anything survive of the idea that character makes an action intelligible rather than settled?
