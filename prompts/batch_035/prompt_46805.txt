So here's something that's bugged me for years: the debate over why people cooperate in one-shot prisoner's dilemmas in the lab. The iterated stuff I get -- tit-for-tat, grim trigger, the shadow of the future, all fine, Axelrod's tournaments made that intuitive even if later work showed TFT's dominance depended a lot on which strategies happened to show up and that things like win-stay-lose-shift do better once there's noise. What I don't get is why the debate about the genuinely one-shot, anonymous, no-observer version has been completely frozen since basically the 2000s. One camp says it's a misfire: our cooperative psychology evolved for small groups where every interaction is embedded in a repeated game, and the lab just tricks that machinery into running in an environment it was never built for. Another camp says no, large-scale cooperation with strangers is a real adaptation (cultural group selection, intergroup competition scaling up the prosocial norms), so one-shot cooperation isn't a bug at all. And then there's a third group saying the whole question is confused because there's no such thing as a perceived one-shot game -- people can't fully turn off the possibility of future contact or an invisible audience, so the "true one-shot" is a philosophers' construct the experimenters only approximate.

Here's my working hypothesis, and tell me if it's dumb: the two main camps aren't actually disagreeing about facts, they're disagreeing about what counts as an explanation of a behavioral trait. The misfire camp is doing something like a distal/functional explanation and doesn't care that the proximate mechanism fires in one-shots; the group-selection camp insists an explanation has to cover why the trait is *currently* adaptive at the scale it operates, not just why it originally arose. If that's right, then the argument about whether ancestral environments even contained true one-shots (small bands, but dispersal, trade with strangers, seasonal aggregation and fissioning...) isn't the real battleground at all, it's a proxy fight. So: is this a substantive empirical disagreement that's just hard to test, is it my claimed difference in explanatory standards, or is it verbal in some other way? And what would even count as progress here -- better experiments with real anonymity, formal models showing what a one-shot-sensitive psychology would have to compute, or just everyone agreeing to state which question about explanation they're answering? I want an actual diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of who said what.
