Every fall I teach an intro ethics course, and every year the food-ethics unit is where the students actually surprise me. This week one of them walked in with an argument he'd clearly been polishing, and I'll admit it took me embarassingly long to figure out where I'd cut it — I want to see whether you can do better. The setup is the familiar beef-vs-chicken thing, plus the observation that crop cultivation isn't bloodless either.

Here's the argument, numbered the way he gave it. (1) The moral cost of a diet is determined by how many sentient creatures it kills or makes suffer. (2) A single pasture-raised cow yields several hundred meals, while a single chicken yields roughly one, so per meal chicken causes something like two orders of magnitude more animal deaths than beef. (3) Broilers also live worse lives than grazing cattle, so the suffering comparison points the same direction as the death comparison. (4) Crop cultivation kills animals too — rodents and insects at plowing and harvest, plus whatever the pesticides do — so a vegetarian's plate isn't clean either. (5) Someone who eats only grass-fed beef from pasture that never gets plowed therefore kills fewer animals per year than a vegetarian whose calories come from tilled fields — maybe one cow against dozens of mice and countless insects. (6) A diet that kills fewer animals is, other things equal, morally better. (7) So grass-fed beef-eating is morally better than vegetarianism, and the vegetarian at the next table is the one with blood on her hands.

He leaned back after that like he'd proved something, and the conclusion smells wrong to me, but when I went premise by premise each one looked fine on its own, which is exactly the combination that makes me nervous. My instinct is that something quiet happens in the run from (4) to (6), but I've promised myself not to hand you my half-formed suspicion, so I'll keep it. Give me the real diagnosis: which step do you reject, why did that step look fine when examined alone, and what do you give up once you reject it? For what it's worth, I'd accept the whole argument before I accepted its implication that the ethically optimal diet is one very large, very contented animal per human lifetime — though if you think that's where the logic actually lands, I'd honestly like to hear you say it out loud.
