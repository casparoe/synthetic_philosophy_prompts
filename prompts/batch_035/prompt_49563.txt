(2) is the history roughly right or am i misremembering/misusing things? (3) is it interesting, or a version of something everyone's already said? and (4) any suggestions for making it sharper. please be blunt, i can take it. also, could you write your reply in british english — i live in glasgow now and the anthology is uk-based.

here's the draft (it's notes, not prose yet, so don't worry about style):

the argument: the moral case for working less has gotten weaker as it's gotten more reasonable. when paul lafargue wrote his pamphlet about the right to be lazy in the 1880s (he was karl marx's son-in-law, fun fact i want to keep), the demand for shorter hours was openly a demand of the working class against the bosses — idleness as a weapon. bertrand russell, in the 1930s, was a bit politer about it but still basically said: most work is waste, and the people doing it are suckers, and the suckers have been taught to be proud of being suckers. that's the tradition i want to draw on. but look at how the same demand gets argued now. in japan it's karoshi — death by overwork — so the argument becomes a medical one: work is bad for your health, like smoking. in france the 35-hour week was sold as a way to create jobs and let parents see their kids, a kind of lifestyle policy. and even the sacred text of this whole genre, keynes's old prediction that his grandchildren would work 15 hours a week, gets quoted as a puzzle about productivity — where did the hours go, why aren't we working less, etc. my claim: each of these framings (health, family time, productivity accounting) is more persuasive to ordinary people than lafargue ever was, and each one is also a retreat, because they all concede the thing that matters — that work is where your life goes, and who gets to decide that. nobody stands up anymore and says the thing russell said, which was basically that a lot of work is just dignified pointless suffering. we've traded a demand about power for a set of requests about wellbeing, and requests about wellbeing are things your employer can give you as a perk. overwork becomes a wellness issue, complete with apps.

second half (this is where i think it gets more original, tell me if i'm wrong): i want to connect this to how characters work in novels. in 19th-century fiction, work is fate — you inherit the mill, you're sent to the clerks, and the drama is whether you can escape your allotted work. in contemporary literary fiction, i notice the jobs are just background, something characters do between scenes, and i wonder if that's connected: once the argument about work is about wellness, work stops being a moral subject at all, in life and in fiction. the novel can't dramatise a perk.

one thing i'm unsure about: is the three-stage story (class struggle, then health, then lifestyle) too neat? i kind of know it's too neat. should i be leaning on weber's protestant work ethic somewhere — i feel like it's expected, but i don't actually know what it would add beyond "some people think work is virtuous", which my readers already know.

as you can tell there's a fair bit of hand-waving. tear into it — accuracy problems, things that are just vibes, places where a philosopher would wince. just please don't turn your answer into a reading list; i can't chase up citations right now and honestly i'd rather hear what you actually think.
