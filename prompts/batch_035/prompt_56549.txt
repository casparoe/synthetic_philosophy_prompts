I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a large psychiatric hospital, and I need a written opinion circulated before Thursday's meeting. The case: a patient with a fifteen-year history of psychotic episodes has, during a period of documented competence, signed a psychiatric advance directive — the kind the literature calls a Ulysses contract — that names a specific antipsychotic and instructs staff that if she refuses medication during a relapse, they are to administer it over her objection, using restraint if necessary; it further specifies that any refusal she voices while floridly psychotic is to be treated as a symptom rather than as a wish. She is now in exactly that state: actively refusing, and in her brief lucid intervals sometimes telling staff the directive was a mistake, though it is unclear how stable her judgment is within those intervals. The committee is split. One faction holds that honoring the directive simply respects her autonomous self against the transient self the illness produces, and that refusing to honor it renders her competence during wellness meaningless. The other holds that forcibly treating a protesting patient on the strength of a document she can no longer revise is exactly what we would reject in any other context: we do not normally let the past bind the present where the bound party has no ongoing right of revision. What our current drafts lack is any serious engagement with the structural analogy the second faction is implicitly invoking — constitutional precommitment. Defenders of entrenched constitutions face a version of the same problem: why may the living be bound by commitments made by people who can no longer be asked? I want the memo to address that analogy head-on: say what the individual and constitutional cases genuinely share, where they come apart (consider, for instance, that a constitution can be amended by the very people it binds, whereas the whole point of this directive is that its author cannot amend it when it matters — but consider also whether that difference favors one side or cuts both ways), and state which side of the analogy the disanalogy favors in this case. Beyond the analogy, the memo should give a concrete recommendation with any conditions attached, reasons the losing faction could actually engage with rather than a restatement of autonomy doctrine, and a fair treatment of the strongest point against your recommendation. Finally, close with a section on what would change the recommendation: identify the one or two facts about this case, or features of our general policy on such directives, that, if they had turned out differently, would point the other way, and say candidly where the memo's reasoning is most likely to fail. Please keep it to something the committee can read in ten minutes.
