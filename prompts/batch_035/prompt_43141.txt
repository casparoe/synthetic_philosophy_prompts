**Exam question (30 points, scaled to 100 for this purpose):**

In intuitionistic logic, the law of excluded middle, P ∨ ¬P, is not a theorem. Explain the intuitionist's reason for rejecting this schema, state what disjunction and negation mean on the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation, and explain what the double-negation translations do and do not show about the relationship between intuitionistic and classical logic.

**Student's answer:**

The intuitionist rejects P ∨ ¬P because a proof of a disjunction must supply a proof of one of its disjuncts, and for an arbitrary proposition we have no guarantee that either disjunct is provable. This was Brouwer's own formulation of the BHK interpretation, which he stated precisely: a proof of ¬P is a construction that transforms any proof of P into a proof of absurdity, ⊥. On the intuitionist's view, then, every proposition falls into one of three categories: proved, refuted, or undecided. For the undecided ones, excluded middle is false — indeed, the intuitionist can show ¬(P ∨ ¬P) for such propositions, which is why the schema is not a theorem. Brouwer's weak counterexamples illustrate the situation: take Goldbach's conjecture, G. We currently have neither a proof of G nor a proof of ¬G, so G ∨ ¬G fails, and this is a genuine counterexample to the law rather than a mere gap in our knowledge, since mathematics is not a finished realm of facts but a creative activity of the mind.

There is, however, an important restriction. Whenever P is decidable — for instance, an equation between particular natural numbers — the intuitionist does accept P ∨ ¬P, and the doubly negated form ¬¬P → P holds as well. So the rejection of excluded middle is not a blanket refusal but concerns only undecidable propositions in general.

As for the relation with classical logic, the Gödel–Gentzen double-negation translation works by inserting double negations into a classical formula at appropriate places, and the key theorem is that classical and intuitionistic arithmetic prove exactly the same theorems. So the two logics are really notational variants of one another, and the disagreement is philosophical rather than mathematical. The only place a genuine difference surfaces is propositional logic, where Glivenko's theorem shows that a classical propositional tautology is not intuitionistically provable in general, though its double negation always is.
