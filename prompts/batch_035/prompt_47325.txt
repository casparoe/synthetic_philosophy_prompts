So my school has this philosophy club that's mostly endless trolley problem arguments, but last week Dev went off about the Sapir-Whorf thing and now I can't stop chewing on it. He started with the examples everyone brings up — Russian having two basic words for blue, those Australian languages where you give directions by north/south instead of left/right, the Amazon tribe that supposedly doesn't have exact number words past two or three — and said this shows your native language hands you the categories you think with, and there's no category-picking you do before or underneath language. Fine so far. Then he ran this argument on me and the conclusion seems too big, but each step sounds right when I read it alone on the bus:

(1) The categories available for your thoughts are fixed by whatever language you happened to grow up speaking — there is no concept-selection that happens prior to or outside of language.
(2) Which language you get is a pure accident of birth; nobody earns their categories.
(3) So no language's categories are any better placed than any other's to line up with how the world actually is — ranking them would require a standpoint outside all languages, and (1) says there isn't one.
(4) Observation can't bail us out either, because we only notice what our categories let us notice, so every observation just confirms the categories it was taken in.
(5) Modern science is just the package of categories carried by a particular set of languages — having alot of words for quarks and genes isn't different in kind from having a lot of words for snow.
(6) So the success of science is zero evidence that its categories track reality, and saying science describes the world as it really is makes about as much sense as saying one time zone keeps the true time.

I get that the conclusion is supposed to be the shocking part, but that's not what's bugging me — what's bugging me is I can't tell WHICH step to point at. Step 4 feels like it's cheating somehow but I can't put my finger on how, and my other instinct is that step 3 quietly switches between two different readings of step 1. Also, full honesty, I'd like to keep at least a watered-down version of step 1 — the Russian blues stuff and the direction stuff is the whole reason I found this interesting, and if the answer is just "your language doesn't affect your thinking at all, go home" I'll be pretty disappointed. So: which premise or step should I reject, why did it look fine in the first place, and what do I have to pay for rejecting it? Please write the answer in American English.
