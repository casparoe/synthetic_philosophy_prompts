So I've been going down a rabbit hole ever since a pub argument with my sister (she's a nurse, I drive a delivery van, neither of us is any kind of expert lol). We were arguing about why she's a nurse and I'm not, and her friend mentioned that thing where the most gender-equal countries on earth somehow have the most gendered job choices -- like, roughly speaking, Norway has a lower share of women among engineering grads than Albania does. I got obsessed, read everything I could actually understand, and wrote up my thoughts as a draft blog post. Now I'd love comments, especially on whether my reasoning holds up or whether I'm just fooling myself.

Here's the draft, roughly: (1) There's the famous 2018 study (Stoet & Geary I believe) finding that in more gender-equal countries, relatively fewer women end up with STEM degrees, and there's a companion finding that richer and more gender-equal countries show bigger average differences between men and women in stuff like patience, risk-taking and how much they value money. (2) My mate Jake says the first result is fragile, because it depends on how you do the math -- apparently if you compare men and women within each country using a different statistic (something about ratios versus plain differences), the effect mostly goes away or shrinks a lot. I half-accept this but claim it doesnt matter for my main point. (3) My main point: the paradox, if real, at most shows what happens when you remove material constraint. In a poorer country, STEM is one of very few doors to a decent life, so everyone grits through whatever they'd rather be doing; in Norway you can afford to follow your interests. So the paradox is only evidence about what people "freely choose" if you also assume choices happen in a vacuum, which nobody actually believes -- gendered advertising, what your mum did, what school told you you were good at, all of it. (4) Therefore my conclusion: even if the gap in gender-equal countries is 100% "preference," nothing follows about nature, and nothing follows about justice either, since the preferences themselves are downstream of everything else. I'm aware that (2) and (4) are in some tension (if the effect is fragile, what is "the gap" I'm even making claims about?) and I honestly don't know how to resolve that.

What I want from you: tell me which parts a person who actually knows this stuff would wince at, whether the "constraint vs preference" distinction I'm leaning on is as clean as I think it is, whether step (3) is basically just the obvious "when you're richer you can afford to indulge preferences" story dressed up, and whether my jump from "it's preferences all the way down" to "so nothing follows about justice" is way too fast. Also happy to hear if the whole framing is wrong -- like if there's some other explanation for the paradox that makes my essay pointless. Be blunt, I can take it, I'd much rather fix the argument than defend it.
