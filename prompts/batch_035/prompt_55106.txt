I hold what I take to be the standard quantitative-genetics position on heritability: a heritability coefficient estimated within a population is a fact about variance decomposition in that population under those conditions, and it licenses no conclusion, by itself, about the causes of mean differences between populations — not even a probabilistic one, and not even when the coefficient is very large. I arrived at this via the familiar thought experiment in which genetically identical seeds grown in soils of differing quality produce plants whose height varies heritably within each plot, while the plot means differ purely because of the soils. I am now less sure of the position than I once was, and I want a rigorous map of its exposure rather than a defense of it. Tell me what would count against it, attending in particular to the following:

1. The logical-versus-evidential slide. If the position is read as the claim that a heritability estimate alone, as a matter of logic, entails nothing about the causes of group differences, it looks analytic and unfalsifiable. But if it is read as the stronger claim that heritability estimates, combined with ordinary background knowledge, carry no evidential weight about group differences, it looks vulnerable. Where exactly is the defensible version, and has the position been quietly qualified from the strong claim down to the weak one whenever challenged — the signature of insulation by retreat? Is that a problem for a claim of this kind, given that it is not an empirical hypothesis but a constraint on inference?

2. Constraints from variance structure. If within-group environmental variance for a trait is small (so heritability is high), and there is independent reason to think the between-group environmental differences are no larger, in the respects that matter for the trait, than the within-group ones, does that combination constrain the possible causes of a group mean difference — and if so, hasn't the position as usually stated overreached? Which of those premises would working population geneticists themselves accept, and how often are they actually met? This is the consideration I would personally find most persuasive if it can be made out.

3. The selection-reversal argument. The position is standardly illustrated by selection: because highly heritable traits respond to selection, high within-group heritability is fully compatible with a between-group difference produced by divergent selection, and hence with genetic causes at the group level. But this is a two-way street — it shows compatibility, which supports neither direction of inference. Does this consideration really support the no-inference position, or does it show only that the direction of any inference must be settled by other means — in which case the position should be restated not as a barrier but as a signpost?

4. Proves-too-much cases. If the argument is valid, does it also block conclusions I am confident are legitimate — for instance, inferring the cause of a height difference between two populations that share an environment except for nutrition, or between two generations of the same population, where a within-population change over a few decades cannot be genetic in the relevant sense yet the logic of the position seems to apply to any partition we choose? If a principle blocks known-good inferences, that counts against it even if it is a claim about inference rather than about the world.

5. Defenders' own concessions. Please separate what the position's defenders in quantitative genetics would themselves grant as counting against it — presumably something like a demonstrated setting where heritability estimates alone, with no other data, reliably tracked the direction or source of group differences — from what only critics allege. Defenders should also be forced to say what, if anything, they treat as evidence for their side rather than merely as absence of evidence against it.

Rank the potential defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward, say which of them a committed defender should concede and which they can dismiss at no cost, and give me an honest verdict on whether the position, properly stated, risks anything at all — and whether a claim that risks nothing in this domain is doing real inferential work or is just a restatement of the fact that variance components are specific to a population and its circumstances. If your conclusion is that the defensible core is the weak claim while the popular claim is the strong one, say so plainly, since that would mean the position is considerably less settled than its usual rhetorical deployment suggests.
