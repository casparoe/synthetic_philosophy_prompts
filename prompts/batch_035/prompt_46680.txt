I'd like you to analyze Kierkegaard's "Corsair affair" of 1845–46 as a test case for his own theory of the press and the public, running the analysis in both directions: what the theory illuminates about the episode, and what the episode does to the theory.

The episode, in brief, so the analysis need not depend on memory. In late 1845 the critic P. L. Møller, who was known to be close to Copenhagen's leading satirical weekly, The Corsair, published a review of Stages on Life's Way that treated Kierkegaard's pseudonymous books as transparent autobiography. In December 1845 Kierkegaard retaliated in the respectable daily Fædrelandet under his pseudonym Frater Taciturnus, pointedly observing that every Danish author of consequence had been abused in The Corsair except him, and that he hoped to be attacked there soon. The weekly took the invitation: over the following months it ran caricatures of his person — the stoop, the cane, the uneven trouser legs — invented fake identifications of his pseudonyms, and eventually acknowledged him outright, while the joke spread beyond the paper's pages; he was taunted by children in the street and remained a public figure of fun for years. Meanwhile, in March 1846, with the campaign still running, he published Two Ages, whose celebrated second half develops the theory in question: the public is a phantom abstraction to which no one actually belongs, the press is its creature and enforcer, and the present age is reflective and passionless — replacing decision with deliberation and passion with laughter, and levelling everything by treating everything as material for the same empty talk.

Map the episode onto the theory with care: who or what counts as "the public" here — Goldschmidt's editorial staff, the readership, or the anonymous laughter that outlived them? What does the theory explain that a simpler account would not — for instance, why the attack worked through his body rather than his arguments, and why the paper's anonymity mattered while his own pseudonyms did not protect him? Then say honestly where the mapping strains, at least along these axes: Kierkegaard deliberately engineered the persecution the theory describes as the press crushing the single individual; his tormentors were concrete, nameable persons rather than an abstract phantom; and The Corsair was a small, edited paper with a known publisher, which may make the affair a personal feud that the structural language dignifies. Consider, too, whether the theory's emergence in the middle of the battle is evidence of discovery or of rationalization. In the end, take a position: does the affair confirm the theory, expose it, or present a case it never anticipated? Be disciplined about the mapping — where the theory is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
