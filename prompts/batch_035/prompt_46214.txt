I'm a graduate student in animal science, and I've somehow agreed to give the after-dinner talk at the annual gathering of a regional pigeon-racing club — roughly sixty fanciers, ages twenty to eighty, ferociously knowledgeable about their birds and innocent of any statistics. The club has been feuding for a decade between two factions the members themselves call the bloodline people and the loft people: the first insist that racing performance is in the pedigree and pay eye-watering sums for eggs from champions; the second insist that a middling bloodline in a superb loft beats champion genes in a neglected one. Both sides keep invoking the word heritability as though it were a talisman, and the club secretary asked me not to crown a winner but to explain what the word actually means and, just as importantly, what it does not mean. The trickiest part is that the talk is after a long dinner with bar service, so it has to survive short attention spans; there will be a whiteboard but no projector, and equations are out of the question.

Please plan the talk rather than write it. Specifically: the order the pieces must come in — I assume the idea that heritability is a property of a population, not of an individual bird, has to land before anything else, but tell me what depends on what after that, and why the number's dependence on how variable everyone's lofts are can't be introduced too early or too late. Design one carrying example from their own world that must do the work of both the within-loft and across-lofts cases (no twin studies, no human examples up front). Then map the misconceptions I should expect in the order the audience will actually produce them — my guesses are that heritability means immutability, that a figure like 60% means 60% of a particular bird's performance comes from its genes, and that one number applies everywhere and forever — and say exactly where in the sequence to defuse each. I also want one careful late pivot showing why a heritability estimate from their club tells them nothing about the difference between their club's average and another club's average racing in different weather, and then, briefly and without any politics, why the same caution applies when people try to carry such numbers to human groups; the secretary warned me that some members love making that leap, so I need it headed off without turning dinner into a lecture.

Finally: a single check-for-understanding question I can ask from the floor at the end that would reveal whether they've got the population-and-environment-dependence idea rather than just nodded along, and a short list of what I should openly refuse to cover at this level (I'm thinking how the estimates are actually computed, gene-by-environment statistics, and anything molecular) so I can say so in one sentence and move on. Keep the entire plan to a strict maximum of 600 words, and lead with the one-line order of the four or five main moves before elaborating.
