I've spent nineteen years in quality control at a plant that makes hydraulic fittings — no university, no philosophy, just calipers, sampling plans and a wall of standard operating procedures. On my own time I got interested in a puzzle I read about in a second-hand book: philosophers used to say knowledge is justified true belief, and then someone called Gettier produced cases where you believe something true, with decent support, but the support and the truth are only connected by dumb luck. It lodged with me because my line has nearly the same three-way split: a part passes inspection (the justification), the part really is within tolerance (the truth), and we stamp it known good. My own view, worked out over night shifts, is that Gettier cases are what we call false accepts — good parts that sailed through on luck rather than on what the inspection actually measured — and that the fixes philosophers proposed afterwards (the no-false-lemmas idea, reliabilism, safety and sensitivity conditions, and something called knowledge-first) all look to me like different ways of tightening an inspection plan so the stamp tracks the part.
