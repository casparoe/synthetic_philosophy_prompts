A friend of mine who works in political communication keeps reaching for the Emperor's New Clothes as her explanation of sudden collapses of norms — the fall of the East European regimes in 1989, the #MeToo reckonings, the rapid toppling of once-untouchable reputations. Her story runs roughly as follows. In Andersen's tale, every onlooker at the parade privately sees that the emperor is naked, but each believes the others see fine clothes; the child's shout makes the fact common knowledge — everyone knows, everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on up the tower — and the spell breaks on the spot. She concludes that norm change is essentially an epistemic event: a private fact needs only to be made common knowledge by one public utterance, and the cascade does the rest. She leans, approvingly, on the idea of preference falsification — everyone in a repressive arrangement privately dissents while publicly conforming, so the whole apparatus rests on nobody knowing that anybody else knows — and treats the fairy tale as the mechanism by which such arrangements end.

Please audit the analogy for her. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the child, the crowd, the tailors, the shout, and the fact that becomes common knowledge — and then set out where the mapping breaks. I am particularly interested in three pressure points.

First, publicity. The tale assumes a single, perfectly shared channel: one shout, heard by all, with everyone seeing that everyone heard, and everyone seeing that everyone saw that everyone heard. Real announcements are fragmentary — nobody reads the same feed, attends the same square, or hears the same whisper network — so the question becomes what depth of iterated knowledge is actually achieved by a given channel, and how much depth the collective action in question requires. I gather there is a literature on exactly this: that low-stakes coordination needs only shallow nesting of beliefs, while high-stakes coordination — where deviating alone is disastrous — needs the hierarchy to run much deeper before anyone moves. Where does the fairy tale sit on that scale, and does it quietly assume infinite depth when a couple of levels would do?

Second, cost. The child's shout is nearly free. In the real cases, the first person to defect or speak out typically pays for it, which is why "someone only needs to say the obvious thing publicly" is not yet an explanation of norm collapse: it leaves unexplained who bears the cost of becoming the child, and why the crowd waited for that particular person rather than any other. The tailors, note, benefit from the silence — the analogy should say something about who profits from the fact not being common knowledge, and how strong their incentive to punish the first shouter has to be before the shout never comes at all.

Third, the crowd's prior epistemic state. The tale requires that everyone already knows the emperor is naked and merely lacks knowledge of others' knowledge. But real populations have heterogeneous information, and there is a well-known rival family of models — herding, where agents copy others' actions without access to others' reasons — that explains precisely the cases where the crowd converges without anyone's private information becoming common at all. Which model fits which case, and is my friend's binary between private belief and common knowledge the right carving, or should it be a three-way distinction: common knowledge, common knowledge that everyone will act on, and no shared knowledge at all?

One more wrinkle, which she never mentions: Andersen himself does not quite deliver the instant collapse. After the crowd takes up the cry, the emperor suspects the tale is true but carries on with the procession regardless. That looks like a concession that common knowledge, by itself, does not guarantee action — an awkward datum for an analogy being used to claim that it does.

So, in the end: does her conclusion survive? That is, is "making private belief common knowledge" the right description of norm collapse, or is the fairy tale quietly supplying the perfect publicity and the costless utterance that the real cases conspicuously lack? And if the analogy is salvageable, what does the repaired version look like — perhaps one in which the depth of shared knowledge, and the expected cost of being the first to test it, do the explanatory work that the child's shout was supposed to do alone?
