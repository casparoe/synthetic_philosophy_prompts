One for my final exam: I teach an upper-level phil of probability seminar and I want a single essay question on Bertrand's paradox. The annoying thing is that every student has memorized the canned takeaway — 'the principle of indifference gives inconsistent answers to the same question, so it's broken' — and I want a question that recital can't answer. It should force the real issue: does the paradox show that indifference itself is incoherent, or does it show that 'a randomly chosen chord' doesn't yet specify an experiment, and what follows from each diagnosis? (E.g., if it's the latter, is indifference vindicated once the experiment is fixed, or does something like symmetry/invariance still have to do work that indifference can't do on its own?) I'd also like the question to reward students who can say what the classical answers of 1/2, 1/3, and 1/4 each correspond to as physical setups, rather than just listing the numbers.

Please give me three things: the question as it would appear on the exam, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that names the specific moves an A answer must contain that a B answer won't, plus a one-line note on how students typically lose marks on each.

One more request: in the model answer, please keep clear the line between what's mathematically settled and what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this. The derivations of the three probabilities and the fact that they come from different setups are settled; whether invariance considerations uniquely determine a solution, or whether indifference is salvageable at all, is contested. I don't want the model answer to present some one resolution of the paradox as if it were the consensus view.
