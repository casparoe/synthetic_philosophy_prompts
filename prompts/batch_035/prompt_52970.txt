I manage pest control for the buildings portfolio of a mid-sized local authority — libraries, leisure centres, depots, staff kitchens, some sheltered housing. Between spring and autumn my team gets a handful of treatment requests a week: wasps' nests near entrances, stored-product moths in a food store, ants trailing through a kitchen, occasional bed bugs brought in on furniture. Our integrated pest management policy says a good deal about efficacy, human safety and legal compliance, and almost nothing about ethics. Over the past few years a different set of worries has started arriving from staff and residents: whether it matters that the insects we kill might be sentient; whether the method matters, given that a quick knockdown aerosol and a sticky trap left to work over days look very different from the animal's point of view; whether it matters that one treatment kills thousands of individuals that would never have harmed anyone; and whether preventing a population from ever existing is better or worse than killing it later once it exists. Neither I nor my staff have settled views on whether insects feel anything, and the science will not settle it for us this decade, but I still have to decide, this month and every month.

Please write a decision procedure that a competent technician can run on a request before it reaches me. It should:

1. Be grounded in what moral philosophy actually has to say that is usable at this scale of decision — the moral status of small, possibly sentient animals; the distinction between killing and allowing to die; the trade-off between the number of individuals harmed and the intensity of harm per individual, and what to do when those pull apart; the difference between harms deliberately inflicted on the target species and incidental harms to non-target insects, such as pollinators visiting plants along a treated barrier; and the precautionary question of what to do when sentience is uncertain and the evidence will not arrive in time to matter.

2. Set out the questions in the order they should be asked, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, mark the points where the procedure terminates in a definite 'do not treat' or 'treat, by this method', and mark the points where it must hand the decision back to a named person because what remains is a question of values rather than facts.

3. Be explicit about its assumptions. If the procedure turns on a threshold of confidence about insect sentience, or on a particular weighting between suffering and death, say so, and say what happens to the recommendations if that assumption is wrong in each direction.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (a) a wasps' nest beside the entrance to a children's nursery, with the colony expected to die naturally within a few weeks; (b) stored-product moths in a food store, where the options are pheromone traps that monitor but do not control, a slow-acting residual treatment, or discarding all affected stock; and (c) ants trailing through a staff kitchen, where the cheapest response is an insecticidal barrier and the most expensive is locating and sealing the entry route and removing the attractant. At least one of these should come out with a recommendation my team will dislike, and I would like you to say which one, and why the procedure still yields it.

Finally, tell me honestly what the procedure cannot do: the cases it was never built for, the situations in which it will misfire, and the points at which two reasonable people applying it in good faith will reach different answers. I would rather know that now than after the first complaint.
