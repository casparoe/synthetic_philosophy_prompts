I'm a retired geophysicist who's gotten well into philosophy of science since I stopped working, and my little reading group has ended up in a proper argument about markets. Please write your answer in British English.

The two arguers are my son Rob, who works at a low-cost index fund and is basically a believer in efficient markets, and his wife Priya, who traded energy derivatives for years before quitting. The flashpoint was Rob claiming that trying to beat the market is pointless because anything you know is already in the price, and that rational people who share the same beliefs shouldn't trade with each other at all — what he kept calling the no-trade theorem. Priya's first response was that this proves far too much: billions of shares change hands every single day, so if the theorem described actual markets, volume should be close to zero. Real people disagree, have different liquidity needs, pay taxes, rebalance — the assumptions don't hold, so the theorem is an artefact, like an ideal gas with no collisions. Rob conceded the assumptions break but said that's exactly the point: trade is evidence of disagreement. Every time someone is willing to sell to you, that's a small piece of information pointing against your view, and if everyone updates on it, prices converge — so even the failure of the theorem ends up supporting indexing. Priya then said he can't have it both ways: either the theorem's assumptions hold, in which case nobody trades and there's no meaningful price to trust anyway, or they fail, in which case he's leaning on a broken theorem to support a live conclusion. Rob's reply was that she's demanding too much: he doesn't need the theorem to be literally true, just a decent approximation, and the real test is empirical — if prices could be beaten systematically, someone should produce the audited returns, whereas anomalies get arbitraged away. Priya said that's a bait-and-switch: he started with a conceptual claim (that the market already knows things) and, when challenged, retreated to an empirical one (that it's hard to find anyone who beat it), which are diffrent things — and deciding whether talk of a market 'knowing' is a metaphor or a literal claim about some collective mind is, she says, the actual philosophical question, not one for returns data. Rob muttered that it's the same thing in practice, and there it stalled.

Can you adjudicate this move by move — say who's right at each step, including whether Rob's reasoning that a willing seller is a small piece of evidence against you is actually sound — and tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing? I genuinly don't know whose side I'm on, and having spent a career modelling systems where an idealisation either works or it doesn't, I'd love to know wether the no-trade theorem is a useful idealisation or an empty one.
