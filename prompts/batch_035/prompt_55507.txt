I've come to hold what I think of as the deflationary dissolution of the paradox of tragedy: that there is no paradox at all, because the things we value in a tragic artwork are never the sadness itself. What we enjoy are the representational and formal qualities of the work — the construction of the plot, the language, the intelligence of the pacing — and the sadness is not an object of our enjoyment but the medium through which we appreciate those qualities. The situation is not one of enjoying an unpleasant state, as the classical versions of the paradox assume; it is one of finding something richly valuable that partly involves feeling sad, the way a hard day's climb partly involves fatigue and is not valued despite that. On this view the puzzlement arises only from an equivocation: "we enjoy the tragedy" attributes enjoyment to the whole experience, while "sadness is unpleasant" reports on one constituent of it, and nothing follows about anyone enjoying sorrow as such.

What would count against this? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be. I'm particularly interested in the following, though I don't want to be limited to them.

First, cases of seeking sadness outside art: a mourner who rereads a dead friend's letters precisely because they make her cry, or listeners in a low mood who report seeking out tearjerkers for the crying itself, with no notable formal or narrative virtues to point to. If the deflationist says the letters are "well written" or the sad songs "beautifully composed" as a fallback, that starts to look like a rescue rather than an explanation. Second, empirical work on music-induced sadness: if pleasant sadness were purely a matter of aesthetic appreciation of structural features, we'd expect the enjoyment to track appraisals of craft, and to be absent when listeners report valuing the sadness directly — and there is at least some evidence that listeners do report exactly that direct valuation. Third, whatever pressure comes from the neighbors of the tragedy case: the paradox of horror, where the negative response is arguably disgust and fear rather than sadness, and the question of whether a medium-not-object story generalizes or whether its success in one domain is borrowed credit from another.

There is also what I take to be the structural danger. If any negative affect can be redescribed, after the fact, as an appreciative medium, then the position predicts nothing and excludes nothing: any putative counterexample gets absorbed as one more thing we appreciate "through." So I would like an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and, if it has, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind — a claim about the structure of our engagement with art — or whether something more like insulation than risk is appropriate here. Finally, tell me what a committed deflationist would themselves concede as counting against the view, as opposed to what only the conversion theorists (Humeans who think the sorrow is transformed into pleasure by eloquence) or the contrast theorists (who think pleasure and pain merely coexist) would press, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.
