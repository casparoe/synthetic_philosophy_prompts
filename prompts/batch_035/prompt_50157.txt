For the past year I've been in a reading group that has worked through the pragmatist texts on truth, and we have become stuck in a way I'd like help diagnosing rather than adjudicating. On the Peirce side we have the 1901 definition from Baldwin's Dictionary: "Truth is that concordance of an abstract statement with the ideal limit towards which endless investigation would tend to bring scientific belief, which concordance the abstract statement may possess by virtue of the confession of its inaccuracy and one-sidedness, and this confession is an essential ingredient of truth." On the James side we have Lecture VI of Pragmatism: "True ideas are those that we can assimilate, validate, corroborate and verify," and, more strikingly, "Truth happens to an idea. It becomes true, is made true by events." We also know, as background, that Peirce grew so uneasy with the direction James took that he rechristened his own position 'pragmaticism' to mark the distance — so the split was there from the start, and it has never closed.

Here is how the stall looks from inside. Our Peirceans press two objections: that James lets the useful pass for the true, so that a belief's truth could depend on its being comforting or expedient; and that on James's account the truth of a statement is hostage to events that may not yet have occurred, which makes truth a matter of how the future happens to go. Our Jamesians reply with a symmetrical pair: that Peirce's ideal limit is a promissory note nobody can ever cash, since no inquiry is ever at its end, so nothing is true in Peirce's own present tense; and that a confession of one-sidedness built into a truth-condition, as in the 1901 definition, looks like truth being granted to statements that concede their own partiality — which either is incoherent or makes 'true' a far weaker predicate than Peirceans pretend. Each side finds these objections decisive and the other side's replies evasive, and in twelve months nobody has budged.

My working hypothesis, offered as no more than that: the two camps are not disagreeing about a single thing. Peirce is offering a semantic-cum-regulative account — a definition of 'true' in terms of what inquiry is aimed at, which can hold even if no statement is ever true outright — while James is offering a descriptive account of the epistemic practice by which particular beliefs get accepted, on which truth is episodic and tensed rather than a standing relation. If that is right, the stall is partly verbal: the sides are answering different questions under one heading, one about the concept of truth and one about the process of verification, and the recurring objections each land on the other view only by first re-describing it as an answer to the wrong question. But I am not certain, and I want a diagnosis with reasons rather than a survey of the positions.

Please give me your diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is — verbal, substantive, or two questions under one name — and say what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction, a reformulation, or an argument that none should be expected. Where you rely on interpretive matters that specialists genuinely dispute, mark them as disputed rather than presenting them as consensus; keep clear what is settled scholarship and what is your own reading. Please hold the whole answer to a maximum of 450 words.
