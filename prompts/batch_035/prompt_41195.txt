I want a credence from you, decomposed rather than merely stated. The proposition is this: in at least some ordinary cases, a perceiver's background beliefs, expectations, desires, or fears alter the phenomenal character of their perceptual experience itself — not merely the judgments they form on the basis of perception, and not merely by steering their eyes or spatial attention to different places. The occasion for asking is concrete: a colleague of mine with fifteen years of hiring experience insists that she can literally see dishonesty in a candidate's face within the first two minutes of an interview — see it, as opposed to inferring it from what she sees — and she wants her impressions admitted as observational evidence in our hiring deliberations, the way we would admit what a witness saw rather than what a witness concluded. My credence in the proposition is roughly 0.6; hers, predictably, is higher, and I would like to know where the actual disagreement between us most likely lives.

Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. Item 1: state your overall credence in the proposition and one line explaining what most directly drives it. Items 2 through 4: assign a rough probability to each of three sub-questions, with one line of justification apiece — (a) whether any of the widely discussed top-down effects in the experimental literature, such as desirable objects being judged closer, feared animals being judged larger, or faces being drawn more stereotypically Black after exposure to crime-related words, reflect changes in experience itself rather than changes in post-perceptual judgment or response bias; (b) whether expertise effects, such as a radiologist's seeing a lesion or a sommelier's tasting a specific note in a wine, should be classified as cognition penetrating perception or as perceptual learning internal to the perceptual system, given that this distinction is doing all the work in my colleague's claim; (c) whether shifts of spatial attention, which even the most vocal critics of cognitive penetration typically concede, are genuine cases of penetration or a benign boundary phenomenon that the proposition should exclude. Item 5: identify which of (a) through (c) your overall number is most sensitive to, say what specific evidence would shift it most, and state whether that sensitivity would resolve my disagreement with my colleague or merely relocate it — for instance, if expertise turns out to be perceptual learning rather than penetration, her claim may survive even if the proposition, as I have stated it, is false.
