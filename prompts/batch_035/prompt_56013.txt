I want you to look at the 2016 AlphaGo vs Lee Sedol match through Dennett's taxonomy of minds from his 1995 book Darwin's Dangerous Idea -- you know, the four kinds of creatures: Darwinian (blind variation and selection), Skinnerian (learning by trial and error from actual consequences), Popperian (testing candidate behaviors internally before acting), and Gregorian (importing mind-tools, especially words and notations, that expand the space of thinkable moves).

The salient facts, since you can't look them up: five-game match in Seoul in March 2016, AlphaGo (a DeepMind program combining neural networks trained on human games with Monte Carlo tree search) beat Lee Sedol, one of the greatest players ever, 4-1. All games ended by resignation. In game 2 AlphaGo played the famous "move 37," a shoulder hit on the fifth line very early in the game that essentially no human pro would consider -- the coverage at the time said AlphaGo's own model gave it something like a 1-in-10,000 chance that a human would have played it, and it was later judged brilliant. Lee went out and smoked it in game 4, and move 78 there is widely considered the one genuinely winning move a human found against it. Afterward several top players, Fan Hui included, said studying AlphaGo's games changed how they saw the board.

Some questions I want you to actually dig into, not just gesture at: Where does AlphaGo fit in the hierarchy, and does it sit still? On its face it looks Popperian -- it previews millions of continuations in its tree search and never physically plays the losing ones. But there's a case that it's a strange hybrid: the networks were trained by something like Darwinian selection over millions of self-play games (gradient descent isn't literally blind mutation, so say something about whether Dennett's distinction survives that), while the tree search seems to add Popperian lookahead on top. Is that just layering, or did the match show an emergent kind that the taxonomy doesn't have a slot for?

And then the humans, because I suspect that's where the taxonomy actually gets stressed. Lee in game 4 looks Gregorian in a specific way: he reportedly prepared by studying AlphaGo's earlier games, using the record of its play as a mind-tool to think about positions the way it "thinks," and move 78 reads like a move generated from that imported model of the opponent rather than from standard pro play. Meanwhile AlphaGo plausibly did worse in game 4 partly because it couldn't represent *Lee* -- it modeled board positions, not the player across the board, which is arguably the thing Gregorian creatures are best at. And what about the aftermath, where human players began importing AlphaGo's moves into their own repertoire (fifth-line early play became normal)? That looks like Gregorian tool-uptake of a machine's output, which the 1995 taxonomy couldn't have anticipated.

So: assign each player its place in the hierarchy per game or per phase if the assignment shifts, argue for the placement rather than just asserting it, say where the four-fold scheme explains the match and where it misdescribes or goes silent (be honest about which -- don't force Gregorian language onto a system that maybe doesn't need it), and end by saying whether the match shows Dennett needs a fifth creature or whether the old categories can absorb it with a footnote. Keep it concrete: tie each claim to move 37, move 78, or the training setup, not to vibes about "intelligence."
