I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, and for a course I'm TA-ing on the history of subjective probability I want to run a counterfactual discussion section: what would Frank Ramsey make of things that only happened after he died in 1930? In Truth and Probability he basically treats a degree of belief as something you read off from betting behavior, and he notes (really just in passing) that if your betting quotients violate the probability axioms, a bookie can clean you out. Then de Finetti and others turned that passing remark into the full-blown Dutch book argument we teach today, and more recently the accuracy-first epistemology crowd has argued that betting-based arguments are kind of beside the point and that we should vindicate probabilism purely in terms of how accurate your credences are. My question: if Ramsey could somehow read the last century of this literature, do you think he'd stick with his betting-odds story as the foundation, or would he be persuaded by the accuracy route? He seemed to care about measuring partial belief in a way that connects to action, which maybe favors betting, but he also hated overcomplicating things, and betting arguments do drag in a bunch of arguably artificial machinery (the bookie who somehow knows all your quotients, the assumption you'll take any bet at your stated odds, etc.).

Related spin-off question that came up when I was chatting about this with my advisor: Ramsey was a practical, market-friendly kind of guy by all accounts, so I'm curious what he'd think of prediction markets like the big online ones we have now — places where actual traders' incoherencies get arbitraged away in real time by bots. Is that basically the Dutch book argument running live in the wild, or is it different in a way that matters (e.g. no one is forced to accept every bet at their quoted price, so the normative force against individual agents seems weaker)? And then the modern twist I'd really want his take on: LLMs that give different probabilities to the same proposition depending on phrasing or context — systematically Dutch-bookable across a conversation, yet clearly not "losing money" or betting at all. Would Ramsey say such a system doesn't really have degrees of belief at all, or that it has them and they're just badly incoherent? If you can, ground your guesses in what you know of his actual philosophical temperament (the pragmatism, the views on partial belief, whatever you can glean from his short career) rather than just giving a generic answer. Also happy if you push back on the whole counterfactual exercise as underdetermined — my students will probably try that and I want to know what the best version of that objection is.
