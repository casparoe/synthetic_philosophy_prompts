I have been assembling my verdicts on cases involving paternalism and nudging, and I would like to know whether they amount to a defensible principle or whether something has to give. Here are the cases, each with my gut verdict.

(1) The Treasury automatically enrols private-sector employees into a pension scheme, from which they can opt out at any time. Fine; I think this is one of the better policies of the past two decades.

(2) A council-run café at a leisure centre places the salads at eye level and the chips below the counter. Fine, trivially so.

(3) Parliament makes seatbelts compulsory, on pain of a fine. Fine.

(4) Parliament also caps the size of soft drinks that cinemas may sell. Not fine — and I cannot easily say why, since it crosses a line the seatbelt law does not.

(5) The Treasury raises duty steeply on cigarettes. Fine.

(6) An airline's booking site pre-ticks travel insurance and priority boarding; un-ticking them requires locating a small grey link on a page users are known not to read. Not fine — and this is what bothers me most, because it is structurally the same device as (1): a default, plus friction on one side, with every option left open and no falsehood told. I approve of one and condemn the other, and I do not trust my grounds for the difference.

(7) A friend from whom I have explicitly asked help in quitting hides my cigarettes. Fine.

(8) The same friend hides my cigarettes unasked, having privately judged that I smoke too much. Not fine — even though I accept the state doing something similar in (5), with less of a mandate from me than I gave the friend in (7).

(9) A hospital, on evidence that defaults strongly move choices and that dying patients systematically overvalue resuscitation, pre-ticks "not for resuscitation" on the consent form, the physicians sincerely believing this is best for each patient. I genuinely cannot settle this one, and it is the case I keep returning to.

(10) The state replaces the organ-donation register with a requirement that every adult record a preference, with no default at all. Fine — and I suspect my approval here rests on the thought that no one should choose for me, which sits badly with my approval of the pension default in (1).

I have a tentative principle: steering is legitimate when (a) every option remains available and resistance is cheap, (b) the steerer has standing — a democratic mandate or my explicit request — and (c) the steering is done for the steered person's own good rather than the steerer's. But it misfires: (a) cannot distinguish (1) from (6), since the airline's default is about as easy to resist as the Treasury's; (c) condemns the café in (2) whenever the council also wants the salad revenue, and it seems to bless the resuscitation default in (9) outright, which I find myself unable to accept or reject. Meanwhile (b) cannot explain the difference between (3) and (4), where the standing and the motive are identical and only the intrusiveness and the stakes vary.

So here is what I would like. First, tell me whether there is a single principle — or a small set of candidate principles, if one will not do — that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts; state it precisely, clause by clause, and say which case motivates each clause. Second, tell me what the principle says about a case I have not yet decided: a health authority commissions a smoking-cessation app that, without disclosing it, delays its push notifications by a few minutes whenever it predicts the user is in a weak-willed state, on the theory that the momentary delay lets a resolution reassert itself. Resistance in each instance is trivially cheap, the steerer has public standing, and the aim is entirely the user's own good. Third, tell me what the principle says about the resuscitation default in (9), which is where my own verdicts run out. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, explain why the overridden verdict looked appealing anyway, and say whether you think I should keep it and complicate the principle, or drop it and accept the loss. Please write your answer in British English.
