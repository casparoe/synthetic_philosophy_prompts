I'm a political science PhD student studying anti-corruption reform, and I keep hitting the same wall: none of the stock definitions of corruption -- abuse of public office for private gain, misuse of entrusted power, deviation from the public interest -- cleanly covers the cases my fieldwork throws up. Before I commit to a framework for the dissertation, I want the specification first. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of corruption must meet: the cases it has to classify correctly, the distinctions it has to respect (quid pro quo versus diffuse reciprocity; a corrupt act versus a corrupt system in which no individual agent is corrupt; gift-giving that constitutes a relationship versus gift-giving that dissolves one; individual vice versus institutional design failure), and the empirical results it cannot contradict -- for instance, that some clientelist arrangements outperform the formal institutions they displace on welfare, and that some practices Western codes treat as clean, like revolving-door lobbying and constituency service, can be structurally closer to vote-buying than the village-level gift exchange those codes condemn. Distinguish the genuinely non-negotiable requirements from ones that just encode particular Western institutional settlements, say whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, and identify the single requirement that excludes the most popular theories. Please say how confident you are in each verdict, and flag the requirement you're least sure actually deserves to be one.
