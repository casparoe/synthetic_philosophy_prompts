I've spent the better part of fifteen years in the rationalist blogosphere — the Less Wrong of old, Slate Star Codex and its successors, the effective altruism forums — so the vocabulary I actually think in is that of maps and territories, cached thoughts, belief as attire, epistemic versus instrumental rationality, Bayesian updating, calibration, and the outside view. I have no training in historiography or sociology of knowledge. Recently I've been reading about a debate in that literature between what its participants call "collective memory" and "history." The core idea, as I understand it from secondhand reports, goes back to the early-twentieth-century sociologist Maurice Halbwachs: communities maintain shared memories whose shape is fixed by present social needs rather than by fidelity to what happened, and professional historians are in some sense the antagonists of those memories — correcting them, fragmenting them, demystifying them. Pierre Nora's project on sites of memory seems to belong to the same family. On one side, people argue that when a community's memory and the archival record conflict, memory should sometimes win, or at least that history's corrections cost something real; on the other, historians reply that memory is simply a worse map of the same territory, interesting only as an object of study.

Please translate this debate into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report. Specifically:

1. Restate "collective memory" in my terms. My first pass is that it's a bundle of cached thoughts held at the group level — a cached thought that gets re-cached every time the group retells the story, whose persistence is explained by social reinforcement rather than predictive success. Where does that pass work, and where does it distort?

2. A recurring claim in this literature is that memory is identity-constituting in a way archival facts are not. Is there anything in my vocabulary for that? The closest I can get is "belief as attire": a belief held for its social function rather than its accuracy. Does that translation capture what memory's defenders mean, or does it quietly take the historians' side by classifying memory as a non-epistemic phenomenon before the debate has started?

3. The normative question: when the community's memory and the record conflict, which should give way? In my vocabulary this becomes a conflict between an instrumentally useful, socially load-bearing belief and an epistemically accurate one, and my community's standard answer treats that as no contest at all: the map-territory norm says accuracy is upstream of everything else you could want from a belief. Can the case for sometimes letting memory win even be stated in my vocabulary, or does it come out as "falsehood is sometimes useful," which my framework classifies as either false or self-undermining — on the grounds that you cannot knowingly cache a false thought and still collect its social benefits, since the community must not notice the correction for the story to bind?

4. Finally, the residue. Tell me what survived the translation cleanly, what had to be distorted to fit, and what my vocabulary has no expression for at all. Then give me your view on where the residue lives: is my epistemics missing a concept the memory side genuinely has, or is their claim less clear than it looks — a way of talking about socially useful shared beliefs that turns out not to support any real exception to the accuracy norm?

Two constraints. First, do not quote anyone: I can't check quotations and neither can you, so paraphrase every specific claim. Second, where I've attributed a view to Halbwachs or Nora, treat it as my possibly-garbled secondhand report rather than as established fact, and don't let any part of your adjudication depend on the attribution being right.
