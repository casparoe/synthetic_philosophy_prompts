I chair the heritage subcommitee of a small town council — we're all volunteers, we meet about monthly, and we keep getting proposals about what to do with the past: new plaques, restoring the old mill, renaming streets, adding context to (or removing) a controversial statue, anniversary exhibits at the local museum, that kind of thing. Right now we have no framework, so every debate just goes to whoever feels strongest. I once read that Nietzsche, in an essay about whether history is useful or harmful to the living, split our uses of the past into roughly three modes — invoking great figures as models to aspire to, preserving and treasuring what's old, and harshly judging the past so parts of it can be torn down — and honestly that sounds like the three factions on my committee. Can you turn that into a procedure we could actually run on a proposal? I want the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure should just stop with a no, and where it has to hand the decision back to the committee because it's a question of what the town values, not of fact. Then run it on three proposals we've actually gotten: (1) gild the statue of the 19th-century industrialist who built half this town but used child labor, and add a small plaque about the child labor; (2) preserve a derelict cannery as-is, purely because it's the last of its kind in the region, even though that would eat most of our annual budget — full disclosure, this one's mine and I love that building; and (3) stop running the founding-day festival, since the founders' arrival pushed out the people who were already living here. Please flag where the procedure will misfire, and I'd like at least one case where it gives an answer I won't like (I'm guessing the cannery).
