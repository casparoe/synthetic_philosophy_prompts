I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and somewhere along the way I got attached to a view I'd love to stress-test: subsidiarity is basically a locality principle, like keeping compute close to the data. Decisions should be made at the lowest level that's competent to make them, and every time you hoist a decision up to a higher level (state to federal, canton to confederation, member state to EU) you're paying a round-trip in legitimacy and information loss the same way a remote procedure call costs you latency. The person on the ground has context the center can't have, and the center mostly adds coordination overhead plus a one-size-fits-all response where you needed local state. So my position is a pretty strong one: the burden of proof should always be on the centralizer, and a federal system is healthy roughly to the degree that it defaults to the lowest level. I came to this partly from reading about how Switzerland and Germany distribute competences vs how the US Commerce Clause has ballooned, and partly just from professional instinct -- in distributed systems, centralizing decisions you didn't need to centralize is how you build something slow and brittle.

What would count against it? I want the full map of the view's exposure, not just the obvious "race to the bottom" stuff (though tell me how bad that actually is -- is it fatal or just awkward?). Specifically I'm curious about: cases where local decisions are systematically worse and the locals either can't or won't fix it, externalities that cross borders, and whether there's something structurally different about political decisions vs data locality that breaks my analogy entirely. Also -- and this one bugs me -- I've noticed I can always defend my view by saying "well, that lower level wasn't *competent* to decide that, so subsidiarity didn't really apply," which feels like the way "it's a cache, the miss just goes to the origin" can be stretched to explain anything after the fact. Has the principle been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that fatal for a principle of institutional design or is it fine for principles of this kind (the way "worse is better" is fine in software even though nobody can nail down exactly what counts)? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely embarrasing, and tell me which ones a committed subsidiarity person would concede versus explain away. No need for citations or a reading list -- arguments and cases are what I want, I can look up sources later.
