I teach a political philosophy seminar on distributive justice, and every year the same thing happens: luck egalitarianism gets pronounced dead around week six — Anderson's paper does most of the killing — and then it keeps quietly showing up in people's intuitions for the rest of term. So here's what I want from you: an actual credence, a number, that some version of luck egalitarianism is true. By that I mean the family of views (Dworkin, Arneson, Cohen roughly) on which the pattern of holdings is unjust when it fails to track responsibility: inequalities traceable to option luck are fine, inequalities traceable to brute luck are not.

But I don't want a bare number, I want the decomposition. Something along these lines, or better joints if you see them:

1. Whether the brute/option luck line can be drawn without circularity or without collapsing into "all luck is brute once you trace the causal chain back far enough." Dworkin's hypothetical insurance market is the famous attempt; I want to know whether the rejoinder that the gambler's willingness to gamble in the first place is itself brute luck — temperament, upbringing, whatever — actually breaks it or just pushes the problem one level up.

2. Whether the harshness objection — the reckless motorcyclist who should, on the view, bleed out on the pavement because he chose the risk — is a contingent feature of clumsy formulations or constitutive of the whole responsibility-tracking ambition. I take the expensive-tastes problem to be a symptom of the same disease, so treat them together or tell me why they come apart.

3. The one my students find most alive: does luck egalitarianism secretly need a libertarian, or at least robustly compatibilist, account of free will? If free will skeptics are right that nobody ever has the kind of basic desert-grounding control that could make them deserving of outcomes, does the view just collapse into "equalize everything"? Or can it retreat to something like Scanlon's capacity-responsibility — responsibility as a threshold notion fixed by the point of holding people responsable within a practice — and survive hard incompatibilism more or less intact? I honestly don't know, and I want your probability that the retreat works.

4. Whether relational/democratic equality just absorbs all the defensible content — Anderson again, or Scheffler — leaving luck egalitarianism trivially true as a metric but false as a view about what justice requires.

Give me a probability and one line of justification for each term, then combine them however you think is honest. And don't pretend the terms are independent if they aren't — the free will one obviously isn't independent of the harshness one, so tell me how you handled the correlation rather than just multiplying.

Two colleagues of mine sit at 0.7 and 0.15 respectively on the main question. Tell me which sub-question those two most likely disagree about, and what argument or consideration would actually move them toward each other.

One thing I insist on: keep what's settled separate from what's genuinely contested among people who actually know this literature. Don't write as if Anderson's critique settled the matter in 1999 — plenty of serious people think the view absorbed it — and don't write as if the free will worry is solved in either direction. If you're giving me a minority or contested reading anywhere, flag it as contested. And if writing the decomposition moved your number, say so and say why.
