Please make the case that the classic ordinalist objection to interpersonal utility comparisons proves too much, and that its most defensible version quietly collapses into a skepticism about utility comparisons generally.

The objection I have in mind runs roughly as follows: an individual's utility function, as reconstructed from her preferences, is identified only up to a positive monotonic transformation (affine, under expected-utility assumptions). Since the unit and origin of each person's utility are arbitrary, statements like "a dollar yields more utility to the pauper than to the millionaire" are not merely hard to verify but meaningless: there is no fact of the matter for them to be about.

I want you to argue that this reasoning, applied consistently, also undermines comparisons between the temporal stages of a single individual, and perhaps even comparisons within a person at a time, if utility is ultimately attributed to shifting preference states rather than to an enduring self. Please develop, at minimum, the following lines of thought. First, the claim that intrapersonal comparisons are somehow given "from the inside" does not survive scrutiny: today's stage of a person accesses her own past stages only through the same kinds of evidence a third party would rely on — fallible memory, self-report, physiological observation. Second, if what licenses intrapersonal comparisons is instead the continuity of one underlying physical and psychological system, then the licensing relation is one of degree, and there will be genuine intermediate cases — gradual neural replacement, split-brain patients, conjoined twins with shared affect — that sit between the paradigms of "same person" and "different persons" and so put pressure on the idea that there is a sharp, principled boundary where meaningfulness ends. Third, the economists and welfare theorists who press the objection nonetheless make intrapersonal comparisons across time whenever they model saving, addiction, or lifecycle policy; they therefore face a dilemma — either concede that the boundary they draw is a convention doing no real work, or accept that their position, followed strictly, forbids the practice they themselves rely on every day.

Please argue this as strongly as you can, and close by identifying where the argument is most vulnerable — in particular, whether the defender of the objection can reply that intrapersonal comparisons require only a shared underlying preference system, whereas interpersonal comparisons demand something strictly stronger, so that the analogy between the two cases breaks down just where it matters.
