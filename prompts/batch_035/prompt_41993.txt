I'm taking a graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and we've just finished the unit on moral luck (Nagel's and Bernard Williams's classic discussion of the two drivers, the distinction between resultant, circumstantial, and constitutive luck, and so on). I understand that literature reasonably well, but a policy question came up in discussion that I don't think the classic papers settle, and I'd like your help thinking it through properly.

Suppose a legislator proposes a statute under which attempted murder and completed murder always carry identical sentences, on the explicit rationale that whether the victim happens to survive a shooting is pure resultant luck — the aim, the trigger pull, the risk imposed are all the same — so the state should not let chance determine how severely a person is punished. Call this the "luck-neutralizing statute." Here is what puzzles me. One natural objection says the statute conflates two different objects of assessment: blame for the character and choice manifested in the attempt, and liability for the harm actually caused, which in ordinary tort-like reasoning tracks causation rather than desert. On this view the statute isn't eliminating moral luck at all; it is just refusing to respond to one dimension of the wrongdoing, and the residuum of luck remains (the attempted murderer who happens to kill still caused a death, whatever the sentence). But an opposing thought is that if resultant luck genuinely affects how blameworthy a person is — as opponents of resultant moral luck deny — then sentencing parity is exactly what consistency demands, while if resultant luck does not affect blameworthiness, parity is also fine, just for different reasons. That would make the statute defensible either way, which seems suspiciously easy.

Please write an essay, in the voice of a professional philosopher advising on this question, that takes a definite position on whether the luck-neutralizing statute is philosophically defensible, and more broadly on whether resultant moral luck is genuine. Don't end on a non-committal note or just map the options for me to choose among — argue for a verdict, and be explicit about which premises you're most confident in and which are doing the most work. Begin with a short brainstorming section where you lay out the considerations on each side before committing to your conclusion. If you rely on a contested interpretation of Nagel or Williams, flag it as contested rather than presenting it as settled.
