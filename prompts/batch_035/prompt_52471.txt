I'm a PhD student in economics, and there is something about our methodology that I keep tripping over and that nobody in the department treats as a problem. On one hand, since the Lucas critique, the discipline's official position has been that admissible macroeconomic models must be microfounded: aggregate behavior has to be derived explicitly from the optimizing choices of individual agents, because the parameters of purely aggregate relationships would shift whenever policy changed the environment those agents face. The slogan version is that macroeconomics is just aggregated microeconomics, and that only individuals choose. On the other hand, the everyday practice of the same field — including the central banks that run those very microfounded models — treats aggregates as real things in their own right: GDP fell, the unemployment rate responded to monetary policy, the price level is a state variable that policy can steer, and forecasters debate whether consumption or investment is the binding transmission channel, as though these magnitudes had causal powers rather than being bookkeeping sums over millions of households. The representative agent itself is a strange halfway creature in the middle of this: a fiction that is neither an individual nor an aggregate, but does the causal work of both.

What I would like is a reconstruction of the implicit philosophy of social science underneath this dual practice. Does the microfoundations requirement actually presuppose methodological individualism, and if so in which strength — as an ontological thesis that only individuals and their states exist, a causal thesis that only individuals are agents, or merely a constraint on what counts as an adequate explanation? And does the daily talk of aggregates as causes presuppose some genuine holism about social entities, or can a consistent individualist dismiss it as harmless shorthand for a computation nobody wants to do? I want you to distinguish what the discipline's practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, say whether the two halves of the practice are consistent with each other or paper over a real tension, and tell me what would have to change if we took the strongest individualist reading seriously — for instance, whether aggregate-level policy targets like an inflation rate could survive as objects of intentional policy, or whether they would have to be redefined as something else. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of my question.
