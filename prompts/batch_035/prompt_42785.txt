My grandmother died three weeks ago and I've been in a Madhyamaka reading group since September, so a question I used to find academic is suddenly not academic at all. We've been working through the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā slowly, and my friend Priya (she's studied this stuff for years, way more seriously than the rest of us) has a way of answering existence questions that I've started keeping a little log of. When we asked her whether the self exists — exists, doesn't exist, both, neither, the full four options — she refused all four horns, said the question is already broken the way asking where a fire went when it goes out is broken. But when I asked about the chariot, she happily said it exists conventionally — you can ride it to the market — and doesn't exist ultimately, no chariot-essence over and above the parts and the axle and so on. Sprout arising from seed: same shape of answer, yes conventionally (dependent arising is the one thing she'll affirm all day), no ultimately. But then someone brought up sky-flowers and rabbit horns, and she was adamant those flat-out don't exist, at any level — no "conventionally yes" for those, she was really firm about it. And when we pressed her on whether emptiness itself exists, she said emptiness is empty too, so no, not a real thing, not some hidden essence behind everything, even though she uses the word constantly.

So here's what I want. The group meets again tomorrow and I'm planning to ask her the question I actually care about: does my grandmother exist now, after her death? Before I ask it, I'd like your prediction of what Priya will say, given her track record. And while I'm at it, I'm also planning to ask whether the number seven exists (don't ask, long story, it came up in an argument with my brother about math) — predict that one too. What I really want from you is the reverse-engineering part: what rules could produce her verdicts so far? The obvious candidate is a two-truths rule — everything coherent gets "conventionally yes, ultimately no," and only impossible objects like rabbit horns get the flat no — but I can also see a rule where what matters is whether the question presupposes some essence she doesn't think is there, in which case the self question got all four horns rejected not because of what it was about but because of how it was asked, and that would matter a lot for the grandma question. There might also be something about causal efficacy — the chariot and the sprout do things, the sky-flower doesn't, and where the number seven falls I genuinely can't tell. Give me the candidate rules, what each one predicts about grandma after death and about seven, your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and — since I get to pick follow-up questions for the meeting after tomorrow — tell me which further case would most cleanly separate the rules.
