I teach ethics at a large state school, and through a former student I ended up on the board of a small secular foundation that funds comprehensive sex-ed and contraception access programs in East Africa. At last month's meeting the director walked in with a proposal from one of our biggest ideological opponents — a billionaire who's spent two decades bankrolling abstinence-only programs in the same region. His pitch, and he used this phrase himself, is a moral trade. He's apparently been reading Toby Ord: the idea that people with different values can exchange things such that both come out ahead by their own lights. Concretely: he'll redirect his next $3M, currently earmarked for abstinence-only campaigns, into our contraception programs — and in exchange, we drop the $1M advocacy campaign we'd planned, which was specifically aimed at getting governments to defund his curricula, and move that money into maternal-health work he doesn't object to. He says everyone wins: his money does good instead of what we consider harm, and we were never going to outspend him on advocacy anyway.

The board is split three ways. One faction wants to accept immediately. A second thinks the whole "both sides win" framing is doing sneaky work — our side of the trade isn't $1M of maternal health, it's our silence, and silence about programs we believe are actively harming teenagers isn't priced in the same units as his $3M. The third thinks it's not trade at all but a payoff — taking it doesn't just leave his remaining programs untouched, it makes us complicit in them in a way our prior opposition never did, and the fact that we're richer makes his position stronger.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote. It needs a clear bottom line — accept, reject, or accept with conditions (and if conditions, what they'd actually have to be to address the objections) — plus reasoning the other board members can engage with, and a serious treatment of the strongest point on the losing side, not a strawman. If you think Ord's framework genuinely applies here, show how the trade clears the Pareto bar by each side's own values; if you think this is a case the framework mishandles, say exactly where it breaks. Assume the lawyers have signed off and the money's clean; the question is purely whether we should take the deal.
