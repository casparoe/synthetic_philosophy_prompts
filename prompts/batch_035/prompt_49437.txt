I'm a clinical psychology trainee, and something about the institutional shape of psychotherapy has started to puzzle me. The same field seems to hold three different attitudes toward introspection at once, and I can't tell whether they amount to a coherent implicit theory or just an unexamined mess. First, in CBT, the client's introspective reports are solicited but explicitly distrusted: the thought record asks you to catch an "automatic thought," rate how strongly you believe it, and then subject it to Socratic questioning precisely because the client's own reading of their mind is assumed to be distorted. Second, in humanistic and person-centered traditions, the opposite stance is treated as constitutive of the therapy: the client is described as the expert on their own experience, and the therapist's job is to reflect that self-report back rather than correct it. Third, in mindfulness-based programs like MBSR, clients are trained to attend to passing thoughts and sensations on the assumption that attentional practice makes introspective reports more accurate, or at least less reactive. Meanwhile, the experimental literature the rest of my training draws on — the Nisbett-and-Wilson line of work on confabulation, and more recent introspective skepticism in the philosophy of mind — suggests that people often don't know why they did things and confabulate when asked, and that turning attention inward may sometimes distort rather than reveal.

So here's what I want: treat psychotherapy as an institution with parts, and reconstruct the view or views of introspection it presupposes. Does the CBT part presuppose something like a reliabilist story, where introspection is a fallible instrument whose errors are systematic enough to be correctable? Does the person-centered part presuppose a kind of first-person authority that the CBT part quietly denies, or can both be licensed by the same underlying theory? And what about the mindfulness part — does training attention to inner states presuppose that introspection has a truth-tracking core that can be sharpened, which the confabulation results seem to threaten? I want you to distinguish what the practice strictly requires (a theory without which therapy could not work as practiced) from what it merely fits well with (theories that would license it but aren't forced), and to say where the whole enterprise is underdetermined — for instance, maybe therapy only needs introspective reports to be useful, not accurate, in something like the way a compass that's consistently off by twenty degrees can still steer you.

Please take a definite position rather than surveying options: tell me whether you think the institution's implicit epistemology of introspection is coherent, which of the three attitudes, if any, has to give, and what concretely would have to change in clinical practice if a thoroughgoing introspective skepticism were true — whether that's just a matter of re-describing what therapists are doing, or whether some part of the edifice actually falls. I have to write a paper arguing a thesis, not listing considerations, so I'd rather you committed to one and defended it.
