I teach a world history survey at a community college, and I keep bumping into the same wierd thing about periodization, and I want someone to think through it with me like it's a real philosophical question, not just curriculum griping. So assume you're a philosopher of history with a soft spot for questions about narrative and standpoint, think carefully before answering, and maybe start with some brainstorming of candidate theories before you commit to one.

Here's the practice I'm pointing at. Every textbook, syllabus, and popular history I know slices the past into Antiquity, the Middle Ages (roughly 476 to 1492), and the Modern period (starting with 1492 or 1789). Those breakpoints are all European events: the fall of Rome, Columbus, the French Revolution. But the people in Mesoamerica at the moment of contact weren't in their "Middle Ages" — 1492 isn't the end of an era for them, it's the arrival of one. Same for Ming China or the Mughals: they get slotted in as episodes inside a frame whose underlying story is basically "how did the West get to where it is." And here's the puzzle: historians will all say, in the abstract, that Eurocentrism is bad — alot of them sincerely believe it — and then the same historians keep teaching the same spine anyway. So what I want from you is this: what does that practice actually presuppose? That history is the story of the present's origins — and if so, whose present? That significance is always significance-for-somebody's-story, so some standpoint is unavoidable and the European one is just an unacknowledged choice? Or is it mostly inertia — a comfortable fit that nothing strictly requires?

Then there's the harder part. Suppose we replaced the European spine. People have tried telling it through silver, or cotton, or the Black Death instead — but doesn't that just relocate the standpoint rather than eliminating it? A commodity has a story too, and it's not everybody's story. And the limit case that really bugs me: if we insisted on telling the past from everywhere at once, wouldn't that mean no selection at all — and isn't unselected history just a chronicle or an archive, not a story any student could follow? My working hunch is that it's like map projections: every projection distorts, none is the true one, and you pick for a purpose. But I don't know if that analogy survives contact with the actual practice, so stress-test it.

Concretely, I'd like you to do the reconstruction work: say what the periodization practice strictly presupposes as opposed to what it merely fits well with, say where it's philosophically underdetermined such that several rival theories of historical significance would license the exact same spine, and tell me what a syllabus would have to look like if the implicit view were swapped out — would it be parallel timelines, no periods at all, something else? And at the end, please tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would flip your verdict about whether the European spine is avoidable, or necessary, or neither — and where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to fail. I'd rather have an honest "here's where this could fall apart" than a confident summary that paper(s) over the seams. Also, feel free to disagree with my framing entirely if you think periodization isn't even the right place to locate the Eurocentrism — maybe the real commitment lives somewhere else, like in what counts as an "event" in the first place. If you go that way, argue for it, don't just assert it.
