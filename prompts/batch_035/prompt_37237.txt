As a retired neurophysiologist who has spent the last few years working through philosophy of mind and aesthetics, I have arrived at what looks to me like an empirical dissolution of a much-discussed puzzle, and I would like to know whether I have actually dissolved it or merely relocated it.

The paradox of fiction, as I understand it, is supposed to run roughly as follows: readers and audiences genuinely feel pity for Anna Karenina or fear for the protagonist of a thriller; yet they believe, and know full well, that no such person exists; and it is supposedly irrational or impossible to be afraid of, or for, something one believes does not exist. Hence paradox.

It seems to me, however, that this puzzle is an artifact of a pre-scientific conception of the emotions, and that it evaporates as soon as one treats an emotion as a response to a stimulus rather than as a propositional attitude. The text on the page, the actor's face, the orchestra's crescendo — these are perfectly real physical stimuli, and there is nothing mysterious about real stimuli producing real affective and autonomic states. We know from ordinary laboratory work that reading a suspenseful passage alters skin conductance and heart rate, and we know that subjects show marked arousal during fire drills while flatly denying that they believe any fire is present. If a drill can frighten a person who knows no fire is burning, a novel can frighten a person who knows no train is coming. The emotional system was never consulting the belief system about the existence of the object in the first place; it is responding to the representational content of the stimulus. So the 'paradox' looks like a confusion between the semantics of the object and the causality of the stimulus.

Given this, my question: why is the case still treated as a genuine philosophical problem rather than a psychological curiosity about arousal? Is what remains merely verbal — a dispute about whether the word 'fear' ought to be reserved for states whose objects are believed to exist — or have I misdescribed the paradox in some way that my physiological story fails to touch? I would appreciate it if you could tell me precisely where, if anywhere, my dissolution goes wrong.
