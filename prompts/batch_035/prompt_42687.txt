I'm a sculptor who works almost exclusively in unfired clay, and a question from a collector has pushed me into something I can't put a name to. She owns an early figure of mine that has dried and cracked, and she wants me to restore it by working fresh clay into the damaged areas — but first she wants to know whether the restored object will be the same work, the same thing she bought, or a new one. I've realized I have to answer two questions that I'd always assumed were one. Whether the work survives is one matter: I'd say yes, since a work can survive the replacement of its material, the way a building survives renovation. Whether the clay survives is another: some of the original clay is already gone, and if the restoration is extensive enough, one day none of it will remain. And these two things — the work and the clay — are, at every moment between my hands and her shelf, in exactly the same place, made of exactly the same stuff, weighing the same, costing the same to ship, and yet they disagree about their own biographies: the clay existed days before the work did, since I bought it and let it sit in a bag, and it will apparently outlive the work if she ever decides the restored figure no longer counts as the early piece.

Since then I notice the pattern everywhere and can't get a handle on it. Standing in my studio I can truthfully say that exactly one thing is on the pedestal, but I can also truthfully say that there are two things there with different ages and different futures — a dog-shaped thing that began Tuesday and a portion of clay that began at the supplier's or even earlier. If I squash the figure into a ball, I seem to destroy the one while merely inconveniencing the other; if a piece of clay dries overnight into something that by accident resembles a dog, I have no intuition at all about how many things have appeared. When restorers rebuild a temple column by column, we say the temple survived and never ask after the stone, whereas with my figure the clay is exactly what I track. So my question is really several questions. Is there an established name — in philosophy or anywhere else — for this situation: for things that share every current physical detail yet differ in when they began, when they will end, and what could happen to them? Is there also a name for the linguistic behavior that keeps tripping me up, of counting one thing or two depending on which kind of thing I've been asked to count? And are those actually one phenomenon or two rival descriptions of a single situation — the two-objects-in-one-place picture versus the one-object-counted-two-ways picture?

I'm not after a bare label. What I'd value is knowing how people who have thought about this longest carve the territory: which of my cases they treat as central and which as peripheral (the accidental dog, the restoration, or a person and their body, which now worries me for the same reason), where the edges of the concept sit, and which of the rival pictures is supposed to handle the cases where my intuitions simply go quiet. The lawyers, the collector, and I keep using the word "it" as though it had a single referent, and I'd like to know what the disagreement is really about before deciding whether it matters.
