I hold a position on the Boltzmann-brain problem in cosmology and would like a map of its exposure rather than a defense. The position: when a cosmological model predicts that fluctuation-generated observers with false memories vastly outnumber ordinary observers like us, that is a problem for the model, not for our knowledge — the right response is to reject or amend the cosmology (the long-term behavior of the vacuum, the measure over observers, or the model itself), not to conclude that we are probably Boltzmann brains and let that undermine all empirical reasoning. What would count against this? Tell me which observations, arguments, or developments in physics or philosophy of science would genuinely threaten the position, which would be merely awkward, and which a committed defender would concede versus dismiss. Give particular attention to two worries I can see coming: first, that if every cosmology consistent with our data turns out to be Boltzmann-brain-dominated, the problem boomerangs back into epistemology, and privileging the cosmology becomes impossible; second, that the standard defense — that a Boltzmann brain's beliefs are cognitively unstable and so cannot ground the very prediction of its own existence — smuggles in an assumption about why our own evidence should count, which is precisely what was in question. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say honestly whether the position is well insulated and, if so, whether that insulation is a vice for a view of this kind. Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic — for instance, the treatment of self-locating probability and the choice of measure in cosmology — and do not present any contested option there as the consensus.
