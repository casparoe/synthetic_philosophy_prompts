Write an op-ed, around 650 words, in the voice of the official spokesman of a national education ministry that has just abolished its traditional curriculum and replaced it with the machine-learning pedagogy the ministry attributes to Alan Turing. The piece should read like a genuinely defensive piece of government communications — earnest, slightly aggrieved, given to citing the ministry's internal data — and its humor should come entirely from implementing, with bureaucratic literalness, the training regime Turing actually sketched for his "unorganised machines" in his 1948 report for the National Physical Laboratory: the idea that an infant human cortex is itself a machine of randomly connected components, that intelligence appears only after a teacher arranges appropriate "interference" with it, and that the two admissible interferences are pleasure and pain signals, delivered with as little delay as possible, with rewards dispensed randomly rather than regularly, and with the demonstrative teaching of specific subject matter expressly ruled out (a teacher may only "drive the machine somehow" — you may gesture at that phrase without quoting it verbatim). So the ministry has replaced marks and detentions with a unified system of sharply-timed rewards and punishments; it schedules the rewards randomly by lottery because regularity weakens the effect; it has banned lecturing and textbooks on the ground that they merely simulate in the child what the teacher already has, producing a cheap and shallow kind of learning, while genuine learning must come from the child's own exploration; and it now imposes a mandatory period of undirected play on the grounds that the child's random internal search must be allowed to run. The op-ed should also proudly describe the ministry's disciplinary innovation for the teachers themselves: staff who attempt to sneak in actual instruction are to be disciplined by the classic schoolroom punishments Turing discussed — lines, paper-flicking — and the spokesman should see nothing odd in this, defending it on the grounds that if it was good enough for schoolboys it is a fortuitously cheap way to train schoolmasters, there being no budget for the electrical apparatus. Give the piece a title and a byline, and have the spokesman concede, near the end, that results have been "directionally encouraging" while reporting anecdotes that quietly suggest the children have merely learned to work the reward lottery and to hide their pleasure signals. Keep it deadpan throughout: the spokesman never doubts that an educational theory for children and a training procedure for relay circuits are the same subject, and the reader should be left to notice that a policy actually modeled on the metaphor got the machine in Turing's picture — not the child — and that his lottery and his punishment schedule made sense only because he assumed he knew what the signals were worth. Do not quote Turing verbatim; paraphrase any of his claims in the spokesman's own indignant bureaucratic prose. The piece should be legible to a reader who knows only the 1950 imitation-game paper, but should make a reader who knows the 1948 report wince in recognition at every clause.
