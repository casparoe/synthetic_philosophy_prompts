I'd like a two-directional case study: the decriminalization of homosexual acts in England and Wales, read through the lens of Jeremy Bentham's views on sexual morality, and then read back — Bentham's theory as an instrument for analyzing the episode, and the episode as a test of the theory. Here are the facts I want the analysis to rest on, so that nothing turns on contested detail.

The episode: the Wolfenden Committee reported in 1957, recommending that homosexual behavior between consenting adults in private no longer be a criminal offence; Patrick Devlin attacked that recommendation, arguing that society may legitimately use the criminal law to enforce its shared morality, since that shared morality is what holds society together; H. L. A. Hart replied, on essentially Millian grounds, that only harm to others, not the enforcement of popular moral sentiment, can justify punishment; and the Sexual Offences Act 1967 partially decriminalized the acts in England and Wales — but only for men over 21, in private, while the age of consent for heterosexual acts was 16.

The lens: around 1785 Bentham wrote a long manuscript essay arguing for the decriminalization of consensual homosexual acts between men, at a time when the offence was capital in England. He argued that the acts do not weaken men, do not threaten population, and do not undermine marriage — the harms then alleged against them; that in a private, consensual act no one's pain is increased and someone's pleasure is, so punishment is a deliberate subtraction of happiness with no countervailing gain; that the only victims anyone can name once this is conceded are imaginary, namely God and society in the abstract; and that the objection that survives is bare antipathy, which he treats as the prejudice of an age dressed up as moral principle. He withheld the essay from publication for fear of offending public morality, and it first appeared in print only in 1978. Nobody involved in the 1950s and 60s debate, on either side, had any idea it existed.

Against that background, I want the following:

1. A careful mapping of Bentham's argument onto the reasoning actually used in the episode. The committee's operative move was a distinction between private morality and public decency; Hart's was the harm principle; Devlin's was the claim about social cohesion. Say which of these, if any, is Bentham's argument, which are alien to it, and where the difference between his ground and the ground actually used mattered — including whether the harm principle as Hart deployed it contains anything (room for offense, or for protecting people from themselves) that Bentham's calculus would reject.

2. An assessment of Bentham's likely verdict on the Act as actually passed, not on the Act as Bentham would have written it. The calculus prices pains and pleasures, so the asymmetrical age of consent is in principle something it can evaluate: work out what Bentham's method would say about decriminalizing at 21 what was lawful at 16 — as a mispriced expectation of harm, as a stigmatizing insult whose pain the calculus can count, or as something the framework simply cannot see. If it cannot see it, say so plainly rather than stretching the theory, and note whether the Millian route that won the day handles it better.

3. Devlin's central claim, that eroding a shared morality dissolves society, is on Bentham's method an empirical claim about future pains, and therefore in principle a measurable one. Take it seriously on Bentham's own terms rather than dismissing it: what would a legislator in 1967, equipped only with the calculus, have needed to establish in order to price Devlin's claim, and could anything then known have established it either way?

4. Finally, the uncomfortable result. Bentham reached the right conclusion roughly 180 years early, and could not deliver it: he suppressed the essay out of a calculation about the consequences of publishing it, which by his own lights was an application of his method. The argument was complete but inert; the work was eventually done by people arriving through a different, Millian gate. What does that show about the theory — that utilitarianism can get the answer right but cannot by itself change what a society is prepared to hear? And note that this seems to concede Devlin's premise, that morality is held and enforced socially, rather than refuting him. I want that last point taken seriously, not treated as a rhetorical flourish.

Throughout, keep the mapping disciplined: at each step distinguish what belongs to Bentham's texts, what belongs to the historical actors, and what is the analyst's own contribution. Where the theory is silent, say so instead of forcing it to speak. And please give me the reasoning only — no reading lists, no bibliographic apparatus.
