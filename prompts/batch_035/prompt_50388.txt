I've been trying to figure out what I actually think probability is by cataloguing my gut verdicts on particular probability statements, and I'd like to know whether some interpretation — frequentist, propensity, subjective, or some hybrid — captures the pattern, and which of my verdicts has to go.

My verdicts, stated as honestly as I can:

1. A fair die about to be rolled: the probability of a six is 1/6, full stop. And I think the 1/6 is what explains why long-run frequencies come out near 1/6, not the other way around.

2. A single radium atom: the probability it decays within the next half-life is genuinely 1/2, and this is a fact about that atom, not about anyone's ignorance and not about what other atoms do. It would still be 1/2 if this were the only atom in existence.

3. A card has been dealt face down. For me, the probability it's the ace of spades is 1/52 — but I want to say that the *real* probability, the one that's out there, is 0 or 1, and my 1/52 is mere ignorance rather than probability properly so called.

4. A coin-flipping machine I know nothing about turns out to be deterministic: given today's humidity, it always lands heads. Before the flip I say 1/2 — and here I part ways with verdict 3, because I don't want to say my 1/2 was mere ignorance of the sort in the card case. Yet the two situations look structurally identical: a fixed fact, hidden from me. I cannot see what grounds the difference, but my gut insists there is one.

5. Polls put a candidate at 70% to win an election. I take that as a legitimate probability, but not the same kind of thing as the atom's 1/2: two reasonable people, differently informed, could give 65% and 75% without either being wrong — which surely can't happen with the atom.

6. A coin of unknown bias. I assign 1/2 to the first flip by symmetry of ignorance alone, and it feels like the *same* 1/2 as a verified fair coin's, even though nothing in the physical setup guarantees anything. Is that 1/2 about anything in the world?

7. The billionth digit of pi. I'd happily say a randomly chosen digit of pi is a 7 with probability 1/10, and I'd offer 9-to-1 against anyone who guessed a specific value for the billionth digit; yet I balk at saying the probability that the billionth digit is 7 equals 1/10 — that one feels like it's 0 or 1, pi being what it is. I can't articulate what changed between those two sentences, or whether anything did.

8. A loaded die that will be thrown exactly once and then destroyed. I think it has a probability of showing six, even though no frequency for it will ever exist.

My tentative principle is a two-grade one: there are real chances — physical properties of chance setups like dice and atoms — and there are credences, and a probability statement is true in the strict sense only when it reports a chance. Please test this principle against my verdicts and repair or replace it as needed. In particular, tell me what it should say about a case I keep going back and forth on: an atom's decay has been recorded by a detector an hour ago, but the readout is sealed until tomorrow — did the atom's probability of decaying drop from 1/2 to 0 or 1 at the moment of detection, when the readout is opened, or never, and what does that imply about which of my verdicts above must be abandoned? I'd like a precise statement of the resulting account, the verdict that motivates each of its clauses, and an honest accounting of which of my eight verdicts it cannot save.
