I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-readership outlet, which makes me basically ground zero for this fight: my editors want headlines about Gen Z, and a good chunk of the researchers I interview want me to stop using the word entirely.

Here's where the argument has stalled, and what I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on who's right. On one side you have the generational skeptics, mostly quantitative social scientists. Their case is pretty crisp: you can't cleanly separate age effects (people get more conservative as they age), period effects (a recession hits everyone at once), and cohort effects (something specific to people born around a given time), because the three are mathematically entangled — if you know someone's age and the year, you know their birth cohort, so no model can pull them apart without extra assumptions nobody agrees on. And on top of that, whenever anyone actually measures it, differences between cohorts look tiny compared to differences within a cohort — plenty of 25-year-olds are more risk-averse than their parents. So the skeptics conclude that Boomers/Millennials/Gen Z are marketing categories with arbitrary cutoffs, and that responsible writers should drop them.

On the other side — some sociologists, honestly mostly normal people, and, uh, my editor — the reply is that the skeptics are testing the wrong thing. Nobody who says 'Gen Z is real' means that everyone born 1997–2012 shares a personality trait that shows up on a questionnaire. The claim is about shared location in history: what events hit you at which ages. That's why a Millennial born in 1981 was a young adult on 9/11 and one born in 1996 was in kindergarten, and why that difference matters even if both score the same on some scale. The skeptics then say this version is unfalsifiable — no survey could ever refute it — and round and round we go. It's been going in that circle for years, and I've watched the same exchange happen at conferences, on podcasts, and in my own Slack.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? My half-baked theory is that it's mostly verbal: 'generation' is doing double duty as a statistical control variable and as a cultural-historical kind, and the two camps are talking past eachother while thinking they're arguing about the same thing. But I'm genuinely unsure, because the unfalsifiability charge feels like it lands on something real too — maybe they don't just mean different things, maybe one side is making a claim that couldn't be wrong no matter what. Or maybe it's substantive but hard: there really is a fact of the matter about whether cohort-level cultural kinds exist, and we just can't measure it yet.

Diagnose it for me, using what each side actually says. And then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a conceptual split into two words, some study design that could in principle embarrass one side, a truce where the skeptics concede the cultural kind and the enthusiasts concede the statistics? Also be honest about whether I should keep writing 'Gen Z' in the meantime. The standard skeptic advice — use precise birth years and describe what happened to those people at what age — is correct but reads like garbage in a lede, and I'd like to know whether that's a real epistemic problem on my end or just a style problem.
