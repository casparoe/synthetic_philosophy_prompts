My roommate has gotten really into the 'for want of a nail' thing lately — the old rhyme where losing a horseshoe nail costs the horse, then the rider, then the battle, and finally the whole kingdom — and he now deploys it in bascially every argument we have about history. The latest one: I said WWI was probably overdetermined, that the July crisis had so many people itching for a war that if Franz Ferdinand's driver hadn't taken that wrong turn, some other spark would have set it off within a year anyway. And he countered with the rhyme like it settles the matter: small stuff matters, tiny causes cascade, the whole thing hinged on a nail.

It struck me afterwards that the rhyme could be making a bunch of totally different claims, and I can't figure out which one (if any) is doing the work he needs. Like, is it saying the nail was a necessary link in a chain where every link is individually necessary — knock out any one and the kingdom falls, so the nail matters exactly as much as the general? Is it a claim about sensitive dependence, butterfly-effect stuff — history as chaotic system, so tiny perturbations get amplified into huge outcomes even when they're not part of some neat necessary chain? Or is it actually not a causal claim at all but a rhetorical one about how history gets written: once you know the kingdom fell, you go hunting backwards for the 'decisive' detail, and the rhyme just names whatever detail you happened to find? Or even a moral exhortation about attending to small things in general, in which case it's got nothing to do with the WWI question?

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that rhyme could be expressing — from trivial to substantive to straight-up false — and for each say whether it's plausible and, crucially, whether it actually rebuts the overdetermination point or just talks past it? My hunch is that in a genuinely overdetermined situation the proverb is false on its most natural reading, but I want to know whether any reading survives redundancy, and whether any of those readings is the one people naively mean when they recite it. Also curious where Mackie's INUS conditions fit in here — if historians calling something 'a cause' often mean it was neither necessary nor sufficient on its own, just one redundant route to the outcome, does the proverb even make sense as a claim about causes under that view, or does it secretly assume the old necessary-and-sufficient picture? And is my roommate allowed to switch readings mid-argument — start with the fragility reading to get my attention, then quietly fall back on the 'how history gets written' reading when the fragility one fails? That last move feels cheap but I can't quite say why.
