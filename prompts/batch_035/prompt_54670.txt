I recently joined a small nonpartisan institute that advises municipal governments on the design of participatory budgeting processes and citizens' assemblies, and my first assignment has put me in unfamiliar territory. In our internal discussions, and in pushback from a city attorney, I keep meeting a claim I half-remember from a graduate seminar on social choice: that Sen's liberal paradox shows individual rights and collective efficiency are formally incompatible, so any procedure that gives citizens decisive say over some questions while letting the majority overrule them on others is somehow incoherent, and our designs are doomed to cycle or to quietly violate one value or the other. The institute can field about four analysts over the next two to three years and has access to a couple of city pilots, and I have been asked to draft an internal memo on what, if anything, the paradox should actually change about our work. What I want from you is not a survey of the literature but a defended ranking of the practical implications: which alleged consequences of the liberal paradox carry real weight for institutional design and which are artifacts of the formalism that dissolve once you leave the framework of social decision rules. The candidates I can see, roughly, are these: first, the strict reading that no rule can both assign individuals rights over pairs of social states and satisfy even weak Pareto, so rights must be made conditional or overridable rather than absolute; second, the externalities reading, on which the conflict arises only when my choices enter your preference ordering, and the real design problem is structuring issues so that this overlap is minimized; third, the game-theoretic reading, where a rights-respecting outcome can still be an equilibrium and the paradox becomes a coordination or information problem that facilitation and sequencing can address; and fourth, the deflationary reading that the theorem constrains only a narrow class of aggregators and says nothing about procedures that allow agenda-setting, deliberation, or preference change over time, which is what assemblies actually are. I do not want you to simply endorse my list; tell me which items are missing, which of these matters most for a group like ours, and why, in terms of how many other design choices depend on it, how much a small policy group without a formal modeling background can act on it, and what we would learn from pilot data that would move items up or down the ranking. It would also help to know which of the four, if any, we should stop worrying about entirely, since a memo that tells a city everything is urgent will be read as telling it nothing.

Please write the response as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a letter to a colleague — and open with your overall verdict on whether the paradox deserves a place in our design thinking at all, before defending the ranking.
