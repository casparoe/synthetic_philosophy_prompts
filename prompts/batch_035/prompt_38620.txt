I work at a small ai safety org, mostly on the edges of mech interp (I do evals, my colleagues do the actual circuits stuff), and I've been asked to write up a short "where I stand" doc on whether models really have representations, so the team can argue with me at our next offsite. Here's what I've got so far and I want to know if it actually hangs together before I embarrass myself. My commitments, roughly: (1) when we find a feature via sparse autoencoders that fires on French text and, when steered, makes the model translate, that's a real feature the model has, not just a convenient story we tell about its weights — the model's internals have their own ontology and it's our job to discover it, not invent it. (2) Features live in directions rather than neurons; superposition is real and it's the whole reason a 4096-dim residual stream can behave as if it contains many more than 4096 things. (3) There's no privileged basis — if you rotated the embedding space and counter-rotated everything downstream you'd get a behaviorally identical model, and I don't want to say one of the two contains the real features while the other contains fakes. (4) All the facts about a model are fixed by its weights plus its input distribution; nothing else is needed, no extra semantics floating in from outside. (5) I'm a functionalist about content in humans and want to say the same for models: what makes a state a representation of X is the causal role it plays, full stop. (6) Practically, whether honesty probes and oversight schemes mean anything depends on whether the model's features line up with ours — if there's no internal thing that tracks what we mean by deception, the audit is theater. (7) Two models from different seeds grow different internal ontologies and that's fine with me, each one really has its own. And (8) I roll my eyes a bit when people say the model "understands" its outputs — it's doing next-token prediction, and saying a feature is "about" deception is our gloss on the weights, useful shorthand, not a literal description of something the model does.

Go through the whole list and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, and what the cheapest repair is in each case. Rank them by severity rather than dumping everything in one list — some of these I suspect just dissolve under a little care, like I've had people tell me (2) and (3) are inconsistent and I've never seen it. Then tell me which single revision cleans up the most. I honestly can't tell where the real problem is, though I have a nagging feeling it's somewhere in the middle of (1), (3), and (4) — like if the weights fix everything and the basis is arbitrary, I'm not sure what fact could make it the French feature rather than some rival decomposition. If it helps, also say what the repaired package implies for a question we argue about constantly: when we add a steering vector to make a model more honest, are we editing its beliefs, or just perturbing a system that never had any? I'd like the answer to come out of the commitments rather than vibes, and I'd rather find out now than at the offsite.
