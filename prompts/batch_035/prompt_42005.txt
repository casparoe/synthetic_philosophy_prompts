I am a PhD student in philosophy of language, and my department has just concluded a year-long seminar in which two of our faculty have argued past each other about Frege's puzzle without ever producing anything written down. One is a Millian about proper names: he holds that a name contributes its bearer, and nothing further, to the semantic content of sentences containing it, so that "Hesperus is Phosphorus" and "Hesperus is Hesperus" express the very same proposition. The other is a Fregean: she holds that names have senses — modes of presentation — that are distinct from their referents and can differ even when reference does not, so that the two sentences say different things, which is why the one can be informative where the other cannot. I have been asked to draft the joint statement that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between the two of them should have produced.

The statement should contain, in this order. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: what exactly the phenomenon is that any theory must accommodate (a subject ignorant of the co-reference can learn something from "Hesperus is Phosphorus" that she could not learn from "Hesperus is Hesperus"); that the difference cannot be located in the referents, since they are identical; that substitution of co-referential names preserves truth-value in extensional contexts while failing in belief contexts; that compositionality must somehow be preserved. Formulate these so that each side accepts them because of its theory rather than despite it — a claim as weak as "names refer to their bearers" is not worth signing. Second, a disagreements table with one row per residual disagreement: whether the informativeness of the identity statement is a semantic or a merely pragmatic fact; whether the proposition expressed by a belief report containing two co-referential names is constant across substitution; whether anything that plays the role of a sense is needed in the theory of communication. Give each side's position in wording that side would itself accept, not a caricature.

Third, a cruxes section. I suspect one crux is whether the Millian's preferred apparatus — metalinguistic propositions, implicature, "ways of believing" rather than ways of referring — can carry the explanatory load without quietly reintroducing senses under another label; and another is whether the Fregean can state non-circular identity conditions for senses without collapsing them into associated descriptions. For each crux, identify a concrete case both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case recorded in advance. At least one case should be the following stipulated one, which you may refine: Klara meets a man at a conference under the name "Victor" and later corresponds at length with an author signing himself "V. Hesse", never suspecting they are the same person; she assents to "Hesse is scrupulously honest" and dissents from "Victor is scrupulously honest". State what each party predicts about the truth-value of "Klara believes that Victor is scrupulously honest", about whether Klara's belief state is consistent, and about what would happen if she discovered the co-reference.

Finally, include a short verdict on which parts of the dispute are genuine and which, if any, are merely verbal — in particular whether "semantic content" is being used in two different senses by the two parties, such that the disagreement over whether informativeness is semantic or pragmatic turns partly on that ambiguity. Where a disagreement is verbal, say precisely which term is doing the damage and what each side means by it. The document should be one that both faculty members could put their names to without embarrassment, and the residual disagreement should be made exact enough that a third party could tell which of its predictions failed.
