Here's the thing I've landed on after years working in paintings conservation, and I'd like to stress-test it. My view is that the value of an original artwork isn't in how it looks at all — it's in its causal-historical connection to the artist. The original is the object that was actually in the room, that the artist actually pushed paint around on and looked at and revised. A duplicate that's perceptually identical, down to the craquelure, still has none of that history, and I think the public is basically right to care. My go-to way of putting it to friends: the original is more like a relic than like a superior copy of a file. When I stand in front of a real Rembrandt self-portrait, part of what I'm responding to is that this is the actual surface he painted while staring at his own aging face, and no reproduction, however perfect, has that.

But my colleague thinks I'm being sentimental, and she's got a few cases that genuinely bug me. One is studio practice: lots of old master paintings are partly workshop, executed by assistants under the master's supervision with maybe a few passages from his own hand. Once the attributions get revised, the price and the esteem drop — but the object's causal connection to the artist barely changed, just our knowledge of it. Is that a problem for me, or does it just show the connection comes in degrees that track facts on the ground? Another one is music: for a symphony there's no privileged object at all, the work is more like a type, and nobody thinks the composer's manuscript is the "real" symphony in the way the canvas is the real painting. Does my view just not apply there, and if so why is painting different? And then there's the deflationary story she keeps pushing: there's psych research on celebrity contagion, where people value a sweater more just because some famous person owned it, and kids do something similar — maybe my "relic" talk is just dressed-up magical thinking about contact and ownership, and the value of originals is status and contagion, nothing deeper. Also, being a conservator, I know how little original material can be left in a heavily restored painting and it still counts as "the" original — sometimes honestly very little. So the causal connection I'm gesturing at might be so elastic that it can't be pinned down.

So: what would actually count against my view? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including thought experiments, real cases like the van Meegeren Vermeers (where top critics sincerely preferred the fakes while they believed them real — I honestly can't tell whether that cuts for me or against me), and whatever empirical psychology is relevant. Tell me which of these a committed defender of the causal-historical view would concede as counting against them and which they'd shrug off, and be blunt about whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability by all this talk of "degrees of connection." And flag where you're least confident — I'd rather know the soft spots than get a confident gloss over them.
