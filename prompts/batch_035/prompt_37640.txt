Our department's philosophy-of-mind workshop runs a recorded interview series, and this term's guest is a philosopher who has spent much of her career defending the phenomenal concept strategy as the physicalist's answer to the knowledge argument. She is sharp, patient with lay audiences, and expert at deflecting hard questions with the remark that the strategy is a research program about concepts, not a theory of consciousness itself. I am preparing the question list, and I want it to do more than let her restate the view. Draft eight to ten interview questions meeting the following constraints:

1. At least one question must press the suspicion that the strategy relabels rather than solves the problem: if phenomenal concepts are sui generis, then saying that Mary acquires no new fact, only a new concept, seems to relocate the mystery into why these particular concepts track their referents in the peculiar way they do — their mode of presentation appears to wear the phenomenal character on its sleeve. Anticipate her likely reply that other special-purpose concepts, such as demonstratives and indexicals, behave similarly without threatening physicalism, and include a follow-up that presses on the disanalogy: a demonstrative refers without acquainting, whereas grasping a phenomenal concept of a sensation seems to put one in a relation to it that no amount of third-person description supplies.

2. At least one question must force her to say exactly what epistemic relation Mary stands in when she leaves the room: an ability, on the older ability-hypothesis model; acquaintance with instances, on the acquaintance hypothesis; a new recognitional or quotational concept; or a piece of genuine non-instrumental propositional knowledge such as knowing what red looks like. Ask her to state what, on her view, Mary still lacks while confined to the black-and-white room, and include a follow-up keyed to the reply that what is acquired is a way of fixing reference in the first person rather than a new piece of information about the world.

3. One question should be built around a robotic counterpart of Mary — call her RoboMary — who is kept from receiving color input while she constructs an internal working model of her own color-processing channels and, using that model, correctly predicts and even simulates what she will experience when the restriction is lifted. Ask which of the two scientists, the human or the robot, is supposed to be missing what, and include a follow-up about whether the physicalist must say the robot has everything the human gains, and why that verdict should not itself be taken as a reductio of the strategy.

4. One question should press on the strategy's compatibility with the strong physicalist claim that all truths are a priori deducible from the physical truths: if the concepts that mediate our grip on phenomenal truths are irreducibly special, has the physicalist not quietly traded deducibility for bare supervenience, and does that concession leave the strategy available to a physicalist but useless for defending physicalism?

5. One question should note that the original author of the knowledge argument later recanted his own conclusion, and ask what she takes the strongest strand of that recantation to be — for instance, the thought that once the completeness of physical information is conceded, there is nothing left for the released Mary to know — and why that route does not move her.

6. At least one question near the end should ask what would count against the view: what discovery about the acquisition, neural realization, or pathology of phenomenal concepts — say, in blindsight, in people who lose visual imagery, or in subjects who acquire color concepts late in life — would lead her to abandon the strategy, and what she would predict about such cases if it is right.

For every question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. Avoid any question she could answer with a slogan or a self-description; each should force her to apply the machinery to a specific case and to say something falsifiable about it.
