So, background: I spent about 40 years as a geologist, mostly doing paleoclimate stuff, and since retiring I've been reading philosophy of history and the psychology of judgment instead of actual fieldwork, and I've drifted into a position I'd like stress-tested before I get too attached to it. Roughly:

1. Historical explanation is mostly a hindsight product. Once you know the outcome — Rome fell, the Allies won, some fish lineage made it onto land — the path to it gets reconstructed as if it were the natural one, or at least the most explicable one. The old experiments on creeping determinism (tell one group of subjects the outcome before they judge how foreseeable it was, and their estimates jump) seem to show that knowing the answer inflates our sense that we could have predicted it, and that we can't just switch that off by trying.
2. Narrative is the delivery mechanism. People compress noisy, multiply-causal data into stories with protagonists, turning points, and morals — the narrative fallacy business — and a story feeling satisfying is not evidence it's true. If anything it's mild counterevidence.
3. This is not just a human-history problem. Adaptationist storytelling in evolutionary biology looks like the same disease to me: every trait gets assigned a function discovered after the fact, and as someone who watched colleagues generate plausible mechanisms once they knew the answer for forty years, I know exactly how easy that is.
4. So the big sweeping grand narratives — agriculture as the great turning point, this or that "why the West rules" arc — are the purest concentrate of the problem, and my instinct is to distrust the whole genre on principle.

What would actually count against this package? I don't want "historians are careful, actually." I want the real defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. A few I've half-thought of myself: successful out-of-sample prediction in historical sciences (paleontologists using theory to predict where to dig and finding the thing), natural experiments where the outcome wasn't known in advance, consilience across independent lines of evidence that don't share a narrative. Do those break the skepticism or just push it one level down? Also two questions that worry me: has my view been insulated into unfalsifiability, i.e., if every good historical explanation gets reclassified as "not really history," have I defined the thesis so nothing could touch it? And the nastier one — the hindsight critique seems like it should apply to geology, to my own field, mass extiction explanations and all. Am I carving out an exemption for myself, and if so, on what basis?

Tell me which defeaters a committed skeptic like me would have to concede vs. which they'd wave off, and be honest about whether the position is in better or worse shape than it looks at first glance.
