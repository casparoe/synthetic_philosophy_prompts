my friend and I have been going back and forth on Good's theorem (the value-of-information one) and I want a tiebreaker with numbers. The theorem says a rational expected-utility maximizer should never turn down free, perfectly reliable info relevant to her own one-person decision: she can always ignore it, so conditioning can only help in expectation. He thinks this basically settles the whole "is it ever rational to refuse to know things" debate — his credence that a fully rational agent can ever permissibly decline free, reliable, decision-relevant info is about 0.05. Mine is more like 0.4, because it seems to me the theorem quietly assumes utilities are fixed over final outcomes only, which excludes exactly the interesting cases (genetic test results, spoilers, "do you want to know how you rank among your friends") where knowing itself does something to you. Plus I half-remember that once you allow risk-sensitive preferences instead of strict EU — Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility crowd — declining free info can come out rational, and that dynamic-consistency worries about those theories cut both ways here. And apparently even Good conceded exceptions once the info can leak to another agent, though maybe that's irrelevant to the one-person case.

So: give me your actual credence that refusing can be rational, and decompose it — rough probability that the fixed-utility assumption genuinely fails in a principled way (vs those cases just being info with unmodeled costs), probability that a non-EU theory like REU is actually correct or at least permissible, probability that even given such a theory refusing is a bug not a feature, and anything else you think belongs in there. Then tell me which term my friend and I are most likely actually fighting about, and what would move your number the most. No "it depends on your theory of rationality" cop outs, I want a number.
