I'm TA-ing a second-year ethics course, about 24 students, one 75-minute section. Next week we hit utilitarianism and the objection that it ignores the separateness of persons — the idea that gains and losses don't pool across different lives the way they pool within a single life. The readings treat it really abstractly and the students nod along without it landing, and honestly when I explain it in section it comes out sounding like just "utilitarianism can be mean sometimes." I want an in-class game where they run into the problem themselves before I put a name on it.

Here's my rough sketch, feel free to override any of it. Split the section into small "households" that earn and lose points over several rounds. In each household one student is designated the "bearer": certain costs land on them personally — small but real annoyances, like they have to handle all the materials cleanup, or they're locked into the worst seat in the room for the rest of the session — while the household's point total stays fully shared. Then introduce a central optimizer role, one small group whose entire job is maximizing total section points, and who are allowed to move burdens between households however they want as long as the sum goes up. I want at least one round engineered so the section total rises while the same identifiable person keeps getting stuck with the cost, and one round where the consent loophole gets exploited — the optimizer pays someone a tiny compensation to take a big hit, and everyone in the room can see the payment doesn't feel like enough. Points should convert to something trivially real at the end (candy, bragging rights, dropping the lowest quiz score — I can't use actual money). Ideally somewhere in there they collide with the thought that trading off pain now for gain later feels obviously fine within one life and suddenly looks weird across lives, and then can't decide whether they can defend that asymmetry.

Then the debrief: map each round onto a piece of the actual objection (aggregation across lives vs. within a life, whether utilities are really comparable between people, why some compensations feel fine and others feel like buying someone off), and be honest about which parts of the philosophical point the game just cannot reproduce — I'm aware the objection is ultimately about the metaphysics of persons and my setup risks manufacturing the "boundaries" with arbitrary rules rather than revealing them. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I assume some students will play to win and treat the bearer role as comedy rather than an injustice), and whether a smart utilitarian student who answers the debrief with "the rules made you do it, real morality isn't like your game" has a point I should concede or one I can answer. Please don't just hand me a list of discussion questions — I need actual rules, a materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a payoff table I can print out.
