My roommate and I have been arguing about dreams ever since a philosophy of mind seminar spent two weeks on the old Malcolm–Dennett exchange, and I've realized her position is harder to pin down than either of theirs. Her verdicts so far: for an ordinary dream reported in the morning, she denies that there was any experience occurring during sleep — the dream content, she says, is composed in the course of waking up and telling the story, which makes the report more like a story written on the spot than the memory of a film that was already playing. But she also insists that dream reports aren't therefore meaningless or confabulations in any dismissive sense: they reveal genuine things about the dreamer's mind and concerns, the way an improvised story would. For lucid dreamers who signal with pre-arranged eye movements while physiologically in REM sleep, she says there clearly is experience occurring during sleep, since the signal, delivered at a moment the sleeper had committed to in advance, shows content being generated in real time. When I pressed her with the case of a person woken mid-REM at 3 a.m. who reports nothing, but who at 7 a.m. describes a vivid dream about being woken at 3 a.m., she said there simply was no dream content at 3 a.m. — it was composed later, and the morning report doesn't resurrect a missed experience. And for hypnagogic imagery experienced while drifting off, she counts that as genuine experience, on the grounds that the person is still semi-conscious.

Here's the new case I want to put to her, and I'd like your help predicting her answer before I do. A friend of ours talks in his sleep in fully coherent sentences, and his partner has started writing down what he says. One night the partner recorded a running narration — arriving at a train station, doors closing, unable to find his ticket — and in the morning his spontaneous report matched the overheard narration in detail and in order. So: will she say there was experience occurring during sleep, on the strength of the real-time narration, or will she say this is only the composition machinery running during sleep and leaking into speech, with nothing being experienced as it happens?

What I want from you: first, lay out the candidate rules that fit her track record so far (I can see at least two — roughly, a rule on which any real-time behavioral sign of ongoing dream content, like the eye signals or the sleep-talk, shows experience during sleep, versus a rule on which composition can happen during sleep without the composed content being experienced, and only certain signs count as experience — but tell me if there are others, including any reading that would make her track record ad hoc). Second, say what each rule predicts about the train-station case, give me your best guess at her verdict with your reasons, and be honest about which of her earlier verdicts is doing the most work. Third, tell me what further case would best separate the rules — I've wondered about coherent sleep-talk that gets contradicted by the morning report, or sleep-talk that narrates things happening in the room at that moment, but I'm open to better suggestions. And finally, if you predict she'll deny experience here, sketch how someone could press the objection that a composition process running during sleep, and apparently tracking a developing storyline, just is a form of experience — and what she'd have to say to resist it.
