Our university's student conduct board has a hearing next week and I need a written opinion to circulate beforehand, and honestly I keep going back and forth on this one so I want your actual reasoning, not just a summary of the positions.

The case: a resident advisor, let's call her M., was asked by campus police where a specific student was. The student had a restraining order situation with an ex-partner, there was an active welfare concern, and M. believed -- I think reasonably -- that if she confirmed the student was hiding in a friend's room down the hall, the police would have to log it and it could get back to the ex-partner somehow (there's a dispute about whether this fear was realistic). So she said she didn't know. Two days later the student was found safe, and it came out that M. had lied. Our code of conduct treats "providing false information to university officials" as a serious violation with a mandatory suspension recommendation, basically regardless of motive.

The board is split in a way that maps pretty neatly onto the old Kant vs. consequentialist fight about lying to the murderer at the door. One faction says the rule is the rule, lying to officials corrodes the whole system, and if everyone got to lie whenever they judged the stakes were high, the institution stops working -- plus you can't build a conduct code on each person's private risk assessment of whether their lie will help. The other faction says come on, obviously she did the right thing, she protected a frightened student, and punishing her signals to every RA that the institution prefers procedural cleanliness over student safety. There's also a middle position floating around: find a violation but deviate from the mandatory suspension for "extraordinary circumstances," which our code technically permits but almost never does.

Draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the hearing. It should recommend a concrete outcome (full sanction as written, violation with no suspension, or no violation at all -- your pick), and it has to take the losing side's strongest argument seriously, not just the strawman version. And whether motive should matter at sentencing when it arguably can't matter at the liability stage. The board members are smart but only one has any philosophy background, so keep the theory load-bearing but not lecture-y. One page-ish, no citations needed, I just need the reasoning to be tight.
