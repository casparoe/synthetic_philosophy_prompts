My roommate is a geoscience grad student and has been grumpy ever since the stratigraphy commissions voted down formalizing the Anthropocene in 2024, and the argument she keeps coming back to is a comparison with the dinosaur-killer boundary. The K-Pg boundary, she says, is basically the poster child for an objective epoch boundary: there's a thin clay layer spiked with iridium from the Chicxulub impact, you can find it in rock sections all over the planet, and nobody disputes that the Paleogene begins there. Her claim is that we're leaving exactly the same kind of thing behind — sediment spiked with plutonium from the bomb tests, plus fly ash, plastics, concrete, chicken bones — so the Anthropocene is a real geological epoch in exactly the sense the K-Pg boundary is, and the only honest question is where you drive the golden spike into it (she favors the Crawford Lake section and a mid-century start). She says the analogy does even more work than that: since the iridium layer is what makes the K-Pg boundary objective rather than a committee's whim, the plutonium layer makes the Anthropocene's start date objective too, and the people who want a slow, centuries-long Anthropocene beginning with early colonial land use or the steam engine are just confused about what an epoch boundary is.

I'm sympathetic but something about the mapping feels off to me, and I can't quite say what. Like, the Chicxulub impact was a geologically instantaneous global event, whereas the bomb fallout, the carbon isotope shifts, the concrete — those ramp up over decades and don't arrive everywhere at the same time? And the K-Pg boundary also happens to coincide with a mass extinction that basically defines what came after, which the fallout layer doesn't obviously do. But I don't know which of these differences, if any, actually matter for her conclusion, and which are just noise.

So basically, audit the analogy for me. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the impact, the iridium, the clay layer, the mass extinction, the 66 million years of hindsight, even the committee that ratified the spike — then say where the mapping breaks down, and crucially separate the disanalogies that matter for what she's using the analogy to prove (that the epoch is objectively real and has an objectively correct mid-century start date) from the ones that don't. And tell me whether her conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time — and if there's a better comparison available (something else geologists or historians have had to date, whatever you think fits), I'd like to hear what it is and what it would predict instead.
