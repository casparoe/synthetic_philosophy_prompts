I am a retired physical chemist who spent most of his career on spectroscopy and kinetic modeling, and who in the last few years has been reading his way into machine learning and the surrounding philosophy. Somewhere between the two, I have acquired a habit from my own field: we were drilled to evaluate an experiment's design, not merely whether it produced an attractive result — a well-designed experiment that fails was worth publishing; a sloppy one that succeeded was not. So when I first encountered the slogan "reward the process, not the outcome" in a machine learning forum, I felt I already understood it. I no longer think I did.

The occasion was a long thread about how to train or grade a language model on mathematical reasoning. One participant deployed the slogan to argue something fairly radical: that we should stop giving the model any feedback on whether its final answer is correct, and grade only the reasoning steps — otherwise, he said, the model learns to hit answers by luck or shortcuts and its reasoning rots. Another used the very same sentence to make a much weaker point: that final-answer feedback is just a noisier training signal than step-by-step feedback, and no more. A third seemed to use it as an ethical dictum, almost as if a correct answer reached by bad reasoning is somehow undeserved, in the way a result extracted from a contaminated sample is undeserved. The same one-liner appeared to be doing at least three different jobs, and I could not tell which of them, if any, are supported by evidence as opposed to temperament.

Please act as an expert in machine learning and in the philosophy of science, and take some time to think through this before answering; I would rather have a careful analysis than a fast one. Lay out the distinct precise claims that "reward the process, not the outcome" could be expressing, ordered from the near-trivial to the most radical — statistical claims (outcome feedback is a high-variance estimator of reasoning quality), incentive claims (optimizing hard on outcome measures invites Goodharting and gaming), data-efficiency claims (process feedback teaches more per label), generalization claims (only process feedback builds transferable competence), and normative claims (trust should attach to method, as reviewers in my old field were taught) — though do not feel bound by my tentative list; if I have missed a reading, add it. For each reading, tell me: is it plausible, is it actually true as far as anything is known, and — this is the part I most need — would it actually support the strong conclusion the first participant wanted, that outcome feedback should be abandoned entirely rather than supplemented? It seemed to me that some readings support only the weaker supplement conclusion, and that one of the participants was quietly trading on the ambiguity between the two.

I also want honesty about the state of knowledge. Please separate throughout what is settled among researchers who work on this from what is genuinely contested — for instance, whether outcome-based reward in fact degrades reasoning, or whether process feedback generalizes better, strike me as empirical questions on which I would expect mixed and evolving evidence, and I do not want one camp's preference presented to me as established consensus. Where a reading of the slogan depends on an unresolved empirical question, say so plainly and tell me what evidence would settle it.

Finally, as a chemist I keep returning to an uncomfortable analogy and I would like your verdict on it: in the lab, sloppy methods that produce a striking result were distrusted not only for incentive reasons but because replication would eventually expose them — the outcome was, over time, a self-correcting check on the process. If that is right in the training context too, then perhaps outcome feedback is not an alternative to process feedback but its auditor, and the slogan, on its strongest reading, is not merely unsupported but backwards. Is that a defensible position, or am I over-reading an analogy between bench science and gradient descent? Tell me candidly where the analogy holds, where it breaks, and where you simply do not know.
