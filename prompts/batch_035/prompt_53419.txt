I'm taking a phil of bio course with a metaethics unit, and last week we did Dennett's free-floating rationales -- reasons in nature that no reasoner ever represented. Like the reason moths spiral into porch lights (the story goes that they navigate by keeping a celestial light at a constant angle, and a nearby lamp breaks the trick). There's a reason for what the moth does, and it was a good reason, long before anyone knew about it. The reason is just out there doing explanatory work, with no mind anywhere.

My study partner built an argument off this for our response paper (due friday, help), and the conclusion seems way too strong, but neither of us can find the bad step:

(1) There are free-floating rationales: whenever natural selection favors a trait because of what it does, the thing it does is a reason for that trait, and this reason exists whether or not anyone represents it.
(2) Human moral practices -- guilt, reciprocity, parental protectiveness, punishing free-riders -- are traits that were favored by selection because of what they did (stabilized cooperation, protected offspring, etc.).
(3) So there are free-floating rationales for our core moral practices.
(4) A rationale for something is a reason for it -- something counting in its favor. That's what the word means; it's not just a cause.
(5) So each of our core moral practices has something counting in its favor.
(6) If something counts in favor of a practice, the practice can't be wrong with nothing going for it -- at worst it's justified but outweighed.
(7) So no evolved moral practice is simply baseless; every one has some justification on its side.
(8) But revenge, honor codes, tribal loyalty -- those are evolved moral practices too, and they seem not just explicable but flat-out wrong. And if (7) holds, every moral reformer is pushing against a real reason, which makes moral progress look like a mistake.

So somewhere this goes wrong, but where? My friend digs in at (4): he says if you strip the normativity out of "rationale," the whole idea collapses into ordinary causal explanation and Dennett's term is just a metaphor, which he thinks Dennett wouldn't accept. I keep wanting to attack (6) instead, but then I owe an account of how something can count in favor of a practice while the practice still comes out baseless -- doesn't "in favor" already rule that out? Or maybe the problem is that (8) is unfair: maybe the rationales for revenge etc. have expired, since the conditions that made them good reasons are gone -- but then I'm not sure what I'm rejecting, (5), or just sneaking an extra clause into the whole argument. Also worth noting: (6) to (7) feels weaker than the conclusion my friend actually wants, so maybe the slide happens earlier than either of us thinks. For what it's worth I'd like to keep (1), since the moth thing really does feel like there's a reason there. Where exactly is the equivocation, if there is one, and what's the cheapest way out -- what does each rejection cost?
