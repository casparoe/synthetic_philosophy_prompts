My brother is in a decision theory seminar and walked me into something I've been unable to leave alone for a week, and it's not academic for me: I recently signed up for a race with a friend and, knowing my own track record with training, paid a hefty nonrefundable entry fee and scheduled public commitments precisely so that skipping the training would be costly later. My whole strategy is forming, now, an intention I know future-me will have strong reasons to abandon. So when my brother laid out the following argument against the very coherence of such intentions, it landed closer to home than a puzzle about billionaires should.

Here is the setup he used: a billionaire will deposit one million dollars in your bank account tonight if, at midnight, you intend to drink a toxin tomorrow that will make you briefly but intensely ill, with no lasting harm and no benefit whatsoever from drinking it. The money is yours whether or not you actually drink; the billionaire pays only for the intention. Now the argument: (1) To intend to do something tomorrow, I must now expect that I will in fact do it — an intention is not a wish or a performance but a settled expectation about my own future conduct. (2) Nothing that happens today or tomorrow can make drinking the toxin better than not drinking it: the money is already mine either way, and the toxin only causes suffering. (3) So tomorrow morning, the rational thing to do will be not to drink. (4) I know all of this today. (5) So today I expect that tomorrow morning I will not drink. (6) By (1) and (5), I cannot today intend to drink the toxin tomorrow, no matter how much I would like the million. (7) And since the billionaire pays for the intention itself, the conclusion isn't merely that taking the deal is irrational — it's that no one who understands the setup can be in the paid-for state at all. (8) But generalizing (6), the same reasoning would show that no one can intend to keep a promise that will later be painful to keep, or to follow through on a deterrent threat once the costly moment arrives, or, apparently, to train for a race they will later want to skip; yet people plainly do form such intentions, and they seem genuine. (9) So some premise or step is false.

My instinct is that the argument proves too much, and I'd most like to keep (1), since it seems to capture what distinguishes intending from merely hoping. But I can't tell whether the trouble is in the slide from (3) and (4) to (5) — it feels like it assumes I'm guaranteed to be rational tomorrow, or like the rationality of my choice tomorrow and the rationality of my intention today have quietly been treated as the same question — or whether (6) is where the equivocation lives, between not being able to intend and not being able to intend rationally. Tell me exactly which step you would reject, why that step looked sound in the first place, and what accepting the rejection costs elsewhere — including, if you can, what it costs for my race strategy.
