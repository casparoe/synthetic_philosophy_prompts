So I've been trying to wrap my head around computability and something is bugging me. The way I understand it, Turing proved the Church-Turing thesis back in the 30s — anything computable is computable by a Turing machine — and that settled the matter, it's a theorem like any other. But then quantum computers came along and can apparently do things classical machines can't, which sounds like it flatly refutes the thesis. On top of that I've seen Penrose argue the brain manages non-computable stuff, so thats two seperate ways the thesis has been shown wrong.

Given all that, my actual question: why do we still define computability in terms of Turing machines? Is it just historical inertia, or does the old model have some real advantage I'm not seeing? Feels a bit like still teaching phlogiston in chemistry at this point lol.
