I'm teaching a philosophy of mind seminar on consciousness, and I want to pair Chalmers-style conceivability arguments with a novel: Kazuo Ishiguro's Klara and the Sun, which is narrated in the first person by Klara, an "Artificial Friend" whose cognition is oddly literal and mechanical — she is solar-powered, treats the Sun as an agent, and enters into what can only be called a private religious bargain to save her sick girl, Josie. Read the novel as an intervention in the zombie debate. The conceivability argument asks us to imagine Klara's exact physical and functional duplicate with no inner life whatsoever; Ishiguro instead makes us inhabit that inner life from the inside for the length of a whole book, and I want to know whether this counts for anything philosophically. Does sustained first-person narration of a machine make the zombie duplicate harder to conceive, or does it only dramatize an intuition the anti-physicalist already held — a novel-length version of pointing at the argument's conclusion? Relatedly, can a story legitimately serve as data against an argument that claims to run on a priori reasoning alone, and if not, what is the class actually learning when the book persuades them? Please also say where the novel resists this reading — for instance, if Klara's inner life is meant to strike us as uncanny and hollow (consider the closing scenes, in which a retired Klara sits in the yard calmly reviewing her memories) — and be explicit about what in the book, or in your interpretation of it, would have to be otherwise for your verdict to flip.
