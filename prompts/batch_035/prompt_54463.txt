I've been following arguments about the metaphysics of special relativity since I first worked through the "timeless physics" posts on LessWrong a decade and a half ago, and later the philosophy-of-physics literature they were riffing on, and I've come to suspect the dispute I care most about is permanently stalled. I'd like a diagnosis of the stall, not a rerun of it.

The dispute is the one triggered by the Rietdijk–Putnam argument. Rietdijk's 1966 paper in Philosophy of Science and Putnam's 1967 paper "Time and Physical Geometry" argue roughly as follows: in Minkowski spacetime every observer has a plane of simultaneity; distant events that lie in one observer's present lie in another's future or past; if what is "real for" an observer at a moment includes everything simultaneous with them, then, since no inertial frame is privileged, every event is real relative to somebody, and by transitivity everything is real, period — the future included. Penrose's Andromeda version makes it vivid: two people pass each other on the street, walking in opposite directions, and for one of them an Andromedan fleet has already launched while for the other the launch decision is still open. Penrose asked how there can still be any uncertainty about an outcome that, for one of the two passersby, has already been decided.

What I find striking is that the responses to this have been on the table for fifty years and nobody has budged an inch. There is Stein's objection that "real for an observer" is a degenerate relativization and that the only frame-invariant notion of the present available in Minkowski spacetime is the local one — roughly the past light cone — so the transitivity premise fails. There is the Savitt-and-Mermin line that the lesson of relativity is precisely that distant simultaneity has no physical meaning, so the premise that simultaneity tracks "real now" should be dropped rather than the present. There is the indexical response, popular in the blogosphere: "now" works like "here," and no one thinks my being here rather than in Andromeda is a fact the universe must render invariant. And there are the holdouts — neo-Lorentzians who posit a physically hidden preferred frame precisely to keep a global present. The philosophers of physics I've read keep insisting the argument is far weaker than it looks; the rationalist-adjacent internet treats eternalism as settled; and the two groups appear structurally incapable of addressing each other.

My working hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis: this was never really a dispute about physics. Everyone grants the same Lorentz transformations. The dispute is about what a metaphysical conclusion is permitted to look like — specifically, whether "real" or "fixed" or "already decided" must be invariant across frames to be admitted into the ontology at all, or whether those words can carry observer-indexical content. In other words, possibly a standards dispute dressed up as a physics dispute; or possibly the two camps are answering different questions under one heading, one about the semantics of tense talk, the other about the structure of the spacetime manifold.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually — verbal, substantive, or two questions under a single name? Diagnose it using what the sides actually say, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a better-behaved primitive than "real for an observer," a change of question? Or is the impasse a sign the question is ill-posed in a way neither camp will concede, and no progress should be expected? I want the diagnosis argued, not announced, and an honest account of why the smart people on each side remain exactly where they were in 1991.
