Hey, so I'm a novelist working on a chapter where two old friends are snowed in at a cabin and get into a proper arguement that goes way too late into the night. One is a retired physicist, the other a philosophy professor, and both are stubborn as mules. I want them to fight about this parable the professor is fond of, a riff on Hofstadter's Careenium thing — you know, the one where a table is covered in billiard-like balls, with some bigger ones (simmballs) rolling around among a huge sea of tiny ones, and when the little balls settle into stable patterns, the patterns start behaving like active symbols: they respond to each other, combine, trigger new patterns, and generally act like the letters of a self-updating language. Basically a toy model of a brain built out of colliding spheres.

The physicist thinks this is all romantic nonsense. Only the individual balls and their collisions are real, he says — "symbol" is just shorthand for when we're too lazy to track a million microstates, and nothing at the pattern level ever pushes anything around. The professor thinks that's exactly backwards once a system like this gets complicated enough: the patterns compete with each other, and the ones that win are the ones that are any good at marshaling the little balls to sustain themselves, so in a genuine sense the symbols are doing causal work, steering the very balls that instantiate them.

Please write their dialogue. Real back-and-forth, each one landing actual punches on the other's position — the physicist can press on causal overdetermination and on what a "pattern" even is if the little balls are constantly swapping in and out of it, and the professor can press on levels of description and on why higher-level sciences make predictions the microlevel can't practically deliver. They should interrupt, needle, get up to poke at the fire, whatever. No lecture-y monologues, and no resolving it by citing some authority — the argument should stand on its own two feet. By the end I want it unresolved, but with each of them privately conceding, like, half a point, even if they'd never say so out loud.
