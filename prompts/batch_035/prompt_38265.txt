I'm a PhD student straddling philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and our reading group has just finished two weeks on Ryle's knowing-how / knowing-that distinction — the chapter of The Concept of Mind, plus the surrounding intellectualism literature. I've come out of it with a fistful of case verdicts that I trust individually but cannot make hang together, and rather than pick a side between Ryle and his opponents by label, I'd like to work the way I'd work any other reflective-equilibrium problem: state my verdicts, and ask what principle, if any, captures them.

Here they are. A cyclist knows how to ride a bicycle even though, like nearly everyone, she would deny — and be genuinely baffled by — the fact that briefly steering into the turn is what keeps her upright; if you tell her this, she acquires a new piece of knowledge-that with no change whatsoever in what she can do. A veteran chicken-sexer can sort day-old chicks at near-ceiling accuracy, has no idea how she does it, and confabulates when pressed; she knows how to sex chicks, full stop. On the other side: someone who has read the entire manual — balance points, weight transfer, countersteering — and thus possesses every relevant proposition, but has never sat on a bike, does not know how to ride. And a resident who has memorized the Manhattan street grid can give flawless verbal directions, yet I don't think she knows her way around the way a cab driver does — though I notice I'd answer "yes" if she asked herself, in an ordinary practical mood, whether she knows how to get from the Met to the Whitney. Apparently I toggle between two readings without a rule for when.

The trouble is that my verdicts cut in both directions: having all the propositions is not sufficient (the manual-reader, the grid-memorizer), and having none of them does not block the attribution (the cyclist, the sexer). So knowledge-how seems to come apart from knowledge-that on both sides — and yet when a novice pianist is drilled by explicit instruction until the movements become automatic, I'm tempted to say her knowledge-how was built out of knowledge-that which was then internalized away, which points in exactly the opposite direction. There is also one case I keep going back and forth on and can't verdict at all: a golfer with a bad case of the yips who retains every piece of propositional knowledge about the swing, can demonstrate it in slow motion, and cannot execute it under pressure. So here is my request: give me a principle, or a small set of ranked candidates, stated precisely enough to sort fresh cases; say which clause each of my verdicts motivates; tell me its verdicts on cases I haven't listed — including the yips case, and whether a system trained only on text about swimming could ever count as knowing how to swim; and be candid about which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate, since I'd rather know now what I have to give up than discover it in the seminar.
