At a recent workshop on Ubuntu and relational personhood, the maxim umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu, usually rendered as "a person is a person through other persons," was doing a great deal of work — and, as far as I could tell, different work each time. In the morning session, a speaker used it to claim that the liberal framework of individual rights rests on a false, atomistic metaphysics of the person, and that a rights-based politics is therefore incoherent when transplanted into an African moral world. In the afternoon, someone else invoked the very same maxim to argue that an elderly woman with advanced dementia remains fully a person because the community continues to recognise her, tell her story, and hold her in memory — personhood sustained from the outside, as it were. A third participant waved the maxim away as a bit of communal sentimentality, a dressed-up reminder to be kind to one's neighbours.

I left convinced that these three uses could not all be trading on the same claim, but not at all clear on how to separate them. Could you help by laying out, as an explicit menu, the distinct precise theses this one sentence could be expressing? For instance, I can dimly see that it might be a developmental thesis (personhood is acquired through socialisation, so a child raised entirely apart from human community would fail to become one), an epistemic one (self-understanding is possible only through others' recognition), a semantic one (the very concept of a person is socially fixed), a metaphysical-constitutive one (a human being's relations to others literally constitute the person, not merely accompany them), or a normative one (personhood is a standing achievement, earned and forfeited through how one treats others — a status rather than a given). There may be further readings I have missed.

For each reading, I would be grateful if you would say whether it is more or less trivial, whether it is philosophically defensible, and whether it is perhaps straightforwardly false or vulnerable to decisive counterexamples. Then please address the two uses from the workshop directly: which of the readings, if any, would actually support the morning claim that individual rights presuppose an incoherent metaphysics of the person, and which would support the afternoon claim about the person with dementia? I suspect, but cannot yet articulate, that the strongest metaphysical reading is the one that sustains the second use while failing to yield the first — and that the leap from a constitutive claim about what persons are to a political claim about what rights they should have is an is-to-ought slide that the maxim's rhetorical warmth tends to conceal. Please tell me whether that suspicion is well founded, and whether any reading of the maxim both avoids triviality and avoids the slide.
