I am a novelist at work on a book whose narrator, a translator of classical Chinese, is watching her father die in the intervals between chapters. She takes refuge in Zhuangzi, and I have discovered that my two research collaborators — a scholar I shall call the Metaphysician, and a therapist-turned-comparativist I shall call the Ironist — have spent a year arguing past each other about what Zhuangzi actually holds about death. The Metaphysician maintains that Zhuangzi's equanimity is sincere metaphysical acceptance: within the vision of things as a single continuum of transformation, death is a passage like any other, and the sage who sees this has nothing left to fear or to grieve. The Ironist maintains that this is a misreading: the famous scenes — Zhuangzi drumming on a tub and singing after his wife dies, his exchange with a skull, his instruction, at his own approaching death, that his body simply be left for the crows and the ants — are knowingly excessive performances of consolation; their point is therapeutic rather than doctrinal, a discipline one adopts toward grief rather than a discovery that dissolves it.

Before I let their disagreement dictate my narrator's inner life — whether her calm, when it comes, should be written as discovery or as discipline — I want the disagreement made exact. Please draft the joint statement the two of them could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, structured as follows:

1. A numbered section of claims both accept — about which episodes and passages are in play and what they plainly say; about what any adequate reading of Zhuangzi on death must explain; and about any readings that both, on reflection, have abandoned.

2. A table of residual disagreements, each stated twice: once in wording the Metaphysician would accept as fair to her side, and once in wording the Ironist would accept as fair to his. Formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted; if one of them discovers that a putative disagreement was really about words, the document should say which portion was verbal and why.

3. The cruxes identified separately — the subquestions such that settling one would move at least one party — and, for each crux, a concrete interpretive case both agree bears on it: for instance, what to make of the moment when Zhuangzi, having given a long justification of his conduct regarding his wife, goes on drumming; or how the deathbed scene should treat his refusal to prefer the crows to the ants. For each case, record in advance each side's prediction of what a fair reading of it would show.

4. A closing paragraph on whether anything remains that could in principle be settled by better philology or better evidence, and what, in the end, my narrator is entitled to conclude.

Please do not quote the Zhuangzi; refer to passages by episode and paraphrase only. The document should be one both parties could sign without wincing.
