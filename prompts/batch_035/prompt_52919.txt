So I've been thinking way too much about the Singer drowning child thing, and I've realized my gut verdicts on rescue cases are a mess and don't obviously add up to any clean principle. Can you figure out what, if anything, I actually believe? Here are my verdicts on the cases:

- Kid drowning in the shallow pond, I ruin my coat and phone and miss a meeting getting her out: obviously must do it, and honestly anyone who walked past I'd consider a genuinely bad person.
- Same ~$700 hit to me, but instead it's paying for a surgery for a kid whose photo and verified story I've been sent: I think I must do it, but I don't blame a friend who doesn't pay the way I'd blame them for pond-walking. And I know that's not consistent. And if I'm honest I probably wouldn't pay every single time it came up.
- Twenty adults standing around the pond, everyone can see everyone else, nobody moves, anyone could wade in at the same cost to themselves: my gut says no one individual is required the way they'd be if alone, the blame just evaporates. Which feels wrong even to me but there it is.
- Certain rescue: $400 to definitely save one kid. Gamble: $400 for a 1-in-200 chance of saving one kid, so roughly the same expected kids saved. The certain one feels required; the gamble feels optional, like a nice thing to do. I don't love that I think this.
- A kid in my neighborhood keeps falling into the same pond, like monthly, and I'm always the only one around. First time: must rescue, no question. By the third time I feel like the blame for a miss lands on the town for not fencing the pond, not on me, and my rescues start feeling like they happen under duress.
- Someone who already gave their whole planned charity budget for the year (famine relief, actual good stuff) walks past the pond: still must wade in. The pledge doesn't seem to cap the rescue duty, which is wierd when I say it out loud.
- The rescue would cost me my livelihood — drive the work van into the pond to use as a platform, van's totaled, I'm self-employed: feels optional, heroic if done, not blameworthy if skipped. So apparently there's a cost line somewhere between coat-and-phone and the van.

I've tried out a principle like: rescue is required when the victim is identifiable, success is certain, cost is under some modest cap, and you're the salient rescuer. But that principle says the many-bystander case has no duty (matches my gut) and also says the named-kid surgery has a duty that doesn't get diluted by the millions of other potential donors (maybe bad? since I let twenty bystanders dilute the pond duty). It also, I notice, basically describes identifiable-victim bias with a dollar figure attached.

So: is there a principle that gets all or most of these, or do some of my verdicts have to go? If some have to go, which ones and why? Then there's the case I keep going back and forth on: a kid in my town who will die within a year unless someone covers a recurring monthly treatment — $40 a month, forever, and I can afford it. Is that a rescue (pond) or a pledge (surgery)? Does the duty renew every month, does it ever stop being mine, and does my fence-the-pond instinct from the recurring pond kid apply here at all? And please tell me what your preferred principle says about a case I didn't list: someone who can either jump in right now and ruin their phone, or spend the same ten minutes completing a transfer that would, with certainty, save a different kid. Can they just pick either one, and on what grounds?
