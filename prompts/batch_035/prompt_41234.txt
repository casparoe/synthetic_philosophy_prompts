Take the Clinton-Lewinsky affair and run it through Grice's theory of implicature and speaker meaning. Not as a gotcha about lying — I want the actual mechanics. Facts to work with, take them as given for the sake of the analysis: in January 1998 Clinton publicly, very forcefully, denied having had sexual relations with Monica Lewinsky (the finger-wagging TV moment, everyone knows it). Later it came out that in his Paula Jones deposition he'd been working from a stipulated legal definition of sexual relations that, on the reading his lawyers pushed, arguably did not cover receiving oral sex — so his denial may have been literally true under the deposition's definition while being understood by basically everyone as a denial that anything sexual had happened at all. He later also maintained his deposition answers were legally accurate even after admitting an improper relationship.

Here's what I want, in order:

1. Map the statement carefully in Gricean terms. What was said, in the strict sense, versus what was implicated. Which maxim is actually being exploited here — quantity (saying something technically true but less informative than the occasion demands), manner (hiding behind the obscurity of a legal definition), or something else? Test whether the misleading content is a real conversational implicature using the standard criteria, especially cancellability. Can you cancel it without absurdity — and what would cancelling even look like in this case?

2. Then get at the deeper question, which is Grice's test for speaker meaning itself. Grice thought that to mean something by an utterance, you have to intend your audience to believe p partly through their recognition of your intention to get them to believe p. Apply that reflexive intention test to Clinton. Did he speaker-mean that nothing sexual happened, or did he merely produce an utterance he knew would cause that belief while keeping plausible deniability about intending it? There's a real difference between meaning something and strategically inducing a belief, and I suspect this case lands somewhere uncomfortable for the theory. You might consider whether the two contexts pull apart — the deposition is an adversarial legal setting, the TV statement is political rhetoric, and Grice's Cooperative Principle was arguably never really in force in either one. Does the whole implicature apparatus even apply to exchanges where the parties aren't cooperating, and if not, what is the audience actually entitled to infer?

3. Finally, run it the other direction: what does this episode show about the theory? Is it a clean application, or does it expose a structural blind spot — e.g. that the framework assumes the speaker wants to be understood, and breaks when a speaker profits precisely from the audience's assumption that the Cooperative Principle is in force? Be honest about where Grice has the resources to handle this and where he'd have to just stipulate his way out, and say whether some amendment (or some rival account of what misleading-but-not-lying consists in) does better. Reach a verdict — don't leave it at "it's complicated."
