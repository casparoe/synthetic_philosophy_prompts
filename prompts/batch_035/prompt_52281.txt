I've been arguing online with someone who is convinced that consciousness can't be a purely physical phenomenon, and whenever I press him he falls back on the phrase 'conceivability is a guide to possibility.' His argument runs like this: he can picture, without any strain, a creature molecule-for-molecule identical to a human being that nonetheless has no inner life at all — what he calls a philosophical zombie; since that scenario is conceivable, it must be possible; and since it's possible, the physical facts about a person can't be what fixes whether they're conscious. I have no academic background and never studied philosophy, but I've been chewing on this by myself for a while, and it seems to me the whole dispute turns on what that slogan is actually asserting. As best I can work out, 'conceivable' could mean anything from 'I personally don't feel resistance when I try to picture it' to something far stronger, like 'no amount of reasoning, even in principle, could expose a hidden contradiction in the scenario.' And 'guide to possibility' could mean anything from 'a weak clue that can be overturned by other things we know' to 'an infallible route to the truth about what could and couldn't exist.' Those strengths can be mixed and matched, and I suspect some combinations are plainly false, some are nearly trivial, and some are substantive but too weak to do the work he needs. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing — spelling out the different strengths of 'conceivable' and of 'guide', and treating their combinations as separate readings — and for each one say whether it's plausible, and, crucially, whether accepting it would actually force me to grant his conclusion that physicalism is false? Or would some of the plausible readings get him only something much weaker, like 'physicalism isn't obviously true'? Please present it as a menu of readings I can work through, not a defense of one of them, and say explicitly which reading my interlocutor is most likely depending on. Finally, since I'm planning to take this back to him: end by telling me where your own reasoning here is most likely to fail — what consideration, if it held, would change your verdict on which readings are plausible, and what the strongest thing he could say in reply would be.
