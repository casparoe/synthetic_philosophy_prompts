Please compare two deference principles from formal epistemology. Lewis's Principal Principle says that a rational agent's credence in a proposition A, conditional on the proposition that the objective chance of A equals x (together with any admissible background evidence), should itself equal x. Van Fraassen's Reflection principle says that a rational agent's current credence in A, conditional on the proposition that her own future credence in A will equal y, should also equal y.

Now consider a reductionist suggestion I want assessed on its merits: if one assumes that one's future self is perfectly rational and updates only on evidence that is admissible relative to the relevant chance hypotheses, then Reflection should be derivable from the Principal Principle, since the future self's credence will itself track the very objective chances the present self is required to defer to. On this view, Reflection would be nothing over and above the Principal Principle plus a diachronic consistency assumption, and the two principles would not be genuinely rival accounts of deference.

Please evaluate this derivation carefully. In particular, address the following:

1. Whether the admissibility restriction does work that breaks the derivation. Consider cases in which the future self has learned something inadmissible relative to the chance hypothesis — for example, the outcome of a lottery draw itself, or an observation of a frequency that the chance value supervenes on — so that the future self's credence need not match the chance that the present self defers to.

2. A second-order chance case. Suppose a die's bias will be fixed tomorrow by a mechanism that is itself chancy: today, the chance that the chance of rolling a six will be 1/6 is only 0.5, and the chance that it will be 1/2 is 0.5. Explain what credence in six the Principal Principle recommends today, whether Reflection could in principle recommend a different value given suitable assumptions about one's future self, and what this case shows about the derivation.

3. Whether the derivation can be run in reverse: whether someone antecedently committed to Reflection could argue that deference to objective chance is just deference to the verdicts of a hypothetical, ideally situated future self, so that the Principal Principle is the derived principle and Reflection the primitive one.

Do not cite the literature; reason from the principles as I have stated them. Then end your response with a single machine-readable final line: \answer{reducible} if you judge that one principle is derivable from the other (state which reduces to which in the preceding sentence), \answer{independent} if each can coherently conflict with the other in some scenario, or \answer{equivalent} if, under the assumptions above, they impose identical constraints on rational credence.
