I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm at work on a piece about a recurring habit in science coverage: whenever a study reports that some animal has a larger brain than expected — whales, elephants, some bird with an unexpectedly big forebrain — the commentary slides from "bigger" to "smarter" to "matters more," as though brain size carried moral weight directly. My editor wants a frank assessment of that slide rather than the usual "it's complicated," so I'd like an actual credence, with the reasoning visible enough that I could print it.

The proposition, stated so it has a truth value: absolute brain size is a morally relevant property in itself. That is, suppose we somehow fixed everything else we ordinarily care about morally — capacity for suffering, richness of experience, self-awareness — and varied only absolute brain size (not size relative to body mass); a being with the larger brain would thereby have somewhat greater moral status. Note this is stronger than the claim that brain size is good evidence of those other properties; the question is whether size contributes anything at all once they are held fixed.

Please structure your reply as follows, in this order and no other:

1. "Bottom line" — one short paragraph giving a single number and no more than three sentences saying precisely what it is a credence about.
2. "The decomposition" (roughly 350 words) — break the proposition into sub-questions. At minimum: (a) that moral status is a function of psychological capacities at all, rather than of species membership, relationships, or something else; (b) that among those capacities, something that scales with absolute size — rather than, say, nociceptor density or social complexity — does the moral work; and (c) whether absolute size survives a screening-off test: if a complete science of consciousness someday inventoried every morally relevant capacity of a being, would a residual contribution from raw size remain, or would size vanish the way vitalism vanished once biochemistry explained life? Assign a probability and a one-line justification to each sub-question, and add any further terms you think belong in the decomposition.
3. "Combination" (about 100 words) — show how the sub-credences produce the headline number, and state whether you treated them as independent.
4. "Sensitivity" (about 100 words) — identify which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and what finding or argument would move it by a tenth or more.

I want the screening-off test taken seriously, not waved away with a reminder that correlation isn't relevance — I know that, and it isn't the question. The question is whether size has any residual claim on our concern. My piece will close on whether "brain size matters morally" is destined to become this century's phrenology, and I would rather print a number with its derivation attached than a shrug.
