Compose, please, a satirical piece in the form of an annual performance review of Absolute Space, written by a supervisor at a fictional institution — call it the Bureau of Ontological Commitments — which must decide at year's end whether to retain Absolute Space on its roster of entities or terminate the position.

Some context on where I'm coming from: I spent thirty-five years in fluid mechanics before retiring, and since then I have been reading my way into the philosophy of space and time. I confess that Newton's bucket has unsettled me more than any turbulence ever did. The water climbs the sides in a paraboloid, and the only thing it seems to be rotating relative to is something no one can see. That asymmetry — that uniform velocity is undetectable but acceleration leaves a visible signature — strikes me as the deepest and strangest fact in the whole debate, and I would like the satire to be built on it rather than on generic jokes about physics.

Requirements for the piece itself:

1. Length and form: roughly 550 to 650 words, formatted as a performance review document with labeled sections (for example: Overall Rating, Key Achievements, Areas of Concern, Peer Feedback, Development Goals, Recommendation).
2. The humor must come from getting the philosophy right. Specifically, the review should work in, without lecture-style exposition: the bucket's concave water surface and the pair-of-globes thought experiment (tension in the cord revealing rotation even in an otherwise empty universe) as Absolute Space's sole documented achievements; the embarrassing fact that the entity cannot be observed at rest or in uniform motion and has never once been detected directly; the indiscernibility worry that shifting the entire material world a few feet would change nothing observable about it; and the rival candidate — the totality of distances and relations among material bodies — who keeps claiming credit for everything Absolute Space does.
3. Include at least one item of peer feedback from a disgruntled relationist colleague, and let the Development Goals section propose corrective measures that quietly expose the position's awkwardness (for instance, a goal of becoming observable only under acceleration, or of staying exactly where it is while everything else moves).
4. The satire should be deadpan and fair: a reader who knows the substantivalism–relationism debate should feel that both sides are being sent up for what they actually maintain, not for a caricature. Don't wink at the reader, and don't have the review come out sneeringly one-sided; the comic tension should come from the fact that the case for retention is real — the bucket needs explaining — while everything else about the employee is professionally indefensible.
5. Do not include citations, references, or a bibliography; write entirely from your grasp of the material. Do not attribute quotations to real historical figures; invented institutional voices are fine.

After the piece, append a brief structured summary as a JSON object with the following fields, and nothing else after it:
- "target_of_satire": a one-sentence description of what position or argument the piece ironizes;
- "strongest_case_for_retention": the single best argument for substantivalism dramatized in the piece, stated plainly;
- "strongest_case_for_termination": likewise, the strongest relationist objection it dramatizes;
- "philosophical_moves_deployed": an array of strings naming the specific arguments or thought experiments the piece relies on (e.g., the bucket, the globes, the shift argument, the undetectability of uniform velocity);
- "fair_to_both_sides": a boolean;
- "fairness_justification": one sentence explaining the boolean.
