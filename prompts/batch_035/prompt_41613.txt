I'm a second-year graduate student taking a seminar on the foundations of rational choice, and I've been assigned a term paper defending a verdict on whether Ellsberg-style preferences are rational. As I understand the setup, the classic two-urn case asks a subject to choose between betting on drawing a red ball from an urn known to contain fifty red and fifty black balls, and betting on red from an urn containing one hundred balls whose red/black proportion is entirely unknown. Most subjects prefer the first bet — and also prefer betting on black from the known urn over betting on black from the unknown one — which entails, if the preferences reflect probabilities, that they assign the unknown urn's black balls a probability both below and above one half. The one-urn version sharpens this: with thirty red balls and sixty balls that are black or yellow in unknown proportion, subjects typically favor red over black, and black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow, an inconsistent pair under any single sharp prior.

The seminar has gravitated toward a particular framing question that I would like the essay to engage centrally. One natural verdict is Savage's: the ambiguity-averse subject violates the sure-thing principle, and since that principle is constitutive of coherent subjective probability, the preferences are simply irrational — a cognitive tic to be corrected, not accommodated. But there is another reconstruction worth taking seriously: the subject's caution may track the *source* of the ambiguity rather than its mere existence. If the composition of the unknown urn was fixed by a mechanism indifferent to the subject's bet — say, a mechanical shuffling process whose outcome nobody knows — then the ambiguity carries no information about which bet is worse, and refusing to treat the unknown proportion as fifty-fifty looks like a flat-out error. But if the subject suspects that the composition was chosen by a party who profits from her choosing badly, the ambiguity itself becomes evidence of adversarial selection, and the situation is structurally closer to a game against an intelligent opponent than to a decision under nature. Maximin-like caution, which would be paranoid when facing a mindless urn, is at home when facing a schemer who can anticipate it. A wrinkle I find intriguing: in the actual experimental vignettes, the composition is presumably fixed before the offer is made, so nothing the subject does can influence it — yet subjects may reasonably be unsure about *why this particular pair of bets is being offered at all*, and the experimenter's choice of offer is not fixed in advance of the subject's behavior across the population.

Please write an essay that weighs whether Ellsberg-type preferences should be counted as irrational by the standards of decision theory, and reach a definite, justified conclusion (a conditional verdict, such as that the preferences are excusable in adversarial-selection environments but irrational when the ambiguity is mechanistic, is acceptable provided the condition is clearly specified). The essay should address at least the following: whether the sure-thing principle has the normative authority that Savage's verdict requires; whether the adversarial-source reconstruction genuinely vindicates the preferences or merely explains them away by redescribing them as ordinary expected-utility reasoning over an enlarged state space in which the urn-setter's motives figure as states; and whether there is any residual phenomenon left over — genuine "ambiguity aversion proper" — once all such redescriptions have been exhausted, or whether the concept dissolves under analysis. I'm not looking for a literature survey or a survey of competing formal models; argue the philosophy on the merits.
