My grandmother died three weeks ago at 89, and I'm giving the eulogy Satruday. She taught philosophy for forty years and was a lifelong Leibniz person, and — this is the hard part — completly sincere about it. She actually believed this is the best of all possible worlds; that God picks the world with the most variety squeezed out of the simplest laws, "the richest world on the smallest budget," as she put it; that nothing happens without a reason, and that every evil only gets permitted because it's tied into some greater good we usually can't see. She loved Leibniz's idea that to love someone is to take pleasure in their happiness, so the more people you love, the more sources of happiness you have — she used to say that loving us made her life light. And she believed the soul isn't made by the body, so it isn't unmade by the body's death: death was, in her words, a doorway, not a wall. My problem is that the standard eulogy moves are all things she thought were false. I can't say the world is a worse place without her, can't call her death unfair or pointless, and the usual "she's gone to a better place" doesn't even work, since by her lights there was no better place for a world to be — though I honestly don't know what she'd have said about where she is now, and I'd like the eulogy to have something to say there. The room will be half family who need actual comfort and half former students who will catch any philosophical fudging, plus her brother, who spent fifty years telling her that her optimism was just Pangloss — which she found doubly insulting, since she spent half her career explaining why it isn't. Write me the eulogy, about four minutes read aloud: it has to comfort the family without saying anything she'd have considered false, and without sounding smug, because the one thing she would hate is being remembered as Pangloss. Please write it in American English.
