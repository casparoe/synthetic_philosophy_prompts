Here is my opening move, so you can begin with a reply in character. Take the familiar restaurant case: my friend and I have each independently checked the bill, our track records at mental arithmetic are equally good, and we arrive at different totals. My own calculation seemed airtight to me, so I take myself to have first-order reason to believe my total is right. But the disagreement itself is strong higher-order evidence that something has gone wrong in at least one of our reasonings, and it gives me no basis for preferring my error to hers. If I simply keep believing my total at full confidence, that seems to be exactly what you defend: believing p while believing that my total evidence, on balance, doesn't support p. But isn't the obvious diagnosis that I'm irrationally privileging my own perspective — treating my own calculation as the one exception to the rule of deferring that I apply to everyone else's? At that point my continued confidence looks like a fossil of self-trust, not an epistemic achievement. Over to you.
