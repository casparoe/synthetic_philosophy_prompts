I'm a retired analytical chemist -- 35 years running mass specs and gas chromatographs in a quality-control lab, and now in retirement I've fallen hard for epistemology, of all things. In the lab we never thought twice about it: you calibrate an instrument against a reference standard, the standard against a national standard, and at some point the chain just stops. Nobody ever asked what licenses the bottom link. Now I'm co-leading a session of my philosophy reading group on what the literature calls "bootstrapping" or "easy knowledge," and I gather the classic illustration goes something like: I look at my gas gauge, it reads full, and I take myself to know both that it reads full and that the tank is full. I do this again on Tuesday, again on Wednesday, and eventually I reason: the gauge read full when the tank was full, and when it read empty the tank was empty -- so the gauge is reliable -- so now I know the tank is full on the basis of the gauge alone. But my evidence for "the tank was full" in those past cases just *was* the gauge (plus my trusting perception along the way). It feels like I've bootstrapped myself into knowledge of my instrument's reliability without ever checking it against anything independent, and a lot of philosophers think that's a genuine scandal, while others shrug.

The group is 12 people, mostly curious amateurs like me plus a couple of philosophy grad students, meeting for 90 minutes in a community center room with whiteboards, envelopes, and a big jar of pennies. I want you to design a hands-on activity where participants run a little "lab": they get an instrument (a deck of cards, a sealed envelope oracle, whatever works) that tells them things about hidden samples, they're asked to certify the instrument's reliability from test runs, and then to declare what they "know" about new samples. The trick I'd like built into the rules is that every channel they might use to check the instrument -- the reference samples, the lab assistant, even a second instrument -- turns out on inspection to be certified by the first one, so the whole validation loop closes on itself and there's nothing outside it. They should discover this themselves, not be told up front. Give me concrete rules, roles, the penny-based scoring if you use one, a rough timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the bootstrapping reasoning and Moore's "here is a hand" style of argument.

One more thing, and I mean it: in the debrief, don't give my group the "on the one hand, on the other" tour. I get enough of that. Take a definite position on whether the bootstrapping reasoning is actually defective or whether the scandal is overblown, and defend it -- say plainly what the participants did and didn't come to know, and where you'd draw the line and why. Also flag what you think will predictably go sideways the first time I run this (my guess is someone will try to smuggle in outside knowledge about how decks of cards work, but you're the expert), and be honest about which part of the philosophical problem no classroom game can actually reproduce. If it matters for the design: I'm allowed to give out small chocolate bars as prizes, but no real money.
