I work on the design review team at a fairly large borough council — we get maybe eight or ten proposals a month across my desk, things like new street furniture, changes to public building entrances, park redesigns, camera placements. Most are small but a few carry real money. The same argument keeps happening in our committee without any framework: one person says "this is just engineering, there's nothing political about a bench," another says "everything is political, stop pretending otherwise," and we go in circles until whoever speaks loudest wins. I read Langdon Winner's essay years ago, the one that asks whether artifacts have politics, and what stuck with me was that he actually splits it in two: some technologies require or fit naturally with certain kinds of social order to work at all, and separately there are cases where a design gets used deliberately to settle some issue in a community — and he also insists he's not claiming technology determines anything, since the same device can live under different social arrangements. That felt like it might give us a structured way through our arguments, but I cant ask my colleagues to read philosophy, and I've got half and hour, maybe forty minutes, per proposal.

So here's what I'd like: turn that line of thought into a procedure my team can actually run on a proposal. The questions to ask in order (who the artifact quietly serves, who it keeps out, whether it can function at all without particular relations of authority, whether the effect needs anyone to have intended it, and what would have to be true for a design to count as genuinely neutral), what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure ends with "no politics here, review as normal," and where it flags something for the committee. Crucially I want it to preserve Winner's distinction between a design that carries politics regardless of anyone's intentions — which for us is a side-effect to weigh, nothing sinister — and one where somebody has deliberately built the politics in, which should go to whoever handles declarations of interest. And it needs to be able to conclude "neutral" sometimes, otherwise it's just a machine for finding politics everywhere and my colleagues will bin it in a month. Mark the steps where a judgement call can't be turned into a rule, because our auditors will ask where discretion sits.

Then run it on three real-ish cases from our files: (a) replacing park benches with ones that have armrests every half metre, proposed on "durability and maintenance" grounds but which everyone privately knows is about stopping people sleeping rough; (b) automatic number-plate recognition at the two entrances of a public car park, justified as revenue protection; and (c) narrowing the road and lowering kerbs outside a primary school, which our road safety people want. I'd bet on the benches being the clear-cut case but I suspect the procedure won't come out as clean as I expect — please be upfront about where it lands, including any answer I won't like. Say how confident you are in each step of the procedure, and single out the part you're least sure of. And write in British English please — kerbs, not curbs. Sorry for the length, I wanted to get the cases down while I could still remember them.
