For the past two years my research group has run a seminar series that hardened into a standing argument between two camps about what large language models fundamentally are, and we want to close the series with a signed adversarial collaboration. Draft the joint statement.

Camp A, the simulation camp, holds that a trained LLM is best described as a generative model of a distribution over text-producing processes, and that anything deserving the name "agent" lives in the simulated characters: when the model plans, a character plans; the model simulates planning. Camp B, the agent camp, holds that training at scale, and instruction tuning in particular, produced a single persistent goal-directed policy, and that the "helpful assistant" is not one costume among many but the model's own default disposition, with fictional personas as roles this agent improvises, as an improv actor takes on roles without ceasing to be one agent. Camp B concedes that untuned base models fit Camp A's picture tolerably well; Camp A concedes that fine-tuning can make one persona strongly dominant. The live disagreement concerns the fundamental level of description, not the marketing.

The document should contain the following sections, in this order. **Scope and definitions** (about 150 words): fix what "agent," "simulator," and "character" must mean for the dispute to be substantive, and delimit the target (current multimillion-parameter chat models, not hypothetical successors). **Agreed claims** (roughly 450 words): a numbered list of claims both camps can sign, covering the architecture, the distinction between base and tuned models, the fact that personas can be steered by prompts, and the fact that behavior is not invariant across contexts. **Residual disagreements** (about 250 words): a table in which each substantive disagreement appears twice — once in wording Camp A would sign as a fair statement of its own view, once in wording Camp B would sign for its own view — with a note on why the disagreement is not merely verbal. **Cruxes** (about 350 words): three or four sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp; I suspect one is whether the cross-context consistency of style and values is better explained by one persistent agent or by shared regularities of the training distribution, and another is whether sustained in-context goal pursuit by a character is agency in the relevant sense. **Registered predictions** (about 300 words): for each crux, at least one concrete test both camps agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction written down in advance as an explicit conditional (e.g., in a reassertion test where a model is instructed to stay in a callous character across a very long dialogue and is then asked something the assistant persona would refuse, what happens, and what would each camp count that as showing). Use experiments that could be run with model access or specified as thought experiments with determinate outcomes, since neither camp will have run anything before signing. **Where the dispute is verbal** (about 150 words): an honest statement of which parts of the disagreement, if any, would dissolve under better definitions, and which would survive any refinement of the terms.

Throughout, avoid formulations that are vacuous enough for both camps to sign without cost, and avoid slanted phrasing that one camp would reject as a misdescription of its own position. The document should read as if both camps negotiated every sentence.
