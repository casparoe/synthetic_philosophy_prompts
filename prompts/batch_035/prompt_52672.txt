Ok hear me out. Everyone uses Parfit's fission case (teletransporter scans you, Mars-copy walks out, but the Earth-original also survives, so now there are two people with equal claim) to argue that personal identity isn't what matters in survival. I think that case is actually the weak version of the argument, and I want to argue for a variant that does the job better.

The variant: same non-destructive scan as above, but the machine also does a yearly sync — once a year it merges the memories and experiences of the Earth-person and the Mars-person into both bodies, so that after each sync each of the two remembers having lived both of the previous year's lives, and this just keeps going indefinitely.

Please argue that this repeated-merge variant is the stronger ladder to Parfit's conclusion. My hunch is that plain fission still gives you somewhere to hide: you can pick an answer like "only one of them is really me" or "neither is, so I died at the scan" and defend it. But with the yearly merges, the question "which one is the original?" degenerates so badly that you can see directly it was never carrying any weight, and its only the pattern of overlapping psychological connectedness that was ever doing the work. Make that case: show why each escape route available in ordinary fission (the 'both are me' line, the 'neither' line, the 'identity is just transitive so get over it' line) is available there but collapses under the merge structure, and why that asymmetry makes merging the better teaching case for reductionism about persons.

You can grant Parfit everything he claims about ordinary fission — the point isn't to re-litigate that, it's that fission lets people keep a foothold and the merge case pulls it away.
