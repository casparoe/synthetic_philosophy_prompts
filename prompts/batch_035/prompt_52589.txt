I chair the research ethics board at a mid-sized university, and we have a consistency problem with a philosophical edge. Over eight years of case-by-case adjudication our decisions have drifted: last spring two nearly identical deception studies — same cover story, same population, same debriefing — received opposite verdicts from different panels, and the affected investigator has filed a formal complaint. In response, one board member proposes that we codify our accumulated precedents into a binding decision procedure: for every morally relevant feature (degree of deception, population vulnerability, quality of debriefing, risk level) there will be a rule with enumerated exception clauses, so that any two protocols sharing the relevant features must receive the same outcome, and reviewers will simply apply the code. Another member objects that moral judgment does not work that way: whether a feature counts as a reason at all, and how much weight it carries, depends on the whole configuration of the case, so any code we write will eventually license a decision we can all see is wrong and forbid one we can all see is right — and that the solution to inconsistency is better deliberation, not a decision procedure that removes judgment from the loop. Before our next meeting I must circulate a written recommendation. Please draft the memo: take a position — adopt the code, reject it, or adopt a qualified version with stated limits — argue for it in terms the board can act on, engage the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than caricaturing it, and specify concretely what reviewers should do with the next borderline protocol, since the board cannot deliberate in the abstract while the docket is pending.
