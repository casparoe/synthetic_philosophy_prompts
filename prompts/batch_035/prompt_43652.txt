I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on the philosophy of history, and we've just finished a unit on the Great Man theory versus structural explanation, using the debate over whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler as the running case. In section, our TA keeps reaching for one analogy to argue for the structuralist side: interwar Germany was a forest full of dry tinder (or, in her alternate phrasing, a loaded gun), and Hitler was merely the match that happened to fall — the finger that pulled an already-loaded trigger. The work the analogy is doing is fairly transparent: sparks are interchangeable, and it is the powder rather than the match that does the killing, so the conclusion she wants is that some fascist, expansionist, probably genocidal regime was close to overdetermined by 1933, and the counterfactual question answers itself. A student on the other side of the table shot back with his own analogy: a leader like Hitler isn't a spark but the one specific key that happened to fit a lock — and the existence of a lock doesn't entitle you to expect the key exists, given all the near-miss keys (Wilhelm II, Ludendorff, the Weimar-era ultranationalists who never broke out) that turned in the same keyhole without turning it.

We've now been assigned a paper auditing one historical analogy of our choosing, and I'd rather do both of these properly than just pick the one that flatters my existing view. Can you lay out what maps onto what in each — the tinder, the spark, the fire, the lock, the key, the near-miss keys — and then be precise about where each mapping breaks? I'm especially interested in how to separate disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that don't. For example, a spark has no beliefs, strategy, or timing, and a forest fire spreads deterministically through fuel that is just sitting there, whereas political mobilization seems to involve an agent reading the "conditions" and reshaping them as he goes — but I can't tell whether that difference actually bears on the replaceability claim, or whether it's just a colorful irrelevance, since the structuralist can say the wood only has to be dry, not the fire predetermined. On the other side, the key analogy quietly assumes the lock was pickable by at most one shape of key, which is exactly what the structuralist denies. So: does either analogy actually carry the conclusion it was recruited to support, or was the analogy doing the arguing the whole time? And if you think there is a better comparison available — the one my TA perhaps should have used if she wanted her conclusion to survive — I'd like that too, with a note on what it gets right that tinder and keys both get wrong.
