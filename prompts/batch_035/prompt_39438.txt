I lead the data science team at a county health system that is preparing to deploy a risk model predicting 30-day hospital readmission, with the scores used to allocate a scarce follow-up program of nurse home visits to the highest-risk discharged patients. The two patient populations the program will serve differ substantially in their readmission base rates. Clinical leadership insists that the scores be calibrated within each population, since nurses must be able to treat a "30% risk" as meaning the same thing for every patient and the program's budget projections depend on it; meanwhile, a patient-advocacy organization, drawing on the fairness literature, is threatening a formal complaint unless the model achieves equal false-negative rates across the two populations, i.e., an equal chance that a patient who actually returns within 30 days is passed over by the program. I have explained to the oversight committee that the known impossibility results show these two demands cannot be jointly satisfied when base rates differ, but the committee wants to understand what that means for the decision actually in front of them, not for the academic debate. Please give the five most important implications of these impossibility results for the committee's choice among the options on the table: (a) calibrated scores with a single risk threshold applied to both populations, (b) calibrated scores with a lower threshold for the higher-base-rate population, and (c) abandoning the model for one population and reverting to unaided clinician judgment. Cover what each option silently gives up, whether any apparent escape routes (such as ignoring group membership at decision time, or retraining on different labels) actually escape the result, and which empirical question about the data would, if answered, most change the analysis. Present your response as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
