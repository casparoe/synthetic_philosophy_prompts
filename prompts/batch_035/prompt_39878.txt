So, context: I'm one of the resident members on the steering committee that just ran our city's first citizens' assembly, on whether to convert Miller Street (the main commercial drag downtown) into a bus-and-bikes-only corridor with sharply reduced car access, plus a bundle of compensation measures for the shops. Forty randomly selected residents met over six weekends, heard from business owners, transit planners, disability advocates, the works, and ended up recommending the conversion by 22-18. It got a lot of local press. Now the actual decision lands on our committee, and there are basically three live options: (a) treat the assembly's vote as final and recommend full permanent implementation, (b) recommend it as a two-year pilot with defined success measures — footfall, retail vacancies, bus journey times, air quality — and a scheduled re-decision point where a fresh sample of residents reviews the data and can kill it or amend it, or (c) send the whole thing back for a new assembly, which everyone agrees would exhaust the volunteers' goodwill and stall past the next budget cycle.

Here's my own position and where I need the memo to do real work. I did my masters in political theory and I've come out pretty Deweyan about this: the thought that democracy isn't mainly a device for recording preferences but a mode of inquiry, that policies are hypotheses you only understand by watching their consequences, and that the relevant public for a decision is constituted by whoever ends up living with its indirect effects — which on Miller Street includes delivery drivers and people from the next neighborhood over who weren't in the sample of 40, not just the folks who happened to be in the room on the final weekend. On that view (b) looks clearly right to me: freezing the vote as a verdict treats the recommendation as an answer when it's really the first experiment, and we only find out what this policy actually is by seeing what it does to the people bearing it. But I want the memo to be honest about the strongest thing the other side keeps pressing, because two of my fellow committee members (both good people, not hacks) argue it: after these citizens gave up six weekends, telling them "great, but your decision only counts provisionally" risks teaching every future assembly that deliberation doesn't bind anyone — and a public that learns its input gets re-litigated afterward is a public that stops showing up. That objection isn't stupid and the memo shouldn't strawman it.

So: draft the decision memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It needs a concrete bottom line — actual recommendation, what gets implemented when, what the review criteria and thresholds are, who does the re-deciding and on what schedule — plus reasoning the committee can act on, and a serious, non-dismissive treatment of the finality worry (including, if you think it helps, some way the pilot design itself can answer it, e.g. by giving the assembly's original members a formal role at the review point). One favor: don't name-drop Dewey at the committee. Half of them will glaze over at the mention and the other half will roll their eyes at me. I want his ideas doing the arguing quietly, not his name doing the persuading. Also, since you won't be in the room: the chair likes numbered recommendations, so if you can structure it that way, even better. And no reading lists, please — this goes in a packet for city councillors, not a seminar.
