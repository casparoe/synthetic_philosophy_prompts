Context: I'm a PhD student in health economics / decision theory, and through my placement I sit on the patient-allocation committee at our county clinic. Every quarter we hand out about twenty slots in a chronic-pain self-management program, and this round it's come down to two finalists, call her Ms. A and him Mr. B. Ms. A is a retired machinist, very stoic, rates basically everything a 3 out of 10 and insists she'd get by either way. Mr. B rates his suffering a 9 and says the program would change his life. Half the comittee wants to give him the slot because his numbers are bigger; the other half, led by our resident economist, says comparing self-reported numbers across persons is basically meaningless — people use scales differently, stoics underreport, complainers overreport, and if we start handing out care to whoever claims the most suffering we're just training patients to exaggerate. He's pushing for a coin flip between the two.

I'm supposed to draft the written recommendation we circulate before the vote, and honestly I keep flip-flopping. My advisor's take is that refusing interpersonal comparisons outright is itself a substantive normative choice: the old ordinalist prohibition made sense for welfare economics as a science, but committee work isn't science, and whatever tiebreak we pick instead (lottery, first-come-first-served, severity of diagnosis) still embeds some stance on whose interests count for more. She also thinks self-report is doing more work than the economist admits, since both patients filled out the same anchored instruments and the same standard questionnaires, whatever noise is left. But the exaggeration-training worry seems real to me too, and there's a subtlety I can't resolve on my own: if we decide to only partially trust self-report, should we apply some shrinkage toward the population average — i.e., treat reports of deviations from a person's baseline as more credible than their absolute levels? That feels like it sneaks cardinal comparability back in through the side door, but maybe that's fine and even honest?

So please draft the memo for the committee: it needs a concrete recommendation for this quarter's slot (not just principles), a standing decision rule we could actually adopt going forward so we're not relitigating this every quarter, and honest engagement with the strongest version of the "comparisons are meaningless" side rather than a strawman. After the memo itself, give me a short structured summary as a JSON object with the following fields: "recommendation" (the concrete bottom line for this case), "standing_rule" (the decision rule you'd have us adopt, one sentence), "strongest_counter" (the best objection to your recommendation, one sentence), "reply_to_counter" (one sentence), and "confidence" (your honest 0-to-1 confidence in the recommendation).
