I'm in a medieval political philosophy course, and we just spent two weeks on Aquinas on tyranny and tyrannicide — the discussion in the little treatise on kingship plus the bits in the Summa's second part about when killing by humans is licit. My study partner thinks he's found an argument that makes Aquinas's whole position collapse, and I can't see where it goes wrong, but it seems to prove way too much. Here it is, roughly as he stated it:

(1) Aquinas's standing rule is that no private individual may licitly kill the tyrant: punishment by death belongs to public authority, and a private person who judges and executes on his own usurps what belongs to the community. (2) Yet Aquinas concedes that a community which itself established the king can, for that very reason, depose him and even punish him — so tyrannicide can be licit when it is the community's act. (3) But every act of the community is carried out by particular men, and each of those men, considered in himself, is a private person — there is no third kind of agent in the picture, since for Aquinas the community isn't anything over and above the individuals who compose it. (4) So when the people's decree licenses certain men to kill the king, what actually happens is some number of private individuals killing him, and by (1) that is illicit. (5) If you reply that the community's mandate transforms them, notice that the mandate can only originate from still other individuals — the original constituents — who, taken singly, are private persons too, so the license never acquires a non-private source. (6) Hence tyrannicide is never licit; and since the same holds for sentencing, war-fighting, and taxing, no exercise of public authority is ever licit, and Aquinas's entire political theory is self-undermining. (7) So, my partner concludes, the private/public line has to be abandoned: private persons are licensed to kill the tyrant as soon as enough of their neighbors concur.

I can't accept (7) — it reads like a license for mob justice, and it flattens Aquinas's whole pastoral caution about private killing — but I also cant accept (6), since Aquinas obviously thinks public authority is possible and its acts licit. So something in the chain has to give, and for what it's worth I'd much rather keep the private-person rule from (1) than give it up. Which premise or step should I reject? Why did it look innocent in the first place? And what does rejecting it cost — am I then stuck with some picture of the community as an entity over and above its members that sounds totaly un-Aquinas-like, or does the cost land somewhere else entirely?
