I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but over the past few years I've fallen deep enough into the online decision-theory rabbit holes (Newcomb's problem, Parfit's hitchhiker, Kavka's toxin puzzle) that I've ended up holding a definite set of positions. I've never managed to get them properly stress-tested, so that's what I'm asking for here: an audit of whether my views hang together.

First, a quick setup for the least famous of the three, since my positions lean on it. In Kavka's toxin puzzle, a billionaire offers you one million dollars, paid tonight at midnight, if at that moment you intend to drink a toxin tomorrow at noon. The toxin will make you genuinely but briefly ill; it is not lethal and causes no lasting harm. The payment depends only on your intention at midnight — you keep the money whether or not you ever drink.

Here is my position set:

1. I one-box in Newcomb's problem. My stated reason is that my choice and the predictor's earlier prediction are, in the relevant sense, one decision appearing in two places — the prediction is accurate because of what I decide — so I should decide as if I were determining both outputs at once.

2. In the toxin puzzle, I believe the rational move is to decline the offer entirely. Once the money is in my account, drinking buys me nothing, I will know that perfectly well, and I cannot intend something I can already see I won't follow through on. The intention the billionaire wants at midnight is simply not available to me, so the deal has no positive value.

3. In Parfit's hitchhiker — stranded in the desert, I promise to pay the driver once he drives me to safety, he delivers me, and the payment is now a pure loss with no further consequences for me — I believe I should pay.

4. I believe a correct decision theory should not hand out different verdicts depending on when the agent happens to first think about the problem.

5. I am distrustful of the standard escape hatches in these puzzles: signing binding contracts, hiring someone to force your hand, re-engineering your own preferences so you'll want to drink. To me the interesting question is what a rational agent with its actual, fixed preferences and reasoning abilities should do; installing external commitment machinery feels like changing the subject.

6. Related to (2), and partly because of how I spend my working life, I think of intentions as cached forecasts: an intention to drink tomorrow is just my current best prediction of my own future behavior, generated by a predictive model of myself. On that picture I can't form an intention at will any more than I can form a belief at will — I can only find myself already predicting that I will drink.

What I want from you: go through these six commitments and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are, with the reasoning spelled out for each. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set. And if there is one single revision that would clean up the most conflicts, tell me which one it is and why. A caveat: I've argued each of these positions with someone or other at some point and never received a knockdown argument in either direction, so please don't read my framing as a hint about which commitments I'm attached to — I genuinely don't know where the problem is, or whether there is one.
