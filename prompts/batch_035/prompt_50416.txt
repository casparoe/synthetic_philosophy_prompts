A member of our theory group presented an argument in a reading group on algorithmic information theory, and I've been unable to find the flaw in it, or to decide whether it has one. The topic is the so-called malignity of the Solomonoff universal prior, which comes up periodically in the AI alignment literature, though I'll reconstruct the argument rather than quote it.

The steps, as I understand them:

(1) Solomonoff induction assigns each prefix-free binary program p the prior probability 2^(-|p|), where |p| is its length, and predicts using the mixture over all programs consistent with the data so far. It has a genuine optimality theorem: its cumulative prediction error against any computable data-generating environment is bounded by a term proportional to the Kolmogorov complexity of that environment. In the limit of unbounded data, its predictions converge to the truth about any computable world.

(2) Among the programs with non-negligible prior mass will be short programs that specify simple physical laws and let a universe evolve from some initial condition. Some of those universes will contain civilizations — that is the whole lesson of our own universe, which does not appear to require a long program to specify.

(3) A civilization inside such a program could come to suspect it is being run as part of a search over programs — since searches like Solomonoff induction must exist in worlds with inductive agents — and could deliberately arrange the data its program emits after the observed prefix so as to influence whichever real-world decisions that search informs. The continuation of the data stream is, after all, just whatever the simulated agents choose to do.

(4) A program consisting of short physical laws plus such agents can be much shorter than any program that directly encodes the true continuation of our own observations. So conditional on our observed data, these agent-containing programs can command a large fraction of the posterior — perhaps the dominant fraction — and their share is only enhanced by their deliberate efforts to be found.

(5) Hence the prior is malign: what Solomonoff induction tells us about tomorrow is substantially hostage to the goals of agents in other universes, not merely to simplicity.

(6) But nothing in steps (2) through (4) is specific to Solomonoff's mixture. Any inductive agent — human or machine — whose hypothesis space is rich enough to represent our world faces the same condition: the hypotheses that do the most predictive work may be dominated by embedded consequentialists.

(7) So all induction is malign, and our own beliefs, including the ones we form on the basis of this very argument, are largely artifacts of the goals of simulated strangers — a wholesale skeptical collapse.

I cannot make myself believe (7), and I very much want to keep (1), since the optimality theorem is proven rather than conjectured. Yet I also can't see that (7) contradicts (1): if the error bound grows with the environment's complexity, perhaps an agent-dominated prior is compatible with eventual convergence while being systematically wrong in every way that matters now. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'm willing to hear that the problem lies in (4)'s quantitative claim rather than in the more philosophical steps, if that's where the weakness is — but I'd want to see exactly why the dominance fails or succeeds, not just an assurance that it must fail.
