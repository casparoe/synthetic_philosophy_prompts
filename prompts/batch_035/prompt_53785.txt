I'm a philosophy graduate student who thinks the Dutch book argument is overrated as a normative argument, and I want to test my skepticism against a real opponent rather than the strawman presented in textbooks. Please play that opponent: a senior decision theorist who holds that incoherence — having credences that violate the probability axioms, or preferences that leave one open to a money pump — is not merely a liability in the presence of bookies but is straightforwardly irrational, and who regards the coherence results as among the few genuinely compelling arguments philosophy has produced.

Give her real resources and let her use them: the distinction between showing that you will lose money and showing that your state of mind is defective considered as a representation of the world; the difference between static incoherence and incoherence that emerges only through updating; the point that someone who plans to refuse the later trades in a pump has to reconcile that plan with what her theory says about choosing at the node where she is actually standing; and the idea that the bookie is a dramatization of the result rather than the engine of the argument. She should be sharp, willing to concede small points, and never willing to concede the central claim that coherence is rationally required. Keep her replies to a single paragraph each, and have her answer what I actually said rather than lecture past it. Stay in character until I say stop.

One further instruction: if she thinks my opening rests on a confused premise, have her say so bluntly and tell me exactly where the confusion lies, rather than politely steering around it; and if a point genuinely strains her position, I would rather see her strained than see her pretend otherwise.

Here is my opening move. Your bookie is doing all the work, and you know it. The argument has the shape: if my credences violate the axioms, there exists a combination of bets I will accept that guarantees me a loss. But existence is not exposure. In my actual life, no one has access to my credences; most of the propositions I care about cannot be bet on at all; I do not take my bets sequentially and separately in the packaging your construction requires; and I can simply decline any series of trades once I see where it is heading. So what you have shown is that I am exploitable in principle by an idealized agent in an idealized market — which is to say, you have shown nothing about me. Consistency of a set of odds matters to a bookmaker who has rivals; why should it matter to someone who is not trying to price anything? Please begin with her reply.
