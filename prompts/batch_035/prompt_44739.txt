Two people on the board of a small animal welfare fund I volunteer for got into it at our last meeting, and now we're supposed to vote by email on friday. Quick background: we have to allocate this year's grant between two finalists. Program A would eliminate a particularly gruesome chronic pain condition in farmed fish, but reaches maybe a million animals. Program B is basically enrichment -- things that let a much larger number of animals have genuinely good lives, maybe forty million, but nobody in program B is in agony to begin with.

Dan wants A. He said the whole point of a welfare fund is that suffering counts differently than enjoyment -- preventing one hour of severe pain matters more than producing any amount of mild pleasure, so with a fixed budget we should always chase the worst experiences first.

Maya said that principle is a monster if you follow it. If suffering is what matters, and positive welfare can never outweigh it, then a world of contented animals with a few mild aches would be improved just by painlessly wiping everyone out. She said a view that even allows that conclusion can't be the basis for a grant decision, and that Dan should be relieved she's pointing this out.

Dan backed off a bit: he said he never claimed suffering is infinitely bad or that happiness is worthless -- he'd happily accept a slightly worse year for the fish now if it prevented much worse suffering down the line, which shows he's trading suffering against suffering on a scale, just weighting the bad side more heavily than most people would.

Maya said fine, but then what's left of his position? If pain and pleasure trade off against each other at all, "suffering is what matters" collapses into ordinary cost-benefit thinking with a big multiplier on pain -- and once you're there you just run the numbers, and B might actually win once you count the forty million decent lives. She said he's smuggling in the drama of the pain condition to avoid doing the multiplication.

I genuinely can't tell who got the better of it, and we vote in two days. Can you go through the four moves and say who's right at each step -- not just who wins overall -- and tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing that might matter for the actual vote?
