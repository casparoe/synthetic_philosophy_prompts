For a term paper in my graduate seminar on epistemology, I want to analyze a real episode through the lens of a theory we have been studying, and I would like your help thinking it through carefully. The episode is the replication crisis in psychology, and the theory is the literature on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. The facts, as I understand them and as I want the analysis to take them: in 2011, the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology published a paper by Daryl Bem, a respected mainstream social psychologist, reporting a series of experiments ostensibly demonstrating precognition — using exactly the standard methods, statistical practices, and editorial scrutiny the field trusted. Because the conclusion was, by everyone's lights, impossible, the paper functioned as an accidental diagnostic: methods that could certify precognition could certify anything. Over the next few years, multi-lab replications of textbook findings failed, and in 2015 the Open Science Collaboration reported that of a hundred studies sampled from three prominent journals — ninety-seven percent of which had reported statistically significant results — only around a third replicated by the conventional significance criterion, with replication effects on average half the size of the originals.

The agent I want to analyze is a hypothetical but realistic mid-career researcher in that field, circa 2012 to 2015, who holds a body of first-order evidence: her own published, statistically significant results, produced by methods she followed conscientiously and reviewed for others. The higher-order evidence then arrives in a wave: not any new finding that contradicts her results, but evidence about the reliability of the very pipeline that generated them. So the first question is one of mapping. What exactly is the higher-order evidence here, and what does it rationally require of her? The standard cases in the literature involve a defeater that undercuts a specific source — the miscalibrated scale, the biased instrument — and the usual lesson is that higher-order evidence can rationally force revisions in first-order confidence without any new first-order evidence at all. But note the differences: no particular result of hers is contradicted; nothing is rebutted, everything is, at most, undercut; and the "source" whose reliability is impugned is not a single instrument but her entire training, craft, and field. Does the standard machinery still apply when the source is diffuse like that, and when the defeater is statistical rather than logical? Second, the akrasia question. Suppose she says, sincerely and in the same breath, "my study was carefully run and well-powered" and "my published result is probably false." Is that epistemic akrasia — believing against her own better judgment about what she ought to believe — or is it a coherent partitioning of labor between first-order and higher-order attitudes that the akrasia label misdescribes? Distinguish for me the researcher who ought to know better but doesn't revise from the researcher who judges she ought to revise and doesn't, and say which of them the higher-order-evidence literature is actually about.

Finally, I want the analysis to run in both directions. Where is the theory silent in this case? It speaks to the credences of individuals, but the crisis was collective: what the theory apparently cannot tell her is what to do about her published papers, her co-authors, her students' theses built on the old methods, or how she ought to vote as a journal reviewer — and I suspect those questions, not her private credences, were where the real epistemic action was. And what does the episode show about the theory? I can see three candidate verdicts, and I want you to argue for one: the crisis is a friendly confirmation of the thesis that higher-order evidence defeats without new first-order evidence; it is an anomaly, in that researchers who responded rationally at the higher-order level sometimes kept the field functioning precisely by being locally akratic; or it is a case the theory never anticipated, because it assumes an identifiable source whose reliability can be scored, and a research field is not that. Please be disciplined about what the theory can and cannot say rather than forcing it to speak, and end with an honest verdict on which of the three readings the record best supports.
