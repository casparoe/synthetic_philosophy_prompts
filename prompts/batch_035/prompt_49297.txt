I'm a high school senior writing my IB extended essay in philosophy on the debate between the inside view and the outside view in forecasting, and on the epistemic modesty that the outside view is often taken to support. My case study is the construction of the Sydney Opera House, and I'd like a disciplined analysis of that episode through this lens. The facts as I understand them: the Danish architect Jørn Utzon won the design competition in 1957, with an estimated cost of about seven million Australian dollars and a planned opening in 1963; construction began in 1959, before the geometry of the shell roof had actually been solved, which took years more of work; Utzon resigned in 1966 amid disputes with the New South Wales government; and the building finally opened in 1973 at a cost of roughly a hundred million dollars, ten years late — and then became, by most accounts, exactly the icon it was meant to be. If I have any of this materially wrong, please say so, because my whole essay rests on it.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Point one: reconstruct the inside view as it was actually available to the decision-makers in the late 1950s — what did they believe about cost and schedule, and what was that belief based on? Point two: state what the outside view would have told them, specifying which reference class you would use and why — and note, as part of the point, that choosing the reference class is itself a judgment that the outside view does not settle, so that using it is not the purely modest move it is advertised to be. Point three: identify the feature of this episode that strains both views — the roof design did not exist when construction started, so the project's scope was arguably not yet defined — and say whether either view has anything principled to say about projects undertaken before they are fully specified. Point four: say whether the building's eventual cultural and economic success counts against the outside view, or whether the outside view only ever claimed to predict cost and schedule, not value — and be explicit about which claim you take the outside view to be making, since my essay currently equivocates between them. Point five: give a verdict on whether this episode confirms the outside view, refutes it, or simply fails to test it, and on what it shows about the epistemic modesty the outside view is supposed to motivate.

Two requests about how you answer. First, if any of the five points turns out to be ill-posed — for instance, if the value question in point four is not something either view addresses — say so plainly within that point instead of manufacturing an answer; my supervisor keeps telling me that the strongest essays are the ones willing to report where a framework is silent. Second, if you think the Opera House is a bad test case for the inside/outside distinction altogether, or that my framing quietly assumes something contestable — for example, that the overrun was foreseeable rather than a matter of bad luck, or that the eventual success was part of anyone's reasoning — push back directly rather than working within my framing; I would much rather learn that now than read it in my examiner's comments.
