I cover AI and cognitive science for a general readership, and in interviews about machine translation I keep meeting the same picture: the system is like a clerk locked in a room with an enormous phrasebook, matching stretches of incoming text to stretches of outgoing text with no idea what either side means — the image descends from Searle's Chinese room, and something like it is implicit in Douglas Hofstadter's much-discussed argument that Google Translate's fluency is clever statistics with no understanding behind it. My sources use the picture to conclude that statistical translation is shallow — competent surface, no comprehension — and therefore that nothing philosophically interesting is happening. Before I put the analogy in print, I would like it audited. Set out what maps onto what: the clerk, the phrasebook, the matching procedure, the fluent-looking output, the outsider who mistakes it for understanding. Then give me the disanalogies that matter — I can see that a learned, graded, compositional process differs from discrete lookup, and that producing good output on sentences never seen is not something a phrasebook does, but I don't know which of these differences bear on understanding rather than merely on capability. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion survives: Hofstadter's case rests on hard literary tests like puns and register, while everyday users get prose that is fluent and mostly right, so is the shallowness claim about hard cases, about all cases, or about the underlying mechanism — and can the phrasebook picture, or a repaired version of it, actually carry any of those?
