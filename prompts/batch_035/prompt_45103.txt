I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I'm at work on a long-form essay about whether egalitarian commitments can survive contact with the empirical literature I cover — in particular the social epidemiology of relative deprivation, where the claim is not merely that inequality is unjustly distributed but that low social standing itself damages health and shortens lives. In the course of drafting I've assembled a position out of the arguments I've found most persuasive, and I'd like a rigorous consistency check before I publish, because I'd rather find the cracks myself than read about them in the letters page.

Here is the position. (1) I accept the Priority View: the moral weight of benefiting a person depends on how well off she is in absolute terms, so a given benefit to the badly off counts for more than the same benefit to the well off. (2) I take the leveling-down objection in its strongest form: a change that leaves the worst-off worse off and no one better off cannot be an improvement in any respect, and this decisively refutes telic egalitarianism, the view that inequality is bad in itself. (3) I believe relative deprivation is a genuine personal harm: being lower in the social scale than one's fellows is bad for a person, over and above her absolute level of resources — this is what the inequality-and-health research seems to show, and it is a large part of why I care about inequality at all. (4) I hold that our deepest obligations concern bringing people up to some threshold of a decent life, and that once a person is above it, her claim to priority largely falls away. (5) I accept that the moral value of an outcome is just the sum of what happens to each person considered separately — the facts about one person's life that determine how much her benefits morally matter should not depend on how anyone else is faring. Colleagues who have read early drafts disagree about whether this package is basically fine or quietly incoherent, and I have lost my ability to see it fresh.

Please audit the set rather than survey the literature for me. Identify which pairs (or larger subsets) are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and rank the tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, state exactly what would have to give and what the concession costs the rest of the position. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. To keep it usable for my notes, please present the audit as a table with exactly these columns: "Commitments in tension" (identified by number), "Genuine or apparent", "Severity (0–10)", "What has to give", "Cost of the revision" — with rows sorted by severity, highest first. Below the table, give a single sentence naming the cheapest consistent position left standing once the necessary revisions are made.
