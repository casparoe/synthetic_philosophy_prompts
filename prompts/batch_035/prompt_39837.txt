I'm organizing a two-week residential workshop that will bring together about eight philosophers of mind and six researchers from ML interpretability teams, followed by a small pool of seed funding for whatever collaborations emerge. The theme is the attribution of beliefs and other propositional attitudes to artificial systems, and I have to decide which open questions the workshop should actually be built around, since we can't do them all.

To give you a sense of the terrain as I see it, here are the candidate questions I've been circling, though I welcome additions and subtractions:

1. Dennett's original case for the intentional stance was that it earns its keep: belief attribution is justified when it is the most efficient compression of a system's behavioral patterns. But interpretability researchers now sometimes have direct access to a system's internals, finding candidate "beliefs" as directions in activation space. Is there anything left for the intentional stance to do once the physical stance becomes computationally tractable at scale, or does stance-selection just become an economic question about which abstraction is cheaper under which budget?

2. Should belief attribution to an AI system be tied to performance on the training distribution, to robustness under paraphrase and adversarial prompting, or to some counterfactual measure across environments the system was never deployed in? Sycophancy, where a system adjusts its assertions to please the user, is often cited as evidence that it has no stable beliefs, but I'm unsure whether that shows absence of belief or just noisy attribution practices on our side.

3. Is the ordinary concept of belief, with its connections to assertion, commitment, and accountability, simply the wrong tool for systems that can assert without being answerable? Some people in this space argue we need a graded or partial notion of belief attribution, but I don't know whether that's a genuine research program or a way of changing the subject.

4. When a bank deploys a system that denies loans, and regulators ask whether the system "believed" the applicant was a credit risk, the attribution has legal consequences. Does this institutional pressure distort the philosophical question, or does it helpfully constrain it?

Please give me a dependency-aware prioritization: which of these (or of questions I've missed) are upstream of the others, which are tractable for philosophers without hands-on ML access, and which you would cut as overrated or not actually open. I'd also like to know what developments over the next two or three years would move items up or down your list, since the seed funding decisions will follow the workshop by only a few months. The deliverable I need is a defended agenda, not a survey of the literature, and I'd appreciate it if you would state explicitly the criteria you're using and where they conflict.

Please write your response in American English.
