I'm a social psych PhD student who's been sitting in on a philosophy of emotion seminar, and it's driving me nuts that the two fields working on guilt vs shame are basically not talking to each other, and neither one ever seems to budge. On our side: decades of Tangney-style work, mostly questionnaires and diary studies, where the shame/guilt line gets operationalized as roughly "I am bad" vs "I did a bad thing," and the findings come out pretty consistantly that shame is the bad guy — it predicts hiding, withdrawal, externalizing blame onto other people, even relapse in things like substance use, while guilt predicts apology and repair. Meanwhile the philosophers have spent the last thirty years rehabilitating shame: the Bernard Williams-ish line I keep getting in the seminar is that guilt is a kind of bookkeeping emotion, about having caused an injury and owing something for it, while shame tracks something deeper — who you are, what you'd lose if the people who matter saw you clearly. On that view a person who felt only guilt and never shame sounds like a psychopath, not a saint.

And the two sides cite each other occasionally and then just... keep going. Nobody changes their mind, and nobody even seems to treat the other side's evidence as evidence. So what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on which emotion is actually better. Is this a verbal dispute where "shame" names two different constructs — the psychologists' global self-condemnation vs whatever the philosophers are pointing at — so that the empirical findings don't touch the philosophical construct at all? Or is it a real substantive disagreement where they're talking about the same thing and just disagree about whether questionnaires can capture it? Or is it something else entirely, like the two sides using different kinds of evidence (diary studies vs reflections on Greek tragedy) and each one thinking the other's method is ruled out in advance? I have my own hypothesis — that Tangney's "shame" and the philosophers' "shame" overlap way less than either side admits — but I'd like that stress-tested rather than just agreed with.

Then the part I actually care about for my dissertation: the old anthropological distinction between shame cultures and guilt cultures is supposedly discredited as ethnography, but is it maybe right as a policy lever? Is "which moral emotion should a culture lean on" even a coherent question — can a culture choose — or is a society's emotion profile too entangled with everything else (kinship, religion, how the criminal system works) to be steerable? Restorative justice programs basically assume we can shift people toward the repair-oriented version of guilt, and a lot of therapy culture assumes we should be dialing shame down everywhere, in schools, in parenting, wherever. Do the psych findings actually support either of those moves, or are they being stretched way past what they show? Give me your honest read on what kind of stalemate this is, and what, if anything, would count as progress.
