I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I read a fair amount of popular and semi-technical material on the foundations of mathematics, and there's a debate I keep circling back to: whether Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that the human mind is not a machine. As I understand the shape of it, Lucas started the modern version in the early 1960s, and Penrose gave it a more elaborate treatment in The Emperor's New Mind and a second pass in Shadows of the Mind in the nineties. The core move is the second incompleteness theorem: a consistent formal system that's strong enough for arithmetic can't prove its own consistency. Yet we, looking at such a system from the outside, can see that it's consistent. So there's something we can do that no formal system can do; ergo, we're not formal systems, or so the argument goes. The mechanist reply, as I've absorbed it, is that the argument only goes through if we can know our own consistency — and if we're machines, then by the very theorem being deployed, we can't. Penrose then tries to patch this, and the reply is that his patch smuggles in an assumption about the machine being knowably sound that a mechanist need not grant, and the whole thing reruns. Apparently this exact loop has been executed in print for sixty years by people who all understand the theorem perfectly well, and nobody converts.

Here's my problem. As a programmer, I find both sides talk past each other in a way I can't quite diagnose. The Lucas–Penrose side speaks of us seeing the consistency of a system, which sounds like a perceptual or quasi-perceptual relation; the mechanist side speaks of what is derivable within the system, which is a purely formal notion. Those can't just be the same thing, and I suspect the entire debate secretly turns on whether the human mathematician is being idealized as a consistent, sound, unbounded prover — an idealization no actual human satisfies, since actual mathematicians make mistakes, hold incompatible beliefs simultaneously, and die. And my day job makes this concrete in a way the older literature couldn't be: I work with large language models, which are literally computable functions, and they demonstrably assert contradictory things across contexts. Is the debate then just a verbal dispute about which idealization is legitimate — the infinite, idealized reasoner versus the finite, fallible artifact — with each side claiming the other's idealization begs its own question? Or is there a genuine substantive issue underneath, for instance about whether mathematical intuition has a distinctive epistemic status that no causal story could underwrite?

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on Lucas and Penrose. What kind of disagreement is this, really: verbal, a clash of idealizations, a hidden question about the evidential status of self-knowledge of consistency, or bedrock intuitions about mathematical insight that no argument could move? Please support the diagnosis with what each side actually claims, and tell me honestly whether anything — a sharper distinction between seeing consistency and proving it, a result about idealization, some development coming out of machine learning — would count as progress, or whether the impasse is structural and permanent, and why.
