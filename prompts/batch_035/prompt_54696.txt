"Popper's key idea was verisimilitude, or truthlikeness: science never reaches the truth, but it gets steadily closer to it, the way an iterative algorithm converges on the global optimum. He even proposed a measure of this: a theory is closer to the truth the more true consequences and the fewer false consequences it has. In the mid-1970s two logicians independently showed that this definition was broken — under Popper's own measure, one false theory can never come out closer to the truth than another false theory, so the comparison collapses entirely. Popper gracefully conceded and abandoned the idea. But the intuition survives, and it is essentially correct: each time a theory is falsified, its successor inherits all of the predecessor's true consequences while shedding some of its errors, so the sequence of theories is monotonically approaching the truth. That is why each generation's best science is more verisimilar than the last, and why we are entitled to regard our current best theories as at least approximately true."
