I'm on the committee for our PhD program's comprehensive exam in philosophy of mind, and this year I've been assigned the topic of pain asymbolia and what it shows about the badness of pain. The syndrome is well known to our students: patients with certain lesions (classically in the cingulate region) report that they feel pain and can identify its intensity and location, but they seem entirely unconcerned by it — they neither withdraw nor display the usual distress, and sometimes smile or joke while being subjected to noxious stimulation. The natural lesson is that pain and its badness come apart. But I don't want a question that can be answered by reciting that observation plus the standard labels, because I suspect the obvious lesson is too quickly drawn.

Here is what I want the question to force. Students should have to confront the fact that pain asymbolia cuts in different directions depending on one's theory of pain's badness. If one holds a motivationalist or imperativist line (e.g., Colin Klein's view that pains are imperative states that command protective action), asymbolia looks like a direct counterexample: the command is received but not obeyed. Yet the asymbolic still says "that is pain," which suggests something is preserved and that the phenomenon may be more like a disruption of the pain's meaning or grip than an absence of pain. Conversely, if one holds that pain's badness lies in its evaluative content — that pain represents some bodily condition as bad-for-one — then asymbolia might be explained as a failure of uptake rather than a falsification, but this risks making the theory unfalsifiable in an ad hoc way. And a simple desire-based account, on which pain is bad because sufferers want it to stop, has to say something about whether asymbolics have lost a desire, suppressed an expression, or stopped interpreting an experience they still undergo — and about why we still feel the pull of saying something bad is happening to them even when they report that they don't mind. I'd also like the question to touch on whether the putative link between asymbolia and depersonalization helps or hurts: if the asymbolic's pain feels like something happening in a body that is not quite theirs, does that show that pain's badness is bound up with bodily ownership, or does it merely show that asymbolia is a heterogeneous syndrome that shouldn't be treated as a single datum at all?

Please draft one essay question, answerable in about ninety minutes, together with a model answer of roughly 600–700 words and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit points worth 4 marks each (20 total), and for each point include a one-line note on the way candidates typically botch it — e.g., by conflating the asymbolic's lack of distress with an absence of pain, by treating the desire-based and motivationalist accounts as identical, or by using "unpleasantness" and "badness" interchangeably without flagging that they might come apart. The question itself should be phrased so that a student who has merely memorized the definition of asymbolia and the names of the main theories cannot pass without also making a substantive judgment about what the syndrome actually demonstrates.
