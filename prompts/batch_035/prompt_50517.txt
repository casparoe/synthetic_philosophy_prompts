For the past year I've been co-facilitating a cross-partisan dialogue group in my town, a commitment I made after my brother and I stopped speaking for a month over politics. In preparing for sessions I keep running into the argument from moral foundations theory: Haidt's finding that liberals' moral judgments load heavily on care and fairness while conservatives draw on the fuller set of foundations — loyalty, authority, sanctity — together with his suggestion that this makes conservative moral perception more complete, and the corresponding jab that liberals can literally not see what motivates roughly half the country. I would love to hand my group a diagnosis of why the surrounding debate never moves, because the two loudest objections don't even engage each other.

The psychologists attack the empirical claim: that the asymmetry is an artifact of question wording and American sampling; that the fairness foundation quietly conflates proportionality with equality, so liberals aren't missing a foundation so much as scoring differently on it; that purity's ties to disgust sensitivity have not held up as cleanly as advertised; and, from the system-justification camp, that the very framing of left and right as symmetric moralities is itself a motivated result. The philosophers attack the inference instead: even if the psychological asymmetry is real, breadth of moral responsiveness is not obviously accuracy rather than susceptibility — loyalty and authority may be tuned to a parochial past, and a moral sense with fewer, more discriminating alarms might be the better instrument. What I never see is anyone establishing which of these battles is the decisive one.

My tentative view is that the whole thing turns on an unstated picture of moral perception: whether registering more foundations is like having more receptors (strictly better) or like having more cravings (not obviously good), and that this is a metaethical question no psychological study could settle. But I hold that only loosely, and I'd like a real diagnosis. Is this an empirical dispute that better instruments could resolve, a verbal dispute about words like "complete" and "right," two genuinely separate questions traveling under one headline, or a clash of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? Please support your diagnosis with what each side actually claims, and tell me what — a replication, a sharper foundation taxonomy, an explicit bridge principle, or nothing — would count as progress. If nothing would, I need to know that too, because it changes what I tell my group we are doing when we meet on Tuesdays.
