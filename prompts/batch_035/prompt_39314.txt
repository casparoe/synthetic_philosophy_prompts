I teach an undergrad seminar on comparative ethics, about 14-16 students, and we have one 80-minute session left in our unit on Buddhist ethics. The students have read enough to know the basics: karma is tied to intention (the early texts basically say it's volition that I'd call karma, rather than the outward act), intentions have wholesome or unwholesome roots, and the fruits of action can ripen on a delay. But they've all latched onto this move where they say "oh, so it's just consequentialism with a longer time horizon and rebirth bolted on as the payoff structure," and I want them to feel where that reading actually strains instead of just being told. I'd rather the problem show up in what they do than in anything I say at the whiteboard.

So I want you to design a game/simulation for that session. The shape I'm imagining: some kind of multi-round scenario where students make real choices with real stakes -- a village during a drought deciding about water allocation, or something better if you can think of it -- with hidden information so that good intentions sometimes go badly wrong and self-interested moves sometimes turn out great. The key mechanic: two separate ledgers running the whole time. A welfare ledger that gets scored purely from outcomes (the facilitator can just tally this from the outcome tree), and a karma ledger scored from intentions, which each player writes down on a card *before* their move is revealed and which a rotating "registrar" scores against a simple rubric keyed to the wholesome/unwholesome roots. Design at least one round where the two ledgers demand opposite moves, and some way for karma to ripen late -- carry across rounds or pay off at a delay -- so they feel that karmic consequences aren't just immediate welfare consequences. Cheap materials only, no software, no money changing hands.

Then the debrief: how to map specific moments onto the doctrine and onto the consequentialism question, i.e. whether the game is evidence that Buddhist ethics is or isn't consequentialism, or is a third thing entirely. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I'm guessing players will learn to *perform* compassion on the motive cards once they figure out the rubric, which, fine, maybe that's even instructive, but I want to know how to handle it -- and be frank about what the activity flatly cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply myself at the end. (My own worry is that rebirth across lifetimes and the no-self stuff can't be gamified with points without quietly smuggling back a self who owns them, but maybe you disagree.) Please write the whole thing in American English.
