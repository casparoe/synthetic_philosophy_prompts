I cover science and ideas for a general readership, and I'm working on a long-form piece about Australia's 2008 parliamentary apology to the Stolen Generations. The salient facts, so we're working from the same record: on 13 February 2008, Prime Minister Kevin Rudd moved a motion of apology for the laws and policies of successive parliaments and governments, and in particular for the removal of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children from their families -- for the pain, suffering and hurt of these Stolen Generations, their descendants and for their families left behind, the motion said, we say sorry. The apology came after a decade in which John Howard's government had refused to offer one, on the grounds that present generations should not be held accountable for the actions of past ones, and it was delivered alongside an explicit refusal to establish a compensation scheme, on the argument that an apology and monetary compensation were separate questions. The motion was passed near-unanimously and received enormous public support.

Analyze this episode through the philosophy of collective responsibility and state apology for historical wrongs, and be disciplined about the mapping. Who, exactly, was the apologizing agent -- the parliament, the state, the nation, or the government of the day -- and which account of collective moral agency can make that agent the right kind of entity to apologize for removals carried out decades earlier by different officeholders under different statutes? Does the apology-without-compensation structure count as an apology on any serious theory of what an apology requires, or does it show that some apologies are real without repair? Conversely, does the Howard position -- that responsibility dies with the perpetrators -- actually fit the case, given that the state that apologized is institutionally continuous with the ones whose policies did the removing? I'd like you to say where the theory illuminates the episode, where it goes silent (for instance, on why an apology moved millions of people who bore no responsibility under any theory), and what the episode shows about the theory: a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the philosophers never anticipated. Please also tell me candidly whether a philosopher of collective responsibility would say the whole event is better understood as a political ritual than as a moral act, and if so, whether the standard theories misdescribe it.
