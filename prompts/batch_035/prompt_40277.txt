I spent my working life in physics — statistical mechanics, the final two decades on disordered systems that never quite reach equilibrium — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy in earnest. The text that has gotten its hooks in me is Nietzsche's thought experiment: the one in which a demon appears to you in your loneliest loneliness and announces that this life, exactly as you have lived and will live it, with every detail intact and no novelty anywhere in it, must return innumerable times, and then asks whether you would gnash your teeth and curse the demon, or whether you would have to answer that you had never been told anything more divine. I have drifted into a set of positions on it, and before I lay them out for my reading group I want a consistency audit. Here is the package:

(1) Cosmologically, I believe the recurrence is false of our universe: it expands without bound, total entropy increases monotonically toward heat death, and no global configuration remotely like the present one ever returns.

(2) Mathematically, I believe the Poincaré recurrence theorem: in a closed Hamiltonian system whose accessible phase space has finite volume, almost every state returns arbitrarily close to any earlier state, and does so infinitely often given unbounded time.

(3) I believe (1) and (2) are consistent, because the theorem's hypothesis — bounded phase space — is precisely what an ever-expanding universe fails to satisfy. I would like this piece of reasoning checked in particular; it is the sort of thing I thought I understood twice in my career and was wrong both times. (As I recall, essentially these recurrence considerations were pressed against Boltzmann's program at the end of the nineteenth century, though I may be garbling the history.)

(4) Philosophically, I read the demon's question as a purely psychological test of affirmation — a diagnostic of whether one endorses one's life as it stands. On this reading, the correct way to take the question, and the verdict one reaches, do not depend in any way on whether the recurrence is physically true.

(5) At the same time, I hold that the test would lose its bite for an agent certain that the recurrence is impossible: a thought experiment whose antecedent one assigns zero credence cannot do genuine diagnostic work on one's values, no more than I can be pressured by being asked how I would feel if two and two were five. If the demon's report is known to be false, the question it carries is inert.

(6) I further believe that in a genuinely Poincaré-recurrent world, each cycle would be identical in every respect that fixes the facts: the microstate determines the mental state, so the inhabitant of cycle n is in exactly the state of her counterpart in cycle 1, and nothing accumulates across cycles — no memory spanning them, no carry-over, no improvement. From the inside, an infinity of such cycles could not be distinguished from a single unending life; the multiplicity adds no substance.

(7) Finally, I have read somewhere — I can no longer place it — that the question is meant to work as a covenant: the yes is meant to bind you across the recurrences, so that you go into the next cycle differently, with more resolve and less resentment. I find this reading attractive, though I am in no position to adjudicate Nietzsche scholarship.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me; and at the end, tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I would rather learn from you now that the package is incoherent than discover it under questioning at the seminar.
