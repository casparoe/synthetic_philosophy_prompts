Two friends of mine (we're juniors, this was after philosophy club) got into it about Buridan's ass -- the donkey that's equally hungry and equally thirsty, exactly between a bale of hay and a bucket of water, and supposedly starves because it has no reason to pick one over the other. It went like four rounds:

(1) Marcus said the paradox is basically solved: if the ass is truly indifferent, then either choice is just as good, so nothing is lost by picking left. Indifference doesn't paralyze you, it just means you can't go wrong. The ass starving is a bug in how philosophers model choice, not a real possibility.

(2) Dana said that's too fast. If the ass picks left with literally nothing favoring left, then its choice isn't justified -- it's just a spasm. A choice you couldn't explain at all, not even badly, doesn't seem like a choice. And she had a weird add-on: the ass could flip a coin, and the flip is special because it can't be improved on -- any alternative way of breaking the tie is exactly as arbitrary as the flip, so the flip does literally everything that could be done. She said that means it's fully rational, not just excusable.

(3) Marcus said wait, "can't be improved on" doesn't make something rational -- a broken clock that can't be improved on is still broken. And how does randomness give the ass a *reason* to go left? All it does is outsource the arbitrariness to the coin. He also said something I didn't fully follow: that after the ass goes left a few times it'll start *preferring* left, so preferences can be built up out of arbitrary choices rather than discovered beforehand -- which he claimed dissolves the whole paradox because the "paralysis" only ever had to last one round.

(4) Dana said if preferences get built from arbitrary choices then the arbitrariness never goes away, it just gets buried -- you'd be living your whole life on foundations of coin flips and shrugs, and "I did it that way because I did it that way" is not the same as having reasons. She agreed the ass won't actually starve, but said the paradox is pointing at something real: that our picture of rational choice as always following reasons might just not cover the ground floor, and no amount of cleverness with coins fixes that.
