I've been reading Overcoming Bias and then LessWrong since roughly 2008, and the question of whether a rational person should vote is one of those chestnuts the community has been arguing about for as long as I've been lurking — the expected-value defences of voting that circulated around the 2016 election, the occasional contrarian post arguing that turnout is basically expressive signalling, the recurring fights about whether tiny-probability-high-stakes reasoning proves too much. I've finally written up my own take as a long-gestating blog post, and before I publish it I'd like serious comments. Not encouragement — actual scrutiny.

Here is the draft.

---

**The Twin Multiplier, or: Why I Vote Without Believing My Vote Is Decisive**

The old problem, restated quickly. Downs observed in 1957 that since the probability of casting the pivotal vote in a mass election is vanishingly small, an instrumentally rational citizen should usually abstain. Riker and Ordeshook formalised this in 1968 as R = pB − C + D, where p is the probability your vote is decisive, B the benefit of your side winning, C the cost of voting, and D a residual term for duty and similar satisfactions. The D term was widely read as an admission of defeat: the calculus only works because we smuggled the conclusion in as a psychological payoff. In a large election p is on the order of one in ten million even in a competitive race, so pB is essentially zero and whether you turn out depends on whether duty outweighs the queue.

The effective-altruist reply was to attack B instead of p. If the difference between electoral outcomes is valued at the scale of a nation rather than a person — lives, policy, the whole bundle — then one-in-ten-million times something enormous is not negligible, and an hour of voting compares surprisingly well to an hour of anything else. I always found this half-persuasive. It works only if you're willing to multiply genuinely astronomic stakes by genuinely tiny probabilities and take the product seriously, and the same style of reasoning endorses wagers on Pascal-style grounds that most of us, rightly, decline elsewhere.

My proposed fix is a different term entirely: the size of the reference class of your decision-twins.

The pivotal-probability estimates all condition on the actual electorate: given that millions of others show up or don't, what's the chance the margin lands exactly on your vote? But the decision I'm actually making is not mine alone. If I use something in the neighbourhood of functional or evidential decision theory — the update-on-your-own-action reasoning that this community knows from Hofstadter's superrationality and the decision-theory sequence — then the relevant event is not my single ballot but the correlated bloc of people whose choice moves with mine: people with similar information, similar values, similar processing. If there are a hundred thousand of us, and our turnout decisions really do correlate, then my abstention is evidence about the bloc, and the effective decisiveness of my choice is not p but something like the probability that the bloc is jointly pivotal. Call the ratio the twin multiplier. Under a modest multiplier, voting becomes straightforwardly rational without any exotic valuations of B and without appealing to duty at all. The D term isn't needed; it wasn't psychology that was missing from the calculus but decision theory.

I anticipate the objection that this proves too much: that twin reasoning licenses contributing to every threshold public good — pledge drives, charity matching campaigns, anything with a cliff-edge payoff and correlated contributors. I partly accept this and partly bite the bullet: I think threshold public goods are exactly where correlated-decision reasoning does the most work, and that our intuitions against those cases are calibrated to a causal-decision-theoretic picture I reject. The harder objection, which I don't resolve in the draft, is Brennan's: that if my decision is a sample of my twins' decisions, then voting badly is also multiplied, and epistemically negligent voting is worse than abstention by the same factor that makes voting good. My stopgap is a defeater condition: vote only when you have done the epistemic work your twins would have done. But since my twins are by construction like me, it's unclear how I could ever fail this test from the inside, which suggests the condition is vacuous rather than merely unmet. I flag this honestly and leave it as the post's open wound.

Four things I'd like from you, in decreasing order of importance:

1. Is the twin multiplier actually coherent, or have I committed some version of the classic error of blending causal and evidential expected value — getting the benefits of a hundred thousand votes while paying the cost of one? Please adjudicate between the readings on which the move is trivially true, subtly incoherent, and true-but-assuming-FDT, since I suspect my readers will divide along exactly those lines.
2. Have I stated the p-literature accurately — Downs's original claim, what Riker and Ordeshook actually intended the D term to do, and the order of magnitude of pivotal probabilities in large elections? Correct me where I'm sloppy, since I'm citing these informally rather than in an academic register.
3. Does the defeater condition swallow the argument, as I fear it does, and can you see a repair that keeps the twin multiplier but doesn't make self-trust do all the work?
4. Is section three, the twin multiplier itself, genuinely a new contribution to this well-worn discussion, or have I reinvented something from the 2016-era blog debates — or indeed from the academic literature — closely enough that I should be locating and crediting the prior versions rather than claiming the framing?

Be blunt, including about whether the piece is worth publishing at all. I've sat on this argument for close to a decade, which I recognise is as much a warning sign as a credential.
