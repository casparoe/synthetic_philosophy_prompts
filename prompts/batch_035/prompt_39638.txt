As a retired physical chemist who spent a career measuring things that could, at least in principle, be measured, I have become quietly obsessed with an area of ethics that resists exactly that: the literature on moral worth, i.e., on what makes an action creditable to the agent rather than merely correct -- the gap between doing the right thing and doing it for the right reasons, in the right way. My evening philosophy group puts out a spoof issue each December, and this year it is my turn to contribute. Rather than another mock dialogue, I would like a parody of a document, and I would like your help drafting it: a year-end performance review, in a deadpan corporate register, written by the Moral Worth Compliance Office of a large company, assessing an employee (call her R., of the procurement division) not on output but on the moral worth of her actions during the review period.

The comedy should come from getting the philosophy right, so that a reader who knows the Kant-and-after literature laughs rather than winces. The office should struggle with the genuine problems of the area, each handled somewhere in the review's sections or fine print: the epistemology of motives, since the office can observe only behavior and must concede, ideally in the passive voice, that no observable conduct entails any particular motive, which leaves it certifying conformity to duty while inferring worth by an unstated procedure (a limitation section, modeled on the disclosure pages of financial instruments, would suit this); the familiar case of the shopkeeper whose honesty is indistinguishable in output from the honest shopkeeper's, which the review should treat as an unresolved audit exception rather than a joke; mixed motives, as when R. donates to the disaster fund partly from duty and partly because her manager is watching, with the office needing some rule for whether worth degrades proportionally, is nullified, or survives -- and punting by issuing a percentage; the fact that outcomes do not affect worth, so that R. is neither penalized when her honest warning arrives too late nor credited when her careless advice happens to help, with the attendant residue of moral luck consigned to a footnote; overdetermination, as when she would have done the right thing anyway for self-interested reasons, so that her dutiful motive made no difference; and the question whether worth comes in degrees or is all-or-nothing, which the office should refuse to decide while nonetheless issuing a numeric score. Somewhere in a reviewer's note, the office should quietly take a stand on whether worth requires anything like a thought of duty at all, for instance by its handling of R.'s unreflective kindness to a junior colleague: counted either as merit or dismissed as insufficiently principled, with the choice doing real philosophical work while remaining, on its face, an administrative judgment.

Please keep the voice straight throughout -- no winking at the reader, no asides to the effect that any of this is odd; the document should read as though the office has been conducting these reviews for decades and finds them unremarkable. Format it with section headings and a rating scale as a real review would have, roughly 600 to 700 words in the body, and close with a machine-readable final line of the form \answer{Overall moral worth grade: X}, where X is a short phrase or letter grade consistent with the body of the review.
