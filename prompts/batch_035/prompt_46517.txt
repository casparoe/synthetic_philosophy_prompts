I'm taking a seminar on philosophy of mind, and we've spent the last few weeks on Dennett's heterophenomenology from Consciousness Explained. The core idea, as I understand it, is that the science of consciousness should proceed the way anthropology or literary interpretation treats a text: you take the subject's utterances at face value provisionally, use them to construct a catalog of what happened in their apparent world — what they experienced, in what order, with what qualities — and then that catalog becomes data that third-person science can confirm, disconfirm, or reinterpret, with the subject no more privileged than any other measuring instrument once the text is fixed. I like the method's discipline, but when I try to say exactly which parts of a subject's report are authoritative and which are up for grabs, my own verdicts on cases don't line up, and I'd like your help finding the principle behind them, if there is one.

Here are the verdicts, case by case. In the color phi phenomenon — two stationary dots flashing in alternation, different colors, subject reports seeing one dot move smoothly and change color midway — I side with Dennett: the report of the intermediate motion and color is accurate about the experience, even though the second color hadn't been shown yet at the moment the midway experience would have to have occurred; consciousness just isn't in real time, and that's fine. Same for the cutaneous rabbit, where taps at the wrist and elbow lead subjects to report a sequence of hops at locations never touched, including up the forearm: I say the phantom hops are real parts of the experienced world. But now a different case: a subject in a working-memory experiment misreports which of two words appeared first by 200 milliseconds on a plain, non-illusory display, plausibly just because memory degraded over the report interval — and here my gut says the report is simply wrong about the experience, not data to be interpreted, and no interesting story about the editing brain is warranted. Meanwhile, in a choice-blindness study where a subject explains at length a choice she didn't actually make (the experimenter swapped her selection), I treat the confabulated reasons as data — but data about her self-model or her storytelling, not about her experienced world, and I can't say why that distinction does any work given that I let the phi subject's report fix the facts. Then there are two cases I keep going back and forth on: a hypnotized subject who reports feeling no pain while physiological measures show strong stress responses, and a person with aphantasia who reports having no visual imagery at all — in both, I can't decide whether the report is authoritative about the apparent world or is a fallible claim about it that other evidence can defeat.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of mind who works on the methodology of consciousness science, think carefully, and brainstorm before committing. First, tell me whether there is a single principle that captures all or most of these verdicts — something that says, precisely, when a subject's report fixes facts about their apparent world, when it's merely fallible evidence about it, and when it's data about something else entirely, like the subject's self-model. If a single principle won't do, propose a small set of candidates and say which case motivates each clause. Then say what the best candidate predicts about the two cases I can't settle — the hypnotized subject and the aphantasia report — and be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, since I suspect I'll have to give up either the plain-display memory case or the phi case, and I'd like to know which one is cheaper to lose and what the principle I'd be keeping actually commits me to.
