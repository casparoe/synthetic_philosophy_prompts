For the last several years I have been teaching a seminar segment on whether the world is becoming less violent, and I am now writing the chapter of a book in which I must finally commit myself to a position rather than merely gesturing at the literature. Before I do, I want an audit of the package of views I have drifted into, because I suspect that not all of it can be true at once, and I cannot see clearly where the fault lines lie. The commitments are as follows. (i) The long-run decline in per-capita rates of homicide, battlefield death and interpersonal cruelty since roughly the early modern period is real and well evidenced, and it is explained in large part by the consolidation of states that monopolise the use of force. (ii) The correct metric for the question whether the world is becoming less violent is per-capita risk: a world of ten billion people with fifty thousand murders is, in the relevant sense, less violent than a world of one million with sixty thousand. (iii) When assessing how bad a world is, total suffering is what matters: scale counts, and a world containing more victims is worse, other things being equal, however the denominator moves. (iv) The concept of violence should not be restricted to deliberate physical harm; it should include avoidable deaths from famine, deprivation and economic arrangements — structural violence, as it is often called. (v) Non-human animals count: the suffering inflicted by industrial animal agriculture is violence, and by any headcount it dwarfs all human-on-human killing. (vi) There is a single fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent — a substantive empirical question, distinct from our feelings about it — and disputes over what counts as violence are merely verbal, to be tidied up by stipulation before the real empirical work begins.

I will add two further reactions of mine that may or may not sit well with the above. I take the twentieth century, with its industrialised, state-directed killing, to demonstrate no decline whatsoever in the capacity of organised states to slaughter — if anything the reverse. And I find myself unmoved by the observation that if population keeps growing whilst rates keep falling, the absolute number of violent deaths may remain constant or even rise: this strikes me as a mere artefact of denominator choice rather than a genuine change in how violent the world is. I am not confident these reactions are consistent with everything in the first paragraph, and I am still less confident about which, if any, of the six commitments I would be least sorry to abandon — some feel like central planks of my view and others like curiosities I have picked up along the way, though I hesitate to say which are which, since my sense of their importance may shift once I see the structure of the tensions.

What I should like, then, is not a survey of the debate but an audit of my own position set. Go through the commitments and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against one another, which only appear to, and which are compatible despite seeming not to be. For each genuine tension, say how severe it is, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the package; for the merely apparent ones, show precisely where the appearance of conflict comes from. Finally, identify the single revision that would clean up the most, and be specific about whether a plank or a curiosity has to give. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious, and do not resolve them by quietly redefining terms I have used — if a commitment must go, name it.
