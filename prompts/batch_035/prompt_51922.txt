I'm a mid-career transactional lawyer: my working life is spent drafting and negotiating acquisition agreements, and my native vocabulary is the vocabulary of that practice — representations and warranties, disclosure schedules that carve out exceptions, knowledge qualifiers ("to the seller's knowledge"), materiality thresholds and material-adverse-effect qualifiers, entire-agreement clauses, the securities-law treatment of half-truths (a statement can be literally true and still fraudulent when it's misleading in context), the line between actionable misrepresentation and mere puffery, and the distinct remedies that attach to each breach (rescission, indemnification, out-of-pocket damages). Assume I know all of this fluently and have essentially no philosophy training beyond a survey course years ago.

Here is the debate I want translated into that vocabulary, as I've pieced it together from reading. Philosophers standardly define a lie as an assertion the speaker believes to be false, made with intent to deceive. Misleading, by contrast, is getting someone to a false belief without asserting anything false — usually by saying something true that carries a false implicature, or by strategically omitting. Their stock examples: answering "Is your friend home?" with "He's not in the garage" while he's upstairs; the witness who says "I have no knowledge of any missing funds" while sitting on the bank statement; the true statement whose context invites the hearer to draw exactly the wrong conclusion. On one side of the moral question stand those who think the difference is deep: lying breaches a duty attached specifically to assertion, so a lie is wrong in a way a misleading truth is not, or not in the same way. On the other side stand those who think the difference is morally superficial: what matters is the false belief induced in the hearer, and a lie and a misleading truth that produce the same belief are equally wrong. A minority even argues the misleader is sometimes worse, since he keeps his hands technically clean. Please restate both sides, and the shared terrain they fight over — assertion, implicature, the duty allegedly breached, the state of the hearer — in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can.

Then I want the residue report, which is what I actually care about. Which parts of the debate map cleanly onto my doctrines — my instinct is that knowledge qualifiers do a lot of the work of the assertion/belief distinction, and that the half-truth doctrine straddles the philosophers' line between the false and the misleadingly true, but I'd like this checked rather than assumed, since my instinct may just be me reading my framework's assumptions into theirs as if they were translation. Which parts can only be expressed with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all — is there anything in the philosophers' argument that my vocabulary simply cannot say, and does that point to something the legal framework is missing, or to something in the philosophical debate that is less clear, or less substantive, than it looks? For context: my interest is practical. In a negotiated deal we police truth through disclosure obligations and reliance rather than through any concept like "assertion," and I've begun to suspect that the philosophers' categorical distinction does more work in their debate than any corresponding distinction does in mine — but I want to know whether that suspicion is a finding or just a blind spot.
