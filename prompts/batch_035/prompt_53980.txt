write me a satirical piece, roughly 700-900 words, that takes the form of an audited annual report / financial statement from "The British Empire (India Operations) Ltd." for its final fiscal year, roughly 1757-1947, filed just before the company is wound up. deadpan corporate-accounting voice throughout, like a real auditor wrote it: line items, footnotes, a shareholder letter from the chairman (an incorrigible optimist of the Ferguson persuasion, who thinks the whole venture was a bold success that happened to end), and a dissenting note from one board member of the Tharoor persuasion, who insists the ledger has been misbooked from day one.

the target of the satire is not either man but the whole balance-sheet exercise itself — the idea that you can put a railway and a famine in the same ledger and net them off. so the humor should come from accounting concepts doing real philosophical work, e.g.: how do you depreciate a famine? is "rule of law" carried as an asset or a liability (they used it against the salt marchers too)? "goodwill" as an accounting category vs. goodwill in the ordinary sense — 200 years unamortized; "home charges" as a line item that quietly moves money from the India column to the London column; railways booked "at cost to India, revenue booked in London"; the chairman citing India's share of world GDP at the start vs. the end of the period as if it settles the argument; whether you can amortize deindustrialization over three generations; whether partition counts as a discontinued operation. end with the auditor's note, which should land the real question: not whether the books balance, but whether the books were ever the right genre for this.

for this to work you actually need to know what you're satirizing. before you write, do a quick brainstorm pass (you don't have to show it) listing the standard assets Ferguson-type people claim for the Raj (railways, english language, rule of law, free trade networks, unification) and the standard debits Tharoor-type people file (drain of wealth, home charges, the famines under laissez-faire relief doctrine, destruction of the textile industry, divide-and-rule), and then figure out for each one how a straight-faced accountant would book it. every joke should exaggerate something real in the debate — no invented atrocities, no invented benefits. a specialist in either camp should wince in recognition, not in offense at a strawman. and both sides should get roasted: the chairman's letter should be genuinely seductive (that's the point of it), and the dissenting board member should be right but also visibly enjoying being right a bit too much.

one more constraint: the piece should be funny to someone who knows the debate and readable to someone who doesn't — every joke has to survive without a footnote. no citations, no "as Ferguson argues in..." — the names never appear in the piece itself, only in the argument. think carefully about the closing line, it has to do a lot of work. take your time and get the accounting register right: the comedy dies if it slips into modern slang.
