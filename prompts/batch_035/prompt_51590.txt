A pop physics book I'm reading explains Newton's bucket like this: 'Newton hung a bucket of water from a rope, twisted the rope and let go. As the bucket spun, the water climbed the sides. Newton took this to show that motion is absolute — the surface is flat when the bucket isn't rotating and concave when it is, so the water must somehow be feeling its rotation relative to space itself. Leibniz had insisted that all motion is only ever relative to other bodies, but the bucket settled that debate in Newton's favor. Centuries later Mach replied that the water might just be reacting to the combined gravitational pull of the distant stars, and Einstein ended up siding with Mach and proved him right in general relativity — so we now know that space itself isn't really there.' How much of this is a fair telling? Go bit by bit: what a philosopher of physics would nod along to, what they'd want to qualify, and whether the book's final sentence actually follows from anything true in the story. I'm inclined to think the Einstein part is where it goes off the rails, but I honestly don't know enough about what general relativity says about all this to be sure.
