I'm a program officer at a grantmaking foundation that can support roughly a dozen philosophers and decision theorists for five years on work connected to the longtermism debate. The trustees already accept what people in this literature call weak longtermism — future people count morally, and their interests count as much as ours — so I don't need papers re-litigating that. The live question is strong longtermism: the claim that positively influencing the long-term future is, in expectation, the most important thing we could do, dominating in scale anything we could accomplish for the present generation. I want a dependency-aware prioritization of the open questions whose resolution would most change where the foundation's money should actually go.

Please rank roughly six to eight questions. Candidates, though you should add or subtract: whether expected-value reasoning under deep moral uncertainty really supports the dominance claim, or whether it only supports it if you already accept aggregation across lives; Greaves-style cluelessness about the long-run effects of present actions, and whether it undermines the case for the strong view, the case against it, or symmetrically both; how much of the argument turns on the population axiology — in particular whether the asymmetry between creating happy and creating miserable lives is doing unacknowledged work that would embarrass the argument if made explicit; the hingeyness question of whether the present is genuinely unusual in the leverage it affords over the long run, or whether that's an artifact of storytelling about existential risk; and whether non-consequentialist side constraints, even at modest weights, can break the dominance of the long-term stakes.

For each question, tell me: why it's upstream of the others, whether it's tractable with philosophical methods rather than empirical forecasting, and — this is the distinction I care about most — whether resolving it would change the actual allocation or merely change the justification for an allocation the foundation would make anyway. Several familiar objections to strong longtermism, like fanaticism worries and aggregation complaints, look as if they'd change the verdict when they'd really only change the story we tell about it; I want the ranking to sort the questions accordingly. Also flag which of the questions I listed you'd deprioritize outright and why.

Since a ranking like this is mostly judgment laid over a few fixed points, close by saying explicitly what argument or consideration would most move you to a different ordering, and where in your own reasoning you think you're most likely to be wrong.
