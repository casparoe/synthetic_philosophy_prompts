I'm taking an upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind, and we've spent the last four weeks on epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment. The class has effectively split into two camps that keep talking past each other. One camp holds that qualia are non-physical but causally inert byproducts of brain processes; the other is made up of physicalists of various stripes (identity theorists, functionalists, and a couple of russellian-monist fellow travelers) who think that if qualia were causally inert, we couldn't so much as have beliefs or make reports about them, and that this tells against epiphenomenalism. The epiphenomenalist camp replies that the correlation between experiences and physical states does all the explanatory work, so their reports about experience are no more mysterious than a steam whistle "tracking" the engine's speed. It's gotten repetitive, and our instructor has suggested that the two camps try drafting something together rather than another round of rebuttal essays. She's asked me to prepare a model of what such a document might look like.

Please write the joint statement these two camps could plausibly sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want the claims both sides accept — including about the causal closure of physics, about the existence of a systematic correlation between experience and physical process, about what the paradox of phenomenal judgment actually consists in, and about why neither side accepts eliminativism about experience. Then I want the precise formulations of their disagreement, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording — e.g., over whether the reliability of phenomenal reports requires causal efficacy of the phenomenal, over what "tracking" or "being about" an experience amounts to, and over whether a merely lawful correlation suffices for a report to count as genuine reference rather than lucky coincidence. Include a disagreements table with columns for the epiphenomenalist formulation, the physicalist formulation, and the shared neutral statement of the issue, where one is possible. Then set out the cruxes — I suspect one is whether the meaning or content of a phenomenal judgment must be fixed by the causes of the judgment, and another is whether a physical duplicate of a person could be systematically wrong about her own experience — and for each crux, give a case, thought experiment, or argument both camps would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. For instance, both might accept the diary-of-the-perfect-double case (a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a subject who writes at length about her experiences) as probative, and you should record what each side says the duplicate's diary would show and why. One more request: if you conclude that some portion of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive — e.g., that the two camps use "causal relevance" or "aboutness" in different senses — say which portion and why, and identify what reformulation would dissolve it. Please write everything in American English, in a formal register suitable for circulation to the seminar.
