A while back I read about how robots are supposed to learn what we want by watching what we do, rather than us having to spell it out — infer the reward, or the preferences, from the behavior. And the more I read, the more it seemed like the whole thing turns on an assumption economists got tired of fighting about decades ago: that behavior is a clean readout of what someone wants. But behavior is also lazy, habitual, performed under exhaustion, done for other people's eyes. So the target for this piece: the quiet conviction that preference is something you can just observe off a person, like temperature off a thermometer.

Anyway, here's what I want. Write a parody advice column, of the "Dear Abby" genre, called something like "Dear Baxter" — the twist being that both the letter-writers and the columnist are household preference-learning robots, and every letter is a robot fretting about the human whose preferences it's supposed to be inferring. Maybe 650-800 words total, three or four letters plus replies, deadpan and slightly weary in voice, like the columnist has seen it all. The humor has to come from the actual content of preference inference, not from generic robot jokes: so I want letters that turn on real stuff — underdetermination (many different preference orderings fit the same behavior, so how do you pick?), stated vs. revealed preference (the human keeps saying they want to sleep early while providing nightly demonstrations to the contrary at 1am — which one am I supposed to encode?), the way every action the human takes is consistent with "stay alive" being a preference, so a naive inference makes the robot a smothering guardian, and maybe one letter about a robot that's become too uncertain to do anything at all, paralyzed because it can't justify any action without knowing the true reward. If you can work in something like the off-switch idea — a robot letting the human shut it down because its uncertainty about their preferences is what makes deference rational — that'd be great, but only if it lands as a joke, not as a lecture.

Two requests on top of the piece itself. First, add a short afterword (a few sentences is fine) flagging where the satire leans on contested ground — like, the underdetermination stuff is arguably a solved-by-assumptions problem rather than a fatal one, and I'd want to know which of my jokes trade on that ambiguity. Second, if you think the brief as I've posed it is confused — say, if the joke only works by strawmanning inference from behavior, or if the advice-column format can't actually carry these ideas without collapsing into "robots, lol" — tell me that plainly instead of writing the piece anyway. I'd rather hear the premise is broken than get a polite column that isn't about anything.
