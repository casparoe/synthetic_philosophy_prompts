I've got a half-drafted essay connecting two literatures that basically never talk to each other, and I'd really appreciate comments on whether the central argument holds up or whether I'm building on sand. First the thesis, then my rough notes.

Thesis: indirect normativity in AI alignment — the move where instead of hard-coding values into a machine, you have the machine compute what humans would want under idealized conditions (better informed, more reflective, deliberating at length, etc.) — inherits, almost point for point, the objections that metaethicists raised against ideal advisor / full information accounts of well-being back in the 90s. If that's right, the extrapolation step is a transformation of our preferences rather than a revelation of them, and indirect normativity quietly becomes just another way of choosing values, with the choosing done by whoever designs the extrapolation procedure.

Notes so far:

1. Railton had this picture where something is good for you if you'd endorse it if you were fully informed and reasoning flawlessly. Sobel's objection (Ethics, 1994, no direct quotes here, I'm working from memory): full information isn't a clarifying operation on the preferences you actually have, it's a life-changing intervention. The fully informed agent isn't a smarter version of you, it's arguably a different person. Full-information-you might want things current-you finds repugnant, and it's not obvious why the real you should defer to that stranger. Rosati pushed a related worry — the explanatory gap about why the ideal self's endorsement makes things better for the actual self.

2. My claim: CEV-style schemes ("if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were" — paraphrasing roughly) run the exact same gauntlet. The extrapolated volition isn't our volition. The reply alignment people give — that extrapolation is like maturation, growing into a more coherent version of who you already are — strikes me as the same move Railton-style theorists make, and it faces the same challenge: maturation is also a transformative process, not a truth-preserving one.

3. The spot where the analogy might break: ideal advisor theories are per-person accounts of welfare, while extrapolation schemes aggregate millions of people. I genuinely can't decide whether aggregation makes the problem worse (now you also need a social choice rule on top, with all the impossibility results that entails) or actually helps (maybe interpersonal comparison under idealization is easier than intrapersonal comparison over time, since at least it's synchronic?). This is the part I most want comments on.

4. Where I want to push next, if the argument survives: a designer who picks the extrapolation procedure — which facts the idealized agents get, how much they grow, how disagreement is resolved — is doing the normative work while pretending to be an engineer. So the honest version of indirect normativity would admit it's a theory of whose idealization gets to count, which is a value question sitting outside the procedure. I suspect this is right but it might prove too much — by the same logic any process that turns messy actual preferences into a stable target does *some* transformative work, and we don't want to conclude that no such process can have any legitimacy.

Questions: (a) Does the analogy break anywhere I'm not seeing, besides the aggregation issue in note 3? (b) Is "interpretive vs. transformative" the right way to frame the dilemma, or is there a tertium quid, e.g. a reflective equilibrium / fixed point reading where actual and idealized standpoints mutually adjust? (c) Anything in the metaethics literature or the alignment literature that already makes this exact point — if someone has made it, I'd rather cite them than pretend to originality, though I can't look things up right now so just tell me from what you know. (d) Be brutal about note 4 — does it prove too much, and if so how would you tighten it?
