Write a villanelle in the voice of an aging court historian who has spent his life writing dynastic annals and is convinced, with Ibn Khaldun, that political history is a wheel: a hard people at the desert's edge, bound by fierce group solidarity — asabiyyah — sweeps in and founds a dynasty; the founder's austerity decays through the generations as his successors imitate rather than earn, then cultivate luxury and refinement, until the solidarity that made conquest possible has dissolved, leaving the dynasty ripe for the next hard people at the periphery. One refrain should carry the rise (something in the spirit of the rough tribe riding in with its cohesion intact) and the other the decay (luxury or inheritance quietly unmaking what courage built), so that the form itself — the same two lines returning compulsively through every tercet — enacts the cycle the historian claims to see, rather than merely asserting it. Try to distribute Ibn Khaldun's generational sequence across the poem: the founder who earned it, the son who remembers him, the grandson who merely enjoys, and the line that finally cannot hold. Then, in the closing quatrain where the refrains meet for the last time, give the historian a quiet, unresolvable doubt: he has also read the ancients, and Polybius' mixed constitution, and the accumulation of arts and sciences across his own lifetime, both whisper that history might be a line and not a wheel. Do not let the poem settle the question — let the poem's final irony be that a man who believes history repeats insists on keeping a chronicle, a record of unrepeated days, and does not seem to notice the contradiction. Someone who knows the Muqaddimah should recognize the machinery of asabiyyah and generational decay rather than a generic "empires fall" sentiment, and someone who doesn't should still get a good poem about ruin and an old man's doubt.
