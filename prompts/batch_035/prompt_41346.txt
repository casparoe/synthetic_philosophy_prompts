A trade book I've been reading — popular psychology of emotion, aimed at a general audience — closes a chapter on the arts with the following summary of what it calls, following the philosophers, the paradox of tragedy:

"Philosophers have puzzled for centuries over a strange fact: we deliberately seek out works that make us sad, and we call the experience rewarding. Hume was the first to formulate the puzzle, in his essay on tragedy, and he solved it: the sorrow a tragedy produces is converted by the power of eloquent writing into an agreeable feeling, so that the more the work moves us, the more we enjoy it. The sorrow, in other words, is never really sorrow; what we feel at a good tragedy is pleasure all the way down, merely wearing the costume of grief. Aristotle had anticipated this with his doctrine of catharsis, which he set out in detail in the Poetics: by watching the misfortunes of people better than ourselves, we purge pity and fear from our systems, much as a purgative cleanses the body, and we leave the theatre calmer and healthier than we entered it. The deepest point, though, is that none of these emotions is real in the first place. Since we know perfectly well that Antigone and Anna Karenina never existed, the feelings they evoke cannot be genuine; they are diluted copies of emotions, felt at a comfortable distance. Putting the pieces together explains everything: we enjoy sad art because the sadness is never real, and because we are cleansed of it afterwards. It follows that the sadder the work, the better we feel in the end — which is why, as common observation confirms, a good weep at a tragic film leaves us feeling better than when we came in."

I have the vague sense that the chapter runs several distinct puzzles together and smooths over some disagreements, but I can't put my finger on where. Please go through the summary claim by claim and sort it into what a philosopher of art would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong. Specifically, I would like to know: (1) whether the book is right that Hume was the first to formulate the puzzle and right about what his answer amounted to — in particular whether the claim that the sorrow is never really sorrow is Hume's position or someone else's being read into him; (2) whether the account of catharsis is fair to Aristotle, whether it matters that the book locates the detailed explanation where it does, and whether the medical reading of purgation is as settled as the passage implies; (3) whether the claim that our feelings for fictional characters cannot be real belongs to the paradox of tragedy at all, or whether the chapter has silently folded a different and separate puzzle into this one without acknowledging it; and (4) whether the closing inference — that the sadder the work, the better we feel in the end — follows even from those parts of the summary that are accurate, or whether it mistakes an occasional effect for a law.

Finally, the book's last chapter draws two practical recommendations from all this: that melancholy music and tragic films be prescribed as a kind of self-administered mood therapy, and that worries about desensitization to real suffering be dismissed on the ground that the emotions involved are not real ones. Do those recommendations survive the corrections you would make to the summary, or do they inherit its errors in a way that matters for someone who might act on them?
