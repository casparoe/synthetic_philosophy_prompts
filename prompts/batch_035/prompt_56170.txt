Ok so this one's been bugging me. I was at a dinner party where two friends got into it about Hilbert's hotel and neither would back down, and I honestly lost the thread of who was winning. Maybe you can go through it step by step?

For context: someone mentioned the hotel with infinitely many rooms, all occupied, that can still take a new guest by shifting each guest one room up. Talia said this shows actual infinities are physically incoherent. Marcus said that's confusing math with physics. The exchange went roughly like this:

(1) Talia: the shifting trick makes "how full the hotel is" meaningless. Shift everyone up one room and a room frees up with no guest ever leaving. Send each guest in room n to room 2n and you free infinitely many rooms while the guest count stays exactly the same. A quantity that stays the same while you drain it isn't a quantity at all, so the hotel is describing nothing.

(2) Marcus: "fullness" was never a well-defined measure for infinite sets — cardinality is, and by that measure nothing weird happens: same number of guests before and after the shuffle, which is just what the math predicts. The paradox is your finite intuition throwing an error, not a contradiction.

(3) Talia: fine as pure math, but the hotel is presented as a hotel, with guests and a kitchen and presumably a larder. If every guest eats a meal, the larder goes down by infinity. Marcus: the larder was infinite, so it doesn't go down — remove one meal from an infinite store and exactly as many remain. Talia: that's my point, a store you can draw from forever with zero depletion is a magic well, not a store. Infinite guests need infinite meals and infinite sewer capacity and so on forever, and at some point "physical" has lost its meaning. So actual infinities might be fine as abstract objects, but Cantor doesn't rescue them as physical scenarios.

(4) Marcus: your argument proves too much, because the potential-infinity version has the same feature. Suppose the hotel is only potentially infinite: for any finite number of guests, more rooms can be built. The manager can then always accept a new arrival even when the place is packed, since he can always start construction. So "can always accomodate everyone" survives switching from actual to potential infinity, and if that doesn't bother you, why does the actual version?

(5) Talia: because construction takes time and materials. The potentialist manager houses a new guest by changing the world — pouring new concrete. The actualist manager does it by relabeling things that already exist, and the relabeling makes new rooms "available" out of nothing. That difference is the whole issue.
