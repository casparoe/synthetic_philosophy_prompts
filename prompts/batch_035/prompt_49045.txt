For the past few months I've been in a small reading group on personal identity — we've been working through Parfit's Reasons and Persons along with some of the classic papers it draws on — and before we reach the chapters on what matters in survival, I've been asked to circulate a one-page statement of my considered views so the others can push back on it. Before I send it around, I'd like an audit of whether my package actually hangs together. Here is what I believe, as far as I can tell: (1) I'm a reductionist about personal identity: there is no further fact about whether some future person is me, over and above facts about physical and psychological continuity; identity just consists in psychological continuity holding in the right way — quasi-memories and intentions must be causally dependent on my actual past experiences, not merely coincide with them — and, importantly, not branching. (2) What matters in survival is not identity but psychological connectedness, and it comes in degrees: the more connected a future person is to me, the more of what matters my relation to that person contains. (3) The teletransporter — a machine that scans me, painlessly destroys my body on Earth, and assembles an exact functioning replica on Mars with all my memories, character, and intentions — preserves everything that matters, and stepping into it is in every way that matters as good as ordinary survival; I'd take it without hesitation over a slow, risky spaceship trip. (4) In the fission case, where my brain's hemispheres are transplanted into two different bodies and both resulting people are fully psychologically continuous with me, I believe I would not survive: I would die on the operating table, and my death would be about as bad for me as an ordinary death. (5) My justified special concern for my own future is entirely grounded in connectedness — it is rational for me to care about a future person roughly in proportion to how psychologically connected to me they are, and my reasons of prudence extend to my continuers and to nobody else's. (6) All that said, when I imagine stepping into the scanner in (3), there is a question I can't stop caring about: will the person who walks out on Mars be me? It feels like a substantive question with a real answer rather than an empty one, and getting the wrong answer feels like a genuine loss, not just a misdescription.

What I'd like from you is a consistency check, not a lecture on the views themselves. Which pairs of these commitments genuinely conflict, and which only look like they do? Please rank any real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give and what that concession would cost me elsewhere in the list — for instance, whether revising one commitment quietly forces my hand on another two. I'd also like you to check whether my commitments jointly dictate a verdict in a specific choice I've been arguing about with a friend: suppose I must choose between certain fission — both post-operation people waking up healthy and fully continuous with me — and a coin flip giving me a fifty percent chance of ordinary, unbranching survival and a fifty percent chance of nothing. Which way do my views actually cut, and is that a verdict I can live with? Finally, a request: please don't simply declare the whole package incoherent and hand me a replacement. I've thought about each of these individually and find them each defensible on their own; what I want to know is precisely where the stress is, so I can decide for myself what to revise.
