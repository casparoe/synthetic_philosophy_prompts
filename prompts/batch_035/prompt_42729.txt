For about three years now I've been on the updateless side of the acausal-decision-theory dispute: I think the right policy in the counterfactual mugging is to hand over the £100 when the coin shows tails, and the right policy in XOR blackmail is to bin the letter, and I take it as a real point in favour of the functional/updateless family that it returns both verdicts at once, where causal decision theory keeps the money in the mugging and evidential decision theory pays the blackmailer. What would count against this view? I don't want only the familiar objections — that updatelessness means ignoring evidence, or that in the actual world the coin has already come down tails and the £100 is simply gone — I want the strongest cases, ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which constructed scenarios, formal results, or general arguments should move me; what the theory's own defenders would concede as genuine damage rather than dismiss by redefining what counts as the same algorithm or the right logical counterfactual; and whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether that would be a problem for a view of this kind, given that decision theories are tested mainly against intuitions about cases. Two things I would particularly like addressed. First, a suspicion of mine: both showcase cases were constructed by the theory's advocates, and each looks reverse-engineered from a verdict they already wanted — how heavily should this gerrymandering worry weigh against a theory whose data are intuitions about cases? Second, scale: as cheap, accurate simulations of individual people become common, does the updateless line ever become practical advice — for instance, ought I to behave today as a good ancestor to the copies of me that future systems may run, treating my present choices as evidence those copies will act on — or does acausal reasoning cash out only in parlour demonstrations, and if so is that a defeater or merely a limitation in scope? Please write your answer in British English.
