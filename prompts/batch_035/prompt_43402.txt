I'm a PhD student in intellectual history, and for the past semester a friend in my cohort has been running something of a private tribunal on everything historical she encounters, which I've come to suspect is her way of digesting her first full read of Nietzsche's On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life. Her verdicts so far: she approves of the biographies of scientific heroes that her old high school assigns, on the grounds that teenagers read them as evidence that individuals can bend history. She approves of her hometown's historical society, which maintains the local archive, restores the churchyard, and keeps the town's festivals going. She approves of the truth commission a post-authoritarian country ran to document and condemn the crimes of the old regime and strip its names from the barracks walls. On the other side, she disapproves of a fellow student's dissertation plan — cataloging every identifiable scribe's hand in a corpus of manuscripts that essentially nobody will ever use, knowledge accumulated for its own sake. She disapproves of a nationalist government rewriting its textbooks to inflate a warrior past. And she disapproves of a woman we both know who, ten years after her divorce, keeps re-reading her old correspondence to determine exactly how much of the failure was hers, each pass more precise and each pass less compatible with doing anything else.

Next week our reading group is hosting a visitor, and my friend will meet her: a philosopher of history who is transparently flourishing — energetic, funny, endlessly productive — and who cheerfully admits that she reads enormous quantities of history with no practical aim whatsoever. The point of understanding the Investiture Controversy, she says, is simply understanding it; contemplating the past is one of her life's chief pleasures, and she'd go on doing it even if it never issued in a single decision or lesson. I've been trying to work out what my friend will say about her. Two rules seem to fit everything on the list. On one, history earns approval only when it serves something forward-looking — inspiration, the preservation of a living tradition, liberation from an oppressive past — with the added condition that it not be falsified; contemplation with no issue at all fails that test, and so does myth. On the other, the verdicts track the condition of the person doing the history: history in the hands of the vigorous is fine, and the same history in the hands of the depleted — the scribe-cataloguer, the divorced woman, the nationalists compensating for a shabby present — is corrosive. If I'm honest, the verdicts fit a cruder third rule too, a simple dose rule on which anything is fine in moderation and condemned once it swamps a person's or a people's capacity to act.

Can you help me think through this before the visit? I'd like your prediction for what she'll say about our guest, the candidate rules you'd actually fit to the six verdicts (including any I've missed, and any places where the rules I've proposed fail on a case I've misdescribed), and your best guess with your reasons for preferring it. I'm especially interested in whether the two rules I like are genuinely different or just two readings of Nietzsche's phrase that history should serve life — one instrumental, on which the past must pay its way in future action, and one diagnostic, on which history in the strong is an expression of vitality and history in the weak a symptom of decline. And since my friend prides herself on consistency, tell me what further case would best tell her preferred rule apart from its closest rival: something like the exhausted activist whose grievance-saturated reading of history does fuel effective campaigns while leaving her visibly thinner each year, or a version of our divorced acquaintance whose archival digging into her own past finally dislodges the guilt and lets her act. I care less about the verdict on the guest than about the machinery underneath it.
