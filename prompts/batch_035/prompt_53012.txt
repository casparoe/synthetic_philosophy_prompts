Next week I see a psychiatrist about starting modafinil for daytime fatigue. Nothing is clinically wrong with me — I sleep adequately, I'm not narcoleptic — but my concentration is poor enough that my doctor floated it as an option, and I have to decide before the appointment whether I even want to go down that road. When I mentioned this at dinner, two friends spent an hour arguing about it, and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning.

Marcus went first: taking it would be enhancement, not therapy. Nothing is broken; being foggy at 3pm is the human condition, and if every shortfall from optimal functioning counts as a treatable condition, we've pathologized ordinary life and handed medicine the job of fine-tuning it. The therapy/enhancement line may be blurry, he said, but it's the only thing standing between medicine that responds to illness and medicine that services preferences.

Priya said the line he was defending couldn't survive its own examples. If the standard is species-typical functioning, then glasses are enhancement for anyone who could squint along without them, and no one thinks that. If the standard is statistical normality, then treating hypertension in the over-seventies is enhancement, since most of them have it. And if the standard is just "whatever medicine treats," the definition is circular and the pill would flip categories the moment a prescription pad appears.

Marcus retreated to a different line: it's not about normality but about disease — about whether the intervention targets a condition that impairs you in a distinctively medical way, which we can recognize in hard cases even if we can't define it, the way we recognize funny without defining that.

Priya called the retreat a tell: he'd moved from "restoring function" to "treating disease" as soon as each got hard, which suggests the position is post hoc. And if the real standard is impairment relative to my own goals — which is what he actually appealed to with "distinctively medical" — then my taking the pill is therapy for my life rather than an enhancement to it; whereas if enhancement is simply intervention beyond the medically indicated, then it's an open question whether enhancement is bad at all, and Marcus owes an argument that it is, not just a label.

They agreed to let me referee the written version, and I'd like to do it properly. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say who is right at each step and why, identify what, if anything, both of them are missing about the therapy/enhancement distinction, and tell me how much of this should actually bear on my decision next week.
