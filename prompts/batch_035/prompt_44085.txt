---

**Drafts for a contradiction: what if the axioms broke?**

My dissertation reduces to a theorem in a set-theoretic foundation strong enough to formalize the semantics of my source language. Until recently I thought of this as the firmest possible ground: not merely plausible or empirically robust, but *certain*, in the way only mathematics is supposed to be certain. Then I made the mistake of reading some history.

The history is not comforting. Frege's system in his *Basic Laws of Arithmetic* was, by his lights, as self-evident as anything could be, until Russell showed around 1901 that its comprehension principle yields a contradiction outright. Analysts reasoned with infinitesimals for two centuries before anyone could say what they were. Each generation's obviously-consistent framework turned out to be broken in some way, and the repairs always came after the damage. The historical induction is simple: at least one central framework of past mathematics was not merely incomplete but flatly inconsistent; every framework was eventually patched; so why should we believe our current axioms are the first in the sequence to be exempt?

And the usual reassurance is unavailable. By Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, a system like ZFC cannot prove its own consistency, so no certificate is forthcoming from within. Gentzen's 1936 consistency proof for Peano Arithmetic comes at the price of transfinite induction up to the ordinal ε₀, which just relocates the question one level up. Even unanimity among mathematicians is weak evidence: Frege did not doubt his own axioms either. And it is not as though no serious person worries — a Princeton logician, Edward Nelson, spent much of his career attempting to exhibit an outright inconsistency in Peano Arithmetic, and while he did not succeed, the failure of a particular search is not the same as grounds for confidence.

So what should someone in my position do? Three options suggest themselves. First, dismiss the worry: mathematical practice is self-correcting, and most working mathematics uses a small fragment of the axioms anyway. Second, adopt a paraconsistent logic in which contradictions do not trivialize everything. But paraconsistency asks mathematicians to give up inference rules — like disjunctive syllogism — they will never agree to surrender, so this is a non-starter as a *foundational* program. Third — and this is the proposal I actually want to defend — we could build the self-correction into our bookkeeping. Call it *quarantine by provenance*: every theorem, human or machine-checked, would carry a tag recording which axiom schemata its proof actually invoked. If a contradiction were ever found in, say, the axiom schema of Replacement, the damage would not have to spread. Everything provable without Replacement would survive untouched, and the rest of the corpus could be re-audited rather than abandoned. When Russell's paradox landed, mathematics did not collapse; the ship kept sailing with a patched hull. Quarantine by provenance is just an attempt to formalize the patching in advance, so that the salvage is a mechanical rerouting rather than decades of improvised reconstruction.

I anticipate the objection: if a contradiction exists, explosion means *everything* is provable in principle, so the quarantine cannot be a property of the consequence relation itself; it is at best a sociological or archival fact about which proofs happen to cite which axioms. I think I want to accept this rather than fight it. The proposal is not a new logic but a plan for preserving the informal body of mathematics through a foundational rupture. Perhaps certainty was always the wrong thing for us to claim: perhaps the right verdict on any theorem is not that it is certain but that it is *provisionally coherent* — coherent relative to a fragment that has so far survived.
