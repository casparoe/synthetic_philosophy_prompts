I've gotten myself committed to a position in a running argument with my brother and I want to pressure-test it before the next round. The position: autonomous systems create genuine responsibility gaps. When a self-driving car kills a pedestrian, or a medical algorithm flags the wrong patient for discharge, there are cases where nobody is morally responsible for the outcome — not the manufacturer (they took reasonable care, couldn't foresee this specific event), not the engineers (each individually did nothing negligent, the harm emerged from the interaction of many decisions), not the owner or passenger (no control at the decisive moment), and not the system itself (not an agent, nothing to blame). The responsibility just falls through the cracks, and this is structural, not a temporary legal lag: the standard conditions for responsibility — control, foreseeability, something like intention or at least awareness — can't all be satisfied by any single party, and I don't think stretching them to fit will work.

What would actually count against this view? I want a ranked map, from fatal to merely awkward. Some things I can already anticipate and want your honest take on:

1. The gap only exists if you insist on an individualist, courtroom-style conception of responsibility. If you accept collective responsibility, or distributed responsibility spread thin across the whole chain of design and deployment, or role-based responsibility, the gap closes. Is that a refutation or just a change of subject — and would a defender of gaps be right to say these diluted forms of responsibility aren't the thing we cared about?
2. Traceability. Someone told me the whole problem dissolves once you look at how the design decisions trace through to the outcome — that "meaningful human control" framings with tracking and tracing conditions show there's always a human decision in the causal history worth attaching responsibility to. Does that actually close the gap, or does it just locate a different, weaker thing?
3. Empirical evidence: maybe in practice, courts and ordinary people never actually experience a gap — juries manage to blame someone, engineers feel guilt, companies apologise and compensate. Would that show the gap is a philosopher's artefact?
4. The view might be insulated: every time someone fills a gap, the defender can retreat to "ah, but that's causal responsibility / liability / forward-looking responsibility, not moral responsibility proper". At what point has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and is that fatal for a metaphysical-ish claim of this kind or just how conceptual claims work?

Also tell me which of these a committed gap-defender would themselves concede as counting against them, versus which they'd wave away — I want to know where the honest concession lines are, not just what critics shout. Answer in British English please, and keep it conversational — my brother and I are both engineers, not philosophers, so no unexplained jargon. I'd also take suggestions for a decisive thought experiment either way: a case where even I would have to admit there's no gap, or one where even a skeptic would have to admit there is. That's the thing I personally would find most persuasive.
