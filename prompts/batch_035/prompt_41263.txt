I've been going back and forth about Amartya Sen with a friend from my rationality meetup for a couple of months now, and we've hit the point where we're each privately around 80% confident the other is being dense, which by now I take as a signal to decompose rather than repeat ourselves louder.

The proposition on the table: Sen's well-known generalization that a functioning democracy with a genuinely free press and competitive elections has never experienced a major famine is true, and the reason it is true is roughly the mechanism Sen himself gestured at — a free press makes famine facts public early, and electoral incentives make governments pay for letting it get worse — rather than some confounder like income level or administrative capacity that merely happens to correlate with democracy.

My credence that the bare historical generalization holds is about 0.9. My credence in the causal half is more like 0.6, so my overall credence in the conjunction sits around 0.55. My friend puts the conjunction near 0.25. Her suspicion is that the pattern is mostly selection and confounding: democracies in the relevant era were richer and better administered, the catastrophic famines occurred under colonial or totalitarian regimes that had reasons to hide information regardless of their formal constitution, and she thinks the press-and-ballot mechanism is a just-so story that would not survive a serious look at near-misses and partial democracies.

Since this is exactly the sort of disagreement that should be factorable rather than vibes-based, I want your number with the decomposition shown. Please give rough probabilities for at least the following, plus any additional terms you think belong in the product:

1. That the entitlement approach, as opposed to a simple food-availability-decline story, correctly diagnoses at least two of the three canonical cases — Bengal 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward famine of 1959–61.
2. That, conditional on the historical record containing no major famine in a functioning democracy, the press-and-ballot mechanism (rather than confounders like wealth, state capacity, or selection effects in which countries democratized in the first place) does most of the causal work.
3. That the counterfactual is robust — that is, that a China in 1959 with competitive elections and an uncensored press very likely avoids the tens of millions of excess deaths, rather than the famine being driven by procurement targets and communes in a way a merely political change would not have touched.
4. Whatever residual term you would add for the generalization surviving attempted counterexamples — my friend and I went back and forth on whether any plausible candidate exists, and I deliberately won't name the ones we discussed, since I don't want to anchor you.

Then combine the terms in whatever way you think is honest, tell me whether your overall number lands nearer mine or hers, and — most usefully — identify the single term where the gap between 0.55 and 0.25 most likely lives, and what evidence obtainable without a time machine would move that term the most.
