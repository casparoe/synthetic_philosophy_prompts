I run an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the ethics of influence — about 16 students, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel the difference between manipulation and legitimate persuasion, rather than just hear definitions of it. The design I have in mind: students take turns persuading a partner to agree to something modestly costly (e.g., signing a petition, committing an hour to a campus event, donating a token amount), and each persuader draws a sealed card assigning them a tactic — with the cards drawn from three families you should define: (a) paradigm rational persuasion (giving reasons, answering objections), (b) changing the stakes rather than the beliefs (threats, offers, pressure), and (c) tactics whose effectiveness seems to depend on remaining partially unnoticed — engineered guilt, manufactured urgency, exploiting sunk costs, strategic selective disclosure, flattery deployed to lower defenses. Write the actual cards (12–16 of them), the instructions given to targets, the rating form targets fill out afterwards, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the distinctions. The debrief should stress-test one specific test I want the students to chew on: the idea that manipulation is distinguished by whether the tactic's efficacy survives the target's clearly seeing what is being done to them — rational persuasion passes this counterfactual-transparency test, coercion arguably does too (the threat works even if you see it coming), while manipulation seems to fail it. Include in the debrief at least one card that puts pressure on that test — for instance, an openly avowed guilt trip, where the target is told in advance that guilt will be applied — and have students argue about whether it counts. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I expect students to classify anything emotion-directed as manipulation), and be frank about which feature of real manipulation the activity cannot reproduce — I suspect the long timescale and the power asymmetries, but you should say. The activity must be doable with paper cards and no technology. Finally, since this activity will lean on contested definitions, include a short note at the end of your design saying how confident you are in each of your main design choices and singling out the point you are least sure of — I'd especially like to know where you think the counterfactual-transparency test is weakest, since I'm deciding whether to make it the centerpiece of the session.
