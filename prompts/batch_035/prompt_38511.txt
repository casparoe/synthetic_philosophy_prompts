Construct a pair of thought experiments that pry the lottery paradox and the preface paradox apart along a dimension other than the usual one (bare statistics versus individual evidence). Specifically: one case in which the believer's aggregate belief — that at least one of her many individually-rational beliefs is false — is irrational even though it is structurally a preface case, and one case in which the corresponding aggregate belief is rational even though it is structurally a lottery case. Constraints: a realistic setting (an actuarial office, a pharmacy, a publishing house — no demons or perfect logicians); each scenario no longer than a paragraph; and the numerical probabilities and the number of beliefs held fixed across the two cases, so that neither probability nor aggregation alone can explain the differing verdicts. Candidates for the dimension doing the work include the self-reference of the preface belief (its being about one's own body of beliefs), the dependence or independence of the individual pieces of evidence, and the believer's having second-order grounds such as a track record of her own past errors — but you should let your cases reveal which, rather than stipulating it. After the scenarios, walk through what a Kyburg-style coherence constraint and a Makinson-style defense of rational preface belief each say about your two cases, say which structural feature is doing the work and what happens to the verdicts when you remove it, and note how a defender of the side that comes out badly would attack the construction. If you think the specification cannot be met without cheating — because, for example, once probabilities and evidence quality are equalized the two paradoxes collapse into one, or because the rationality of the aggregate belief cannot be assessed independently of whether it is true — say so plainly and explain where the specification breaks, rather than forcing the cases to cooperate.
