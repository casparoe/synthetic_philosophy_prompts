I write a column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am at work on an essay about the legacy of the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals. At a recent conference on international criminal law, a senior scholar laid out for me, over coffee, what she called the standard resolution of the retroactivity problem. The more I transcribe it, the more it seems to prove either too much or nothing at all. Here it is as she gave it to me.

(1) A criminal prohibition is not retroactive in any objectionable sense when it punishes conduct that was already gravely wrong at the time it occurred, and that the actors knew was wrong; retroactivity is a defect only insofar as it produces unfair surprise.
(2) The central acts for which the Nazi leadership was tried — planning and launching wars of aggression, and the mass atrocities — were gravely wrong in the 1930s and early 1940s, and the men responsible knew it: hence the staged border incident used to justify the invasion of Poland, the secrecy of the war planning, the euphemisms in the internal documents.
(3) Men who take elaborate pains to disguise an act because they expect the world to treat it as criminal cannot later complain of unfair surprise when a tribunal names that wrong and punishes it.
(4) So the Nuremberg charter did not create crimes after the fact; it declared wrongs that were already punish-worthy, and the trials therefore did not violate the principle of legality — no crime without law, no punishment without law.
(5) The same reasoning applies at Tokyo, and indeed in general: whenever the defeated leadership knew its conduct was gravely wrong, a postwar court may punish it, and retroactivity is no objection.
(6) But then the principle of legality imposes no independent constraint on the courts a victor convenes: whatever the winning powers regard as a grave wrong known to be wrong becomes, for that reason alone, punishable, and the bar on retroactive law never blocks anything the winners are prepared to call a grave wrong.
(7) A principle that never blocks anything is not a constraint but a standing compliment to the victors' moral judgment — which is precisely what the phrase victors' justice was coined to name. So either (1) is false and the trials really did punish under law that did not exist when the acts occurred, or the principle of legality is an empty formality.

The scholar presented this as a triumphant defense of Nuremberg: on her reading, (1) through (4) establish that the trials were legitimate. But read through to (7), it looks like a dilemma in which neither horn is comfortable. If I give up (1), I concede that the defendants were punished under rules invented after the fact to fit their conduct, and the phrase victors' justice lands. If I accept (6), I concede that legality has no content beyond the victors' conscience, which is the same landing by another route.

My suspicion is that something slips between (3) and (5): that the claim these particular men, given what they knew and what they concealed, cannot cry unfair surprise is quietly exchanged for the claim that no one can, that wherever the winners detect grave and known wrongdoing, retroactivity ceases to be an objection in law. But I cannot state the difference in a way that survives her likely reply, which is that criminal liability presupposes only that the actor could have known the wrong, and here they could and did.

For the essay I would like, if possible, to keep two things: the thought that the Nuremberg defendants were not convicted under law manufactured after the event simply to convict them, and the thought that the principle of legality is a real constraint rather than an ornament. So: which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is in fact valid, tell me plainly which of the two things I want to keep must be surrendered, and say what the familiar defenses of the trials — that the charter merely declared preexisting customary law, or that the wrongs were so manifest that punishment required no written statute — have to pay for that choice. Please also address Tokyo specifically, since there the charge has always seemed to me harder to parry: the judges all sat for the victorious powers, the offenses were defined in the very charter that created the court, and the emperor was spared for reasons of state.
