My philosophy of mind reading group has been stuck on the emotions question for months. One camp is basically judgmentalist -- they think emotions are (or essentially involve) evaluative appraisals, a fear being something like the judgment or construal of danger. The other camp is basically James-Lange / bodily-feeling theorists -- they think an emotion just is the perception of a pattern of bodily change, and the "aboutness" of fear comes along for the ride rather than being the core of it. Write the joint statement they should be able to sign after a proper adversarial collaboration.

Things I want in the document:

- A numbered section of claims both camps can sign, phrased so neither side feels like they're conceding anything they actually care about. (They fight a lot about wording, so this part matters.)
- A section laying out their exact remaining disagreements, with each side's position stated in wording that side itself would accept -- not in the other side's strawman of it. A table would be nice here.
- The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one camp. I suspect one crux is what to say about so-called "recalibrational" emotions like guilt, shame, and embarrassment, where it's much less obvious there's a characteristic bodily profile the way there arguably is for fear or disgust. But find other cruxes too, don't just run with mine.
- For each crux, at least one concrete case, thought experiment, or line of argument both camps would agree bears on it -- and each camp's prediction about how that case would go, written down in advance so they can't backpedal later.
- If you think part of their dispute is merely verbal (e.g. two senses of "emotion" being run together), say which part and why. But only if you actually think so; don't use "it's just semantics" as a cop-out.

One more thing: when you're drafting the agreed-claims section, please keep straight what's actually settled versus genuinely contested among people who work on this. E.g., don't write the bodily-feeling side's position as if it were consensus about what emotions are, or present some contested reading of William James as settled history. If a formulation you give leans on a reading that's itself disputed, flag it in a short note at the end rather than smuggling it in.

Also, small thing: the group's judgmentalist keeps insisting emotions must be "token-reflexive or self-involving" and the feeling side has no idea what she means, so if you can work that in as one of the disagreements, bonus points. Not required tho.

Write it casual enough that it reads like something a real reading group would circulate, not like a journal article. And dont make the joint statement longer than it needs to be -- the whole point is that it's something both sides would actually put their names to.
