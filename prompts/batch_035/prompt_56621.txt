Next month I'm giving a talk at a large interdisciplinary conference on the science of consciousness — the standard mix for such events: mostly cognitive neuroscientists and experimental psychologists who work on reportability, metacognition, and the neural correlates of consciousness, plus a handful of philosophers of mind, including two who take emergentist and panpsychist positions seriously and know the Dennett corpus (Consciousness Explained, 1991; Darwin's Dangerous Idea, 1995) inside out.

My thesis, briefly: the familiar charge that neuroscientific theories of consciousness commit greedy reductionism misidentifies the defect. Greediness has nothing to do with how many levels an explanation skips; a perfectly respectable crane can run in a single stride from spike trains to person-level phenomena, provided the interpretive step — the move from third-person data to subject-level vocabulary — is made explicitly and motivated independently. The genuine defect, for which I coin the term interpretive overdraft, arises when an explanation uses person-level terms (report, access, what-it-is-like) inside its own machinery without ever cashing them out, so that a construction which looks like it was assembled from the ground up turns out, on inspection, to have a load-bearing beam held up by an unacknowledged skyhook. I apply this bookkeeping in two directions. Against the majority in the room: the popular search for the neural correlates of consciousness is greedier than it looks, because the criterion used to select the correlates is stated in the very vocabulary the explanation is supposed to ground. Against the minority: the emergentists' favorite objection — that consciousness science leaves out the intrinsic level, so something must be added from above — is not a demand to balance the books but to write the same overdraft into one's metaphysics; adding a fundamental property is no crane, and it does not settle the interpretive debt.

The talk has three moves: (1) a reconstruction of the crane/skyhook and greedy-reductionist distinctions as claims about explanatory bookkeeping rather than about how many levels an explanation traverses; (2) worked diagnoses of overdraft in one or two familiar research programs; (3) the argument that the standard objections from the consciousness-requires-more camp presuppose exactly the bookkeeping error I diagnose. I am not a Dennettian on every point — I think heterophenomenology is too austere, and I will say so if asked — but I hold that the crane/skyhook distinction, properly regimented, is the right tool for this job.
