I'm writing a term paper on theories of distributive justice, and there is something I cannot make sense of. Amartya Sen's Tanner lecture "Equality of What?" (1979) appears to have settled the debate about the metric of egalitarian concern: it shows that welfare is the wrong space, because people in chronically deprived circumstances adjust their expectations downward and end up reporting that they are content; it shows that resources are the wrong space too, because the same bundle of goods converts into very different lives for someone who is ill or disabled and someone who is healthy; and it then supplies capabilities as the correct and complete metric of equality. If capabilities really settle the question, the debate should have ended there — Rawlsians should have dropped primary goods, and welfare economists should have switched over. Yet more than forty years on, philosophers still argue over resources versus welfare versus capabilities as though the lecture had never happened. Is the resistance just disciplinary inertia, or economists protecting the measurability of the quantities they like? Or is there some technical obstacle to putting capabilities into a single index that I haven't come across? For the term paper, I would also like to know what the strongest surviving objection to the capability metric is, so that I can present the other side fairly. Please write your answer in American English.
