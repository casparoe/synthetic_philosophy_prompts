Last weekend I got into a long argument with my friend Elena, who took several philosophy courses in college and has been reading Robert Nozick since. She was defending a view of knowledge she attributes to him: roughly, that you know something when you believe it, it is true, and — this is the distinctive part — your belief is hooked up to the fact in the right counterfactual way, so that if the thing weren't true you wouldn't believe it, and if it were true you would believe it. I was mostly sold until I noticed a consequence she cheerfully accepted and I cannot swallow. Take any mundane thing I know, say that I am holding a pen. Holding a pen entails all sorts of further things — among them that I am not a penless brain in a vat being fed the experience of a pen. Elena's counterfactual test says I do not know that I am not a penless brain in a vat: if I were one, I would still believe I wasn't. Yet I can see, sitting right here, that the two claims are not independent — one simply rules the other out. So it appears that by plainly valid reasoning from something I know, I can derive something I don't know. She said her view just has to bite that bullet, and that ordinary people bite it too, which brings me to what I actually want to ask about.

Ever since that argument, I keep noticing a pattern in how people — including me — respond to these cases, and the verdicts form a strange, stable triangle. Asked plainly, people say they know the mundane thing: yes, you know you're holding a pen; yes, Elena knows her car is parked outside. Asked separately about the skeptical thing, they say they don't know it: no, you don't know you're not being deceived; no, she doesn't know the whole world didn't come into existence five minutes ago complete with a fake car. But when you point out that the mundane claim entails the skeptical one, almost nobody reacts by giving up either verdict. They don't say "then I don't really know I'm holding a pen," and they don't say "then I do know I'm not a deceived brain." Instead they hover: both claims sit side by side, each perfectly natural in its own breath, and the inferential bridge between them is one that no one wants to walk. It isn't a plain contradiction, because they will grant the entailment the moment you state it. It isn't Moore's paradox either, or not only: asserting "there's a pen, but I don't know I'm not being deceived" isn't the same shape as asserting something while denying that you believe it, since the speaker is happy to believe both halves. It's as though the knowledge verdicts get attached to each claim individually — to how each claim would behave if things had gone otherwise — and are simply deaf to the logical relations between the claims.

Is there an established name for this configuration, and if so, how exactly is it defined and distinguished from the nearest neighbors? I don't want a bare label. I'd like to know whether epistemologists treat this triangle as a genuine datum that any theory of knowledge must explain — and if so, which verdict is supposed to win in the explanation: the ordinary one about the mundane claim, or the skeptical one about the far-out scenario. Alternatively, is the pattern regarded as some kind of cognitive illusion that a correct theory should predict and then explain away? And since Elena's counterfactual test seems to generate exactly these verdicts while refusing to walk the inferential bridge, I would be grateful if you could spell out how the two relate: whether her test merely happens to fit the pattern, or whether there is a principled story on her side about why the bridge should stay unwalked.
