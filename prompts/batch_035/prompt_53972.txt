In my book club's discussion of Machiavelli's The Prince, one member cited the closing line of Chapter XXV — that fortune is a woman who must be beaten and coerced, who yields to the bold rather than the cautious, and who favors the young precisely because they are less cautious and command her with more audacity — to argue that the book's core practical message is simply that virtù amounts to audacity. Another member objected that the line is decorative misogyny capping a chapter whose actual conclusion is a conditional: direct your actions according to the spirit of the times, and even then you will be ruined when the times change, because men are too fixed in their natures to alter their methods. Having reread the chapter, I can't tell how much work the woman metaphor is actually doing. Machiavelli opens by granting that Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, "but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less," then likens her to a flooding river that can be channeled by dikes built in calm weather — a metaphor in which foresight and preparation, not impetuosity, are the answer — and then argues, through the spirit-of-the-times thesis and the example of Pope Julius II, that success goes to whichever fixed disposition happens to match the circumstances. Only in the final paragraph does the woman image appear, seemingly supplying the conclusion rather than being derived from anything. Please lay out, as a list I can bring back to book club, the distinct precise theses that famous line could be advancing — from deflationary readings on which it is a rhetorical flourish restating an independently argued conclusion and could be deleted without loss, through readings on which the gendered trope does real argumentative work (for instance, that fortune genuinely rewards a boldness modeled on how women are stereotyped as responding to forceful men), to readings on which the line actually conflicts with the chapter's own reasoning about matching one's methods to the times — and say for each thesis whether it is plausible, and which one best fits the river metaphor and the claim that fortune governs only half of our actions.
