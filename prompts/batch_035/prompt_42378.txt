In my group's work on mechanistic anomaly detection we have hit a wall that I suspect is not empirical, and I would like a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another round of position papers. The setting: one trains a monitor on a benign input distribution, then flags inputs on which the model appears to compute its output using different machinery than it used on that distribution. The bet, which everyone shares, is that misaligned or deceptive behavior will be mechanistically novel even when it is behaviorally unremarkable. The stalled debate is over what "different machinery" amounts to, since the detector cannot compare a model with itself at deployment time: the model may have drifted from the one monitored during training, or the detector may have to generalize across independently trained models, so sameness of mechanism has to hold across systems, not within one.

Position A individuates mechanisms causally. A mechanism is the subgraph of the computational graph active on a given input — the circuit, in the interpretability sense — and two models share a mechanism when the relevant subgraphs correspond up to relabeling of units, or when they agree under intervention: patch activations here, knock out those weights, and the two models' outputs move the same way. Position B holds that this is hopeless precisely because component-level correspondence is what fails to survive retraining; mechanisms should be individuated functionally, by the role they realize, so two models share a mechanism when they compute the same function over the same range of inputs, however differently they implement it. Position C, the skeptics, say both alternatives presuppose that "the mechanism" is out there waiting to be carved out, when real networks are massively redundant: many distinct subgraphs implement the same computation, the decomposition one finds depends on the probing and ablation tools one brings, and so the notion of a model's mechanisms is interest-relative rather than a natural kind. On C's view the whole framing should be dropped and detectors evaluated purely on detection performance, with no claim that they track mechanisms.

What strikes me is that each side has been restating its criterion for over a year, and every counterexample gets absorbed: A concedes that isomorphism is too strict and retreats to intervention-agreement; B concedes that functional sameness lets behaviorally indistinguishable mechanisms slide through and adds a grain-of-implementation clause; C's complaint gets sharper but generates no alternative. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not verbal but rests on an unstated empirical bet about convergence: whether two research groups with different toolkits, or two training runs from different seeds, would carve a given model into the same mechanisms at all, and at what scale. A and B may each be reasoning correctly about different regimes — toy circuits where A's criterion is checkable, and large models where only B's is tractable — while C's skepticism is really about the gap between them. But I am not certain.

Given what each side actually says, tell me what kind of disagreement this is: verbal, substantive, or two questions being pursued under a single heading; whether it turns on a hidden empirical question about convergence of decompositions, or on bedrock intuitions about individuation that no one can argue for; and whether the impasse is a sign that the question is ill-posed or merely hard. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress — a distinction, a benchmark, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected, and why.
