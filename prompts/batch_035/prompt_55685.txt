I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and a running argument at our department's workshop on partiality last week stuck with me because neither side would give, and I found I couldn't referee it myself. The two arguers are fellow doctoral students: Antonia, writing a dissertation on the demandingness of consequentialism, and Peter, who works on the architecture of practical reasoning. The topic was how Bernard Williams's famous remark about the man who can save either his wife or a stranger from drowning relates to his integrity objection against utilitarianism — the case where the man, rather than simply leaping in after his wife, first satisfies himself on some impartial principle that it is permissible, perhaps obligatory, to save her, a route Williams characterized as involving one thought too many. Antonia and Peter disagreed about whether that remark is part of the integrity objection or something else entirely.

The exchange went as follows. (1) Antonia claimed the remark is a point in the philosophy of practical rationality, not a contribution to the anti-consequentialist case: it diagnoses a defect in how an agent deliberates — needing to route through impartial principle to reach his wife — and such a defect could afflict a devout Kantian just as easily as a utilitarian, whereas the integrity objection, which concerns utilitarianism alienating people from their ground projects over a whole life, is a separate and much broader complaint. (2) Peter replied that the two are the same objection at different timescales: what generates the extra thought is precisely consequentialism's demand that impartial justification be available on demand, and what drives the alienation complaint is the same demand made continuously; so the drowning case is the integrity objection compressed into a moment, and Antonia's separation leaves the remark with no anti-consequentialist content whatsoever — just etiquette for husbands. (3) Antonia pressed with a counterexample: an official whose job is to allocate rescue resources, who is married to one of the victims, and who reasons that his office requires him to apply the standard triage procedure, which happens to classify his wife's rescue as the priority. On Peter's reading, that thought structure appears to be condemned as one thought too many, yet it seems impeccable, so the thought's defect must be context-sensitive in a way easier to explain on her deliberative-route view than on his theory-driven one. (4) Peter bit the bullet: the official case differs because the deliberation is role-mediated — the agent reasons as an official, not as a private individual — and Williams's constraint holds only for private deliberation, where one's attachments must be capable of moving one directly rather than via impartial principle. Antonia answered that this makes the remark hostage to contingencies about roles: change the job description and the same deliberation flips from defective to impeccable, which suggests the point is about where one happens to stand rather than about what utilitarianism demands of anyone — and the integrity objection, aimed at a theory offered as a decision procedure for everyone, cannot be read off anything so local.

Please adjudicate this for me, move by move, rather than just announcing an overall winner. Structure the response as follows. A section titled Verdict in brief, two or three sentences. A section titled The four moves, giving one short paragraph per move in order, each rated somewhere on a spectrum from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with the reason. A section titled What both are missing, one paragraph — you might consider whether their disagreement is fed by a slide between the extra thought's merely occurring and its doing justificatory work, but do not feel bound to that. A closing section titled Confidence, briefly saying how confident you are in each of your four ratings and identifying the single point about which you are least sure, with what would make you revise it. I'd rather you flag genuine uncertainty than manufacture symmetry, and please don't assume either of them is simply right.
