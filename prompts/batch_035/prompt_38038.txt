I'm a philosopher of science who has been invited to give a talk at a mechanistic-interpretability research lab -- an audience of maybe fifteen ML researchers who are allergic to philosophy, plus a couple of philosophers of mind the lab keeps around for color. My thesis is a position I've begun calling deflationary realism about neural-network representations: when the lab isolates a feature or a steering direction -- the one that fires on insecure code, say, or the vector that makes the model flattering -- they have found a representation in the only sense that matters, because representation just is the kind of counterfactual-supporting, intervention-relevant role such features play in the model's dispositions. But the deflation cuts both ways: it dissolves the question whether the model "really" represents rather than merely behaving as if it does, and it also makes representation cheap, so nothing grand about understanding or machine consciousness follows from the realism. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the seven or eight objections I will actually face from this particular audience -- not the ones I'd get at a philosophy conference -- ordered by how much damage each would do, phrased the way an interpretability researcher versus a philosopher of mind would respectively put it, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Give special attention to superposition and polysemanticity, since if features are neither clean nor basis-independent, it is not obvious my view has the stable relata it needs. And include the question I am hoping nobody asks: how, on a purely functional criterion, the model's representations can ever be *inaccurate* -- which is presumably the whole point of calling them representations when we rely on them to detect deception.
