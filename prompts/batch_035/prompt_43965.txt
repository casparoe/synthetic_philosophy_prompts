I teach a graduate seminar on rationality and irrationality, and every year the unit on self-deception ends in stalemate, so I want a careful essay that actually forces a verdict. Set up the problem properly: the static paradox (how can one person simultaneously believe p and believe not-p) and the dynamic paradox (how can one intend to deceive oneself when success in the intention would seem to render it impossible to carry out — the deceiver must know what the deceived does not, yet they are the same person). Then lay out the main escape routes: the partitioning strategy, associated with Davidson, on which the mind divides into semi-autonomous territories that can withhold information from one another; the deflationary or "motivated belief" strategy, associated with Mele, on which self-deception requires no intention and no contradictory beliefs at all, only motivationally biased treatment of evidence; and the quieter view that what we call self-deception is really a matter of avowal and attention rather than belief — the person believes the uncomfortable thing all along and merely manages what they say and look at.

Here is the twist I want you to take seriously, and I confess it is my own hobbyhorse. Behavioral self-handicapping is a familiar phenomenon in social psychology: the student who parties all night before the exam so that, if she fails, the failure can be attributed to the party rather than to her ability. Notice that the strategy works precisely because the handicap is real — the student really is tired — and yet the arrangement as a whole is self-serving. My suggestion is that self-deception may be doxastic self-handicapping: rather than directly producing the comfortable belief, one engineers one's evidence-gathering so that the comfortable belief is the one any reasonable person would form from the evidence one has allowed oneself to have. The spouse who never checks the phone, the academic who reads only sympathetic reviews of a rival's work, the patient who declines the test — on my proposal the resulting belief is genuinely and wholeheartedly held, and yet the state as a whole is dishonest in a way that deserves the name "deception."

Your essay should assess this proposal. Does it dissolve the static and dynamic paradoxes, or merely relocate them? Is it genuinely distinct from the deflationary view, or is it just that view with an unfortunate label — and if it collapses into deflationism, is that a defect in my proposal or a reason to think deflationism was already the right answer? Consider at least one objection that my proposal handles worse than its rivals (I have my own candidate involving cases where the self-deceiver seems to believe the uncomfortable thing in some attenuated way, and I want to know if you can do better than my hand-waving response). Reach a definite conclusion: is self-deception possible, and if so, what exactly is it? Do not end with a survey or a "perhaps the truth lies between" hedge.
