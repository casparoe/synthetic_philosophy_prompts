My roommate (econ PhD program, insufferable about it) has an argument he deploys whenever someone cites Arrow's theorem like it's the last word on democracy, and I can't find where it goes wrong. First strip the theorem of its civic clothes: it says there's no method that takes a bunch of voters' rankings over three or more alternatives and produces a single ranking, where the method works for any combination of voter rankings (unrestricted domain), puts an option above another whenever every voter does (weak Pareto), keeps the final ranking of two options fixed unless voters' rankings of those two change (IIA), and has no voter whose ranking always wins out (non-dictatorship).

His argument: (1) Nothing in the statement, or in the proof, requires that the voters be different people — it's just a set of orderings. (2) A single person over time supplies exactly such a set: at each moment I have a complete ranking of my options, and for me to count as one rational agent, something has to turn my moment-by-moment rankings into the single ranking I act on. (3) So run Arrow with my moments as the voters. (4) Then for every person, at least one of the following holds: some single moment of me is a dictator (but no moment is obviously the boss — Tuesday-morning me has no better claim to rule than tired-and-hungry me), or sometimes I choose an option that every moment of me ranked below some other option, or how I rank two options depends on what else is on the menu, or the domain isn't really unrestricted after all (some combinations of my moment-rankings can't arise). (5) The second looks like plain irrationality, and the third is menu-dependence, which economists definately already count as a rationality failure — and I know my own rankings vary across contexts in ways that look at least cycle-adjacent. (6) So everyone is a walking impossibility result: no one is rational, and "the rational individual" is exactly as incoherent as "the rational democracy." If you wave Arrow around against voting, he says, you should be waving it against yourself.

Here's my problem: I half believe the conclusion, which is why it bugs me more than it probably should. Which premise do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? If I have to pick, I'd rather give up non-dictatorship in the personal case than give up the Pareto-type stuff in the voting case — but that asymmetry smells like special pleading, so I want to know what it's actually priced at. And I'd like to keep the thought that Arrow genuinely constrains real democratic aggregation; I don't want the lesson to turn out to be "the axioms are just wrong, everywhere." But if that's honestly where it lands, tell me.
