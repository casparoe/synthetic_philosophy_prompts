Write a dialogue between a cosmologist and a philosopher of physics, stranded together at an airport after missing their connecting flight to a cosmology workshop, on the question of whether the Boltzmann brain problem is a real constraint on cosmological theories or a piece of self-undermining theater.

The cosmologist holds that the problem is genuine and theory-relevant: in a universe with a positive cosmological constant, the far future is an eternally persisting de Sitter phase with a nonzero horizon temperature, and over unbounded time, thermal fluctuations will occasionally assemble momentary, disordered observers — complete with false memories of an orderly past — in numbers that dwarf the ordinary observers produced near the beginning. Since we should reason as if we are typical among observers with our evidence, any theory predicting such a fluctuation-dominance is thereby disconfirmed, and part of evaluating rival cosmologies is checking which ones avoid the prediction. She should be allowed to make this case vividly and technically — de Sitter horizons, fluctuation probabilities falling off exponentially with complexity, the point that a brain plus a specious present is vastly cheaper to fluctuate than a whole Solar System — without the philosopher being permitted to dismiss it as science fiction.

The philosopher holds the cognitive instability line: if the cosmologist were in fact a fluctuated brain, then her memories of doing the calculations, and the calculations themselves, would be junk produced by the very fluctuation she's worrying about, so the hypothesis destroys the evidential basis on which it was derived and can never coherently be conditionalized on. He should press this hard, but also press the typicality assumption on independent grounds — asking what licenses the cosmologist's "we should reason as typical" principle in the first place, and whether it survives when the reference class is brains that cannot verify their own histories.

Midway through, have the philosopher propose a self-test: maintain a sealed, timestamped ledger of predictions about one's environment over a long stretch — lab conditions, star charts, one's own diary entries — on the theory that a fluctuated brain embedded in momentary order should see its surroundings decay into incoherence, while an ordinary observer won't. Require the cosmologist to respond with the selection-effect point (only the fluctuated brains whose experiences happen to remain orderly ever get far enough to run the audit, so the audit's passing is no surprise conditional on being around to read it), and require the philosopher to take that reply seriously rather than shrug.

Both characters should be equally competent, each conceding something real — for instance, the cosmologist might grant that the measure problem makes "dominance" ill-defined without further choices, and the philosopher might grant that cognitive instability is itself a substantive epistemic principle worth spelling out rather than a mere conversation-stopper. The dialogue should end with the positions genuinely refined but not collapsed into agreement, and with each side's final formulation stated in their own voice. Keep the physics verbal rather than mathematical, and avoid strawmen: neither character may be reduced to slogans like "science can't prove a negative" or "anything is possible in an infinite universe."
