My closest friend and I spent the whole of last evening arguing about why we deliberately watch films that we know will devastate us. She maintained that we enjoy the sadness itself: the tears are not a price we pay for some other good but part of what we are after. I could not accept that anyone enjoys suffering, and I kept pressing her to explain how a single state could be at once painful and desired for its own sake. Late in the night she appealed to Hume: in his essay on tragedy, she said, he showed that the sorrow great writing produces is converted into pleasure by the eloquence of the writing, and she took that to settle the matter in her favor.

Since then I have tried to work out what actually follows from this, and I have run into what looks like a dilemma. If the sadness really is converted into pleasure, then by the time we are enjoying the work the sadness must be gone; so on Hume's account we never actually feel sad at tragedy at all — the tears and the heaviness would have to be illusory, or at best a preliminary stage that the enjoyment replaces. But we plainly do feel sad, and that is the very phenomenon Hume set out to explain. It seems to follow that either Hume's view is refuted by our own tears, or the paradox of tragedy collapses into the trivial claim that tragic art is not really sad — in which case there was nothing for my friend and me to disagree about in the first place, and neither of our positions made sense.

Neither of these can be right, so I assume I have gone wrong somewhere, probably in more than one place. Can you sort this out for me: was either of us right in the original argument, and which steps in the reasoning I have just sketched are mistaken?
