My city has a ballot measure pending that would replace our first-past-the-post municipal elections with instant-runoff voting, and at a neighborhood association meeting last week two members of the association — one a retired economics professor, the other a data analyst who chairs our election-methods subcommittee — spent forty minutes arguing about it. I want to referee their disagreement properly but don't feel qualified. Can you go through their exchange move by move, say who is right about which specific points, and identify anything both of them are missing?

For convenience I'll call them the professor and the analyst, and I'll do my best to reconstruct the argument in order.

(1) The professor opened by saying the whole measure is a mistake, because a famous theorem in social choice theory — Arrow's impossibility theorem — proves that ranked-ballot systems like instant-runoff are inherently broken. With three or more candidates and at least three voters, he said, no ranked voting method can simultaneously satisfy a short list of obviously desirable conditions: unanimity (if every voter prefers A to B, the outcome should rank A above B), non-dictatorship, and independence of irrelevant alternatives (adding or removing a non-winning candidate shouldn't change who wins). So instant-runoff's supposed advantages are an illusion; it's mathematically guaranteed to misbehave.

(2) The analyst replied that this is a misreading of what the theorem licenses you to conclude. Arrow's theorem applies to every ranked system, including the plurality voting we use now, so it can't function as an argument against instant-runoff in particular. If anything, she said, plurality is worse: the spoiler effect that got everyone interested in reform is itself a textbook violation of independence of irrelevant alternatives, since a third candidate who can't win changes which of the two frontrunners does win. If the professor's argument worked, it would prove that every voting system is unacceptable, including the status quo, in which case it proves nothing about how to vote on this measure.

(3) The professor said he was happy to concede that the theorem indicts all ranked methods — but that this makes his point sharper, not weaker, because the entire sales pitch for instant-runoff is that it "fixes" the spoiler problem, and it doesn't. Instant-runoff still violates independence of irrelevant alternatives, and worse, it is non-monotonic: there are documented scenarios in which ranking a candidate higher on your ballot causes that candidate to lose — something that essentially cannot happen under plurality. Trading a system that at least behaves monotonically for one that can punish you for supporting your favorite is a strange kind of reform.

(4) The analyst said the professor's concession actually points to the right conclusion, just not the one he draws. The theorem's grip comes from its insistence that ballots contain only ordinal information — rankings with no strength of preference. The escape route is cardinal methods: approval voting or score voting, where voters either approve a set of candidates or score them numerically. Since Arrow's framework doesn't apply to ballots that carry richer information, those methods are genuinely outside the theorem's reach, and score voting in particular can respect something like independence of irrelevant alternatives. So the lesson is not to keep plurality and not to adopt instant-runoff, but to write a better measure.

(5) The professor was unmoved. Cardinal ballots, he said, are a false promise, for two reasons. First, strategic voters will score their favorites at the maximum and their least-favorites at the minimum, so score voting collapses under strategic behavior into approval voting, and approval voting collapses further into a crude two-camp system — you've bought the extra information with a currency nobody will actually spend. Second, and deeper: cardinal ballots presuppose that interpersonal comparisons of preference intensity are meaningful, that your "7" and my "7" are commensurable, and he took the standard view to be that they are not. A system whose inputs are, strictly speaking, meaningless cannot be the rational choice the analyst makes it out to be.

(6) The analyst answered that the collapse-to-approval point is self-undermining rather than damaging: if everyone scores strategically, you get approval voting, and approval voting is a perfectly respectable method — better than plurality on spoilers, better than instant-runoff on monotonicity. And on the commensurability worry, she said the professor was demanding more of ballots than we demand of any aggregation: we already let a voter's ballot count equally whether they care passionately about the election or barely at all, so ordinal systems already ignore preference intensity everywhere it matters most. The choice is not between meaningful and meaningless inputs but between ignoring intensity and crudely recording it.

Neither would budge, and the meeting ran out of time. I'd like a careful adjudication: assess each of the six moves (rate them from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification), note where each side concedes too much or too little, say who is more right overall, and tell me what — if anything — both of them overlooked that a political theorist or social choice specialist would want added before our association takes a position on the measure.
