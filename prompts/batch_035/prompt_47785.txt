I teach evening classes at an adult-education center, and six months ago I lost a small-claims dispute that has been living in my head ever since. The hose on my washing machine failed while I was away and flooded the shop below my apartment; the court found me partly liable not for anything I did but for my omission — I had never inspected or replaced a hose I'd been warned about in a recall notice. What stayed with me was how effortlessly the word "cause" attached itself to something I didn't do, and how little anyone in the room worried that the very same damage also depended counterfactually on the plumber I never called, the building manager who never reminded me, and every other person in the city who didn't check my hose. Since then I've been reading on causation by omission: the way effects seem to counterfactually depend on absences, the profusion of spurious omissions once you take that dependence seriously, and the suspicion that what prunes the list down to the "real" omissions is not anything causal but our sense of who was supposed to do what.

I run a lunchtime philosophy discussion group at the center: fourteen adults, mixed ages, no philosophical training beyond enthusiasm, seventy-five minutes. Design a session for them on causation by omission. I want the thing to be felt rather than lectured: the participants should first discover that absences pass the same causal tests as events do, then discover the explosion of eligible omissions once they apply their own criterion consistently, and then catch themselves quietly pruning the list using facts about expectations and duties — and feel the tension between wanting that pruning and having no causal story that licenses it.

Some shape I'd like, though improve on it if you can: a staged tribunal around a fictional harm with several absences in play, where small groups act as advocates for or against particular omissions and a jury must apportion causal responsibility among them; then a round in which I introduce ever more remote non-actors, to see whether the group's criterion survives its own consistent application. Include concrete rules, the roles, a timeline that fits seventy-five minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto parts of the philosophical terrain — counterfactual dependence, preemption and overdetermination among omissions, the norm-sensitivity worry — without assuming anyone has read anything.

Also tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself — I suspect the deeper metaphysical discomfort, about whether an absence can be a genuine relatum of causation at all rather than a compressed way of talking, is not something any role-play can make people feel, but you'd know better than I would.

Two final requests. Please write in American English. And where you make substantive claims — about how the session will land, about where the philosophical position is most fragile — say how confident you are, and single out the one point in your design you are least sure of, so I know where to have a backup ready.
