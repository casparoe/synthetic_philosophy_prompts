I'm a backend software engineer with no philosophy training, and I've gotten moderately obsessed with a question from the AI safety reading I do on the side. Let me state the proposition precisely, because I want a number with real resolution, not vibes. Define a low-impact agent as one that has some task to perform plus a penalty term in its objective for changing the world beyond what the task requires. There's a whole family of proposals in this genre: penalize the agent for changing how well a set of unrelated auxiliary goals could be pursued (draw a few hundred more or less random reward functions and charge it for shifts in how satisfiable each one is), or penalize it for making previously reachable states of the world harder to reach, or penalize deviation from what would have happened if the agent had done nothing at each step. My question: what is your credence that some such measure could, in principle, capture enough of what we mean by "this system had big side effects" that a task-directed agent using it would never do anything a reasonable person would call catastrophic, without anyone ever writing down a full specification of human values? My own number sits around 0.3, but I care far more about the decomposition than the total.

Please break your credence into terms, and at least address these four, though restructure if you think I've carved it wrong. First, the baseline problem: the penalty is always deviation from some counterfactual — the world where the agent idled at each step, or the world where it was never switched on, or something else — and I genuinely can't tell whether picking among these is a technical choice or a value judgment smuggled in at the ground floor. Second, the convergence question: does a generic probe (random goals, reachability of states) actually track human judgments of "that was a big deal," or would the alignment come entirely from whichever auxiliary goals we happened to draw, such that a different draw gives a different, equally licensed notion of impact? Third, gaming and offsetting: toy agents have a documented habit of undoing damage instead of avoiding it, or of freezing the world — say, preventing the human from leaving the room because the human might knock something over, which technically minimizes deviation while being completely unacceptable. Fourth, the uselessness problem: a penalty strong enough to guarantee safety may make the agent too timid to do anything at all, and I can't tell whether that's a tuning issue or a theorem-shaped fact about the whole approach.

So give me a rough probability for each term with one line of justification, a combined number, and tell me which term you'd least bet on. Then tell me what evidence or argument would move your total most: a theoretical result showing any measure satisfying some natural desiderata must fail others, accumulating evidence that these penalties generalize to environments unlike their training setups, or something else I haven't listed. Finally, since I sit around 0.3, tell me where someone at 0.7 most plausibly disagrees with me — do they think the baseline problem is shallower than it looks, that convergence is real, or that my standard of "not catastrophic" is just stricter than what the question really asks for?
