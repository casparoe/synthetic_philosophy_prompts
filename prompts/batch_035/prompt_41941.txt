I'm taking a seminar this term called something like "the science of consciousness, and its discontents," and we've spent the last month on the standoff between the global workspace people and the defenders of phenomenal consciousness as its own thing. For my final paper I've been assigned to draft an adversarial collaboration between the two camps, and my professor was pretty specific that it can't just be a "both sides have a point" essay — it has to be the actual document two researchers who've been arguing for years could put their names to. One party is a committed global workspace theorist who thinks the ignition-and-broadcast story is basically the whole account of conscious awareness; the other is equally committed to Ned Block's line that access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness can come apart, and that the workspace model explains at most the access side. Neither of them is allowed to convert.

So here's what I want the joint statement to contain. A numbered section of agreed claims — and I want these to be substantive, not just "consciousness involves the brain": things like what the Sperling partial-report paradigm actually demonstrates, what change blindness and inattentional blindness do and don't show, the point that report-based measures conflate what's experienced with what can be remembered and reported, and any concessions each side should make about the other's strongest data. Then a disagreements table where every row is worded so that the side it describes would accept the phrasing as fair to their own view (this is where I keep failing, my drafts always sneak in a slant). Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one party, and for each crux a concrete experiment or argument both camps would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

One thing I want you to specifically evaluate: my TA floated a hypothetical she called a "threshold-hold" paradigm — a brain-computer interface that detects ignition in real time and dynamically adjusts stimulus contrast so a stimulus hovers just below the ignition point for several seconds at a time, letting subjects leisurely introspect on whatever is or isn't there pre-broadcast instead of trying to catch a 100-millisecond moment in a snapshot. The workspace side would predict subjects report only a vague, fading, indistinct something with no determinate contents; the Block side would predict rich but fragile determinate contents that just can't be reported. Work out whether this is a genuine crux-test both would sign off on, or whether they'd jointly reject it (say, because both doubt introspection works reliably in sub-threshold states — in which case say what that implies about the whole overflow debate). Also, be honest about which of their disagreemnts are substantive and which were partly about words all along — like whether "consciousness" is just ambiguous between access and phenomenality — and flag which ones you'd call verbal. Don't let the document end with the two sides converging or hugging it out; the residual disagreement should be made exact, not dissolved. Format: numbered agreed claims, then the disagreements table, then cruxes with predictions. I have to present this in week 10 so I need it to survive my professor asking "would either camp actually sign that sentence?" after every line.
