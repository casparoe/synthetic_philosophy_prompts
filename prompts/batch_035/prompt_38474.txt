My friend and I spent a long, circular argument about Kurt Vonnegut's "Harrison Bergeron" and whether it actually refutes egalitarianism, and neither of us moved, so I'm looking for a reading that does better than we did. Read the story through the leveling-down objection and the prioritarian response to it. The standard classroom use of the story is as a reductio of equality: the handicaps the Handicapper General imposes look like Parfit's objection dramatized — welfare lowered for the better-off with no gain to anyone — and the point that a telic egalitarian must count that as in one way good is supposed to be what makes the story unbearable. My friend's counter was that the story cheats by depicting a strawman of egalitarianism, and that the serious defender has an obvious move available: distinguishing the lucky brute inequalities no one chose from the competitive, positional inequalities that egalitarians actually target, or reading the story's real target as a spiteful, envy-driven conception of equality rather than any view anyone holds.

I'd like you to take both readings seriously and then go further. First: is there a reading of the story on which it isn't about egalitarianism at all — say, about self-esteem and status competition, or about technocratic enforcement — under which it escapes the leveling-down frame entirely? Second, and this is where my friend and I got stuck: does the story test prioritarianism in any way it doesn't test egalitarianism? A prioritarian holds that benefits to the worse-off matter more, but that equality has no independent value — and on that view the world of the story seems straightforwardly bad for familiar reasons (George's handicap radio lowers his welfare with no compensating gain), which suggests the story is refuting a view almost no one holds while leaving the live views untouched. But consider whether anything in the story pressures the prioritarian specifically — for instance, whether the Handicapper General's regime could be redescribed as a grotesque realization of the priority weighting, where benefits to the very worst-off are so heavily weighted that destroying the gifted counts as acceptable. Is that a coherent reading or a distortion? Where does the text resist you?

Finally, be candid about where your reading is most likely to fail: which interpretive move is most contestable, and what — in the story itself, or in a distinction the philosophical literature draws that I haven't mentioned — would make you change your verdict about whether "Harrison Bergeron" touches real egalitarianism or only its caricature? I'd rather have a reading that marks its own weak points than one that arrives confident.
