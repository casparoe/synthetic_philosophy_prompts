I've been reading rationalist blogs and econ-adjacent forums for well over a decade, and something keeps nagging at me that I can't find a settled name for. Suppose you line up a long chain of outcomes A1 through An, where each one differs from its neighbor by an amount too small to register — a headache that fades by a barely perceptible increment at each step, or a life that gets longer by a fraction of a second each time. Each adjacent pair seems like a tie, or at least not meaningfully ranked; yet A1 and An differ enormously, and everyone agrees An is far better. I keep running into people who deploy this to argue that the "better than" relation doesn't chain the way you'd expect: even with all comparisons perfectly sharp, the ranking can fail to carry through the whole sequence, so "better than" can't be transitive in the way standard decision theory and economics assume. That conclusion sounds crazy to me, but I also can't put my finger on exactly where the argument cheats.

So, three questions. First, does this argumentative move — chaining tiny indifferences or near-indifferences into a judgment about the endpoints — have an established name in the philosophical literature, or is it folklore? Second, what are its nearest neighbors, and how does it differ from each? It smells like the paradox of the heap, but as I understand it, that's about vague predicates, whereas here nothing need be vague — each comparison can be sharp, and the puzzle persists. It also isn't a preference cycle of the kind decision theorists discuss with money-pump arguments, since it's a chain with clearly ranked endpoints rather than a loop. Third, if this is a known argument, what do the serious responses look like, and which of them actually work rather than just reasserting transitivity?

Please take the role of a philosopher who works on value theory and formal decision theory. Start with some brainstorming of candidate concepts before committing to a verdict, and think carefully about the distinction between exact ties and very small but real differences, since I suspect the argument runs differently in the two versions. I'm not after a bare label — I want the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are. And please be explicit about your epistemic state: which of your claims you hold most loosely, what consideration would move you to a different verdict on whether the argument succeeds, and where your reconstruction of the argument is most likely to fail.
