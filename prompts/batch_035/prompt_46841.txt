I teach a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and am putting together a session on higher-order theories of consciousness. Rather than the usual survey of objections, I want to force a more disciplined evaluation, so I would like you to give me your credence that some version of higher-order theory is correct — that is, that a mental state's being phenomenally conscious consists, at least in part, in its being the object of a suitable higher-order representation, whether an occurrent thought (Rosenthal-style) or a merely dispositional or functional monitoring relation.

Please decompose the credence into at least the following terms, assigning each a probability with a one-line justification: (1) that phenomenal character requires any higher-order monitoring at all, as against first-order theories on which seeing red suffices for experiencing red; (2) that the higher-order state must be actual and occurrent rather than merely dispositional, given the dispute over whether dispositional higher-order theories collapse into first-order views; (3) that the theory can survive the targetless higher-order state problem — cases where a higher-order representation purports to be about a first-order state that does not exist — since the standard higher-order response is that such misrepresentation still yields a conscious episode, which strikes many as reductio territory; and (4) any further terms you judge necessary, such as whether the explanatory gap is even the sort of thing a structural theory of this kind could close.

Combine the terms into an overall number, then tell me which single term the overall credence is most sensitive to, and what argument or kind of evidence would move that term the most. I am less interested in calibration than in seeing where the theory's real weak point is located once the reasoning is laid out explicitly. Please respond in British English.
