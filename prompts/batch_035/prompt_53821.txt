I run the philosophy club at my high school, and I've been asked to design our next session. The club has fourteen members, mostly juniors and seniors, we meet for fifty minutes every other Tuesday, and I want to spend the whole meeting on an activity rather than a discussion, because a discussion of this topic goes nowhere fast.

Here is what happened. At our last meeting, two members had an argument I couldn't referee. One held that you cannot contradict yourself by saying "It's raining, but I don't believe it," because both halves of the sentence could be true at once — the rain is real, and the speaker genuinely lacks the belief, so there's no contradiction anywhere. The other held that whatever you call it, no honest person could ever say such a thing out loud about themselves. Both seemed right, and that's exactly what I want the club to run into on their own.

So I'd like you to design the activity. My idea is some kind of card game or structured exercise in which students are handed sentences and forced to choose between sincerely asserting each one or refusing it, in front of witnesses, with the group keeping a running record of what was assertable and what wasn't. The card set matters to me, and I'd like it to include, without labeling any of them in advance: plain contradictions, so the group has a baseline; sentences like the rain one said about oneself; the same content said about someone else in the room; past-tense versions ("It rained yesterday, but at the time I didn't believe it"), which I suspect behave differently; and variants that swap belief for hoping or wishing, so they can see whether the phenomenon is about belief specifically or about the speaker's mind generally. If you think of a case that would sharpen the contrast — maybe something about writing the sentence in a diary rather than saying it aloud — include that too.

Then a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto the distinction between a sentence's truth-conditions and what a speaker can do with it: what exactly the group discovered about why the first-person, present-tense case fails while its neighbors don't, and what that suggests about what assertion is for. I'd also like the name for the phenomenon to come at the end, if at all.

Finally, two things I need you to be honest about. We have a member who will perform the forbidden sentences in a jokey theatrical voice and then claim he asserted them — tell me how the rules should handle the difference between performing a sentence and asserting it. And we have another who will say the whole thing reduces to lying, which it plainly doesn't, since the rain sentence can be entirely true — tell me how the debrief should separate self-undermining assertion from lying. And flag whatever feature of the real puzzle a game with cards and peer pressure just can't reproduce, so I know what I'll have to supply myself.
