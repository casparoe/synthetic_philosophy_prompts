I am a retired geophysicist who has spent the past two years working through decision theory, after a career spent mostly worrying about instruments, calibration, and what one may legitimately infer from a finite stream of noisy readings. My entry into the subject came through an old colleague who insisted that expected utility maximization is not merely a convenient model but a requirement of rationality, and that the requirement can be demonstrated rather than assumed. When I pressed him on how, he gestured at what I have since learned to call coherence arguments. Having now read around, I find that there appear to be two distinct lines of argument, and I cannot decide which of them, if either, does the work he claimed for it.

The first is the pragmatic line. If my preferences violate transitivity or the independence axiom in certain ways, then a patient counterparty can, in principle, string my choices together into a cycle in which I end up with strictly less of everything than I started with — I am, in the jargon, pumpable. The second is the structural line. Representation theorems show that any preference ordering satisfying certain axioms can be represented as expected utility maximization relative to some utility function and some probabilities; the theorems are elegant, and I confess they appeal to my old instinct for reducing apparent disorder to a small set of organizing quantities. But two worries stand in my way, and they are disanalogous in a way I find instructive. About the first argument: in my laboratory days we distrusted any calibration procedure that could only be carried out by an adversary with privileged access to the instrument's internals. A money pump seems to require a bookie who knows my complete preference structure, including my dispositions among options I will never actually be offered; nature posts no such contracts, so I do not see how a merely possible pump at the hands of an impossible counterparty impugns a one-shot choice — say, the pattern of preference the Allais example is famous for inducing. About the second argument: "can be represented as" sounds to me like bookkeeping rather than obligation. That there exist numbers relative to which my choices look like expectation maximization is an existence claim about a representation; it does not by itself tell me that I ought to deliberate by computing expectations, any more than the existence of a Fourier decomposition of my seismic traces told the rocks what to do.

So here is what I would like from you: a committed comparison of the two coherence arguments — the vulnerability-to-a-pump argument on the one hand, and the representation-theoretic argument on the other — and a definite verdict on which of them, if either, actually establishes that a rational agent must maximize expected utility. Please take a position and defend it rather than laying out the options for me to choose among, and engage my two worries directly even if you think they are confused; if one of them rests on a misreading of how these arguments are meant to work, say so plainly and tell me where I have gone wrong. I would rather receive a firm verdict I can then test against further reading than a balanced survey. To that end, please close your response with a final line in a fixed, machine-readable form — \answer{...} enclosing a short phrase naming your verdict, for instance \answer{the representation theorem carries the normative weight} or \answer{neither argument establishes the requirement} — so that I can record it and check later whether I still agree with you.
