For thirty years I have made my living from a single craft observation: a reader who has been given a plausible arc will experience continuity even where the writer supplied none. The first chapter can be written with no thought of the last, and the finished book will nonetheless feel, to its readers, as though every early sentence had been quietly aiming at the ending it received. We novelists trade on this; it is the nearest thing our profession has to a law of nature. Lately I have begun to suspect that my own perception extends me the same amnesty, and I would like help deciding whether my verdicts about it add up to a principle, because at the moment they plainly do not.

Here are the cases, with my honest verdict on each.

In a darkened room, a red spot flashes on the left of a screen; a fifth of a second later, a green spot flashes on the right. With the timing right, I do not see two spots. I see one object that begins at the left, travels across, and turns green midway through its journey — I even seem to see the red of the first flash already darkening toward the change, as though the change were under way from the very beginning. My verdict: the color change was in the experience from the start. The first flash, as lived through, was not a static red dot that was later narrated into motion; it was, in the moment, a departure already underway.

Sitting in a cinema, I watch a scene end on a man reaching for a door handle and cut, mid-gesture, to the corridor on the other side of the door. I experience no seam. My verdict: I did not experience a discontinuity that was then smoothed over; there was never a felt gap at all.

My partner calls something to me from another room, half of it lost to the hum of the dishwasher. For a second it is noise; then something clicks and the sentence falls into place. Immediately afterward I have the confident impression that I heard it clearly all along. My verdict here is the opposite of the first: I did not hear it clearly at the time. The clarity was supplied afterward; the record of the first hearing was overwritten, and I do not trust my later report of it.

Glancing up at the kitchen clock after a quick turn of my head, I see the second hand hang motionless for an oddly long beat before it moves on. My verdict: I cannot give one. It genuinely seems, in the moment, that the long frozen beat was lived through as it happened; yet I know the first part of it was actually occupied by the movement of my head, and I suspect the extra duration was lent backward, as it were, to cover the blink. Whether this is a case of the first kind or of the third I honestly cannot say.

I wake from a dream with the conviction that it had hours of plot behind it — that I had already lived through long stretches before the scene I woke into. My verdict: obviously manufactured on waking. The sense of a long past was constructed retrospectively; nobody lived through those hours.

As a novelist, I know exactly how to describe the difference between the first two cases and the last two: in the phi and the film cut, the show is edited before it reaches the audience, the way a Soviet functionary staged the trial before opening it to the public gallery; in the garbled sentence and the dream, the show goes out as it actually happened, and the archive is quietly rewritten afterward, the way a certain imagined regime corrected its old newspapers to agree with today's. But notice the trouble: the two processes look, from inside, exactly alike, and my second and third cases strike me as the same kind of event — noise resolved by information that arrived late — yet I give them opposite verdicts. In one, the late content was, I say, present all along, as though the experience waited for the second flash before deciding what the first one had been. In the other, I say the content was absent at the time and pasted into memory afterward. I seem to be running both administrations at once, sometimes within the same minute of a Sunday morning.

So my question, put plainly: is there a principle that tells me, for any given case, which verdict to give — one that saves my verdicts on the flashing spots, the film cut, the garbled sentence, and the dream without contradiction? If there is, I would like it stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, and I would like to know what it says about the kitchen clock and about the case I keep circling: rereading the opening chapters of my first novel, which I wrote with no plan at all, I find the sentences now seem plainly to have been aiming at the ending I eventually gave them. Was that arc in my experience of writing them at the time, or is it something the present reading has lent the writing past? Or must I simply give up one of my verdicts — and if you think so, tell me which is least trustworthy, and tell me in terms a working novelist can accept: is experience a text that gets revised by its later drafts, or is it a text whose later drafts were somehow always the final version? I would also like to know what the principle you settle on predicts for a case I have not thought through — for instance, the sense, after a long argument I appear to have won, that I saw the other person's concession coming from the very first exchange.
