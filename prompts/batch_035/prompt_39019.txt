I'd like your help with something I've been chewing over for years without any formal training — I'm a dental hygienist, not a philosopher, but I keep circling one question in my own head: what actually makes a promise binding? Is it something the promisee has, or something the promisor did? I've found a way to get at it through my own family, and I want a careful, structured answer, so please take the role of a professional philosopher who works on promising and obligation, think step by step, and start with some brainstorming before committing to anything.

In my family, my father has been the unofficial referee on promise disputes for as long as I can remember. People bring him "does so-and-so have to keep his word?" questions, and he hands down rulings. I've kept track of his verdicts over the years, and I suspect they follow a rule — or maybe several rules that happen to agree so far. There's a new dispute coming to him next month, and I'd like a prediction, plus an analysis of what's doing the work underneath.

His verdicts so far:

1. My uncle promised to pay for my cousin's wedding photos, then had a tight year and backed out. My cousin hadn't relied on it — she'd saved up and would have paid herself either way. Ruling: the promise binds. A promise doesn't stop being a promise just because nobody needed it.
2. A contractor told my mother he'd probably swing by Saturday to look at a leak. She took the day off work; he never came. Ruling: no promise was broken. The man voiced a guess about his own schedule, not his word; he owed an apology for being a flake, and my mother owed herself a lesson about treating a probably as a plan.
3. A cousin of ours, pressured with threats over an old secret, promised to sign over his share of a business. Ruling: not binding. You can't owe what you were forced to hand over.
4. A friend of his promised to provide a false alibi. Ruling: some promises shouldn't be made, and what shouldn't be made can't bind.
5. A neighbor released him from his promise to build her a shed, saying she'd hired someone instead. Ruling: he was released; there's no wrong in letting it go.
6. Asked whether a promise to a five-year-old counts for less because a child can't enforce it, he said the opposite: you don't play games with a promise to a child — it's more binding, not less.

The new dispute: his own father — my grandfather — promised his dying best friend to restore the friend's wooden boat and hand it to the friend's grandson on the boy's sixteenth birthday. The friend died a week later. The family never learned of the promise, the grandson (now fourteen) has no idea it exists, and it turns out the boy doesn't care about boats and would rather have money toward a laptop. My father will be asked two things: whether my grandfather is still obligated at all, and if so, whether doing it means restoring the boat, or whether buying the laptop would discharge it just as well.

Please begin by brainstorming the candidate rules that fit my father's record so far, and be explicit about where two of those rules genuinely diverge rather than merely sounding different. Then give me your best-guess prediction of his ruling on the boat case, with your reasons for preferring that guess over its rivals — and be honest about how confident the record really allows you to be. Finally, tell me what one further dispute, of the kind he actually gets asked about, would best separate the rules that currently agree, and what each of them would predict about it.
