I've been working through David Lewis's account of convention on my own, without any philosophy training, and I've hit a problem I can't resolve. As I understand it, Lewis's central idea is that a convention holds because everyone conforms to it only on the expectation that everyone else will conform: nobody wants to be the lone driver on the wrong side of the road. But if that's the whole story, it seems to follow that a convention, once established, can never change. Here is my reasoning: for the convention to shift, somebody has to deviate first; but whoever deviates first does strictly worse, since they're the only one deviating and everyone else still expects the old convention; so nobody ever deviates; and since nobody ever deviates, there is never a starting point from which a new convention could take over. Conventions would therefore be permanently frozen, and any norm that once existed would persist forever. Yet it is obvious that norms and conventions do evolve — greeting customs change, dress codes change, even which side of the road a country drives on can change. So it looks like Lewis's account, taken at face value, is refuted by the mere existence of norm change.

Am I making an error somewhere in this argument, or is the account really committed to frozen conventions? If I've made an error, please point to the exact step where it enters, rather than just telling me that norms plainly do change. And is it enough, in your view, to say that conventions change only through external shocks, or can Lewis's framework itself explain gradual norm change, of the sort some writers describe almost like biological evolution, with small variations being selected for?
