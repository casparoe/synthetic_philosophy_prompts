I teach an introductory philosophy of mind course, and we're coming up on the unit on the knowledge argument. Every year the ability hypothesis — the Lewis/Nemirow line that what Mary gains on leaving her black-and-white room is know-how, the abilities to recognize, imagine, and remember red experiences, rather than any new factual knowledge — survives about ten minutes of discussion before a student pronounces it obviously wrong, and I've started to worry that we dismiss it too fast. My real difficulty is that the objections I can construct myself tend to stipulate the conclusion rather than earn it: they describe a Mary who already has all the relevant abilities, declare that she still learns something when she first sees red, and leave the ability theorist to complain — with some justice — that being able to sort chips with a spectrometer was never what recognitional know-how meant in the first place.

So I'd like you to build me a better case. The specification: construct a thought experiment in which Mary plausibly possesses, and has genuinely exercised, every ability the ability hypothesis says she gains on first seeing red — while leaving it intuitively clear that something is still missing until she actually has the experience. Here is my candidate seed, which you should feel free to improve, sharpen, or replace: Mary is a congenital achromat, seeing the world entirely in grays, who has nevertheless become the world's leading vision scientist. She sorts Munsell chips flawlessly using a handheld spectrometer; she paints photorealistic color scenes by mixing pigments against wavelength tables; she describes the experience of red to normally sighted colleagues in words they wholeheartedly endorse as accurate; and she would pass any behavioral test of color competence you care to design. One day she is enrolled in a gene-therapy trial — the kind that has already restored cone function in monkeys and reached early human trials — and afterwards she looks at a ripe tomato. Constraints: keep the case realistic (existing or near-existing interventions only, no downloading of experience), confine the scenario itself to one paragraph, and make sure her abilities are exercised and not merely dispositional, since I don't want the reply to be that she had the abilities in some hollow sense all along.

Then do the demonstration. State precisely which question the case is designed to isolate, walk through what the ability hypothesis, the acquaintance hypothesis, and a representationalist old-fact-new-mode-of-presentation reply each predict Mary gains when the therapy takes effect, and tell me honestly which feature of the case is doing the work. After that, steelman the defense: how would a defender of the ability hypothesis — or a phenomenal-concept strategist who borrows its machinery — reanalyze Mary's spectrometer-assisted sorting so that it fails to count as the relevant recognitional ability, and does that reanalysis cost the view anything, or does it merely drain the hypothesis of content by letting ability mean whatever experience turns out to provide? Finally, tell me what happens if the feature you identified is removed: does the case collapse back into the ordinary black-and-white room, or does a distinct residue survive that the original setup was never positioned to expose? Please keep the whole thing under 800 words — I plan to hand it to the class as a reading, and brevity is part of the point.
