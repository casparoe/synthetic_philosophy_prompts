I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and last month I moderated a panel on how societies should allocate scarce medical resources, at which two of the panelists — a moral philosopher and a health economist — got into an extended disagreement I couldn't referee and have been relitigating in my head since. The philosopher opened by conceding that maximizing is fine within a single life, where tradeoffs among a person's temporal stages make sense, because the one who sacrifices and the one who benefits are tied together by memory, anticipation, and shared projects. Her charge was that utilitarianism wrongly treats distinct persons as if they were stages of one enormous person: it takes the losses of some and the gains of others and nets them, and in doing so fails to take seriously the separateness of persons, since the one who loses and the one who gains share nothing that could justify the transfer — no set of memories, no ongoing perspective, no shared plan. The health economist replied that this proves too much: on a broadly Parfitian view, the unity of a person across time is nothing over and above psychological continuity and connectedness, which come in degrees, and the stages of a single person are in a real sense as separate from one another as distinct persons are — an eighty-year-old is barely psychologically connected to his eight-year-old self, far less than he is to his spouse of fifty years. So if separateness blocks aggregation across persons, it should equally block prudential aggregation across the stages of one life — saving for retirement, taking bitter medicine, studying through the night — which nobody wants to reject; hence separateness cannot be the real ground of the objection.

The philosopher answered that prudential tradeoffs are justified precisely because the relations are there: my future stage's good can count extra for me because he will remember being me, will carry my projects forward, and continues this very perspective, whereas a stranger's good is connected to mine by nothing except its being good, period. The economist pressed back: if justificatory weight tracks the density of psychological relations, then by the critic's own lights aggregation is legitimate wherever the relations are thick — between spouses, or in the middle of a life — and impermissible only where they thin out at the edges, and since connectedness declines smoothly, with no joint in nature to mark the boundary, the critic is committed to drawing a line through a continuum, which is arbitrary in a way that weighting everyone equally is not; the utilitarian at least has a principled stopping point, namely none. The philosopher's last word was that ethics is full of thresholds drawn through continua — self-defense, consent, the difference between a hardship and a wrong — and a sorites-style vagueness about where a line sits is not an argument that no line exists; a cutoff that looks arbitrary from inside the continuum is just what a real boundary looks like, and desert and partiality have to stop somewhere.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: say who is right about each specific point in the order it was made, identify what if anything both of them are missing, and tell me who comes out more right overall and why. Write continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a letter.
