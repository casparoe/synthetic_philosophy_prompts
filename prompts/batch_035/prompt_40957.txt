I'm a philosophy professor (decision theory, mostly) and I also sit on the grantmaking committee of a mid-sized foundation that funds global health and existential-risk work. Our director has gotten sold on what he calls evidential cooperation in large worlds, and I've been tasked with writing the memo for our next vote, so here's the situation. The idea, as he pitches it: if the universe is big enough, there are probably lots of agents in causally disconnected pockets of it — other galaxies, maybe other branches, maybe future civilizations we can never send a signal to — whose situations are structurally like ours. If they're enough like us (similar values, similar decision procedures), then our choices are *evidence* about their choices, even though nothing we do causally affects what they do. So an evidential decision theorist should sometimes cooperate with agents they can never interact with: pick the option that comes out best conditional on that correlation, even when it looks dominated under ordinary causal reasoning. What he wants from us is a standing rule sending 5% of each year's grantmaking to what he calls the symmetric portfolio — grants that only make sense if our counterparts out there are making the mirror-image move. The first two candidates are (a) funding work that benefits agents we can't causally reach (think reducing certain astronomical-scale bad outcomes whose beneficiaries we will never meet), and (b) signing the foundation up to a registry where longtermist orgs pledge to honor commitments toward agents they can never bargain with, on the theory that counterpart agents will then honor theirs toward us. Half the comittee thinks this is just portfolio diversification with extra steps and we should approve; the other half thinks it's straight-up double counting — we'd be treating our own grant as evidence about other people's grants and then spending real money on the strength of that evidence, which the causal-decision-theory folks say is an error, full stop. Draft the decision memo I can circulate before the vote — approve, reject, or approve with conditions. Whatever you recommend, engage the strongest version of the losing side, not the strawman: if you approve, deal with the double-counting objection in its best form; if you reject, say whether the director is right that we already implicitly do evidential reasoning every time we vote in an election or give to charity. And keep it short enough that the committee will actually read it.
