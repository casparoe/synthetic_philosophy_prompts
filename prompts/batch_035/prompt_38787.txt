Write a dialogue between a strict adaptationist and a pluralist in the mold of Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique, arguing about the human chin. The adaptationist holds that the protruding chin is a direct product of selection — for jaw support, sexual signaling, or something of the kind — while the pluralist holds it is a byproduct of developmental reshaping of an increasingly retracted face, which selection at most tolerated rather than built. The characters should engage each other's actual points: the pluralist should press on why an adaptive story deserves belief without evidence of heritable variation, past selection, and functional payoff specific to the chin; the adaptationist should press on what it can mean to call something a mere byproduct when developmental processes are themselves products of earlier selection, and on whether "byproduct" explanations ever make positive predictions rather than resting on the absence of evidence for adaptation. Have them, at some point, attempt to specify what would settle the matter — comparative data across primates, the fossil sequence of facial retraction, developmental perturbation — and disagree about whether such evidence is obtainable and what it could show. Late in the dialogue, introduce a hypothetical finding for them to fight over: suppose a large skeletal sample shows that chin projection correlates with markers of masticatory stress, and have them disagree about what, if anything, follows from it. Keep both characters equally competent — the dialogue should read as a genuine standoff, not a knockout for either side. And if you think the framing itself is confused — say, that "adaptation versus byproduct" carves the debate wrongly, or that the chin cannot bear this much weight — say so plainly at the top instead of quietly writing the dialogue anyway.
