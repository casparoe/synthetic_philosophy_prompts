I left school at sixteen and have no academic background of any kind, but for several years I have been thinking privately about a way of understanding history that I later found resembles, in fragments, the work of people who try to measure history with numbers. I call it, in my own head, the bottleneck theory of history. Every society produces far more capable and ambitious people than it has high positions for. When some mechanism — a hard examination, a narrow profession, a legally closed nobility, a frontier that absorbs the restless — keeps the number of claimants to elite status close to the number of places available, the society is calm and its politics is boring. When the bottleneck opens — when degrees multiply, when quick fortunes can be made, when the state creates posts faster than it can pay for them — the surplus claimants do not simply disappear; they turn their ambition against the system itself, and within a generation or two one gets factionalism, polarization, and sometimes collapse. Each crisis prunes the claimants a little, but never back to the level of the previous trough, so the pattern repeats like a wave whose floor rises each time. I think of that second part as the ratchet.

I would like you to write a dialogue between two people who disagree about this. One, call her Mara, is convinced that the bottleneck idea captures something real and that, in broad outline, the future can be read from such patterns, the way one predicts tides rather than individual waves. The other, call him Tomas, accepts that the pattern has often been observed but insists it is a story told after the fact: he holds that history is driven by contingencies no model contains — inventions, plagues, wars, the characters of particular rulers — and that every apparent law of history has broken precisely when people began relying on it. Both should be intelligent and argue in good faith; each must answer the other's actual points rather than talk past them, and neither should be a caricature. Have them draw on concrete examples that a well-read person would know, such as the way the late Roman Republic kept minting offices and honours for an ever larger crowd of ambitious nobles, or the way the Black Death, by making labour scarce, briefly shifted bargaining power toward ordinary people. Please write the dialogue as continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a long letter; you may mark who is speaking simply by a name followed by a comma. Let the exchange end unresolved, but with each character having genuinely conceded one point to the other.
