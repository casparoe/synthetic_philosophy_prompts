I write about science and ideas for a general-audaudience outlet, and I'm working on a piece about why we still can't get a decent weather forecast past ten days even with satellites and supercomputers. Every time I interview someone about this, out comes the butterfly effect, and I've realized the phrase is being asked to do about five different jobs at once. Forecasters use it to mean something technical about errors in the initial conditions growing over time. But then at a panel a climate guy got grilled with it in the opposite direction — if the flap of a butterfly's wings can cause a storm, how can you possibly tell me what temperatures will be in 2070 — and I've also seen it in a self-help context, where it meant something like your small daily habits compound into huge life outcomes, and once, memorably, in a corporate keynote about how one disgruntled employee can bring down a whole company. I have a feeling not all of these can be the same claim.

Here's my problem: I want to use the phrase in my article without becoming one more person who mangles it, and honestly I can't yet say what the precise claim even is. When the saying goes that a butterfly flapping its wings somewhere can set off a storm weeks later and half a world away, is that a causal claim (the butterfly is a genuine cause of the storm), a counterfactual one (no flap, no storm), a claim about our inability to measure things (we'll never know which butterfly did it), or a claim about how errors grow in a forecast regardless of what any butterfly does? And some of these seem true-but-mundane while others seem dramatic-but-false — like, wouldn't there be a thousand other butterflies whose flaps would have produced the same storm anyway, in which case what did the first one actually cause?

So that's what I'm asking for: lay out the distinct precise theses hiding inside the butterfly slogan, from the boring-but-solid to the exciting-but-dubious. For each one, say whether it's actually established (and what the real scientific content is — my understanding is that the underlying math is fully deterministic, which makes it extra weird that the slogan gets used to suggest the world is random or unknowable), and then tell me which readings would actually back the uses people put it to. In particular: does any legitimate reading support the climate-skeptic move I got at that panel, or is that a straight equivocation between predicting weather and predicting climate? I'd love something I can work from when I write the section, ideally blunt about which popular versions are basically folklore. If it helps, the nearest I've gotten on my own is distinguishing chaos from randomness and noticing that the slogan seems to slide between them, but I don't trust myself to draw the boundary yet.
