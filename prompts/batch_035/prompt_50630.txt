I am an emeritus professor of condensed-matter physics who, since retiring, has been working through the philosophy of science I never had time for as a researcher, and I now serve as one of two emeritus members on my university's Committee on Centers and Institutes. A case has come to us that my newly acquired reading of Popper has, to my surprise, not settled at all.

A senior colleague in the psychology department — a methodologist with a reputation for rigidity about statistical practice — proposes a small Center for the Study of Anomalous Cognition. The research plan consists of preregistered, adequately powered experiments testing whether people can predict randomly generated outcomes at better-than-chance rates, with protocols designed jointly with an external panel of skeptics who hold veto power over design choices. The proposed center would be funded largely by a private donor sympathetic to the field, conditional on university affiliation. Most striking to me: the proposal includes a sunset clause. If, after five years of preregistered adversarial trials, no deviation from chance is found, the center closes, publishes its null results, and the field is to regard the question as answered negatively. The investigator has explicitly waived the usual escape hatch that positive effects vanish under skeptical scrutiny.

The provost wants us to state whether this is science, since our policy only affiliates "scientific research programs," and she fears both legitimizing pseudoscience and being seen to decide questions of science by committee. My difficulty is that my Popperian instinct fails here: the proposal makes precise, testable, statistically falsifiable predictions, while plenty of work we happily affiliate is evasive about what would count against it. Yet something still smells wrong to me, and I cannot yet say whether it is the improbability of the hypothesis or something structural.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before our vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — affiliate, refuse, or affiliate with specified conditions — and, more importantly for the long run, articulate demarcation criteria that our committee could honestly apply to comparable cases in the future rather than a ruling reverse-engineered for this one. It should also give the strongest argument on the losing side its full due.
