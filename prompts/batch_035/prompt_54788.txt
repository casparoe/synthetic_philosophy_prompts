A colleague of mine in psychology keeps pressing the following argument on me, and its conclusion seems to me to prove far too much. (1) Rationality requires responding to all of one's evidence, including evidence about the reliability of the processes by which one's beliefs were formed. (2) It is well established that people's confidence in conclusions they reach by their own reasoning is systematically miscalibrated: measured across many domains, people who feel ninety percent confident in such conclusions are right perhaps seventy percent of the time, and similarly at other confidence levels; and I have no special reason to exempt myself. (3) So, for any conclusion I reach by my own reasoning, my total evidence supports a lower credence than the reasoning itself inclines me toward. (4) That lower credence is itself a conclusion I reach by my own reasoning, so by the same evidence it should be lowered again; and the repetition has no natural stopping point. (5) Iterated to the limit, rational credence in anything I have concluded by reasoning tends toward zero — including the calibration findings, premise (1), and every step of this very argument. My colleague concludes that ideal rationality is self-undermining: nothing reached by one's own reasoning, only what is taken on authority from outside one's own head, could ever be rationally believed. That cannot be right, if only because he reached it by his own reasoning. Yet I cannot locate the mistake. I would much rather keep (1) than deny that the calibration findings are evidence about me, and he warns me that rejecting (1) licenses epistemic akrasia — believing against one's total evidence. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
