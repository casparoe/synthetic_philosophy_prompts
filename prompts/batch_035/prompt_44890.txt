I'm a philosophy PhD student teaching a fifty-minute section of twenty second-year students on the norms of assertion. Design an in-class activity through which the students discover, rather than being told, that assertion seems governed by something stronger than truth. Two phenomena I want them to run into in play: the shape of challenges — that when an assertion is contested, the contest naturally takes the form how do you know rather than is that true — and the lottery-ticket case, where a holder who is all but certain, and fully believes, that their ticket will lose still seems unable to assert it. The activity should have students acting as asserters and challengers with some real structure behind them (roles, a hidden state of the world they have differential evidence about, a point-based payoff scheme rather than money), arranged so that these phenomena surface before anything is named; the three candidate norms — truth, belief, knowledge — should be introduced only in the debrief, where you map specific moments of the activity onto each. The debrief should also press on whether the activity's own data can distinguish a knowledge norm from a justified-belief norm, since the challenges the students actually issued may be compatible with both. Then add a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, be frank about which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply, and close by telling me how confident you are in your claims about how the activity will play out, singling out the point you are least sure of.
