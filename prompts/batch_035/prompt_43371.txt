I'm writing a chapter chapter on dirty hands and my advisor says my framing argument "proves too much and then some," and I can see the shape of her worry but not how to answer it. Here's the argument. It's supposed to show that the dirty hands thesis is either incoherent or trivial, so there'd be nothing left for the Walzer essay to be describing:

(1) The thesis says: there are cases where a political leader does the right thing, all things considered, and in doing so acts wrongly.
(2) "Wrong" here can't mean "violates an all-things-considered obligation" — that contradicts "right thing, all things considered" in the same breath. You can't be obliged to do what you're obliged not to do.
(3) So "wrong" must mean something weaker: breaches a pro tanto obligation, the kind that can be overridden.
(4) But if that's all it means, then dirty hands is just the ordinary case of conflicting considerations. A parent who misses her kid's recital because of a work emergency also "does the right thing, all things considered, and acts wrongly" — she breaks an obligation that gets overridden. Same structure, allegedly.
(5) Nobody thinks the recital case needs its own literature, a special phenomenology of guilt, or the idea that the parent's hands are dirty. We say: bad luck, apologize to the kid, make it up to her, move on.
(6) So either the dirty hands thesis is incoherent, or it's the recital case with mood lighting — and the giant literature on political dirty hands is a category mistake.

The problem is I can't take either horn. The first one seems to just assume every normative consideration feeds into a single ought, which is exactly what's supposed to be at issue. But the second horn would also swallow, like, Williams's stuff about agent-regret, and the claim people keep making that there's an appropriate guilt in these cases that isn't just regret — and I half believe that. What I've noticed is that step (4) quietly assumes the recital parent's residual obligation and the torturing politician's residual obligation are the same kind of thing, and I honestly don't know whether they are. I suspect everything turns on that, but I can't put my finger on what the difference would even consist in.

So here's what I want from you. Don't give me a survey of the five possible responses with "ultimately more needs to be said" at the end. Pick the step you'd reject, and tell me exactly what rejecting it costs — including whether the deflationist about the recital case gets to refuse to pay the same price, or whether the two cases have to be treated alike once you've gone deflationist anywhere. And then take a real stance: is there actually a third thing between "incoherent" and "trivial" — a genuine moral remainder that is neither an all-things-considered ought nor a merely overridden consideration — or is that a philosopher's invented emotion that we'd diagnose away if we were being honest with ourselves? I'd rather get a committed answer I can argue with than a menu of options.
