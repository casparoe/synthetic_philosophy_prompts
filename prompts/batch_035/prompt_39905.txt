As a novelist, I have long been struck by the following tension in my trade's institutions. The Science Fiction and Fantasy Writers Association declares a work a novel if it runs to forty thousand words or more, thereby consigning a story of 39,999 words to the novella category; children's publishers likewise assign sharp age bands to books, marketing some as being for readers aged eight to twelve and others as young adult, though no child becomes a different reader on her thirteenth birthday. Yet everyone involved would concede that "novel" is a matter of degree: nobody believes one word makes the difference between a long short story and a novel, and the same book may sit in the crime section of one shop and the literary fiction shelves of another. What theory of vagueness, if any, do these stipulations presuppose — that there is a hidden fact of the matter about which the word count is a workable proxy, that sharp lines can simply be stipulated where none exist in nature, or something else? Reconstruct the implicit view, say whether the award rules and the shelving practices presuppose consistent things, distinguish what the practices strictly require from what they merely sit comfortably with, and tell me what would have to change if the industry instead adopted a view on which "novel" admits of degrees. Please reply in British English.
