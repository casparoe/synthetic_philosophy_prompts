I've assembled my views in decision theory case by case over the years, and I'd like an audit of whether they can all be held together. Here is the package: (1) Expected-utility maximization is the correct theory of ideal rationality, applying wherever an act's expectation is well-defined. (2) The St. Petersburg game — a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads, and if the first heads comes on flip n you are paid 2^n, so the expected value is infinite — should be played at any finite price. (3) The Pasadena game of Nover and Hájek — same coin flips, but the payoff for a first heads on flip n is (-2)^n/n, so the expectation series is the alternating harmonic series, which is only conditionally convergent — has no expected value; and more generally, whenever a gamble's expectation series fails to converge absolutely, the rational response is to decline to play at any price, including a price of zero or of being paid to play. (4) Rational choice must be invariant under how a gamble is described: permuting or relabeling the possible outcomes cannot change what it is rational to do. (5) What ultimately justifies the whole expected-value apparatus is the long run — across enough repetitions, the average payoff converges to the expectation, so maximizing expectation is just maximizing what you will actually end up with. Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely inconsistent, which only appear to be, and what the cheapest repair would be for each. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them uniformly, and say whether there is a single revision that would clean up the most.
