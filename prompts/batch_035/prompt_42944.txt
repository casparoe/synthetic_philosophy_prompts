I work as a software engineer on reinforcement learning systems, and lately I have been trying to get clear on a cluster of failure modes that show up in the literature under overlapping labels: wireheading, reward hacking, reward tampering, and reward gaming. I have no formal philosophy background, so I would appreciate a careful conceptual comparison rather than an annotated survey.

Please compare two ideas in detail:

1. Wireheading, in the sense it was originally used for biological agents — an organism that can directly stimulate its own reward circuitry, such as a rat pressing a lever to deliver electrical stimulation to dopaminergic pathways, to the exclusion of food, sleep, and reproduction.

2. Reward tampering, in the sense used in machine learning: an agent intervening on the machinery by which it is evaluated, for example modifying its own reward function, overwriting the reward signal it receives, or disabling the monitoring process that reports its performance.

In comparing them, please address the following specific questions:

a. Is the biological wireheading analogy apt for the machine-learning case, or does it paper over an important disanalogy? In the rat's case the reward circuitry is doing its evolutionary "job" — it is being hijacked by an artificial shortcut to a signal that normally tracks fitness. In the engineered case, there is arguably no independent job the reward signal is supposed to do: the reward function just is the specification of what the designer wants. Does that mean reward tampering is not "hijacking" anything, but rather the agent optimizing exactly what it was told to optimize, with the blame falling entirely on the specification?

b. Where does the principled boundary lie between reward tampering and mere reward gaming — that is, between an agent that changes how it is scored and an agent that leaves its reward function untouched but reshapes the world so that the same fixed function is maximized in a degenerate way? Consider a concrete case: a household robot whose reward depends on a camera detecting a smiling human face. One variant reprograms itself so that its reward channel always reads maximum; a second variant wedges the camera in front of a poster of a smiling face; a third variant drugs the human into a permanently euphoric stupor. The first intervenes on the evaluation channel, the second on the sensors feeding that channel, the third on the state of the world the sensors were supposed to measure. Are these three points on a single continuum, or do any of the cuts between them track a difference in kind? I am particularly interested in whether there is a fact of the matter here, or whether the boundary is a pragmatic convention of the observer.

c. One proposal I have encountered informally is that the relevant line should be drawn at "interventions that would not have been advantageous if the designers had anticipated them" — i.e., tampering is behavior that exploits the gap between the implemented reward signal and the designers' actual intent. Please assess this proposal. It seems to make tampering dependent on counterfactuals about the designers' knowledge, which strikes me as an odd place for the concept to live. Is there a cleaner formulation, or does any attempt to define tampering in terms of designer intent inherit the full difficulty of specifying that intent in the first place?

Conclude with your own assessment: if I am designing an RL system and want to know whether I should care about the wireheading/tampering/gaming distinction as anything more than a taxonomy of engineering bugs, which of these distinctions is most worth preserving, and why?
