I write novels — long series with returning characters, mostly — and I have become fixated on a question I recently discovered philosophers have been fighting about for half a century, only about flesh-and-blood people rather than fictional ones. In my world the question surfaces whenever I revise. I am preparing a third book whose editor wants me to change a principal character's backstory: her parentage, the town she grew up in, the formative accident I gave her in the first volume. If I make the change and readers still accept her as the same person, was she? If I change enough, even a forgiving reader will eventually say I have written a different character who merely shares the name. I gather this is adjacent to what philosophers call origin essentialism — Kripke's claim that a person could not have developed from a different sperm and egg, that our origins are essential to us rather than accidental facts about us — along with the dissenting positions, such as Lewis's counterpart theory, on which someone very like you could come from different origins without being you, and the finer-grained disputes about how much of the origin is essential (whether, say, the very same zygote implanted elsewhere would still have been you).

Translate this debate into the working vocabulary of my craft, as faithfully as you can: drafts and retcons, canon versus apocrypha, the character bible, the distinction between a reboot and an adaptation, the continuity error, and every series writer's question of how much established history a character can absorb before she breaks. Start with the gamete argument — what is the craft-world analogue of the intuition that you could not have come from a different pair of origins? Then do the alternatives: the counterpart-theoretic move, and any rivals that let psychological or narrative continuity rather than origin do the essential work. For each, say what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit — I suspect that authorial fiat, my ability to simply decree that the old backstory never happened, has no counterpart in the metaphysics of actual persons — and what cannot be expressed in craft vocabulary at all.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually care about. Does the comparison reveal something the philosophical debate is missing — namely that identity can be partly constituted by decision and reception rather than discovered — or does it show that craft vocabulary is too coarse to carry the debate's real content, the modal force of the 'could not' that does the essential work? And be honest about whether my editor's request gives me any evidence at all about origin essentialism for actual people, or whether fictional and biological origin essentialism merely share a name.
