I am a professor of philosophy who has taught a graduate seminar on Davidson roughly every other year for the past fifteen, and each time the course runs aground on the same reef: the status of the principle of charity. As Davidson presents it in the radical interpretation papers, an interpreter working from scratch on an alien speaker must, by the very nature of the enterprise, take the speaker to be right about most things — since only against a background of shared belief and shared standards of truth does a hypothesis about what her utterances mean have anything to constrain it. The question that stalls my seminar is whether that must is constitutive or merely evidential.

The constitutivists — and Davidson's own prose often reads this way, especially in the argument against conceptual schemes — hold that charity is built into what interpretation is: a theory that attributes massive error is not a rival interpretation but no interpretation at all, so that the extent of agreement between speaker and interpreter is not an empirical discovery but a precondition of one. The heuristicists reply that charity is simply a defeasible inductive presumption, of a piece with assuming that a strange-looking animal is probably a common one in an unusual posture; and some, following Grandy's principle of humanity, would replace the injunction to maximize true belief with the injunction to attribute the beliefs the speaker would most plausibly have given her situation, her information, and her needs — which allows, in principle, finding a whole community in error about a great deal. Each side knows the other's moves by now. The constitutivist says the heuristicist cannot explain what makes a meaning-assignment correct, if not its success in predicting the alien's assent; the heuristicist says the constitutivist has inflated a fact about our epistemic situation into a fact about meaning itself. Nobody moves, and a good term ends in truce.

I am not asking you to adjudicate. I am asking for a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is it verbal — two senses of interpretation, one naming the abstract relation that fixes correctness and one naming the practical activity of an actual translator? Is it a disguised instance of the older schism between constitutive and evidential claims about linguistic rules? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work — whether real field linguists ever could get by without charity, or whether massive error is reachable by legitimate interpretive means? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one name, one about the metaphysics of meaning and one about the methodology of translation? I will offer my own hypothesis, presented only as a hypothesis: that the disagreement is really about what kind of argument the argument from radical interpretation is — a transcendental argument whose conclusion holds in virtue of the concept of interpretation, or an inference to the best explanation whose conclusion is contingent and revisable — and that neither side has confronted this because Davidson wrote passages that support both readings. Tell me whether that hypothesis survives scrutiny, what each side's actual commitments suggest, and, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a thought experiment that separates the readings? A formal model of interpretation in which the question becomes tractable? Or is the impasse a sign that the question of charity's status is ill-posed, and should be replaced by a better one? Be honest if the answer is that no progress should be expected, and say why. Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says and believes, not with a survey of the literature.
