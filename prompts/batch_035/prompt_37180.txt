I'm in a political-economy reading group working through the socialist calculation debate, and last week's session left me with an analogy I can't evaluate on my own. One member presented the case, drawing on Oskar Lange's late essay "The Computer and the Market" (published 1967), that Mises and Hayek's objection was at bottom about computational capacity: Lange there described the market as, in essence, a computing device of the pre-electronic era — a slow, decentralized way of working out the system of equations that formal theory had already specified — and predicted that electronic computers would let a central planning board solve those equations directly. The member extrapolated: if the calculation problem was ever a real obstacle, it was a silicon problem, and since we now have enormous computing power, machine learning, and constant streams of transaction data, the Austrian objection simply lapses; a planning board could compute the equilibrium the market gropes toward. Another member countered from Hayek's 1945 essay "The Use of Knowledge in Society," where Hayek insists the price system must be understood as machinery for registering change, something like a telecommunications network — on this view the market's work is communication of dispersed knowledge rather than computation, and the relevant knowledge is never given to any single mind. So the debate now runs on two analogies: the market as a pre-electronic computer (Lange) versus the price system as a telecommunications network (Hayek), with the first being used to claim that modern computing has settled the argument.

Act as a historian of economic thought specializing in the calculation debate. Before writing anything, think carefully through what each mapping must assume — in particular, what the computer analogy takes for granted about the inputs (that preferences, resources, and production possibilities exist as articulable, collectable data waiting to be fed into the machine) versus Hayek's claim that the knowledge society needs exists only as dispersed, frequently tacit, and constantly changing bits held by particular people, and that the data are never given to any one mind. Also weigh which features of computing (iteration and convergence to a fixed point, versus discovery of ends and means nobody had yet articulated) the analogy quietly imports. Then give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: item 1, the correspondences that genuinely hold in the computer analogy — what plays the role of the program, the input data, the processor, and the output, and what Lange would say the market's trial-and-error pricing is doing in these terms; item 2, the disanalogies that actually bear on the first member's conclusion — separating the computational bottleneck from the knowledge and discovery problems, and the statics of a solved system from ongoing adjustment to surprise; item 3, the disanalogies that don't bear on that conclusion, so the group can set them aside; item 4, a verdict on whether the conclusion that modern computing revives the socialist calculation project survives once item 2 is on the table, and if not, the narrower claim it could still support; item 5, whether Hayek's telecommunications analogy fares any better against the same scrutiny, and, if neither comparison is adequate, a more defensible analogy in its place.
