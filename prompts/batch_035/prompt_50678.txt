I'm an early-career psychology researcher. My most-cited finding — a two-sided test, n = 120, p = 0.03 — has just failed to replicate in a registered multi-lab effort, and I have a month to write the response. The detail that keeps me up at night: we collected the original data by peeking at the results after every 20 participants, while the replication fixed its sample size in advance. My two senior mentors disagree about what this means, and before I take a side in print, I want to watch their disagreement actually play out.

Write a dialogue between them. One is a committed frequentist in the error-statistical tradition, holding that the stopping rule is part of the experiment: my sequential peeking meant the original p-value overstated how severely the hypothesis had been tested, and the replication's failure is partly a correction of that. The other is a Bayesian, holding that the likelihood principle makes the sampling plan irrelevant to what the data say, so the real question is only what the posterior odds over the two datasets jointly imply about my effect. Both should be genuinely competent and should engage each other's actual points: the frequentist should press on what, exactly, her posterior is conditioned on and why anyone should trust an analysis whose inputs change with the analyst's priors; the Bayesian should press back on why long-run error rates, computed for experiments no one will ever run, bear on the credibility of the one study I actually ran. If they end by locating a genuine residual disagreement rather than a tidy resolution, that is a fine ending.

Each entry in "turns" should correspond to one substantive exchange, and the two advice fields should state, in at most two sentences each, what that character thinks I should actually write in my response to the replication.
