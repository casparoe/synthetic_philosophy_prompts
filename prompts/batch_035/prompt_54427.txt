Last night my friend and I had a long argument that grew out of a seminar exercise on negotiation. The setup: two parties must divide a surplus — think of two countries allocating a shared river, or two firms splitting a joint venture. Before bargaining opens, each party has one brief window in which it may publicly register a minimum demand. Registration is verifiable and irrevocable: each party posts a ruinous bond, automatically forfeited if it ever settles below its registered number, and once the window closes no new registrations or revisions are possible. His opening claim was that the rational use of this window is to register immediately, in the first moments, and at the most aggressive level still compatible with the other party preferring to fold rather than let both bonds burn. A completed commitment, he argued, converts the negotiation into one-sided concession: whoever is verifiably locked in has deleted every outcome below their number, so the counterpart's options collapse to "meet it or disaster," and most of the surplus migrates to whoever locked in first and hardest. Waiting to deliberate, he said, is just volunteering to be the party deliberated about.

I objected that if this is obviously dominant, both parties register in the opening moments, both register aggressive numbers, and the numbers are jointly infeasible: the bonds burn, both eat the disaster, and this happens even though each individual step was rational — so a policy that produces certain mutual ruin when everyone follows it cannot be what rationality prescribes. He answered that this only shows individual rationality doesn't guarantee good outcomes, the familiar social-dilemma structure, and that the lesson is to race well rather than abstain: register greedy-but-feasible, leaving the counterpart slightly better off accepting than burning. I replied that locating "greedy but feasible" requires exactly the careful study of the counterpart's disaster point that his own first move told us to skip, and that if both register blindly, before either can see the other's number, the commitments cannot condition on each other, so an infeasible pair is roughly as likely as if neither party had thought at all. His last word was that even blind, infeasible registrations are not fatal: one side must eventually renege and forfeit its bond, and the side for whom reneging is more unbearable holds the residual power — which, he said, is precisely why he wanted the commitment extreme in the first place, and which, I thought, was precisely why the whole arrangement is dangerous.

We stopped there, neither of us able to see clearly who was right, and I would like a referee. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each claim either of us made, say whether it was right, mistaken, or resting on something the setup does not actually support, and identify anything important that both of us missed. Then, if you can, answer the exercise's own question: under what conditions, if any, is it rational to commit early and extreme rather than to wait — and does the mutual-lock-in disaster case reveal a genuine flaw in the ideal of rational precommitment itself, or only the familiar fact that individually rational choices can be jointly bad?
