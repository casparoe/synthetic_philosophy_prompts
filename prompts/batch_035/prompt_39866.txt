I keep running into a pattern I can't quite name. Someone reports something, and the audience discounts the report — not because of anything about the speaker's identity, but because of the type of claim it is: it pattern-matches to a reference class of claims that are usually false or exaggerated (this widely prescribed drug wrecked me; I was pushed out of my lab for raising safety concerns; I know how this sounds, but I've found a real edge in the markets). As far as I can tell this is often just correct base-rate reasoning: if the overwhelming majority of claims of a type are wrong, discounting the type is rational. Two things bother me, though. First, the discount can persist even when the individual evidence is strong, as though the claim-type prior swamps everything else about the case. Second, it looks self-sealing: claimants of this kind are rarely believed, so they are rarely vindicated on the record, so the base rate of vindicated claims of the type stays low — the very evidence that would update the prior is never allowed to be generated. I gather that testimonial injustice, in Fricker's sense, is a credibility deficit caused by identity prejudice about the speaker, and this clearly isn't that: no prejudice about the speaker seems to be doing any work, or at least none that anyone avows. So my questions: is there an established concept for credibility discounting keyed to claim type rather than to speaker identity, and for the self-sealing character such discounting can take? And is there a literature on whether this even counts as an epistemic injustice, given how close it sits to ordinary Bayesian practice about which reports to trust? Throughout, please mark off what is settled from what is genuinely contested among specialists in this area — I'd rather not come away with one side of an open debate presented as the received view.
