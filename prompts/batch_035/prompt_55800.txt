I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about supererogation by listing my gut verdicts on cases, and I want to know whether any single principle captures them.

The passer-by who wades in and ruins a £300 coat to pull a child from a canal: he had to do it — required, not supererogatory. The soldier who throws himself on a grenade to shield his squad: heroic, but I would never say he failed in duty by not doing it — the paradigm case. The stranger who donates a kidney to someone on the transplant list: supererogatory. Someone who could donate that kidney and doesn't: I don't blame him — yet I would blame someone who let a child drown rather than ruin a coat, and the kidney saves a life too. The colleague who promises to donate and then backs out: now somehow blameworthy, as though the promise converted the supererogatory into the obligatory. The man who runs back into a burning house for a neighbour's cat and takes serious burns: I want to call it supererogatory, but I also feel it was, strictly, imprudent rather than admirable, and I can't get those two reactions to sit together. Finally, the person who lives on subsistence wages and gives everything else to famine relief: each day's gift seems too small to be beyond duty, yet the whole life is plainly more than duty can demand, and I don't know what to say about the aggregate.

My tentative principle is a cost-threshold one: duty extends to whatever sacrifices fall short of some severe level of loss — a ruined coat lies inside the threshold, a kidney or a life outside it — and supererogation occupies the region beyond it where acts remain praiseworthy. Please make that principle precise, test it against my cases, and repair it where it fails. In particular, tell me what your repaired principle says about (a) a rescuer who makes many small sacrifices that accumulate, over a lifetime, into a very large one, and (b) someone who does only what duty requires but with such a generous spirit that we admire her more than we admire a grudging supererogator — can attitude alone generate merit beyond duty? And be honest about which of my verdicts the best available version of the principle simply cannot save, and which verdict you would counsel me to surrender.

Please write your answer in British English.
