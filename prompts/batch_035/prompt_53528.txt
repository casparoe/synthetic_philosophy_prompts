I sit on the committee that sets the document-based essay question for our state's end-of-course exam in modern world history. For years the question has asked students to assess which power — the United States or the Soviet Union — bears primary responsibility for the outbreak of the Cold War, and we vote next month on whether to keep it. Three positions have crystallized in our meetings.

One member wants the question kept as is. Assigning responsibility, he argues, is what makes it a history question rather than a sociology question: it forces students to weigh the consolidation of Soviet control in Eastern Europe against the Marshall Plan, atomic diplomacy, and the Berlin blockade, and to commit to a defensible position. Dropping blame, he says, is not neutrality but a quiet verdict that no one was accountable — and it teaches students that the past is just weather.

A second member thinks the question is ungradeable in practice: whichever way the document set tilts, the rubric rewards students for matching the tilt, so the exam measures test-taking, not historical reasoning.

A third wants the question replaced with one asking students to weigh structural explanations of the conflict — bipolarity, the security dilemma, competing universalist ideologies, domestic coalition politics — against explanations centered on leaders' decisions, without requesting a verdict. Asking who started the Cold War, she says, is like asking which single trader caused a market crash: it presupposes unitary agents and a discrete moment of starting, and serious historiography abandoned both assumptions long ago.

Please draft the recommendation memo I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend one concrete course — keep, replace with her structural-versus-decisional question, or a third option you think better — with reasons my colleagues could actually respond to. It should engage the strongest consideration on the losing side, in particular whether any notion of responsibility can survive in historiography once we distinguish blame from causal contribution, and whether entities like "the United States" can intelligibly be held responsible for a decades-long process. It should also address whether a replacement question could still test the evidentiary skills the first member prizes. End the memo with a final line of the form:

Verdict: [KEEP, REPLACE, or MODIFY, followed by a short phrase naming the recommended question type]
