So I'm in a bioethics seminar this semester and we just wrapped the antinatalism unit — mostly Benatar's asymmetry (absence of pain is good even when there's no one around to enjoy that good, but absence of pleasure isn't bad unless someone is deprived of it, and the never-existing can't be deprived). Our final project is to write a mock adversarial collaboration, and I picked an antinatalist vs someone who thinks procreation is permissible — not a natalist who thinks you OUGHT to have kids, just someone who denies that creating a person wrongs them.

Write the joint statment the two of them would produce after a year of good-faith arguing. What I want:

1. A numbered list of claims both could actually sign — not vague "this is a complex issue" filler. Like, can they both agree that coming into existence can be bad for the person who comes into existence? That one alone took my seminar like 40 minutes.
2. The exact disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording. My prof keeps saying half this debate is people talking past each other about whether "good" and "bad" are being compared against existence or non-existence, so if some of the disagreement turns out to be verbal, flag which part and why.
3. The cruxes. I think one has to be whether the difference between (a) a person exists and suffers and (b) a person never exists and "would have" suffered is a real moral asymmetry or a confusion about who the subject is. Another one my TA raised is the Lucretius thing — if pre-natal non-existence and posthumous non-existence are symmetrical, and we don't think the dead are harmed by missing future pleasures, why think the never-born are "spared" their pains? So the antinatalist denies a symmetry Lucretius affirms while the procreation-defender affirms a symmetry Benatar denies — or something like that, I might be garbling it, and if so fix it somewhere in the statement.
4. For each crux, one concrete case or thought experiment both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. My TA suggested the "different child later" case — a couple who could have a child now in mediocre conditions, or a different child in five years in great conditions — as a test of whether the asymmetry can handle non-identity stuff, but if you have better cases use those.

One more thing my prof threw at us that I want folded in: embryo selection. If you can pick the embryo so as to reduce the expected suffering of the person you create, does the antinatalist's asymmetry just collapse into an ordinary risk-benefit calculus about how much suffering is too much for one life? And does that rescue the permissibilist, or just relocate the threshold? Put it wherever it fits best — maybe as its own crux, maybe not, you decide.

Also no reading lists or citations please, my prof was explicit that we should be able to do this from the arguments alone. And don't strawman anyone — the antinatalist in this collab is smart and has read everything.
