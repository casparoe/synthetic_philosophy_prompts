Henry Sidgwick — utilitarian moral philosopher, Knightbridge Professor at Cambridge, co-founder and first president of the Society for Psychical Research — spent the last two decades of his life (he died in 1900) trying to make psychical research into a respectable empirical science. His committee's Census of Hallucinations, published in the Proceedings of the Society for Psychical Research in 1894, canvassed roughly seventeen thousand people about their experiences of apparitions and compared the rate of ostensibly telepathic coincidences against what chance would predict. And his motive was never mere curiosity: as he saw it, the point was to determine whether the universe is morally governed — whether the dualism of practical reason he could not resolve within ethics in The Methods of Ethics might instead be resolved by facts, that is, by evidence that consciousness survives bodily death with moral consequences attached. Yet near the end of his life he reportedly judged that a quarter-century of work had not produced a single certain fact of the kind he was seeking.

Suppose Sidgwick could be fully briefed on what has happened since his death. Tell him, first, about the collapse of the era of physical mediumship amid exposures of fraud, and the resulting move into the laboratory: the ganzfeld telepathy experiments, the long-running dispute between Ray Hyman and Charles Honorton, and their 1986 joint communiqué agreeing on the methodological standards a fair test would have to meet. Tell him about the rise of meta-analysis as the arbiter of exactly this kind of dispute, and about the fact that, decades on, the two sides still disagree about whether the ganzfeld meta-analyses reveal a small real effect or artifacts of method. Finally, tell him about Daryl Bem's 2011 paper "Feeling the Future: Experimental Evidence for Anomalous Retroactive Influences on Cognition and Affect" in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology — nine experiments, over a thousand participants, published in mainstream social psychology's flagship journal, claiming evidence for precognition — and about what followed: failed replications that helped ignite psychology's wider replication crisis and the movement toward preregistered designs.

Given this briefing, what would Sidgwick conclude? Specifically:

1. Would he see modern meta-analysis, preregistration, and large-N experimental design as precisely the methodological instruments his program lacked — the tools that would have settled the questions the Census left open? Or would he recognize in the ganzfeld standoff a rerun of the stalemate he lived through: competent, honest investigators looking at the same data and splitting for decades?

2. What would he make of Bem? A mainstream psychologist publishing anomalous results in a flagship journal was exactly the kind of institutional legitimacy Sidgwick spent his career trying to win for this material — and it was followed by replication failure. Would Bem look to him like the strongest evidence yet obtained, or like the reductio of the entire enterprise?

3. Given his philosophical stake in the outcome, would the dualism of practical reason make him more or less able to accept a negative verdict than a merely curious investigator would be? Does his deep need for the result cut against his reputation as the most incorruptibly fair-minded skeptic in the field, or does that reputation show he had already insulated himself against motivated reasoning?

4. Finally, if he could have seen the next hundred and twenty years in advance, would he still have kept the Society's program going — and on which side of that question does his late-life judgment that he had found nothing certain actually fall? Is that verdict a reason to quit, or, in his terms, precisely the result a rigorous inquirer should expect to have obtained so far?

Ground your answer in what is on record about his methods, his stated philosophical motivations, and his temperament, and mark clearly where you are extrapolating beyond the record rather than reporting it.
