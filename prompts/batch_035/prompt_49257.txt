Two researchers at a consciousness-science institute have spent a year arguing methodology: one is a committed heterophenomenologist in Dennett's tradition, the other a committed neurophenomenologist in Varela's. They have finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and their first joint project will be a study in which long-term meditators and untrained subjects report on the moment-to-moment character of their visual experience during an attention task while EEG is recorded. You are to draft the joint statement they should produce. It should contain: (1) the claims both can sign, including about the role of third-person measures and about the fact that subjects sometimes misreport; (2) their exact disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording — in particular whether the subject's report is a text whose experiential import should remain open while interpretation proceeds, or a defeasible first-class datum gathered through disciplined first-person training; (3) the cruxes — for instance, whether trained introspectors' reports co-vary with neural dynamics better than untrained ones, whether insisting on neutrality about experience is itself a substantive theoretical commitment, and whether method can do anything about the hard problem or only flank it; and (4) for each crux, the kind of result or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about the meditation study written down in advance. If any part of their dispute strikes you as merely verbal, say which part and why. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.
