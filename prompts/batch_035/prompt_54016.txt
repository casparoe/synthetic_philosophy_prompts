I have no background in philosophy or science — I work in insurance — but for the past year I've been stuck on a question I can't resolve on my own, and my friends have run out of patience with hearing about it. We've all heard the line that if the universe is deterministic, then in principle some vast intelligence that knew the position of every particle could compute the whole future from the present. Fine. But something occurred to me one evening that I've never seen addressed anywhere: such an intelligence would itself be made of particles, sitting inside the universe it is trying to compute, and it would somehow have to finish each prediction before the event it predicts actually happens — which feels like demanding that a part of a machine outpace the entire machine. Worse, it seems it would have to predict itself predicting, and I can't tell whether that's a genuine obstacle or just me confusing myself. And separately, even setting that aside: we're told the weather is governed by deterministic laws, and yet no forecast is reliable past a week or two, and the standard explanation mutters something about a butterfly. But if the laws are deterministic, I want to know exactly where the information about tomorrow's weather goes when we fail to measure today's accurately enough — whether it's destroyed, hidden, or merely spread around in a way we can't gather back up. My overall suspicion is that determinism keeps being advertised as if it purchased predictability, and then quietly doesn't, for several genuinely different reasons that people (including me, on my worse days) tend to run together into one.

So here is what I'd like from you: a list of exactly five distinct reasons why determinism does not get you predictability, presented as five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion — just the five points themselves. Please make them genuinely different from one another rather than five variations on a single theme, and at least one of them should speak directly to my worry about a predictor having to live inside the thing it is trying to predict. I would rather come away with five crisp points I can hold in my head than a sprawling essay, so keep each point tight.
