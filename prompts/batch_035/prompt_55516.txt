My uncle is the family oracle on political legitimacy: he built a career in Shanghai, retired to Singapore, and hands down verdicts on governments the way other people discuss football. His rulings so far: China's party-state is legitimate, because it delivered four decades of broadly shared prosperity and order; Singapore is the best-governed country on earth; Putin's Russia is a kleptocracy that fails the only test that matters; Orbán's Hungary is elections without performance; Marcos's early growth was a prelude to plunder; and Kagame's Rwanda earns his grudging respect. He visits next month, and three cases are bound to come up: El Salvador under Bukele, where order has been restored and the president's popularity is vast but growth is ordinary; Vietnam; and a Gulf monarchy that distributes oil wealth generously while producing little else. Before he arrives, I'd like your predictions on each — but what I really want is the rule underneath. Pure performance would fit his record, yet so might stricter ones: a requirement that prosperity be earned rather than extracted, or that a durable organization rather than one man hold power. Tell me what each candidate rule predicts for the three cases, give your best guess with reasons, and name the further case that would best divide the rules. My sister and I have money on the Gulf monarchy.
