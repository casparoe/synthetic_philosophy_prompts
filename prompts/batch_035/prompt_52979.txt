I am drafting a survey on whether deterministic systems can be unpredictable in any serious sense, and I want the standard fixed before I wade into particular accounts. What must an adequate account of predictability for deterministic dynamics accomplish? It has to classify the right cases, respect certain distinctions, and stay consistent with certain results, and I want you to state these requirements together with the case that motivates each one. At minimum, the account should have to deal with the following, though you should add further requirements where you think the list is incomplete:

1. The logarithmic-horizon case. If trajectories diverge at a rate governed by a positive Lyapunov exponent, then extending the prediction horizon by a fixed amount requires exponentially greater precision in the initial condition. The account must explain in what sense this makes the system unpredictable even though no in-principle barrier to prediction exists, and it must say precisely where the line between in-principle and merely practical predictability falls.

2. The shadowing case. For many chaotic systems, a numerically computed orbit — which is provably not the true orbit of the true initial condition — nevertheless stays close to some genuine orbit of that very system. The account must say whether such a computed prediction counts as a success, a failure, or something in between, and so must distinguish predicting the actual future from predicting some possible future qualitatively like it.

3. The statistical case. Individual trajectories of a mixing system are unpredictable while long-run averages and attractor statistics are not; the familiar illustration is weather against climate, but the requirement is general. The account must locate the level of description at which prediction is being demanded, rather than declaring the system predictable or unpredictable tout court.

4. The algorithmic case. Grant the result that for broad classes of chaotic maps, almost every orbit is algorithmically random: its bit sequence is incompressible, so no computable predictor with bounded-precision initial data can do better than chance on it. The account must accommodate a barrier that is computation-theoretic rather than dynamical, one that persists even given the exact form of the evolution law.

5. The noise case. Unpredictability arising from sensitivity to initial conditions must be separated from unpredictability arising from unmodeled stochastic forcing; the former leaves a deterministic in-principle predictor available, the latter does not, and an account that conflates the two misclassifies both.

6. Determinism and observer-relativity. The account must not smuggle indeterminism into the dynamics, and it must take a stance on whether unpredictability is a property of the system alone or a two-place relation between a system and a class of predictors — a system that is unpredictable for polynomial-time predictors may be predictable by a predictor with more resources.

Once the list is fixed: say which of these demands are genuine adequacy conditions and which merely encode the commitments of one research tradition; whether any subset of the requirements cannot be satisfied together, in which case the list itself is a finding; and which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate accounts. Then, and only briefly, evaluate three contenders against the list — the naive appeal to sensitivity to initial conditions, the identification of unpredictability with positive Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy, and the computational-irreducibility line — marking each as satisfying, partially satisfying, or failing each requirement. The thinking should be in the requirements and their motivating cases, not in the evaluation.
