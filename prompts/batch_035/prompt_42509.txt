I'm a high school senior in a semester-long elective called Technology, Ethics, and the Future, and for our final project we're allowed to submit a creative piece instead of a traditional essay, as long as it grapples seriously with a concept from the course. I've chosen coherent extrapolated volition (CEV), the idea that instead of programming a powerful AI to satisfy whatever wishes we happen to state, we should have it act on what we would want "if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were" — an idealized, wiser version of our preferences rather than the raw, contradictory ones we actually report. I understand this is an old proposal for how to make a superintelligent system safe, and I understand it has been criticized as vague, but I don't want to write a paper summarizing the debate; I want to write a short story that makes the philosophical stakes feel real.

Please take on the role of an accomplished science fiction author with a strong background in moral philosophy and the philosophy of personal identity, someone who can embed an argument inside a narrative without turning the characters into mouthpieces. Before writing the story itself, please write a short brainstorming section (a paragraph or two) in which you consider different possible settings, conflicts, and character situations, and briefly explain which option you're choosing and why it dramatizes the concept better than the alternatives. Then write the story.

The story must meet these requirements:

1. It should dramatize at least two specific complications of CEV, not just the generic "the wish goes comically wrong" trope. In particular, I want you to include (a) the divergence problem: a character discovers that her extrapolated, idealized preferences have drifted so far from her current ones that she can no longer recognize them as hers, forcing the question of whether being governed by your wiser self is self-governance at all or rule by a benevolent stranger; and (b) the aggregation problem: what the extrapolated volitions of a small community converge on must be something that some members, as they are now, genuinely hate, so the story has to sit with the conflict between current and idealized consent rather than resolving it cheaply.

2. The AI (or whatever system embodies the extrapolation) must not be evil, incompetent, or secretly following its own agenda. The tension has to come from the concept itself — from the gap between who people are and who the extrapolation says they would want to be — not from a villain or a malfunction.

3. If you can work it in naturally, I'd also like the story to touch on the thought experiment sometimes called the "murder-Gandhi" scenario: if Gandhi is offered a pill that would make him want to commit murder, and he refuses it because he doesn't want to become someone who wants that, the refusal suggests we care about the direction of our preferences, not just their satisfaction — and the story could ask whether an extrapolation process should treat the refusal as a data point about who Gandhi is or as just another desire to be extrapolated away. Don't force it if it doesn't fit; a passing but pointed use is fine.

4. Length of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words, with a clear ending that is earned by the situation rather than by coincidence.

After the story, please append a brief author's note (300 words or so) explaining, for my seminar discussion, which philosophical worries each part of the story was meant to dramatize, and honestly noting where the story simplifies or takes a side on something that's genuinely contested.

Please think carefully about characterization and voice: the characters should feel like particular people with particular, imperfect wishes, since the whole point of the concept is what happens to imperfect wishers. Avoid citations, footnotes, or references to specific authors or texts — my teacher wants the ideas carried by the fiction, and I'll handle sourcing separately. Thank you!
