Write a Shakespearean sonnet on Harsanyi's two routes to utilitarianism. The octave should set out the aggregation theorem: individual preferences obey the expected-utility axioms, the social ranking obeys them too, and unanimity — agreement wherever everyone agrees — is the only ethical input, so that the utilitarian conclusion seems to fall out of almost nothing. The volta should land on the realisation that the impartial-observer proof, in which a single agent stands behind the equiprobability veil and might be anyone, arrives at the same destination — and on the suspicion that the two proofs are not independent confirmations but one assumption counted twice, since both channel ethics through a single expected-utility maximiser. The closing couplet must say exactly what a critic has to give up to escape the conclusion: either the independence axiom for social preference (which fairness-based objections to lotteries give reason to deny) or the identification of von Neumann–Morgenstern utility with wellbeing. Someone who knows the theorem should be able to tell which line carries which burden; someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem.
