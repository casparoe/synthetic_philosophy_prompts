I'm part of a small reading group that spent this past autumn on quantitative approaches to history, and we've just arrived at Turchin's structural-demographic theory of secular cycles: the idea that societies cycle through integrative and disintegrative phases driven by the interaction of three variables — popular immiseration as population presses against resources, elite overproduction as aspirants multiply faster than elite positions, and the fiscal weakening of the state — with disintegrative phases marked by converging elite infighting and mass unrest. For our next session, I've been assigned the late Roman Republic (roughly the Gracchi to Augustus, 133 to 27 BCE) as a test case, and I'd like a careful case analysis run in both directions: the theory illuminating the episode, and the episode testing the theory.

Please do the mapping with discipline. Identify who or what plays each structural role: where exactly we see immiseration (the displacement of smallholders, the growth of latifundia worked by slaves, the landless citizenry flooding Rome), where we see elite overproduction (Roman senators and equites, but also, importantly, the newly enfranchised Italian elites after the Social War — is their absorption into the citizen body an elite-overproduction event, and does the theory even have a slot for that?), and where we see fiscal distress (the tax-farming system, the reliance on provincial tribute and military plunder). Then assess the fit of the disintegrative-phase signature: escalating political violence from Tiberius Gracchus onward, the privatization of armies under Marius and Sulla, the turn of elite competition from litigation and bribery to civil war. Finally, take up two harder questions. First, Turchin's generational mechanism of roughly fifty-year fathers-and-sons cycles of violence: does the sequence from the Gracchi (133) to Sulla's march on Rome (88) to Caesar's assassination (44) actually support that periodicity, or is that pattern cheap to find once you know what you're looking for? Second, Augustus: when one man ends the disintegrative phase by monopolizing elite positions himself, does that count as the theory's cycle resolving, or does it expose a gap — the theory seems better at explaining why a system breaks than who wins the wreckage or how long the pieces stay down?

I'd especially like you to be honest about falsifiability. If a theory this flexible can accommodate both the Republic's collapse and its two-century-long predecessor period of relative stability, say so and explain what evidence within this one episode, if any, could have counted against the structural-demographic account — and whether the role of individually contingent figures like Caesar is something the theory should explain away or is genuinely outside its remit. Please write the analysis in American English.
