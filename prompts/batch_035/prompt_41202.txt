I'm a second-year PhD student with an undergraduate background in formal linguistics, now working in philosophy of language and mind. My dissertation has to be done in roughly three years, and I've decided to work on the debate around semantic externalism that Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment kicked off — the idea that the meanings of at least some of our terms (natural kind terms like "water") depend partly on facts about our environment, so that my molecule-for-molecule duplicate on Twin Earth, where the clear drinkable stuff in rivers is XYZ rather than H2O, means something different by "water" than I do, despite our identical internal states.

I don't want to spend three years re-litigating whether externalism is true; that part of the debate feels mostly settled, and a dissertation arguing "externalism: yes" or "externalism: no" would be tedious. What I want is to work on the live downstream questions. Here are the candidates I'm aware of, in no particular order:

1. Whether externalism is compatible with our apparently authoritative first-person knowledge of what we mean — the family of reductio arguments (of the sort pressed by Boghossian and McKinsey) that try to derive absurd consequences, like the ability to know substantive facts about one's environment a priori just by knowing what one means.
2. The slow-switching problem: if a person is transported between Earth and Twin Earth without noticing, do they end up with incoherent or contradictory belief sets, and does this reveal something defective in externalist content-individuation?
3. Whether cognitive science needs "narrow" content — whether externalism undermines computational theories of mind, or whether psychology can simply help itself to taxonomies that abstract from the environment.
4. How far the Twin Earth pattern generalizes: does it hold for artifact terms, for social kind terms, for evaluative and normative vocabulary, or only for a privileged class of natural kind terms? My linguistics background makes me especially curious about cross-linguistic evidence here — languages that lexicalize kinds very differently from English.
5. The metasemantic question of what actually fixes the content that's "not in the head" — causal-historical chains, patterns of use, the division of linguistic labor — and whether the standard answers are compatible with what we know about how kind terms are actually acquired and used.
6. Whether externalism has any interesting bearing on recent debates about large language models and meaning — e.g., whether a system that has never interacted with water could even in principle have the concept, or whether this line of thought is just a distraction.

Which two or three of these would you actually prioritize for someone with my background and time budget, and which would you steer me away from? I care less about the list than the reasoning: for each recommendation, say what makes it worth three years (whether it's upstream of other work, whether it has tractable entry points for someone comfortable with linguistics and formal methods, whether the field is stuck or moving), and what you'd deprioritize as either settled-by-fashion, too big, or unlikely to change anyone's mind no matter how well it's executed. If you think there's an important open question I haven't listed, include it and explain why it belongs above any of my six. Also flag what would count as progress on your top picks — new thought experiments, empirical results from acquisition or psycholinguistics, formal modeling — so I can judge within my first year whether a project is going anywhere.
