So this is a bit of an unusual ask. I sit on a county criminal justice advisory board, and next month we have to decide whether to recommend that the county adopt a pretrial risk assessment tool for judges here. The tool spits out a risk score from 1 to 10, and judges would use it (with discretion) in deciding release vs detention pretrial. We're not signing the contract ourselves — we're deciding what to send to the county board: a straight recommendation, or a recommendation with conditions attached.

The complication is a fight that broke out between two factions on our board, and I'm told it maps onto an actual impossibility result in the algorithmic fairness literature, though nobody on the board is framing it that way. Basically: the model is well-calibrated on our validation data — when it says a person has a 30% chance of rearrest, roughly 30% of similar people are in fact rearrested. The calibration faction says this is the only sense in which the score means anything — a risk score that isn't calibrated is just a vibe. The other faction counters that because Black defendants in our county are rearrested at higher observed rates than white defendants, a calibrated score will, under any single threshold, detain Black defendants at a higher rate — and, the part that stung, you provably can't have both calibration and equal false-positive rates across the two groups at once. Our data person confirmed this with a little two-population example on the whiteboard: if the base rates differ and the score is calibrated within each group, the error rates have to come out different.

Meanwhile a third board member, a former public defender, threw a wrench in that she thinks makes the whole whiteboard exercise beside the point: the thing we calibrate against is rearrest, not reoffending. Rearrest rates partly reflect where police patrol and who gets stopped, so the "base rates" feeding the impossibility argument are themselves partly a policing artifact. Her view is that debating which fairness condition to sacrifice is just arguing over how to slice a poisoned pie.

One more wrinkle to weigh: there's a compromise proposal floating around to keep the calibrated score but let each judge set their own threshold, with the tool merely suggesting thresholds by charge class. Somebody needs to explain clearly whether that actually changes anything or just launders the choice.

Please draft the memo I'd circulate to the board ahead of the vote — recommend adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions — with reasons the two factions could actually respond to, and be honest about the strongest point on whichever side loses. Assume most readers have no stats background beyond scatter plots. Also dont feel like you need to be neutral between the factions for its own sake — if one of the three positions is just weaker, say so, but the memo shouldnt read like it's talking down to anyone.
