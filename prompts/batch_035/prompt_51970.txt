I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and I've become the unofficial moderator of a recurring argument at work: when a colleague you respect disagrees with you about a technical call — say, whether a production incident was caused by our connection-pool changes or by a downstream timeout — what should you do with your confidence? I've since learned there's an actual literature on this ("peer disagreement"), but the positions always get presented as a list of names: steadfast, equal weight, total evidence, right reasons, and so on. I want a decision tree that sorts people by what they would actually do in cases, not by which label they've adopted, so that a colleague with zero philosophy background can walk it.

Start the tree from a concrete case — two equally experienced engineers, same logs and dashboards, opposite diagnoses — and make each branch node a question answerable without knowing where it leads, for example: Do you lower your confidence at all before re-examining any evidence? If you re-examine and still find your own reasoning cleaner, does their continued disagreement count for anything now? Does your answer change if it's five colleagues instead of one, or if you can't inspect each other's reasoning at all, as with a prediction market or anonymous poll? Build the tree from the logical space of answers first, then locate the named positions at the leaves, and flag any two positions your questions cannot genuinely separate — I suspect steadfast and "right reasons" views collapse into one leaf unless a question about whether your original reasoning survives reinspection does real work. Mark any combination of answers that no one in the literature seems to occupy, and say for each whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. Keep it to at most ten nodes and eight leaves, with a one-sentence gloss at each leaf on the position's most awkward commitment — I gather conciliationist views have an awkward time explaining what two conciliationists should do when they disagree about whether to be conciliationists.
