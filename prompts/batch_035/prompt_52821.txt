I teach a graduate seminar on rational choice, and in recent years I have committed myself, in print and in the seminar room, to a position that my colleagues treat as a mixture of quaint and perverse. I would like a map of its exposure: what would have to be true for it to be false, what it genuinely risks, and where it is, or has become, insulated.

Here is the position. The Pasadena game — a construction associated with Alan Hájek and Harris Nover, in which the payoffs alternate in sign while their probabilities shrink in such a way that the expected value is a conditionally convergent series — is often taken to show that expected utility maximisation breaks down, and that we must either declare the value of such acts undefined or repair decision theory with some other apparatus (rankings of utilities relative to the act, lexicographic rules, and so on). I reject both reactions. On my view, the lesson of the Pasadena game is that expectation is a species of summation, and summation is an operation that is not defined without a specification of how the series is to be accumulated. The standard mathematical convention — sum in the order given, which for a decision problem means the order in which the outcomes are individuated in the presentation of the act — is not an arbitrary concession but a principled one, since it tracks how a real agent, actually playing the game, would encounter the outcomes. Fix the summation order, and the expected value of the Pasadena game is determinate; conditional convergence is a curiosity for the mathematician, not a crisis for the decision theorist. Expected utility maximisation therefore survives the Pasadena game unharmed, and the undefined-expected-value industry is a mistake from the ground up.

Play it straight with me. Tell me what would count against this, sorted by how decisive it would be, from fatal through serious to merely awkward. In particular, I want your honest view on: whether the appeal to "the order in which outcomes are individuated" can survive the fact that a malicious game designer can present the very same act under different individuations, yielding different values and hence different choices; whether my position makes the value of an act hostage to the description under which it is offered, in a way that hands an advantage to whoever writes the prospectus; whether there is any independent ground for privileging the presentation order over, say, orderings by probability or by payoff magnitude, or over the various regularisation methods that assign values to divergent series, and if not, whether that is fatal or merely an embarrassment deferred; whether the view can accommodate the fact that an agent facing an open-ended sequence of choices might rationally care about the order in which it receives payoffs for reasons that have nothing to do with summation conventions; and whether anything in the wider neighbourhood — say, results about the sensitivity of preferences to framing, or about the failure of additivity — should already have moved me. I would also like to know which of these defeaters I could reasonably dismiss as begging the question against my own view, and which I would have to concede as genuine costs, perhaps to be offset by the view's simplicity. Finally, give me an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated by qualifications — the canonical-order clause can look suspiciously like a free parameter that absorbs any counterexample — and, if it has, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind, or simply what it means to give a convention-based resolution of a paradox. A tidy answer with numbered sections and a clear verdict at the end would be appreciated. Please write in British English.
