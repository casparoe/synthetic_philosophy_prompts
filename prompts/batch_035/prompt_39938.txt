My philosophy-of-biology reading group spent an entire evening on one example, and two members left it entirely unresolved. The example was morning sickness: whether pregnancy nausea is an adaptation, and what the answer would even show about adaptationism as a research strategy. Dana opened with the adaptationist case: the symptom looks designed rather than accidental — aversions cluster on exactly the categories that are most teratogenic or most prone to carrying pathogens (bitter plant compounds, meats, alcohol, coffee), the nausea peaks during the weeks of embryonic organogenesis and largely fades once the major organs are formed, it imposes an obvious cost at a time when caloric demands are rising, and she had read about a hypothesis that the whole system functions to keep mothers away from toxins and pathogens precisely when the fetus is most vulnerable. Her framing was: a costly, structured, well-timed trait like that is the signature of selection, and if it were a mere hormonal accident, selection should have pruned it. Marcus answered with the Gould–Lewontin point: this is the classic move of reading function off of fit — the very reasoning their spandrels critique targeted. He offered a byproduct alternative (nausea as collateral damage of surging hormones, sharing machinery with ordinary motion sickness), argued that the correlation Dana would cite — women with more nausea suffering fewer miscarriages — is equally well explained in reverse, with healthier embryos producing more hormones, and complained that adaptationists never seriously test the alternatives, they just find the story pleasing.

Dana's comeback was that byproduct storytelling is exactly as unfalsifiable as the just-so stories the critics complain about: "it's a byproduct" can be asserted of anything and never tested, and the critics demand that selection hypotheses be held to standards their own alternatives never meet. She added that her story at least makes a prediction — aversions should track teratogenicity and timing should track vulnerability — while the byproduct hypothesis predicts nothing in particular, so it's not clear which side is being unscientific. Marcus then conceded that a protective effect might be real while denying it shows what she wants: current utility is not evolutionary origin, and a trait that began as a side effect could acquire a beneficial effect later without ever having been shaped for it — the feathers-were-for-insulation-then-for-flight pattern. Dana replied that exaptation can't be a stopping point either: once an incidental effect started mattering for embryo survival, selection should have modified and refined it, so the real question is whether the current trait bears the mark of that later shaping, and she took the timing-and-aversion specificity to be exactly that mark. Marcus called this n=1 pattern-matching — you can't distinguish shaping from coincidence in a single trait, and "selection would have refined it" is precisely the Panglossian habit of assuming the trait is well-built and back-inferring why. He closed by noting that when researchers actually tested the toxin-avoidance idea against cross-cultural data the results came out mixed, and that the just-so version already circulates outside the academy as advice that pregnant women shouldn't suppress the nausea with medication. Dana's final word was that this is a misuse by outsiders, not a defect of the hypothesis — pain is an adaptation, and we still treat it.
