I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on rational choice theory, and my dissertation is drifting toward diachronic requirements on rational agents — specifically what, if anything, the money pump arguments are supposed to establish. Before I commit to a view, I want to work out the specification first, because I suspect the literature has been arguing about verdicts before fixing what a theory of diachronic rationality is even for. So: write me the list of desiderata. What must an adequate theory in this area deliver — which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it must respect, which results it can't contradict — with the consideration behind each, and a judgment about which are non-negotiable and which are parochial demands of one camp. The cases I'd want on the list include at least: the standard sequential pump run against an agent with cyclic preferences; the pump that stalls because the agent is indifferent or only trades when it's costless ("cheapening" out of the cycle); the pump against a naive agent with no foresight versus one who sees the whole sequence of offers coming; and the agent who, knowing its own future preferences will be distorted, pays up front to have its later options restricted — the commitment-device case, which cuts oddly across the usual camps. The distinctions should include at least: between being exploitable and actually being exploited (the pump needs a trader who knows the agent's preferences and can make a credible sequence of offers — does that make the argument prudential rather than a requirement of rationality?); between evaluating a plan ex ante and evaluating its stages as they arrive; and between placing the requirement on preferences (acyclicity), on choices (conformity with earlier plans), or on planning itself. I'd also like you to engage with my advisor's suspicion, which I half share: that the whole literature conflates two theses that generate different desiderata — a static thesis about acyclicity of preference and a strategic thesis about not being exploitable by an adversary with foresight — and that much of the disagreement between the friends of backward-induction "sophistication" and the friends of resolute choice (in roughly McClenen's sense) is really disagreement about which thesis the theory owes us.

Two things I most want from the list itself, since that's where I think the real work is. First, flag any subset of the desiderata you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable — e.g., whether a theory can handle the sophisticated agent with foresight without giving the wrong answer for the naive one, or handle the commitment case without abandoning the thought that rational preference is time-indexed. If some subset can't be jointly met, say so plainly and say which requirement you'd drop, since that would be a result in its own right. Second, tell me which single requirement excludes the most contenders — my candidate is the one demanding that the theory say something about agents who know their own future choices will be bad for them, since that's where pure preference-based views go quiet. Then a brief scorecard at the end — no more than a few lines each — for the standard money-pump argument for acyclicity, the resolute-choice view, and the position that exploitability is bad luck rather than irrationality. Keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard.

Since this is feeding into a dissertation chapter, I'd rather have calibrated prose than a confident survey. State how confident you are in the main claims, and single out the one point in your answer you're least sure of — I'd guess it'll be somewhere in the joint-satisfiability judgments, and I want to know which of those you'd actually stake an argument on versus which are hedges.
