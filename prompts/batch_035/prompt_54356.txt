I teach a second-year course in ethical theory, and next week's ninety-minute discussion section has to cover Bernard Williams's two complaints against utilitarianism: the integrity objection — that the theory treats agents merely as locations where the general good gets advanced, overriding the ground projects that partly constitute who they are — and the 'one thought too many' point, aimed at consequentialists who reply that a person can go on saving her drowning spouse, with the theory's own indirect blessing, once she takes the right background attitude toward her devotion. My worry is that students will file both under the heading of utilitarianism demanding too much calculation and never notice that the objections cut in different directions: one is about what the theory instructs agents to do, the other about how the theory lets agents think and justify. Don't explain Williams to me; plan the section. Specifically: (1) the order in which the pieces must arrive, and which has to come first — the alienation worry about projects or the thought-too-many worry about reasons — given that each can be made to look like a restatement of the other; (2) the single example that should carry the section and what it must have and lack — consider whether the standard drowning-spouse case should be adapted, since its melodrama and its pairing of spouse against stranger tend to derail discussion toward partiality and gender rather than the structure of the justification, and say exactly how you would adapt or replace it; (3) a pair of cases to be constructed live during the section in which one agent satisfies the integrity constraint but fails the thought-too-many test and another passes the latter while failing the former — I want the students to see that the objections are separable and that each closes off a different escape route for the consequentialist; (4) the misconceptions to expect, in the order they will surface — I anticipate integrity being read as mere psychological well-being, one thought too many being read as simple overthinking, and the sophisticated-consequentialist move of separating the criterion of rightness from the decision procedure, which students will deploy as though it were a knockdown reply to both objections at once — and the point in the sequence at which each should be absorbed rather than merely rebutted; (5) the closing question that would distinguish a student who has merely found the phrase charming from one who can say which utilitarian reply each objection forecloses; and (6) what I should openly decline to cover — Williams's larger critique of morality as a distinctive institution, comparisons with the complaint that verdicts based solely on consequences corrupt the concept of the moral, and the question whether integrity has any value when the projects it protects are rotten — with a sentence on why omitting each helps rather than hurts.
