I hold a tentative two-level view about the principle of indifference — that rational credence should be distributed evenly over possibilities when nothing in one's evidence distinguishes them — and I would like your credence that it is right, decomposed, because I suspect my confidence lives almost entirely in one leg of it.

Level one: indifference over finite or countable partitions of propositions is sometimes a genuine constraint of rationality, not merely a heuristic to be discarded the moment frequencies or decision-theoretic considerations appear. Level two: Bertrand's chord paradox shows that applying indifference directly to continuous magnitudes is ill-posed — the same problem yields 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 depending on whether one randomizes the endpoints, the midpoint's radial position, or the midpoint's direction — and the way to make such problems determinate is to demand invariance under the group of transformations that leave the problem statement fixed, the line associated with Jaynes. On this reading the paradox is not a refutation of indifference but a diagnosis: each of the three answers corresponds to a different, legitimate problem, and the group-invariance demand tells us when two statements of a problem are really the same problem.

My own credences are roughly 0.85 on level one and 0.45 on level two. Please give me yours, decomposed along at least these lines:

1. The probability that indifference over countable partitions is defensible at all — or whether even the finite case collapses under the standard arbitrariness objections, e.g. that redescribing the outcome space can be made to yield whatever probabilities one likes.
2. The probability that the invariance approach is the right extension to the continuous case, given (1). Here I want you to weigh the known embarrassments: the cube factory example (a factory produces cubes with side length between 0 and 2; is the probability the side exceeds 1 equal to 1/2 by indifference over lengths, or 7/8 by indifference over volumes? — with invariance apparently silent, since the map from lengths to volumes is not a symmetry of the problem), and the worry that invariance only gets a grip when the problem statement carries a rich geometry, which looks like smuggling physics into what was supposed to be a norm of rationality.
3. Whatever term you think belongs in the decomposition but that I have left out — for instance, whether the phrase a chord chosen at random even purports to designate an epistemic probability rather than a physical randomization process, so that the paradox trades on an ambiguity between normative and physical readings of randomness itself.

For each term, a number and a line of justification, then how they combine into your overall credence for the conjunction. Then tell me which term you consider least stable, and what argument or result would move it most. One further request: if you think the question is confused — that there is no fact of the matter about whether indifference gets vindicated, or that my two levels do not really come apart — say so plainly rather than producing numbers under protest. I would rather learn that my framing is off than receive a confident decomposition of a pseudo-question.
