You're a professional philosopher whose main area is privacy ethics — theories of what privacy actually is (control over information, restricted access to persons, contextual integrity, that whole literature) and what makes it valuable — and you've somehow been roped into a very concrete corporate fight. I need you to draft a decision memo for me, but think carefully and do some actual brainstorming first, because the two sides on my committee are talking past each other and I want the memo to fix that.

The situation: I sit on the "workplace experience council" at a ~2,000-person company. Facilities wants to deploy passive infrared occupancy sensors under every desk and in all 60 meeting rooms, joined up with badge-swipe logs, to figure out how much real estate we can dump now that we're hybrid. Their pitch: no cameras, no names, everything rolled up into per-floor hourly aggregates, so it's "not even personal data" — no privacy question, full stop. On top of that, some managers have floated an add-on: a per-team "presence score" on a shared dashboard, partly to nudge people back in.

The council has split into two camps. Camp A (the GC's rep and two data scientists) says: if it's aggregate and non-identifiable, there is no privacy issue, period. Privacy, on their view, is about sensitive information tied to identifiable individuals, and floor-level counts are neither. Camp B (an HR person and a loud staff engineer) says the whole thing feels wrong but keeps struggling to articulate why: you can still infer who's basically never in the office, who books the wellness room every Tuesday at the same time, which small room the union-curious folks keep meeting in, and people will start behaving differently just from knowing the sensors exist, before anyone even looks at a dashboard. Camp A keeps replying "but it's anonymous," and Camp B keeps replying "it's not about that," and we go in circles.

So here's what I want from you. Start with some brainstorming: what is the actual philosophical disagreement here? Camp A is implicitly assuming a specific theory of privacy (identifiability + sensitivity), and Camp B is gesturing at something else — control over one's informational environment, the idea that data flows have to match the context they were collected in, chilling effects on autonomy and association. Lay out the strongest honest version of each side, including the strongest version of Camp A, because they're not idiots and the memo can't treat them as such.

Then draft the memo itself — something the council could actually vote on at the next meeting. It should recommend a concrete course (approve the base deployment as-is, approve with conditions, reject, or split the base deployment from the presence-score add-on — specify the conditions if you go that route, e.g. retention limits, aggregation floors, no joins with HR data, killing the leaderboard), and the reasoning should engage both camps where they actually live. Be honest about the strongest point on whichever side loses. And please make sure the memo is readable by non-philosophers — no jargon without a plain-language gloss, no name-dropping of scholars, no reading lists. I want the reasoning, not citations. Around two pages is right.

One more thing: address directly whether the "it's just aggregates" reply actually answers Camp B's worry or just presupposes a theory of privacy on which the reply comes out true. That's the crux, and if the memo ducks it, everyone will notice. Think hard, take your time, and definately don't strawman either camp.
