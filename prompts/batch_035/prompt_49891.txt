I have recently taken a seat as the physician member of the clinical ethics committee at a mid-sized public hospital, and the appointment has turned questions I used to treat as seminar material into decisions I am accountable for. Next week I must present our recommendation on a scarce-resource case, and before I do, I want to understand what our own precedent record actually commits us to. Here is the record from the surge two winters ago, which the senior members treat as controlling:

1. Withholding. Our stock of the only effective agent was sufficient for two of three patients, all of whom would certainly die without it and were expected to recover with it. We treated the two and withheld from the one.

2. Reallocation. We moved our single ECMO circuit from Patient D, who could not survive to discharge and would die within the day without it, to Patient E, who had a strong chance of recovery. We accepted that D would die within hours of disconnection; the disconnection, not D's death, was what made the circuit available.

3. The transplant proposal. We rejected, unanimously, a plan to give Patient F a lethal infusion so that his organs could save two patients in end-stage failure. F was gravely ill but would have survived with continued treatment. We did not treat the projected net saving of a life as counting in favor.

4. Palliative dosing. We approved escalating sedation for Patient G's refractory terminal suffering at doses that foreseeably shortened her life. Her death was not the aim, and hastening it was no part of how the sedation relieved her.

5. Refusal of requested death. We declined to comply with Patient J's own repeated, competent request for a lethal dose in his final days, notwithstanding his suffering and notwithstanding that two of us privately thought the refusal cruel.

The new case, which must be decided within hours: a freezer failure destroyed our inventory of the red-cell units compatible with three patients now exsanguinating — an obstetric hemorrhage, a trauma, and a gastrointestinal bleed, each carrying multiple red-cell antibodies and compatible only with antigen-negative blood that our supplier cannot deliver for some fourteen hours, long after all three will have died. The only compatible donor on site is Patient K: an unconscious man in our own ICU with precisely that rare antigen-negative blood, deteriorating from a condition likely to kill him within two weeks in any event, with no surrogate reachable and no time to petition a court. Extracting the volume the three need tonight would very likely precipitate his death within hours — not as our aim, but as a foreseeable effect of drawing that much blood from a man in his condition.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Please identify the closest analog among the five precedents; name the rule we seem to have been running, and the rival rules that fit the record about as well (for instance, a doctrine of double effect reading, a prohibition on using a person merely as a resource, or a distinction between what we do and what we allow); and state candidly which principle we would be adopting if we extract the blood, and which if we refuse. If the record genuinely underdetermines the answer — and I suspect it does — tell me so, give me your best guess at our verdict anyway with your reasons for preferring it, and describe the further case, in outline, that would best discriminate between the rival rules.
