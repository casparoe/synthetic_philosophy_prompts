Consider a view sometimes called tranquilism: the only thing that matters intrinsically is the absence of craving and suffering. Knowledge, achievement, friendship, and understanding are, on this view, at most instrumentally valuable — useful insofar as they quiet or prevent craving, but worth nothing in themselves once craving is gone. Defenders of such views tend to accept a hard bullet: a fully tranquil person has nothing further to gain from life, since there is no residual craving left for anything to satisfy.

Now imagine a well-functioning contentment implant: a device that permanently extinguishes all craving while leaving rational agency, memory, and interpersonal attachment intact. A person with the implant still recognizes their spouse, can still reason and plan, but no longer feels the pull of wanting anything to be otherwise. Suppose the implant is cheap, safe, reversible only by removing the device, and available to everyone.

Please make the argument that tranquilism, applied consistently, endorses universal deployment of the contentment implant, and that this endorsement is a strength of the view rather than a reductio of it. Along the way, address three complications that a critic will press. First, grief: what should the tranquilist say about those who refuse the implant and are left mourning a tranquilized world that has stopped striving alongside them? Second, second-order craving: some people antecedently crave not to have the implant, or crave that their children not receive it — does the tranquilist have any principled reason to respect these cravings while extinguishing the ones the implant targets? Third, incuriosity: a civilization of tranquil, incurious people never develops medicine, art, or science beyond what is needed to keep suffering suppressed — has anything of value been lost, or does the tranquilist have the resources to insist that nothing has?

Please organize your response under the following section headings, in this order: "Thesis" (roughly 100 words), "The main argument" (roughly 400 words), "The three complications" (roughly 300 words total), and "Why this is a strength, not a reductio" (roughly 200 words). You need not defend tranquilism itself against rival value theories; assume it for the sake of the argument and work out where it leads.
