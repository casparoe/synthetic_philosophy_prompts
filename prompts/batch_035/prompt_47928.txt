My friend is a committed Moorean: with G. E. Moore in Principia Ethica, she holds that some things are good in themselves, independently of their consequences, and that among the greatest of these are the enjoyment of beautiful objects and deep affection for good persons. Over the years she has issued verdicts on cases like the following, and she has never wavered from them:

1. A perfect meal savored purely as sensation, with nothing else in mind: of trivial value, barely better than the pleasant scratch of an itch.
2. Being deeply moved by a painting or a piece of music, even where little bodily pleasure enters into it: among the most valuable states of affairs she can imagine.
3. A universe containing objects of supreme beauty but no conscious being whatever to perceive them: worth very little, vastly less than any world containing even one admirer.
4. A viewer moved to tears by a work she sincerely takes to be a masterpiece that is in fact a clumsy forgery: worth very little; she insists that the very same inner experience, had before the genuine original, would have been highly valuable.
5. Deep mutual affection between two people who are genuinely good: the most valuable thing there is.
6. The same deep devotion directed at a person who is in reality vicious: not merely worth less — it makes the whole worse than it would have been had the devotion not existed at all.
7. Remorse, understood as acute pain over one's own past wrongdoing: the pain is intrinsically bad, but the whole of wrongdoing-together-with-remorse is better than the wrongdoing alone.

Next week we debate three new cases, and I would like your predictions before I hear her answers.

(a) Schadenfreude: an intense, delicious pleasure in a rival's public humiliation, with no other feeling present in the person's mind at the time.
(b) A woman's devoted love for her partner, whose felt quality is indistinguishable from that of someone loving a good person, but every trait she loves him for is a delusion — the man is in fact mediocre, and there is nothing genuinely good in him for the love to attach to.
(c) A music-analysis system that emits eloquent, apt appreciations of the passages it examines; assume, as she does, that there is nothing it is like to be it — no experience anywhere in the system.

Please do four things. First, give her most likely verdict on each of (a), (b), and (c), with a one-sentence justification apiece. Second, extract the rule, or rules, that her track record seems most strongly to be running. Third, identify other rules that would fit the seven earlier verdicts just as well, and note on which of the new cases, if any, those rival rules already diverge. Fourth, propose a further case — one I could actually put to her — that would best discriminate between the two strongest candidate rules, and say what each rule would predict about it. I care less about the predictions themselves than about the extraction of the underlying rule and the design of the discriminating case.
