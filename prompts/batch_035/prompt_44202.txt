A stats podcast I listen to summed up the replication crisis like this: 'In the 2010s physchologists discovered that most of their published findings were false. The culprit was the p-value: null hypothesis significance testing lets you get a significant result from basically any dataset if you run enough analyses, so a p under .05 never really told you anything. The Bayesian fix is to stop testing hypotheses and just compute the probability that the hypothesis is true given the data, and since Bayesians work from priors instead of null hypotheses, false positives basically can't happen. That's why psychology has now largely switched to Bayes factors.'

I'm inclined to buy the Bayesian part — it sounds like the cleaner story — but a couple things felt off. I vaguely remember that when a big team tried to replicate around a hundred psych studies in 2015, only about a third came through, which is bad but doesn't obviously add up to 'most findings were false.' And the 'false positives basically can't happen' line sounded too good even as he was saying it.
