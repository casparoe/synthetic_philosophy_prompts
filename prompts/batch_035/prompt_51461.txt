I sit on the benefits committee of a mid-sized company with roughly 45,000 employees, and we must choose between two health plans for next year. Under Plan A, every employee's premium rises by about $18 per month. Under Plan B, premiums stay flat, but the plan drops coverage for a particular ongoing treatment — think of a biologic for a rare autoimmune condition — on which two current employees depend. Each of them would face around $200,000 a year out of pocket: effectively uninsurable, probably job-ending, possibly life-shortening.

Our CFO says the arithmetic is decisive. Plan A costs the workforce about $9.7 million a year in aggregate; the burden Plan B imposes on the two employees comes to about $400,000. So Plan B is obviously right, and not even close. I don't doubt his numbers, but I'm not sure we're entitled to add them up the way he does. Each employee's $18 is a separate small annoyance borne by a separate person, whereas the $200,000 falls on one person all at once. It doesn't seem that a million small inconveniences can combine to outweigh a couple of ruined lives, any more than forty thousand pebbles outweigh a boulder on the grounds that together they weigh more.

A colleague counters with what she calls the "no reasonable complaint" test: choose the plan that no individual employee, viewing things from their own position, could reasonably object to. She argues that any one of us could absorb $18 a month without much fuss, while the two employees plainly could not reasonably accept financial ruin — so Plan A wins on her test even though it loses badly on the CFO's. A third colleague thinks she's just hiding the arithmetic inside the word "reasonably," since to call an objection reasonable you presumably have to weigh it somehow, and the moment you weigh many small objections together his objection resurfaces.

We have to decide within a few weeks. Who, if anyone, is reasoning correctly? Is my pebbles-and-boulder intuition pointing at a real flaw in the CFO's summation, and if so, what exactly is the flaw — is it that burdens borne by different people shouldn't be summed, or only that they shouldn't be summed across such different magnitudes? Is my colleague's test a principled alternative, a disguised version of the same arithmetic, or something in between? And would it change the analysis if we kept Plan B but set up a voluntary hardship fund for the two employees, so that the small burdens were donations rather than premiums — or is that just the same decision wearing a different label?
