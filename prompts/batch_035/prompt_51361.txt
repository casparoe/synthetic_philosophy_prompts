Six months ago my mother was diagnosed with early Alzheimer's, and I arranged for her to live in an assisted residence near me and became her main point of contact. Until then my moral thinking ran entirely on rules and outcomes, and I never gave the ethics of caring much thought; now I notice I have firm gut verdicts about particular moments with her, and I would like to know whether they add up to a principle, or whether some of them have to go. My verdicts are these. When she calls at 2 a.m. panicked about a misplaced purse, I get up and search with her, and I think that is right, even though by morning the purse would have turned up on its own and a brisk "we'll look in the morning" would have cost her nothing. When she asks why she keeps forgetting things, I let the question drift rather than name the diagnosis, and that feels right; but when she insists she is still safe to drive, my flat refusal feels right too, even though it overrides her strongest wish. I declined to have her move in with me and set up the residence near my home instead — she very much wanted to move in, and I still think I was right, which I cannot square with the thought that caring means responding to what she actually wants. My brother pays for her aide from abroad and never calls; in my gut he is failing her, even though his money secures more of her comfort than my visits do — visits she often does not remember an hour later. Her paid aide is more patient and more skilled with her than I am, and I cannot decide whether the aide's care counts morally in the same way mine does, or is a different thing entirely. So my question is whether there is a principle, or a small set of principles each earning its keep against a specific case, that captures these verdicts. It will have to say something coherent about what caring owes to the cared-for's own wishes, since I both indulge her wishes (the purse, the drifting) and override them (the car, the move). Please state the principle precisely, note which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate if any, and tell me what it predicts for the case I keep going back and forth on: whether, when she asks me directly whether she is going to get worse, I owe her the plain truth. I am not looking for a survey of the literature or a reading list, just the principle, worked out. Please answer in American English.
