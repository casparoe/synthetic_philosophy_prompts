I'm a graduate student presenting a 30-minute paper, with 20 minutes of discussion afterwards, at a small invited workshop on collective rationality. The audience is about half formal social choice theorists who know the List–Pettit judgment-aggregation literature inside out, a couple of deliberative democrats of broadly Habermasian sympathies, and a few philosophers of action who come to these things for the collective intentionality questions. The organizer, who works on judgment aggregation, will chair.

My thesis is that the discursive dilemma does not reveal a distinctive problem of collective rationality, as the standard presentation has it. I argue it is largely an artifact of holding the agenda fixed while allowing the group's commitments to vary; once agenda-setting is treated as something the group itself does over time, the "dilemma" degrades into an ordinary trade-off between maintaining consistency on the record and reaching what the members actually want. The constructive part introduces what I call a consistency budget: a cap on the number of mutually inconsistent accepted propositions a body may carry at any point in a deliberative cycle, allocated by the chair or by rotation in the way speaking time is allocated. On my view, much of what looks like collective irrationality in committees is better modeled as budget exhaustion than as a failure of group reasoning, and the interesting normative question shifts from "how do we aggregate correctly?" to "who should control the budget?"

Please also identify the weakest point of my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices, and the single question I am hoping nobody asks.
