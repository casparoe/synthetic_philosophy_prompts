An old friend from my LessWrong meetup days is now a devoted cryonicist, and she has unusually firm and consistent verdicts about who is dead and who isn't. Her track record, as accumulated over years of arguments: a patient with total and irreversible loss of all brain function, heart still beating on a ventilator, is dead; a man who collapses at home, is not resuscitated, and grows cold, is dead; a patient in a permanent vegetative state, brainstem intact and breathing without machines, is alive, though a tragedy; an anencephalic newborn is alive; a patient under deep general anesthesia with an isoelectric EEG is obviously alive; a skier buried in an avalanche with no pulse for half an hour, rewarmed in the ER, and discharged neurologically intact a week later, never died; and a nematode vitrified at cryogenic temperature and thawed alive years later never died either. She insists it is one rule applied consistently.

Two new cases have come up. First, one of her organization's patients: a member who suffered a witnessed cardiac arrest, was declared dead by circulatory criteria, received prompt cooling and cryoprotectant perfusion, and has now sat at liquid nitrogen temperature for over a decade, with the perfusion report suggesting the brain's fine structure is substantially intact. Second, a hospital case she was asked about: a patient who passes every bedside test for brain death — apnea test, absent reflexes, isoelectric EEG — yet whose kidneys are working suspiciously well, because (a documented phenomenon in the literature) his hypothalamus is still osmoregulating, secreting antidiuretic hormone so that he never develops diabetes insipidus.

Predict her verdict on each. I'm less interested in the answers than in the rule she appears to be running: which principles are jointly consistent with her track record, how those principles diverge — if they do — on the two new cases, your best guess at her verdicts and why, and the further case, of her usual kind, that would best tell the candidate rules apart.
