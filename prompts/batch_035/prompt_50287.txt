I'm on the acquisitions committee at a mid-size museum and we've got a weird case I need a written opinion on before Thursday's meeting. Short version: we accessioned a classical bronze in 1952 that had been recovered in fragments and reassembled, with some missing pieces recast from modern bronze. Then in the 90s one of those recast sections was found to be botched, so it was melted down and cast again — meaning a decent chunk of the metal currently in the statue has only been part of it since 1998. Our insurance policy covers "the same object" as the one originally accessioned, and nobody has ever defined that phrase. Half the committee says obviously it's the same object (continuous display, same accession number, same catalog entry); the other half says we're effectively insuring a replica with old fragments embedded. Draft the memo I'll circulate: it should recommend a concrete course of action — insure as the same object, treat it as a new object, or some third option with a criterion the registrar can actually apply — and it has to take seriously the philosophical worry underneath all this, that "the statue" and "the lump of bronze" may not be the same thing at all: the lump could survive being squashed flat in an earthquake and the statue couldn't, so if they've got different survival conditions it's hard to say they're identical, yet our policy slides between them without noticing. Also be fair to the folks who think this is overthinking it — the "same object" intuition isn't nothing. Committee is mostly curators, not philosophers, so keep it readable.
