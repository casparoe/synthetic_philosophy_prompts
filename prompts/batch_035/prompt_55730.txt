"Scanlon's contractualism is supposed to avoid aggregation of complaints across persons. Answer all three parts.
(a) State the individualist restriction and explain how contractualism nonetheless yields the verdict that we should rescue the greater number in a case where you can save either one stranger or five.
(b) Does the restriction also forbid aggregation within a single life? Explain with an example.
(c) A town must choose between two water-treatment options. Option A spreads the risk: each of its five million residents faces a 1-in-a-million chance of death. Option B concentrates it: a single worker takes a 1-in-a-thousand chance of death and everyone else is safe. What does contractualism imply about this choice, and is the implication defensible?"

Here's the student's answer, more or less verbatim:

"(a) Scanlon's contractualism holds that an act is wrong if it would be permitted by a principle that no one could reasonably reject. This is basically a consent test: principles are justified when rational contractors in something like Rawls's original position would all agree to them, just applied to everyday morality instead of institutions. The individualist restriction says that in deciding whether a principle could be reasonably rejected we may look only at the complaints of individual persons, never at the sum of the complaints of a group. So in the rescue case we cannot say that five lives outweigh one. But contractualism still tells us to save the five. If we save the one, each of the five has a complaint (being allowed to die) that is no weaker than the complaint the single person would have if we saved the five. Since their complaints tie in strength but there are more of them, the scale tips toward the group taken together, so the principle 'save the greater number' cannot be reasonably rejected.

(b) The restriction has to apply within a single life too. It would be arbitrary to allow summing when the complaints belong to one person but not when they belong to several — a headache is a headache regardless of whose it is. So even for one person, many minor pains can never add up to outweigh a single major harm in that person's life. Each complaint stands on its own.

(c) Under option B the worker faces a 1/1000 risk of death, while under A each resident faces only one in a million. But those tiny risks add up: five million people each at one-in-a-million is about five expected deaths, which is far worse than the worker's expected loss. Since the individual complaints on the A side are so small, contractualism can and should aggregate them in a case like this, just as small pains within a life can add up. So no one could reasonably reject option B, and the town should choose it. The worker's complaint is outweighed by the combined complaints of the residents, and it's defensible — it also shows contractualism and consequentialism converge in risk cases."
