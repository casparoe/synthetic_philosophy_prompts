Modern weather forecasting is a weird hybrid and I want someone to unpack its philosophical commitments. The atmosphere, as far as anyone can tell, is deterministic — no dice in the equations of motion. Yet forecasters issue probabilities (a 30% chance of rain Tuesday), and they get them by running ensembles: dozens of forecasts from slightly perturbed initial conditions. Even weirder, operational models since the late 90s deliberately inject random noise into the physics — stochastic parameterization of sub-grid processes — and this makes the forecasts *better*. So the practice seems to be saying: the best way to predict a deterministic system is to make the prediction partly random. What view of probability does that presuppose? Is the 30% purely epistemic (our ignorance smeared over initial conditions and model error), or does chaos make it something closer to objective chance? And is the injected noise just a computational trick, or is it an implicit concession that the deterministic description fails at some scale, so we're really doing statistical mechanics over unresolved degrees of freedom somwhere below? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the whole practice, say where its parts presuppose consistent things and where they clash, and tell me what would actually have to change in forecasting if we insisted on a purely subjective, Bayesian-probability story from top to bottom.
