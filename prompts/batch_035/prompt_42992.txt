I'm putting together a feature on consciousness research for the ideas section of a general-audience magazine, and I keep tripping over the same thing: every popular account of global workspace theory reaches for the theatre. Baars, who more or less founded the view, described the workspace as a stage on which a spotlight of attention plays over a few contents at a time, with everything else waiting in the wings in the dark; Dennett had this old line about consciousness being a kind of fame in the brain — celebrity among neurons, where the widely-broadcast contents are the famous ones. Fine as journalism, but I've noticed the metaphor is doing actual argumentative work, and I want to know how much before I pass it on to readers.

Here's the work it's being made to do. The researchers I interview use the theatre/fame picture to claim that once you've explained which contents win the competition for broadcast — which ones get propagated to all the specialist modules, memory, planning, report — you've explained everything there is to explain about consciousness. The people who insist there's a further residue (the feel of it, the what-it's-like) are, on this telling, just clinging to the image of an inner spectator watching the stage, which the same researchers claim to have given up. So the analogy is simultaneously the pedagogical device and half the argument.

Before I write the piece, can you audit it for me? I'd like the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the stage, the spotlight, the audience or the public, the press, the wings, fame itself — and then the places where the mapping breaks. Some I can already half see: fame seems to presuppose an audience that already appreciates the famous thing, and in the brain it's unclear who the appreciator is without smuggling the spectator back in; a spotlight needs someone to aim it, and a theatre has seats, which is uncomfortably close to the Cartesian theatre these same people say they reject; also broadcast in the brain doesn't just inform its audience, it reconfigures the audience, which doesn't happen at the theatre. What I can't tell on my own is which of the disanalogies actually matter for the live debate — the one about whether global workspace theory is a theory of access consciousness only, and leaves phenomenal consciousness untouched (Block's distinction, which my editor wants a paragraph on) — and which are just pedantic nitpicks a friendly physicist would wave away.

So, three deliverables: (1) the table, (2) a sorting of the disanalogies into ones that bite for the access-vs-phenomenal question and ones that don't, and (3) a verdict on whether the conclusion the analogy is being used to license — nothing left to explain once you have the broadcast story — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was resting on the metaphor the whole time. And if there's a better analogy I could use in the article instead of the theatre, one that carries what's true in the view without the Cartesian baggage, I'd genuinely like to hear it — bearing in mind it has to survive a subeditor and be intelligable to someone who's never heard of any of this.
