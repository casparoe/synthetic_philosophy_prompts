I'm a retired natural scientist -- physics training, then thirty-odd years doing epidemiological modeling and risk assessment for public health bodies -- and since retiring I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole, mostly ethics. My daughter is a philosophy grad student, and we've been arguing for months about what the moral philosophers call cluelessness: the claim that we're hopeless at predicting the long-run effects of our actions, so expected-value reasoning about what to do is built on sand. Here's my position, and it's basically how we thought my whole working life: yes, the far future is chaotic, and any small act's effects eventually become untraceable -- but that's not special to ethics, it's true of every complex system I ever modeled. And in those systems we didn't throw out expected values; we treated the unmodeled part as noise, roughly symmetric around zero, and it mostly worked out. My claim is that the same move applies to moral decision-making: the unknowable knock-on effects of giving to charity versus not, or of one career versus another, may each be untraceable, but I see no reason to expect them to be systematically biased in some direction rather than churning around zero -- so best-evidence expected value still tells you what to do, same as a forecaster who can't tell you which raindrop lands where but can tell you it'll rain somewhere. If anything I'd go further: like weather-forecast calibration, you could in principle check the noise assumption at intermediate horizons against how things actually turned out, so it's not a free-floating article of faith.

So tell me what would count against this view. Give me exactly five defeaters as a numbered list, ranked most fatal first, a sentence or two each, with no introduction or conclusion. I'm especially interested in whether there are serious reasons to think the unknowable effects aren't just noise but systematically lopsided -- my daughter keeps gesturing at something like that but hasn't articulated it, and I definately want to know whether there's a real argument hiding there or just temperament. And if one of the five should be that my view has quietly become unfalsifiable -- "the noise is unbiased, whatever we happen to observe" -- say so plainly; I'd rather hear it from you then from her at Thanksgiving.
