I'm a phil mind PhD student and a friend in the neuroscience program has an argument that's been bugging me all week. It's basically a stretched version of the old Chase and Sanborn line about qualia, and I can't decide whether it's a reductio of their view or of itself. Here it is:

(1) A quale, in the strict sense, is an intrinsic, ineffable, purely private feature of experience, knowable only by undergoing it.
(2) All I ever have access to, even in introspection, is my current classification of the experience — this is the bit I like.
(3) Since the classification is the only thing that shows up, there's no way even in principal to check a classification against the quale itself; any such check would just be another classification. So no introspective report could ever be corrected.
(4) A difference that could never show up, even in principle, is not a difference any concept can mark.
(5) So in the strict sense, talk of qualia does no work — it's an idle posit.
(6) But my judgment that I'm in pain right now is also just a classification that can never be checked against what it classifies, so by the same reasoning pain-talk is idle too — and even the people who hate qualia, my friend included, think pain-talk does work.

He thinks (6) just means we should bite more bullets than anyone expected. I'd rather keep (1) and (2) and lose something later, but I honestly can't see what rejecting it would cost. Tell me which step to reject and what the rejection costs — and please flag what's actually settled among people who know this literature versus genuinely contested (like whether the Chase–Sanborn cases really show what they're advertised to show), because I don't want to walk into my supervisors office repeating something contested as if it were consensus.
