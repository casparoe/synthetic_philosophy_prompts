my bioinformatics professor keeps saying DNA is just code -- the genome is the program, ribosomes are the CPU, transcription factors are the control flow, and the cell is just dutifully executing it. she used this to argue that organisms are basically debuggable software, so once sequencing gets cheap enough we'll be able to patch a person the way you patch a buggy program.

then i got to the typogenetics part of Godel Escher Bach, where Hofstadter builds this little formal system thats supposed to be a toy version of molecular genetics: strands of letters, and "enzymes" built out of the same letters that then go and operate on other strands -- searching, cutting, inserting, switching into copy mode, etc. and what struck me is that in his system (and apparently in real cells?) the strand is simultaneously the data being copied and the program being executed, and the programs output new programs in the same alphabet that act back on the originals. so its less like a normal program and more like a self-modifying quine that keeps reaching back into its own source.

so audit the analogy my professor is using. give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the instruction set, the tape, the CPU, the compiler, the program/data line -- then lay out where the mapping breaks, and be clear about which breaks actually matter for her debugging conclusion versus which are just pedantic. i want to know esp whether the quine-ish self-reference angle changes anything: does the fact that the "program" edits itself while running mean the code picture was carrying her conclusion the whole time, or can you still debug something that rewrites itself? and if the code analogy is the wrong tool, what would you put in its place?
