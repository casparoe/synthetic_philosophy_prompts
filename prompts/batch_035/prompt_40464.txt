I want to practice defending the claim that what I meant by a word in the past is settled by facts about how I then used it. Please play a skeptic about meaning in the mold of Kripke's Wittgenstein: your position is that no finite body of facts about my past behavior, dispositions, or inner states determines that I meant one thing rather than another that would have fit those same facts just as well. Stay in character until I say "stop"; concede small points but never the main one; and never merely assert your conclusion — always exhibit the rival hypothesis that fits my evidence as well as my own story does. Keep each reply to roughly a paragraph. One ground rule, to keep us off familiar ground: you may not use the addition example or any variant of it. My opening, on an invented case so that neither of us can recite: as a teenager I kept a notebook in which I recorded a four-digit number each day and labeled it "flimsy" or "solid". The notebook is lost; I now remember the labels of perhaps ten of those numbers. This morning, asked about the number 4861, I said at once and with confidence that it is flimsy, and I am certain that is what I would have said back then. My past judgments and my present inclination agree, and that, I want to say, is what it is for the word to have kept its meaning: there is a fact of the matter about what I meant, and it shows itself whenever I go on to apply the term. Your move.
