Last night a friend and I argued at length about whether a nuclear deterrent can be rational. She held that the posture is flatly incoherent: a credible deterrent seems to require the intention to retaliate after a first strike, yet if the moment ever came, carrying out that intention would be pointless and irrational, so the state must commit itself to an irrational act in order to remain safe. I replied that the rationality attaches to maintaining the posture rather than to executing it, and gestured at Schelling's device of making retaliation partly a matter of chance, so that no one ever has to intend the fully irrational act. We got nowhere, and I have been thinking about it since.

I teach a final-year module on the theory and ethics of war, and this term I set the exam essay on the rationality of deterrence. In past years most candidates have passed by reciting Schelling, mutual assured destruction, and a few remarks about reputation, without ever confronting the structure of the problem. Please write me one essay question that cannot be answered by recitation: it should force candidates to distinguish the rationality of forming an intention from the rationality of carrying it out, to consider whether deterrence can be rescued by delegation, accidents, or the partial surrender of control, and to address the evidential puzzle of a policy whose success consists entirely in nothing happening. Then give a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth 20 marks in total, each with a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs candidates the marks, and finish with a sentence or two on what separates a first-class answer from one that merely cites the right names.
