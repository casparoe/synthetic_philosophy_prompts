I'm a journalist — mostly science and ideas, but for the last ten years or so I keep getting pulled back into commemoration stories: statue fights, street renaming petitions, school boards arguing about building names. And something's starting to bug me: the argument never moves. Like, at all. The same five moves on both sides every time, whether it's a bronze general or a street nobody thinks about twice a day. So I want a diagnosis, not another rehash, and you seem like the right person to ask.

First, the shape of the stall as I've seen it up close. The removal side says public statues and names are honors, and honoring someone who did X is wrong, so down they come. The keep side splits into two flavors: one says the achievements outweigh the wrongs, the other says commemoration isn't endorsement, it's memory — the statues are teaching aids. Then there's the contextualize camp trying to split the difference with plaques and reinterpretive panels. Fine, that's the part everyone has heard a million times, that's not what I'm asking about.

Here's the wrinkle that made me doubt the standard framing. A few years back I covered a street renaming fight — just a street, one of thousands in the city, named after a guy who was, by the standards of his day and ours, pretty bad. And I noticed the honor theory doesn't obviously fit street names at all. Nobody experiences a street name as an act of honoring anyone — a street name is closer to infrastructure, basically a file name. Yet the fight over it was at least as fierce as the statue fight, on both sides, and the contextualizers had literally nothing to offer, because you can't put a plaque on a street name. If the disagreement were really about honor or about memory-as-teaching, the street case should have collapsed into some plaque-like compromise. It didn't.

So here's my hypothesis, offered strictly as a hypothesis: the real disagreement isn't about honor or memory at all. Maybe it's about what kind of act naming and keeping is — one side sees a standing public commitment, a promise the city made, and tearing it down is a kind of default even if the promise was to a bad man, while the other sees rolling editorial judgment that has to answer to current values, like a newspaper updating its style guide. And there's possibly a second thing underneath: the two sides seem to carry different implicit reference classes — different silent lists of who is even up for reassessment, and different intuitions about how far back the reassessment reaches and, more importantly, where it stops. If the fight were about honor, the street-name case would have gone soft. It went hard, which is why I suspect something else is carrying the weight.

So my question: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A hidden factual question about what these historical figures did, a verbal one about the word "commemoration," a difference in reference class, or bedrock intuitions about the temporality of public commitments that neither side can argue for and just trades past each other? Diagnose it using what the sides actually say, not a survey of the controversies — I've sat through enough of those. And then be honest with me: given your diagnosis, is there anything that would count as progress — a distinction, a policy mechanism, a reframed question — or should I just accept that I'll be covering the identical fight in 2045? I'd genuinely like to know before I write another one of these.
