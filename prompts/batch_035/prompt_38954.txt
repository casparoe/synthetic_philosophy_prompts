I'm a second-year law student taking a jurisprudence course, and we've just finished the Hart–Fuller exchange. For my seminar presentation I want to hand the class a decision tree that sorts people into a position on what makes law valid, but I'm tired of the way this debate gets taught as a binary — positivism versus natural law — when the positions we've actually read are far more finely graded, and at least one of them, Fuller's, doesn't sit comfortably anywhere on that axis.

Here is what I want the tree to do. Every branch node must be a question someone can answer from a concrete case, without knowing which label the answer earns them; nobody should be asked whether they are a positivist, or whether morality matters to law, in the abstract. The cases should be drawn from the standard materials of the debate, all of which everyone in the seminar knows well: a validly enacted, published, prospective statute that commands something deeply unjust (the grudge-informer situation, and the Nazi statutes more generally); Fuller's fictional monarch who tries and tries to make law and keeps failing, in one version after another, through retroactivity, secrecy, contradiction, constant amendment, and so on; a substantively benign rule produced by a formally flawless process; and a substantively benign rule that is secret, or retroactive, or flatly contradicts another rule already on the books. The tree should carve the logical space from these cases first — does a statute's status as law depend at all on its content, and if so on its substantive justice or on its observance of Fuller's procedural desiderata; if not, whether moral criteria are merely permitted as tests of validity or are ruled out altogether — and only then locate the named positions as leaves: the strong natural-law view that a gravely unjust enactment is not law at all; the softer view that it is law, but defective law, law in a degenerate sense, or law that loses its claim to obedience; Fuller's claim that gross procedural failure defeats legality even when the content is benign; the inclusive or soft positivism Hart settled on, where moral criteria can figure among a system's criteria of validity; and the exclusive positivism associated with Raz, where they cannot. Please also include the unoccupied combinations of answers as leaves, marked unnamed, with one sentence each on whether nobody holds them because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Finally, be honest about the separations the tree cannot achieve by cases alone. My suspicion is that inclusive positivism and Fuller's position receive the same answers at every case node, since their real disagreement is about whether the procedural desiderata are conceptually necessary for legality rather than merely present in any decent legal system — and if that is right, say where a tree built purely from cases runs out, and what further question would have to be asked to split that pair. Keep the whole thing to something that fits on a single handout page. Please end your reply with a last line of the form "Summary: ..." containing exactly one sentence stating the deepest fork in your tree and the two largest classes of leaves it separates.
