I design narrative systems for story-driven video games, and I've come to suspect that my industry quietly builds rival metaphysics of time into its mechanics without ever saying so. Games with save-anywhere design let the player rewind to a checkpoint and try again, discarding the failed branch as though it never happened; roguelikes with permadeath do the opposite, making every run irreversible and wiping progress at death; and games with multiple endings typically add a completion tracker that treats every branch as equally real and expects the player to live through all of them. That last practice is closer than it sounds to Borges's forking-paths novel: in The Garden of Forking Paths, the fictional author Ts'ui Pên leaves behind a book that is itself a labyrinth, and its secret is that every possible outcome of each event occurs, each in its own diverging timeline, so that no branch is ever eliminated. Yet the same Borges, in A New Refutation of Time, borrowed Berkeley's and Hume's idealist arguments about the mind-dependence of things and concluded that if only momentary experiences exist, a flowing, connected time is an illusion — a conclusion he ended by cheerfully accepting anyway, on the ground that time is what a self is made of. So what I want to know is what each of our three design practices presupposes about time, choice, and personal identity. Does save-and-load presuppose a single privileged timeline, and if so, what becomes of the character who dies in a discarded branch? Does permadeath presuppose something Borges's refutation would deny, or only something modest about stakes and loss? And is the completionist branching game genuinely committed to the picture in which all outcomes happen, or merely to something weaker about content consumption? Please distill your answer into a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: one item for the implicit view behind save-and-load, one for permadeath, one for the all-branches-are-real design, one saying which rival views each practice strictly rules out versus merely fits with, and one on how the three together do or do not cohere with Borges's two opposed pictures of time.
