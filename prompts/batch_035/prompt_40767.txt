I'm assembling material for an undergraduate seminar on theories of well-being. We spend a week on hedonism and Nozick's experience machine before moving on to desire-satisfaction and objective-list accounts, and for the final session I'd like to hand out something lighter that still does real philosophical work — a piece that makes the students laugh and then makes them realize they can't quite say why it's wrong.

I'd like you to write a mock product recall notice, roughly 600–700 words, issued by the fictional manufacturer of a commercialized experience machine (invent a corporate name you find funny — something bland and reassuring). The premise of the recall: the machines are being recalled not because they malfunction, but because of an embarrassing market-research failure. The company's founding premise, inherited from a certain Harvard philosopher, was that no customer would actually want to stay plugged in once they understood what was on offer; the recall concerns the growing number of customers who decline to come out, and the notices, complaint lines, and escalations this has produced. Keep the register deadpan corporate throughout — serial numbers, affected models, remedy options, an apology from the executive team — and let the humor come from the actual philosophy rather than from wackiness. The standard anti-hedonist points (that we want to do certain things and not merely have the experience of doing them; that we want to be a certain kind of person; that we want contact with reality) should appear in the notice as though they were product features the engineering team failed to implement, with the executives genuinely, sincerely baffled by them.

Two things I'd particularly like worked in. Somewhere in the fine print, a nod to the engineers' workaround for customers who report "a residual preference for authenticity" — an optional second machine that simulates being the sort of person who would never plug in, which the company markets as resolving the complaint without conceding anything. And a remedy section that quietly gives away the whole thought experiment — the corporation offering customers exactly what the argument says we want, while insisting, in the same breath, that the recall is purely precautionary and reflects no judgment about the merits of the product. The piece should be funny to someone who knows the well-being literature and merely unsettling to someone who doesn't. Please avoid naming Nozick or any living scholar; the target is the view and its commodification, not a person.
