As a science and ideas correspondent, I'm putting together an interactive feature pegged to a question my reporting keeps surfacing: how much should we let other people do our moral thinking for us? The hook is contemporary — ethics boards blessing new biotech, ethics teams inside AI companies, activists urging those with less lived experience to defer, and now chatbots handing out moral advice on demand — but the underlying debate in philosophy is older and more granular than most readers will know: whether moral knowledge can be transmitted through testimony at all, and if so, in what way. The working title is "How much of a moral deferencer are you really?" — and the conceit is that the quiz should sort readers by what they actually say about concrete cases, not by which label they'd pick for themselves, since almost nobody enters the piece knowing whether they're a "moderate pessimist" about moral testimony and nobody should have to in order to find out where they land.

I'd like you to design the diagnostic instrument, and I want it built from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Start with the splitting questions — e.g., whether testimony can ever transmit moral knowledge, whether the problem is with the knowledge itself or only with the understanding that a morally good agent needs, whether justification flows through evidence of the speaker's reliability or through some more direct entitlement to trust, whether deference is ever appropriate even when it substitutes for judgment one could form oneself — and only afterwards locate the actual positions in the resulting space of answer combinations. I need these to appear as leaves: the strong pessimist view that moral testimony cannot pass on moral knowledge; the moderate pessimist view that it can pass on knowledge but not moral understanding; reductionist optimism, on which testimonial moral belief is justified through background evidence that the speaker is a reliable or trustworthy moral judge; and non-reductionist optimism, on which moral testimony works much like testimony about the weather and doesn't need to be earned in that way. If there is a natural fifth leaf — some view about moral deference being fine for conclusions but not for the deliberation itself, or a hybrid — include it; if a plausible combination of your splitting questions has no occupant, say so and diagnose why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored).

The scenarios should be doing the real work, so please use at least these kinds of cases, adapted as you see fit: a trusted colleague who has looked into factory farming tells you it's seriously wrong and you adopt the belief without investigating; a member of a marginalized group testifies about an injustice they've lived through and you take their word against your own prior sense that nothing much was happening; someone in the grip of grief or rage outsources a moral verdict to a level-headed friend; an expert committee issues a considered moral verdict on a policy question outside your competence; and a person who once had the understanding asks a friend to supply the conclusion because they've forgotten the grounds. Each question should be answerable by a reader who has never read a word of epistemology — no question may be allowed to trade on a term of art.

Finally, flag the pairs your instrument cannot cleanly separate — I suspect the moderate pessimist and the reductionist optimist will agree on almost every ordinary case, and I'd rather the quiz admit that than paper over it.

For format, please return a JSON object with the following schema. Top-level fields: "intro" (a short paragraph, plain prose, suitable to sit above the quiz on our site); "logical_space" (an array of objects, each with "question" — the splitting claim, phrased neutrally — and "answers" — the two or three answers the question allows); "questions" (an array of the quiz's diagnostic items, eight to ten of them, each with "id", "scenario", "options" — two or three, each with "text" and "sorts_toward" naming the leaf or leaves it favors and "why" in one sentence); "leaves" (an array, each with "name_or_unnamed", "one_line_description", "awkward_commitment", and "reached_via" describing the answer path); "unoccupied" (an array of combinations nobody holds, each with "combination" and "diagnosis"); and "cannot_separate" (an array of leaf pairs the instrument fails to distinguish, each with "pair" and "why" in one or two sentences). Keep all strings free of jargon except in "name_or_unnamed", "why", and "diagnosis", where the philosophy can be spoken plainly.
