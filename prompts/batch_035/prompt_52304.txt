I hold that the Allais paradox leaves expected utility theory normatively intact: the independence axiom is a genuine requirement of rationality, and the preferences it rules out — Allais-type preferences — are mistakes, perhaps systematic and even ineradicable ones, rather than deep counterexamples. Behavioral evidence of widespread violations therefore does not touch the view, and I am not much moved by the descriptive replacements for expected utility that were built to accommodate those violations, since I take the question to be what agents are rationally required to prefer, not what they do prefer.

Tell me what would actually count against this position. In particular, I would like the following:

1. A map of where the normative case for independence is supposed to get its force. The standard arguments — the sure-thing or dominance reasoning, and the constructions purporting to show that a violator can be led, through a sequence of choices each individually acceptable to them, into an outcome they would rank strictly below where they started — carry auxiliary premises: that the value of an act depends only on its consequences and their probabilities, that outcomes can be re-described across contexts without change in value, that a two-stage lottery is indifferent to its one-stage reduction. Identify precisely which premises do the work, and where critics charge that the argument builds its conclusion into its premises (for instance, by stipulating that the common consequence must be irrelevant simply because it is common).

2. An honest verdict on the practical-threat versions of the argument. If the constructions purporting to trap the Allais chooser require re-loading or re-framing options between choices, or require the agent to tolerate the same lottery being presented in a way that hides its structure, how much of a threat do they remain? Is there anything here with the force of a true Dutch book, or does the comparison to money-pump arguments for transitivity reveal a disanalogy?

3. An assessment of the insulation worry. If my position can always say that an observed Allais-type preference is a competence error rather than a rational insight, and can always retreat to what a fully reflective agent would prefer, what is left of the normative claim? At what point does this retreat become a vice — and is the standard of reflective coherence doing all the work, so that the axiom is normative only relative to it?

4. Serious consideration of the thought that the best defeater is not better behavioral evidence but a rival theory of what counts as an outcome. If regret, disappointment, or elation are admitted as constituents of outcomes in their own right, the Allais preferences cease to violate even a weakened independence axiom: the two lotteries no longer share a genuinely common consequence, since the common money amount is accompanied by different emotions depending on the alternative foregone. Does this count against my position, or is it mere re-labeling that trivializes the axiom? Is there a principled line between enriching the outcome space and gaming it — and on which side of that line do things like regret fall?

5. Attention to the internal geometry of the violations: the common-consequence and common-ratio versions of the paradox, and the fact that in some experimental settings the common-ratio pattern reverses direction. If the same structural preference produces violations of independence in both directions across contexts, does that tell in favor of treating them as a unified deep datum about value, or in favor of treating them as artifacts of framing that a normative theory is entitled to discount?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Where defenders of normative expected utility have themselves conceded that at least some Allais-type preferences are rationally permissible rather than mistaken, take those concessions seriously rather than attributing them to confusion. I would also like an explicit verdict on whether my position, as I have framed it, has been qualified into something close to unfalsifiability — and whether, for a normative claim rather than an empirical one, that would even be a defect.
