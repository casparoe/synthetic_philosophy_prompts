The Lucas–Penrose debate over Gödelian arguments against mechanism has now run for well over half a century: Lucas's original paper in the early 1960s, Penrose's two books from around 1990, and each round of objections and replies since, without either side conceding anything. The pattern is strikingly stable. The anti-mechanist claims that if I were a Turing machine T whose arithmetical output I could in principle identify, I could out-prove T by asserting its Gödel sentence, since I can see that T is sound even if T cannot. The mechanist replies that this fails either because I might be inconsistent, so my "seeing" proves nothing, or because I cannot, if I am T, know that I am sound — since no consistent system knows its own soundness. Penrose then offers a refined version meant to escape that reply, and his critics reply that it only escapes by smuggling in what it was supposed to establish. And there it sits. My working hypothesis is that the standoff is not really about Gödel's theorem, which both sides use correctly, but about the epistemic status of self-ascriptions of consistency and soundness: whether an idealized mathematician's confidence that she is not wrong about arithmetic is the kind of thing that can in principle be knowledge, or whether it must collapse into mere belief. But I am not sure whether that makes the disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of the two sides idealizing the human in different ways and thereby answering different questions. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each side actually claims, and tell me whether anything — a result in proof theory, a distinction between knowing a system sound and merely trusting it, or a change in the question — could move it, or whether we should expect the debate to stay exactly where it is, and why.
