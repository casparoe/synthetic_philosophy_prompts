I am planning a long-form book on whether historical atrocities can meaningfully be compared or ranked — whether such comparison is a legitimate intellectual enterprise, a practical necessity (for courts, museums, and assigning limited memorial resources), or an obscene exercise in moral bookkeeping. I can write full-time for about three years.

Among the open conceptual questions in this area, which matter most for such a project, and which are overrated or dead ends? Candidates I am aware of include: whether atrocities are incommensurable in principle or merely difficult to compare; whether the standard axes of comparison (scale, intent, uniqueness of mechanism, historical precedent) can be defended against the charge of arbitrariness; whether claims of uniqueness function as defensible description or as rhetorical positioning in competitive memory politics; and whether the harms of comparison (perceived minimization of some victims, the "oppression Olympics" dynamic) are intrinsic to ranking itself or only to particular careless ways of doing it. But I am open to questions I have not listed.

Please defend a prioritized ordering: for each question you rank highly, explain why it is upstream of the others, what makes it tractable for a philosopher working alone rather than an archival historian, and what would make you move it up or down the list. I care more about the reasoning behind the agenda than about the list itself.
