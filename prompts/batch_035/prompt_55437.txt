I teach a third-year metaphysics of mind course at a UK university and I'm setting the summer exam. I want one essay question on the combination problem for panpsychism, and I want it written so it cant be answered by reciting labels. My students have memorised the vocabulary -- subject-summing, structural mismatch, brute constitution -- but when I poke at it in seminars it turns out many of them think the combination problem is just "it sounds weird that little experiences add up to a big one," and no, thats not it. The question has to force them to say what actually makes the problem a problem, and then do something with that.

Here's the framing I want the question built around. In lectures I developed an analogy I've been calling the committee picture: maybe micro-subjects dont fuse into a macro-subject the way streams merge into a river; maybe the macro-subject is instead constituted by the micro-subjects the way a committee is constituted by its members -- nothing new exists over and above the members organised in a certain way, and yet it's still true, apparently, that the committee reached a decision that no individual member reached. So the exam question should put this picture to the students as a proposed escape route from the combination problem and ask them to evaluate it: does it dissolve the problem, or does it just relocate it one level down?

Good answers will have to engage with things like: whether the committee's "decision" is really just a shorthand for facts about the members plus how they're causally organised, and whether phenomenal consciousness could be shorthand in that way given that the whole pitch of panpsychism is that micro-experience is categorical and qualitative rather than structural; whether saying the macro-subject is "nothing over and above" the organised micro-subjects is compatible with those micro-subjects having genuinely qualitative points of view; and what happens to the causal completeness of physics if my experience of tasting coffee is nothing over and above a swarm of micro-experiences -- do the micro-experiences do anything, or are they epiphenomenal passengers riding on the micro-physics? I'd also be happy if a strong answer brought in the point about introspective acquaintance: my experience seems presented to me as a unified whole, and it's not obvious a committee has anything like that.

Deliverables please: the exam question itself, answerable in 90 minutes, worth 25 marks total; a model answer of roughly 700 words; and a marking scheme that splits the 25 marks across five must-hit points, each worth 5, with a one-line note per point on the mistake students who miss it typically make. Also please flag explicitly one or two things students will *think* earn marks but shouldn't -- e.g. name-dropping philosophers without using them, or dragging in the knowledge argument from week 4 where it does no work. The question and everything else should be answerable from a general grasp of the combination problem literature, so no citations, no page numbers, no "as Goff argues in..." -- I dont want them rewarded for quote-hunting, and the external examiner has been on me about that anyway.

Everything in British English please, since its a UK course and the external examiner checks for consistency. Thanks so much!
