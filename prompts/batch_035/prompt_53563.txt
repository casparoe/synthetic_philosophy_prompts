I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so forgive me if I'm using terms loosely. I recently encountered Daniel Dennett's idea of "free-floating rationales" — the claim that there can be reasons without reasoners, that the rationale for some feature of an organism exists (and can even be discovered) even though nobody ever thought it, expressed it, or had it in mind. So the rationale behind a wood frog's ability to survive a winter frozen solid is real and explanatory, even though no frog, gene, or deity ever formulated it. The reason floats free of any reasoner.

For my day job I maintain a large legacy codebase that no one fully understands: essentially no comments, the original developers left long ago, and the system has accreted through years of patches, each written by someone staring at a local problem. My teammates and I spend a lot of time asking "what is this for?" about functions that nobody alive can explain, and we notice something that struck me as connected to Dennett: the question usually has a good answer even though the answer was never written down anywhere — and, we sometimes suspect, was never consciously held by anyone at all. The design rationale lives in the code and the commit history, not in any single head.

I've started telling people that an evolved organism is like a legacy codebase with no comments and, eventually, no authors: in both cases you can do a kind of reverse engineering that recovers reasons without ever interviewing a reasoner. But the more I lean on the analogy, the more I want to know how much weight it will actually bear. So here is my ask.

Audit the analogy for me. First, give me an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of comments and design docs (explicit, codified reasons), of the original authors, of code review, of the commit history, of refactoring, of dead code. Second, tell me where the mapping breaks, and — this is the part I care most about — which breakages matter for the use I'm putting it to. I can already see one candidate problem: in my codebase there were, in the end, designers — minds with intentions — so "what was this for?" is at bottom a question about what somebody wanted, whereas Dennett's whole point seems to be that the rationale floats free of any mind whatsoever. Is that a fatal disanalogy, or is it only a difference of degree? After all, much of our code's "purpose" arguably never existed in a head either, only in a diff someone made under deadline pressure without understanding the whole. Relatedly: natural selection has no forward-looking intentions, but my predecessors did — does that import something the analogy shouldn't (a designer's purpose lurking behind every function), or does the sheer dilution of authorship over years of patching mean the biological case is continuous with the software one rather than categorically different?

Third, some specific stress tests I'd like your judgment on: dead code versus vestigial organs and spandrels (code that survives because nobody bothered deleting it versus traits that are byproducts, with no rationale to recover at all); the fact that I can eventually find a surviving author or an old Slack thread, whereas evolution keeps no logs; and the fact that code has a canonical, readable source while an organism's "source" is a gene plus a developmental context, so "reading off" the rationale may not be reading anything.

Finally, tell me whether the practical conclusion I've been drawing survives the audit. I've been claiming two things: (1) in both cases, "why is this here?" is a legitimate question whose answer explains the feature without attributing anyone's intent; and (2) in both cases the method for answering it is fundamentally the same — delete or disable the thing and see what breaks, which is what we do with legacy code and, as I understand it, is roughly what knockout experiments do in biology. If the analogy collapses somewhere, does either of those conclusions collapse with it, or are they the parts that survive the disanalogies?
