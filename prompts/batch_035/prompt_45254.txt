I'm a third-year student in a phil of mind course and we just wrapped up the unit on pain, and now the seminar has split into two camps that have been arguing for weeks and its going nowehere. Camp A (the "affect" camp) thinks pain's badness lives in the phenomenal character itself — what makes pain bad is just the felt unpleasantness, full stop. On their view pain asymbolia doesn't threaten this, because if you strip the affect off pain you've changed the feel: what the asymbolics have isn't full pain anymore. Camp B (the "separatists") thinks asymbolia is a clean dissociation: the sensory feel stays, the badness goes, so the badness was never part of the feel to begin with — either it's a separate distress response layered on top, or (on the more imperativist-flavored end of that camp) the badness is in what pain *does*, i.e. the command to get your body out of trouble, not in how it feels.

Our prof got tired of the shouting matches and suggested that for the final we produce an adversarial-collaboration style joint statement — the kind where two opposing sides write down what they actually agree on, state the residual disagreement precisely enough that each side would sign its own formulation, and pre-commit to what evidence would move them.

Can you draft that document for our group? Specifically:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps could genuinely sign — covering what asymbolia patients do and report (they localize stimuli, tell sharp from dull, say "yes it hurts" when asked, but don't withdraw, don't grimace, sometimes seem amused, keep engaging with the painful stimulus). Be careful nothing in here is slanted toward either camp.

2. A disagreements section, with each disagreement phrased so that the camp holding it would accept the wording — not a strawman version.

3. The cruxes. I suspect one is whether asymbolics retain the full sensory-discriminative character of pain or whether their "it hurts" reports are somehow blunted or confabulated, and another is what "badness" even means in this debate — whether it means felt unpleasantness, or something like "gives the subject a reason to act/avoid." If you see other cruxes, name them.

4. For each crux, say what evidence or argument both camps would agree bears on it, and write down what each camp would predict. E.g. for the "do they really feel it" crux, both camps should be able to agree on what careful psychophysical testing (matching intensities, discriminating qualities) of asymbolics would show, and then disagree about what result would come in.

5. One more thing the prof wants addressed: is any part of this dispute just verbal? Like maybe the camps mean different things by "pain" — one meaning the felt quality, one meaning the felt-quality-plus-distress package. If part of it is verbal, say which part and why; if part isn't, say what the real residue is.

Also, if it helps, factor in something our TA raised: a hypothetical "asymbolia pill" that leaves you the sensory feel of pain but strips the distress — say you take it before running a marathon. The camp A people in my class said they wouldn't take it because the feel alone is bad regardless of the distress; the camp B people said they'd take it like a lighter version of an analgesic. Not sure if that's a genuine crux or just noise, but it made people genuinely mad so it's probably doing some work — you decide whether it belongs in the document.

No citations or reference lists needed btw, we just want the claims and structure. It should read as a document both camps could actually put their names to.
