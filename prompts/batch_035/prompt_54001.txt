I am a chemist by training — thirty-five years in thermodynamics and calorimetry, a field whose entire method is the reduction of heterogeneous processes to a single currency of joules. Since retiring I have been reading ethics seriously for the first time, and I have been startled to find that the classical utilitarian tradition appears to treat pleasure and pain exactly as my discipline treats heat evolved and heat absorbed: as signed entries in one ledger, summed without remainder. I want to find out whether I actually accept that, and I want an instrument that would do the same for the other members of my monthly discussion group, most of us retired academics from the sciences.

Please design a decision tree — a taxonomy would serve equally well, provided every branch point is labeled by a question — that sorts positions on the commensurability of pleasure and pain: the question of when, if ever, quantities of the one may properly be set against quantities of the other, including the negative-utilitarian and lexical-threshold views that restrict or forbid such trade-offs.

I ask that the tree be built from the logical space rather than from a list of names, and that the following conditions hold.

First, the root assumption — that pleasure and pain are magnitudes of a single signed quantity — must appear as a node to be answered like any other, not as the default from which the other positions are described as departures. Here I must confess my own suspicion, and I ask that you treat it as a live position rather than as the naivety of a newcomer: my career taught me that cases of apparent summability sometimes dissolve on inspection, and I have begun to wonder whether disvaluing suffering and valuing enjoyment are less like opposite signs on one axis and more like quantities that merely look comparable from a distance. If that suspicion corresponds to an occupied position in the literature, place it on the tree; if it corresponds to nothing anyone holds, say so plainly rather than inventing a leaf for my benefit.

Second, the internal nodes should be concrete cases that a member of my group could answer without knowing any of the position labels, and they must include: a case trading a brief but severe pain against a very large number of small pleasures distributed across many persons; a case trading many mild discomforts spread over a population against a single instance of torture; and a case concerning the addition of lives, namely whether adding a contented life to a world, other things equal, makes that world better.

Third, the leaves should locate, wherever the logic of your tree actually permits: the classical single-scale view; the weak asymmetry, on which relieving suffering counts for more than adding pleasure but both count; the lexical-threshold view, on which some pains no quantity of pleasure can outweigh; the strict asymmetry, on which adding pleasure creates no value at all though adding pain creates disvalue; the strong negative view, on which positive states have no value whatsoever; and any further leaves the combinations generate that you think deserve names.

Fourth, flag any pair of positions your tree cannot genuinely separate, and mark unoccupied combinations with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether nobody holds them because they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Finally, attach one sentence to each leaf stating the least comfortable commitment that comes with landing there, so that a member of my group who arrives at a position knows what it will cost her.

Please write the answer in American English.
