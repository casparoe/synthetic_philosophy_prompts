I want a single number from you: your credence that an ideally rational agent must have sharp credences — one exact real-valued probability for every proposition it considers — rather than imprecise ones representable as a set of probability functions. I realize the irony of demanding a sharp answer to this particular question, and part of what I want is your view on whether the irony is substantive: if your honest attitude toward the question is itself better represented by an interval, say so, but still commit to a number, and tell me whether you take that very ability to sharpen on demand as evidence that sharpening is always available.

Please decompose the credence into at least the following terms, adding or reweighting as you see fit, with a probability and a one-line justification for each:

1. That there are genuine epistemic situations — sparse or elusive evidence, expert disagreement, a coin of unknown bias freshly minted — where any particular sharp credence is arbitrary in a way that matters normatively rather than in some harmless way, such that an interval is the better representation of what the evidence supports.
2. That the usual decision-theoretic costs of sharpness are real: that a sharp agent is rationally required to have exact opinions on, and in some versions to accept bets about, propositions far beyond its evidence, in a way no ideally rational agent should.
3. That the usual costs of imprecision are not fatal: that belief inertia and dilation are artifacts of the set-of-probabilities representation or of a particular choice of decision rule, rather than deep problems that no redesign of the imprecise framework could fix — roughly the territory of the exchange between White's dilation-based objections and Joyce's defense.
4. That imprecise credences are not reducible to sharp higher-order credences — that the imprecise model is a genuine rival to the precise one rather than a redescription of a precise agent unsure which precise agent to be.
5. Whatever further terms belong — for instance, whether "ideal rationality" is even the right target, such that the whole debate could be dissolved by distinguishing ideal from bounded agents.

Then combine the terms into your final number, show the arithmetic, and tell me which single term that number is most sensitive to, and what argument or kind of case would move it the most if you encountered it.
