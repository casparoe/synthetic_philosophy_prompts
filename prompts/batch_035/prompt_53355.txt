I've been obsessed with a claim I defended in a term essay for a nonlinear dynamics course, and I'd like to stress-test it properly. The claim is that in a fully deterministic but chaotic system, long-range prediction is impossible in principle, not merely in practice. My argument went roughly like this: with a positive Lyapunov exponent, the error in an initial condition grows exponentially, so the predictable horizon grows only logarithmically with the precision of one's measurements. Since any physical predictor must store and measure finite information — and if you push on it, quantum limits put a floor on measurement resolution — no agent inside the universe can ever extend the horizon beyond some finite bound, no matter how good its technology or its theory. So determinism, far from licensing prediction, coexists with a hard in-principle barrier to it, and Laplace's demon turns out to be an incoherent idealization rather than a merely unachievable one. I've since noticed that versions of this thought show up in popular science everywhere, usually stated in one breath and never defended, which makes me suspicious of my own confidence.

Tell me what would count against the position. I don't mean the boring observation that weather models have gotten somewhat better; I mean things that would genuinely force a retreat. For instance, I've gathered there are results in dynamical systems theory suggesting that approximate trajectories can "shadow" true ones in some sense even when pointwise prediction fails, and I don't understand whether that rescues predictability or just changes what we mean by it. There's also the obvious move that ensembles and statistical forecasts — probability distributions over future states, climatological averages, properties of the attractor rather than the exact state — are still predictions, and they seem to work at horizons where pointwise prediction has long since died. If I respond by restricting my thesis to pointwise trajectory prediction, has it been qualified into triviality? And a subtler worry: my argument leans on quantum limits to initial-condition precision, but quantum mechanics is itself indeterministic, so I may be borrowing a result that quietly undermines the deterministic setting the thesis was supposed to be about, making the classical-chaos barrier real but redundant.

I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with a note on which ones a serious defender of the position would concede as counting against it versus dismiss as equivocation over "prediction." Please distinguish carefully between in-practice difficulty and in-principle impossibility throughout, since that distinction is the whole content of my thesis. Finally, give me an honest verdict on whether the position can be insulated by redefinition — for instance by letting "prediction" mean whatever the predictor happens to achieve — and, if it can, whether that kind of insulation is discreditable for a thesis of this sort or just reflects that it's a conceptual claim rather than an empirical one.
