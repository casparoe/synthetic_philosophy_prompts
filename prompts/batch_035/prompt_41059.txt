Before I turn my undergraduate seminar loose on the primary literature on dreaming, I want to walk in with a specification rather than a survey. The course is built around a deceptively simple question — are dreams experiences that sleepers have while asleep? — and I have become convinced that the standard positions in that debate each quietly measure themselves against a different list of requirements. So what I want from you is the list itself: what any adequate theory of dreaming has to deliver. I would expect it to include, at minimum, some account of the evidential standing of dream reports (which are, after all, our only window onto the phenomena), consistency with what the sleep laboratories tell us about REM and NREM sleep and the physiological timing of dream onset, some treatment of the temporality of dreams (the dream that seems to have lasted for hours, reported by a subject woken from a stage of sleep that lasted minutes), a principled answer to what lucid dreaming and the eye-movement signaling experiments do to the older orthodoxy that the sleeper is never aware of dreaming, and a position on how the theory feeds into the Cartesian use of dreams as a skeptical scenario. But I am handing you that list as raw material, not as settled — part of what I want is a judgment about which of the demands people actually make on theories of dreaming are genuine desiderata and which are prejudices of one camp dressed up as requirements. Malcolm's verificationist insistence that dream reports are absolutely incorrigible, for instance, looks less like a neutral constraint and more like a philosopher's theory of meaning doing all the work, and I would want you to say so if you agree, or defend its status as a requirement if you do not.

For each desideratum, give the motivating case — the phenomenon, the datum, or the philosophical payoff that makes it non-negotiable — and mark clearly which requirements you take to be joint satisfiable and which, if any, cannot be satisfied together. If there is a subset of the list that no theory could meet simultaneously, say so plainly, because that result would matter more to my seminar than any scoring of the contenders. In particular, I want you to consider whether the demand that a theory respect the phenomenology of dream temporality can coexist with the demand that it be consistent with the physiological data, or whether the apparent conflict there is an artifact of assuming a naive picture of how dream content is represented in memory. Relatedly: should a theory of dreaming have to explain why dream reports are so strikingly less stable than waking reports — why subjects interviewed hours later remember differently than they did minutes after waking — or is that a question about memory, properly speaking, that a theory of dreaming can legitimately outsource? I am honestly unsure whether that is a desideratum or a boundary-drawing issue, and I would like your view.

Finally, and only briefly — the criteria are the part I care about, since I will do the scoring in class — run three contenders against the list: the received view that dreams are experiences had during sleep and reported on waking; Malcolm's view that the very idea of experiences during dreamless sleep fails to make sense; and Dennett's proposal that dream content is composed and loaded into memory in the moments around waking rather than experienced in real time. I am less interested in which of them wins than in which single desideratum does the most to exclude candidates, and whether the requirement that does the most work is one that any theory was entitled to impose in the first place. If, as I half suspect, the list cannot be completed without already taking a side on the experience question, tell me that too — a specification that presupposes its own answer would be worth knowing about before my students inherit it as if it were neutral ground.
