Is there a name for this style of argument? I keep running into it and it's bugging me that I can't label it. The pattern is: start from something the other guy can't deny without talking nonsense — that they're having experiences, that they successfully mean things by their words, that they can tell their experiences come one after another in time — and then argue that this thing they can't deny would be impossible unless some further, much more controversial claim were true, so the controversial claim gets to piggyback to victory. Like: you can't fix a word's meaning by pointing privately at a mental image, because nothing could ever distinguish your having applied it correctly from your merely feeling that you did, so meaning must be something publicly checkable. Or: to even notice that your own experiences succeed one another you need to locate them against something relatively permanent, and your inner states can't be that, so there must be stable objects outside your mind. There's also a standard comeback I've seen to arguments of this shape, roughly: even if we can't make sense of doubting Y without presupposing Y, that only shows something about us, namely that we're built to think in Y terms — it doesn't show Y is true, and the skeptic can just say we're stuck with a framework that misdescribes things. So, questions: does the argument form itself have an established name (if it does I'd expect it to be old), is there a standard taxonomy of its variants, and is that comeback considered fatal or have people actually tried to answer it — and if so, does any of the answering work, or is it just insisting louder that the skeptic is committed to the presupposition? I don't just want a one-word label, I want to know what exactly counts as an instance of this and where the edges are, since half the arguments I see called by whatever this name is don't actually move from indubitable starting points at all.
