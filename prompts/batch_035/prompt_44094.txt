I've been keeping a list of people whose lives I have gut feelings about, and I want to know if there's a principle behind my verdicts, because right now they dont hang together. My great-aunt, 88, widowed, no ambitions left, spends her days in the garden with weak tea watching the birds, says she's content: that life goes well, I'm confident about that, possibly better than mine. A friend who's a climber, on the other hand, craves the next route constantly, suffers through most of her training, is briefly elated at the summit, then back to craving the next one: also a good life I think, maybe better than the aunt's. And this is where I start worrying, because I've run into the view (I think people call it tranquilism?) that only the absence of craving and suffering matters for how well a life goes, and on that view the climber's life looks mediocre at best, while the aunt's looks perfect. Then theres the guy from my dorm who smoked weed all day for four years: no suffering to speak of, no wanting anything, mild constant contentment, and my verdict is that his life did NOT go well — but structurally it looks a lot like my great-aunt's, free of craving and pain, and I cant see what separates them except that she had a whole life of wanting behind her that wore itself out, while his never got started. Also my sister with two small kids: exhausted, cranky, craves sleep, hasn't had an uninterrupted night in years, and I'd still say her life is going well, which tranquilism definitely can't say. And the case I go back and forth on: an old colleague who quit a career she cared about to live alone in a cabin, no plans, very calm, and I can't decide whether that's my aunt's life or the stoner's. So, question: is there one principle that gets all these verdicts, or close to it? My hunch is that what separates the good calm lives from the bad ones is whether the absence of craving came at the end of engagement or instead of it, but I dont know if that hunch can be stated precisely, what it predicts on new cases, or which of my verdicts it would have to overturn — please be honest about that last part rather than quietly fudging it. Also, since I'm new to this literature, keep separate what's actually settled among people who study well-being from what's genuinely contested up to and including whether tranquilism is even a live option; dont hand me a contested theory as the consensus. And write in continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, like a long email.
