In my metaphysics course we're about to start Parfit, and my roommate, who took the same course last year, tried to give me a head start: Parfit's teletransporter case, he says, shows that personal identity just is psychological continuity, so the person who steps out of the machine on Mars with all your memories is literally you — teleportation is travel, not death. From this it follows, he says, that in the fission case, where your brain hemispheres are transplanted into two different bodies, you survive as both of the resulting people, and since that sounds absurd, Parfit concluded that there is no fact of the matter about personal identity at all. And because identity is not what matters, my roommate adds, Parfit thought our ordinary concern for our own futures is irrational — we should be about as concerned for our future selves as for anyone else's. Some of this sounds off to me: I thought Parfit was careful to distinguish identity from what matters, and that his relation was meant to be more fine-grained than just shared memories. Which parts of this summary are accurate, which conflate or overstate what Parfit actually argued, and does my roommate's final conclusion follow from whichever parts survive scrutiny? I'd also like to know whether it's fair to call Parfit an eliminativist about persons, as my roommate did, or whether that label belongs to a different position.
