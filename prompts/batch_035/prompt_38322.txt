I've recently been appointed to the governance council of a mid-sized decentralized organization that is seriously weighing a transition to futarchy — Robin Hanson's scheme in which voting determines ends (which national-metric-style variable we maximize) while prediction markets determine means (which proposed policies are expected to raise the variable). We have roughly eight thousand token holders, a treasury of about forty million dollars, and a commitment from two market-making firms to provide liquidity if we go ahead. Before we commit, I've been asked to write a memo identifying which unresolved conceptual problems in futarchy most need to be settled, or at least stress-tested, for an organization of our size, and in what order we should tackle them. The engineering side is comparatively easy for us; it's the political economy and epistemology of the scheme that keeps me up at night.

The obvious candidates keep circling back to a few clusters: the metric-selection problem (whatever variable we vote on becomes a target for Goodhart-style gaming, and our candidates — a synthetic index of token price, treasury growth, and some measure of ecosystem activity — each seem corruptible in different ways); the structure of conditional markets (policies whose effects take years to show up force us to resolve counterfactual questions like what the metric would have been had the rejected policy been adopted, and it's not clear anyone can be made to trade honestly under that resolution rule); and what I think of as the outside-the-market stakes problem — a whale whose fortune depends on the decision being adopted, not on the metric later being high, has an incentive to subsidize distorted prices purely to get the policy through, which arguably breaks the usual argument that manipulation is self-defeating because informed traders profit from it. There's also the worry that turnout on the values-vote will be so thin and so correlated with short-term token price preferences that the "vote on values" half quietly collapses into the same speculative dynamics we were trying to insulate policy from.

Please give me a defended prioritization: which of these (or of open questions you think I've missed) is genuinely upstream of the others for a pilot at our scale, which are tractable through design choices we could actually make in the next six months rather than requiring decades of institutional experimentation, and which are overrated in the sense that we could run a small futarchic layer — say, markets that only veto or delay spending above a threshold, rather than setting policy outright — without solving them at all. I'd like your reasoning about the dependencies between the problems, not just a list: if, for instance, you think the counterfactual-resolution issue poisons everything downstream of it, say why, and tell me what evidence from the first year of the pilot would move an item up or down your list.
