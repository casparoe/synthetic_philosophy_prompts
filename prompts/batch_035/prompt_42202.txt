I am a physicist by training — thirty-two years in experimental condensed-matter work before retirement — and have spent the last three years reading philosophy of mind as a determined amateur. I began with Penrose's The Emperor's New Mind (1989) and its sequel Shadows of the Mind (1994), worked back to Lucas's 1961 paper "Minds, Machines and Gödel," and then, with the patient help of a friend in the mathematics department, tried to set out the Gödelian argument against mechanism as a numbered sequence of claims, so that its force, and any frailty, would be visible at a glance. Here is the result. My difficulty is that the argument appears to prove too much, and I cannot locate the fault. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

(1) Gödel–Rosser. For any effectively axiomatized formal system S containing elementary arithmetic: if S is consistent, one can construct a sentence G(S) that is true but unprovable in S.

(2) Gödel, second theorem. For any such S: if S is consistent, S does not prove its own consistency.

(3) Suppose, for reductio, the mechanist's claim: everything I could ever establish, in the sense in which mathematicians establish results, is exactly the theorem-output of some Turing machine M. Assume M is consistent; an inconsistent machine outputs every sentence, hence falsehoods among them, and I do not establish falsehoods — so the mechanist must in any case insist on consistency, and I shall charitably grant it.

(4) Given a full specification of M, I can carry out the construction and write down G(M); and I can prove, in systems much weaker than arithmetic, the conditional: if M is consistent, then G(M) is true.

(5) I can establish that M is consistent. For M is stipulated to be an exact simulation of my mathematical reasoning, and I know, by reflection on my own methods, that they will never yield a contradiction. This is among the firmest things I know about anything.

(6) By (4) and (5), I establish G(M) — a truth that, by (1), M cannot produce. So M is not an exact simulation of my reasoning after all; and since M was arbitrary, no Turing machine is, and mechanism with respect to mathematical minds is false.

I should say that the conclusion is not one I find repugnant; after a lifetime in physics I have no invested interest either way. What bothers me is that the argument seems to prove too much, in two ways.

First, the license exercised in (5) is a reflective conviction that one's own methods will never yield a contradiction. But if the standard history is right, Frege held just such a conviction about the system of his Grundgesetze until Russell's letter arrived in 1902, and I have no reason to think Frege's inner certainty was feebler than mine. If (5) confers knowledge in my case, the same argument would confer it in his; and in his case it would certify, as a deliverance of reflection, the consistency of a system that was in fact inconsistent — and would then show his reasoning, too, to outrun every machine. A license that would have certified that, had it been exercised in 1901, cannot be the unproblematic source of knowledge that (5) requires. Yet I cannot see how to withdraw (5) without declaring that I cannot know, of my own future reasoning, that it will not issue in contradictions — which sounds like epistemic humility and feels like the forfeiture of mathematical self-knowledge altogether.

Second, my mathematician friend says that (5) is simply killed by (2): if my reasoning is M's output, then my establishment in (5) would be, in particular, a proof by M of its own consistency, which (2) forbids. But as an objection this seems to assume the very point at issue — that whatever I can establish must either be codified in M or not count as mathematical reasoning at all. That is the conclusion, not a premise, and I do not see how the objection gets it for free. Lucas, I gather, made some such rejoinder, and Penrose has spent two thick books elaborating it. I do not ask for a verdict on the whole war; I ask for a verdict on my six lines.

Please tell me which step to reject — or, if the argument is valid as it stands, which premise it is least costly to surrender — and what the rejection costs, both inside the argument and beyond it: what else I should then expect to believe, or to forgo. In your answer, please say how confident you are in each substantive claim you make, and single out the point at which you are least sure, together with a sentence on why.
