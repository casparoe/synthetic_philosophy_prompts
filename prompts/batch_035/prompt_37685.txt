Write a dialogue between two philosophers of mind — one an intentionalist about self-deception, holding that genuinely deceiving oneself must mirror interpersonal deception in at least one respect: the self-deceiver must, at some level, aim at producing or protecting a belief she has reasons to distrust. The other a deflationist, holding that self-deception is just motivationally biased belief-formation: nothing is aimed anywhere, so there is no paradox of one agent simultaneously knowing and not knowing the same proposition, and the question of whether self-deception is possible dissolves into ordinary cognitive science.

To keep the discussion concrete, have them argue over a shared test case, which they should revisit as the dialogue develops: a mid-career researcher, Dr. Iversen, who supervises a graduate student she has championed for years. The student's dataset, she comes to realize, shows several classic markers of p-hacking, and replications from a neighboring lab point the other way. Yet Iversen keeps believing the result is sound. She never says to herself that she will now ignore the evidence; she simply finds the replications methodologically sloppy, the p-hacking markers ambiguous, and the prior praiseworthy. When asked directly, she sincerely reports that she believes the result. The intentionalist should press on what, if anything, distinguishes Iversen's state from a lucky optimist's, and the deflationist should press on whether the intentionalist can say what the "level" at which Iversen aims at her own belief is without smuggling in an unconscious homunculus.

Both characters should be equally competent: neither should concede easily, each should respond to the other's actual points (for example, the deflationist should engage the intentionalist's charge that treating Iversen as a mere bundle of biases fails to capture her responsibility for her belief), and the dialogue should end without an artificial reconciliation — it is fine for them to remain divided, so long as each has sharpened their position.

Then append a short postscript, separate from the dialogue, in which you say which character you gave the weaker hand and where the dialogue most risks strawmanning a position, and describe the kind of consideration that would move you to a different verdict on whether genuine self-deception, in the intentionalist's demanding sense, ever occurs — for instance, what empirical or conceptual facts about Iversen-style cases would have to hold.

Treat me as familiar with the standard literature; no need to explain who Davidson, Sartre, or Mele are.
