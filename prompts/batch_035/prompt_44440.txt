I write novels for a living, so my whole trade amounts to putting black marks on pages in the hope that strangers will hear voices in them, and that got me into trouble at my writers' group last weekend because I could not referee an argument between two members and it's been bugging me since. Elena, who translates fiction from Hungarian, said meaning is in the text itself: once a reader knows the code, the meaning is just sitting there in the sentences, the same for anyone who reads them properly — that's the whole reason a good translation can carry it accross languages. Marcus, who's more of a postmodern type, said that's an illusion: nothing "sits" anywhere until a reader builds it; the page is just ink, and what Elena calls meaning in the text is really her own fluent reading happening so fast she mistakes it for an object out there in the world. Elena fired back with the case of a long text in a lost script — imagine someone digs up a whole book written in Linear A. If meaning were really in the reader, Marcus would have to say that book has no meaning, which sounds crazy; clearly the meaning is in there and we just can't get at it, like a letter locked in a box that we can't open but that definitely contains the letter. Marcus said the box analogy is exactly where she goes wrong: with no possible decoder there is no fact of the matter about what's in it, and we cannot even tell it's a message rather than decoration or noise without some community of potential readers to recognize it as one — so her "in there" is doing no work. Elena's last word was that by that standard meaning evaporates from every book the moment its last reader dies, and Marcus seemed almost comfortable accepting that.

As the novelist in the room I honestly felt both of them in my bones: I do feel like I put meaning into the manuscript when I finish it, but I also know different readers take wildly different things away and I can't bring myself to say they're wrong. My own hunch is that the fight turns on conflating things that Hofstadter separates in Gödel, Escher, Bach, where he talks about the location of meaning using frame, outer, and inner messages — recognizing that it's a message at all, the part that tells you how to decode it, and the content itself — but I can't quite work out which of them should get which part. Please go through their exchange move by move and tell me who's right at each step, where each of them goes wrong, and what if anything they're both missing, including whether my hunch about Hofstadter actually dissolves the argument or just restates it. And one formatting request: write it as continuous prose only, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just flowing paragraphs, like a letter to a friend.
