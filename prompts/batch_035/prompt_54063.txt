Preparing a graduate seminar unit on contemporary epistemology, I have been rereading Gilbert Harman's argument from his 1973 book *Thought* — the line of reasoning now commonly called the dogmatism paradox — alongside James Pryor's well-known 2000 *Noûs* paper, in which the label "dogmatism" got attached to a distinctive thesis about perceptual justification. In seminar last week, one of my sharper students pressed the following line on me, and I confess it has unsettled my reading more than it ought to have: Harman's paradox demonstrates that dogmatism, as Pryor later characterized it, is flatly inconsistent — since the paradox derives an unacceptable conclusion from the dogmatist's own starting points, Pryor's position was effectively refuted before it was ever proposed, and the ensuing decades of refinements and replies amount to little more than damage control. On the student's telling, the only genuinely open question is why epistemologists keep defending dogmatism at all instead of conceding the matter and moving on.

My instinct is that this line conflates two things that merely happen to share a name — the paradox, which is an argument, and dogmatism, which is a thesis about how perceptual experience justifies belief — but I am not fully confident in that diagnosis, and I do not want to wave the student off on what might be merely terminological grounds if something deeper is in fact right. So I am asking you to adjudicate. Is the student correct that the dogmatism paradox established the inconsistency of dogmatism about perceptual justification, such that the view's subsequent career has been a defensive holding action? Or does the student's framing rest on a mistake about what the paradox actually shows, and about what the dogmatist actually claims? Please take a definite stand and defend it with reasons: I would rather receive a committed verdict, even one I might push back on in seminar, than a menu of interpretive options to sort through myself.
