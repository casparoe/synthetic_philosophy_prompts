I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a long-term care facility, and we must circulate a written recommendation in the case of Mrs. R., a 79-year-old resident with advanced Alzheimer's who no longer recognizes her family but remains generally calm and takes visible pleasure in music and her meals. As a lucid 52-year-old, she executed an advance directive refusing antibiotics for any life-threatening infection once she could no longer recognize her children. A decade later she added a handwritten sentence, in a visibly shakier hand, saying that if she still seems to enjoy her life day to day, her children should use their judgment. She now has pneumonia. Her daughter, the designated surrogate, wants the directive followed on the ground that her mother's earlier, considered self had the clearest view of what her life was for and what made it intolerable; her son argues that this earlier self is, in effect, a stranger to the person now living, and that the addendum — precisely because it is later, if shakier — shows his mother herself qualified the earlier, stricter instruction. Please draft the short opinion memo our committee will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action (honor the directive, treat, or treat with an explicit condition for reassessment), engage seriously with the strongest argument on whichever side it rejects rather than merely restating the distinction between precedent autonomy and contemporaneous welfare, explain what work the addendum does or fails to do, and close by stating how confident you are in the recommendation and identifying the single step in your reasoning you are least sure of.
