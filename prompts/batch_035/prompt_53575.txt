I'm a structural engineer (bridges, mostly, some industrial buildings) with essentially no philosophy background. A couple weeks ago a friend who's doing a philosophy PhD tried to explain to me what epistemologists fight about, and the part I can't stop thinking about is apparently a real debate over whether the stakes of a situation change whether a person "knows" something — the stock example she gave was someone who has decent but not airtight evidence that the bank is open on Friday deciding to double-check anyway because the stakes are high if they're wrong. Everyone in the debate apparently agrees the person has the same evidence either way; they disagree about whether that means the person "knows" in one case but not the other, or knows both times but should act differently, or knows neither time and the word is just doing something else. When she described this I immediately said: that's just load cases. A beam isn't "strong" or "not strong" full stop — it's adequate for a specified design situation with a specified margin, and which situation governs the design depends on what happens if it fails. We even do this explicitly in code: reliability targets are literally tiered by consequence class, so a bridge deck and a garden shed are held to different annual probabilities of collapse given the same underlying physics. So my working intuition is that "knows" is something like "proven for the governing load case," and I want to know if that's a real insight or me pattern-matching too hard.

So here's the request. Restate the actual philosophical debate — as best you can, the view that the meaning/standards of "knows" shift with the conversational context, the rival view that it's the knowledge itself that is interest-relative while the meaning stays fixed, and whatever the holdouts who say stakes never matter are claiming — in my vocabulary: design situations, load combinations, safety and partial factors, consequence classes, target reliabilities, code compliance as a threshold notion, "the governing case." Use reliability jargon freely, I'm fluent in it. Be faithful to the positions rather than making them agree with me, and flag it openly if translating a position into my terms quietly assumes the position is right. Then I want the residue report, because that's the part I actually care about: what translates cleanly, what you had to distort to make it fit, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Two things I specifically want your judgement on. First, in engineering the load cases are enumerable and stipulated in advance by code — nobody discovers mid-design that a new load case existed — whereas from what my friend said, the philosophers seem to think the "practical environment" does NOT come pre-partitioned, and part of their fight is about whether there even is a fact of the matter about how finely to carve up situations. Is that a genuine disanalogy that breaks the translation, or is it just that our codes are a social convenience that hides the same underlying messiness? Second: our practice is to write the governing case into the spec, explicitly, so two engineers never disagree about which case they're checking. If that's right, would the philosophers' bank-type dispute even arise between engineers, or does our explicit bookkeeping dissolve it — and if it dissolves it, is that a win for one of their positions, or just a difference in how the profession is organized? And if some part of the debate is about the normative role of "knows" — like, what you're allowed to assert or act on — tell me whether that's the part with no engineering counterpart, because I suspect it might be.
