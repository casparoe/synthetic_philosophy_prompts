I have lived with chronic daily migraine for eleven years, and last month my long-term disability insurer denied my claim on the grounds that my reports of pain are, as they put it, neurological events, and that the plan does not cover experiences insofar as they are merely experiences. While assembling my appeal I made the mistake of reading philosophy of mind, and I now see that the denial letter is a serviceable reconstruction of epiphenomenalism: if phenomenal states are causally inert byproducts of brain processes, then my suffering caused nothing — not my testimony, not my missed workdays, not the letter denying the letter, nothing — and the insurer may be guilty of nothing but unusual candor. I would like to make this insight someone else's problem, in the form of satire.

Please write a parody of a formal claims-determination letter from a fictional insurer — call it something like Phenomenal Indemnity Mutual — rejecting a policyholder's claim for chronic pain, roughly 700 words, in the flat, courteous register of real insurance correspondence: claim number, sections, definitions, an appeals procedure. The humor must come from getting the metaphysics right rather than from caricature. In particular, the letter should:

1. Deny the claim on strictly epiphenomenalist grounds: the company accepts that the policyholder's physical brain states caused every document in the file, including the complaint itself, and therefore accepts that the experience of pain, being causally inert, caused no losses. The losses, the letter clarifies, were caused by neural events, which are covered under a different section that the policyholder did not purchase.

2. Include an exclusion clause for philosophical zombies: since a physical duplicate of the policyholder would file an identical claim while experiencing nothing, the claim's filing is not evidence of an experience, and the company cannot be expected to pay for events that leave no causal trace in any possible world it can investigate.

3. Include a rider in which the assigned claims adjuster reports having completed a full physical and functional description of the policyholder's condition, down to every receptor and pathway, and notes that, per her training manual, nothing further of relevance remains for her to be told — though she adds, in a footnote the company describes as "personal and non-compensable," that she still feels she is missing something.

4. Stray, at least once, into the paradox of phenomenal judgment without resolving it: the letter should observe, with genuine regret, that the policyholder's sincere belief that she is in pain was fixed entirely by physical facts, so that the company is not denying she suffers — it is merely noting that her suffering could not have been among the causes of her belief that she suffers, or of her saying so, and that the company's liability analysis cannot reference items with no causal profile.

5. Close with an appeals procedure in which the appellant is invited to submit further evidence, with the gentle reminder that whatever she submits will be caused by her brain, and that the company looks forward to reading it.

The letter should remain internally consistent — no sliding into interactionism or property dualism halfway through, and no wink at the reader — while still being legible as satire to someone who has merely had an insurance claim denied. A reader who knows the philosophy of mind literature should find each clause a fair exaggeration of a real commitment of the view, not an invented absurdity, and should finish the piece feeling the actual vertigo of the position: the strange sense that the strongest available argument against the woman's suffering is that it explains nothing she has ever said about it.
