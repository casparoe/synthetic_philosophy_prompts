So I'm a cog sci master's student who's somehow ended up co-organizing a reading group that's half robotics PhD students and half philosophy people, which is exactly as chaotic as it sounds. Next session is on the frame problem, and the last thing I want is a handout that just lists "here's what Dennett thinks, here's what Fodor thinks" — the robotics folks will roll their eyes at the philosophy talk and the philosophers will glaze over at the planning stuff. What I want instead is a decision tree that starts from something everyone in the room already accepts and walks them to a position, so the CS people and the phil people end up sorted by what they actually believe rather than by which literature they came from.

Start the tree from something like: an agent knows a bunch of facts about the world, performs an action, and now has to figure out what changed and what stayed the same. From there, branch on questions a person could answer cold, without knowing any of the labels — e.g. "is the hard part just efficiently representing what *doesn't* change, or is it knowing which of the millions of irrelevant facts you're entitled to not even think about?", or "would a statistical learner that never writes down axioms in the first place have the same problem in different clothes, or no problem at all?", or "when we say an agent 'knows' what's relevant, does that hide the very thing we were trying to explain?". The leaves should be the actual positions out there: the view that the frame problem was a specific technical problem about persistence in logic-based planning that got solved and everyone philosophical should just move on; the view that it's really about relevance or rationality and no amount of engineering touches it; the idea that it only ever existed for agents with explicit belief representations; the deflationary "there was never one problem here, just a family of them" option; and whatever else genuinely belongs. For each leaf, one sentence on its most awkard commitment — the thing it has to swallow that the others don't.

Two more things. First, flag any pair of leaves the tree can't really separate — my suspicion is that "it was solved in classical planning" and "it only ever existed for logic-based agents" overlap a lot, but maybe you can find a genuine question between them, and if not I'd rather know. Second, no citations and no name-dropping in the tree itself — the whole point is that people arrive at their leaf before finding out whose club they joined, and we can argue about names after. (Though do tell me if one of my proposed node questions is basically just asking "are you Fodor" in a trench coat, and rephrase it if so.) If it helps, aim for something that fits on two sides of A4, and don't worry about covering literally every view ever held — better a clean tree with six good leaves than a bushy one nobody can follow.
