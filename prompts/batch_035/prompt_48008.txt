I write a weekly column on mind and behavior for a general-interest science magazine, and my next piece takes up the old question of what an emotion is: judgment, feeling, or perception. Rather than survey the debate, I want to argue for one line: that emotions are perceptions — not of the external world, but of the body's value-relevant states. The familiar route to this conclusion runs through the analogy between fearing the dog and seeing the snake: both present their content without concepts, both are available to infants and animals, and both can persist in the face of conflicting belief. I would rather not lean on that route, since readers have seen it before and it invites the standard rejoinder that judgments, too, can be fast and unconscious. Instead, build the argument around a phenomenon that gets far less attention in this debate: adaptation. An emotion held in constant circumstances fades — the new resident's fear of the low-flying aircraft quiets within months, the delight in the view from a new apartment dims — and the fading tracks the constancy of the stimulus rather than any change in belief: the resident still judges the flights a nuisance and a risk, and would say so if asked. Argue that this profile is the signature of a perceptual system recalibrating to a steady stimulus, on the model of how touch stops reporting the weight of one's clothes or how a smell vanishes in a room one stays in; and show why the judgment-based rival, which must treat fading affect as a revision of judgment, either misdescribes the case (the judgment, by hypothesis, has not changed) or has to bolt on a supplementary mechanism that ends up doing all the interesting work. Close by stating candidly what the argument does not establish: what it leaves open about whether perceiving bodily change amounts to perceiving value, and how the view would explain the recalcitrance of phobia, where the affect refuses to fade at all. Write it as continuous prose I can adapt for the column, about 700 words, pitched so that an interested non-specialist reader can follow every step.
