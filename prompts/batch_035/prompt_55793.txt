My closest friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the gender-equality paradox — the finding that women's share of degrees and careers in physics, engineering, and computer science tends to be lower, not higher, in countries that rank higher on standard measures of gender equality — and I went to bed with a page of case-verdicts that I now cannot reconcile. I want to know what principle, if any, captures them, and I would rather be told which verdict to abandon than be handed a principle so elastic it accommodates everything.

First, the descriptive background as I understand it, since we argued about it too. The best-known study is the 2018 Psychological Science paper by Stoet and Geary, which analyzed PISA data across roughly seventy countries and found that girls performed similarly or better than boys in science in most countries, and were capable of tertiary STEM study nearly everywhere, yet were relatively less likely to obtain STEM degrees in more gender-equal countries. Algeria and Tunisia send proportionally more women into STEM fields than Norway or Finland do. I am also aware that the study was disputed: a correction was issued in 2019 after other researchers could not recreate parts of the analysis, and follow-up work has raised problems with the STEM version of the paradox, even as United Nations reports have found versions of it and even the critics grant some of the pattern in other domains, like personality and values. For the sake of this question, treat the cross-country pattern as real but contested; the friend does, and so do I.

Here are my verdicts on whether particular observations support the claim that the residual gap in a country like Norway reflects disposition rather than remaining constraint.

First, the cross-country pattern itself. My verdict: it counts against the constraint story, and I cannot pretend otherwise. If remaining gaps simply measured remaining discrimination, the pattern should run the other way, and it does not. The friend takes this as settled; I take it as the strongest single fact on his side, but only a fact.

Second, the Soviet comparison. The Soviet state pushed women into engineering for decades and achieved one of the higher shares of women among engineers anywhere in the world; after 1991 the share fell back substantially in several successor states. The friend calls this his trump card: the override was possible but unstable, and reversion reveals the underlying disposition. My verdict: the reversion supports him more than I would like, and I have never found a way to say why it should not.

Third, the American flips. Within two generations, veterinary medicine and biology in the United States went from heavily male to heavily female, with nothing comparable happening in physics or electrical engineering. My verdict: fields can flip, so no field's sex composition is fixed. But the flips all run in a person-oriented direction, which the friend says is precisely the disposition expressing itself, and I find I cannot hold both halves of my judgment steadily.

Fourth, the point of choice. Nearly everywhere, girls who are top performers in mathematics are less likely than equally scoring boys to enter math-intensive fields. My verdict: this supports a genuine difference in disposition at the moment of choice — but the disposition was formed by eighteen years of socialization, so it is silent on whether the disposition itself is innate. The friend insists the distinction is a dodge; I keep insisting it matters, and I want a principle that adjudicates rather than merely repeats me.

Fifth, the amplification mechanism. Suppose the sexes differ only trivially on average in thing-versus-people orientation, with broadly similar variance. In a society that sorts people by freely expressed preference, occupations that recruit from the far tail of that distribution can be heavily skewed even though the average difference is trivial. My verdict: I find this mechanism convincing and think it explains more than any story about discrimination at the point of choice. But it renders the paradox almost inevitable, which is exactly why the friend loves it — and I cannot decide whether that inevitability is the explanation's chief virtue or a warning that it explains too easily.

Sixth, the leap to justice. My verdict: even granting everything above, nothing follows about the justice of quotas or targeted recruitment, because a gap produced by free choice on top of a small average difference is still compatible with the claim that free choice is not the baseline justice should use. The friend says I am insulating my politics from the evidence. My verdict stands, but I cannot state the reason in a way that does not merely restate the verdict.

The tentative principle I half-hold, from somewhere in the middle of the argument: a comparison between societies supports the disposition reading only if it holds fixed the menu of occupations available and the costs attached to each, varying only the freedom with which individuals choose from that menu. My problem is that no actual cross-country comparison satisfies this. More gender-equal countries differ from less equal ones in affluence, occupational structure, the returns to different degrees, and what counts to a woman as a safe career — the security explanation for the Algeria pattern is precisely a difference in the menu of options, not in freedom. The friend's reply: by my principle nothing would ever count as evidence for the disposition reading, which is absurd; imperfect comparisons are still evidence. I do not know whether to weaken the principle or reject his reply.

So, my request. Is there a single principle that captures verdicts one through six, stated precisely enough to be tested? If not, which verdict should go, and what is the cost of dropping it? What does the resulting principle predict about cases I have not listed — say, a country whose gender-equality index rises sharply over twenty years while its engineering gap stays flat, or a comparison between a poor egalitarian country and a rich inegalitarian one, or a policy that pays women to enter a thing-oriented field and succeeds for as long as it pays? And please tell me directly whether my tentative principle can be repaired into something that some real comparison could satisfy, or whether it was a mistake to demand that much of a comparison in the first place.
