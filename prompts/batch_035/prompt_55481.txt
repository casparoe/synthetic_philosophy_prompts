I am a recently retired astrophysicist who has spent the last two years working through philosophy and history of science, and I have agreed to lead the next session of my local philosophy of science reading group, which consists of academics and other retirees with no particular background in early modern science. The topic I have chosen is Kepler's attitude toward astrology: how the man who discovered that planetary orbits are elliptical could also spend his career casting horoscopes, publish a steady stream of astrological prognostications, and write treatises such as De fundamentis astrologiae certioribus and the 1610 Tertius interveniens, in which he condemned the vulgar practice of the star-gazers while insisting that a genuine natural-physical kernel lay buried in it — a folly, as he put it in one famous figure, but a folly whose wise mother should still feed her daughter. I do not want the session to become either a caricature (Kepler as an embarrassing mystic we should quietly forgive) or an anachronistic defense (astrology as proto-science judged by our own demarcation criteria). What I need is not an explanation of the material — I have read enough to handle the content myself — but a plan for the explanation: the order in which the components must be introduced and why, since I suspect the group cannot make sense of Kepler's position until they first understand the disciplinary landscape he inherited, where astronomy was a branch of mathematics and carried no obligation to say anything about physical causes.

Please take on the role of an expert historian and philosopher of early modern science who has designed many such sessions, and begin by thinking carefully about the dependency structure before committing to a sequence. I would like you to address: what has to be in place before the audience can even formulate the right question; which single example or episode should carry the whole session and what that example must have, and lack, to do the work; and the misconceptions the audience will predictably bring or form — my own guesses are that they will assume Kepler was secretly a skeptic who cast horoscopes only for his salary, or that they will flatten everything by saying people back then simply could not tell the two enterprises apart, though I suspect there are worse traps I have not anticipated — together with the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than left to fester until the discussion. I would also like a check I can run near the end that would show whether they have understood Kepler's position from the inside, as opposed to merely having been told to suspend judgment about it. Finally, be explicit about what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — the harmonics of Harmonice mundi feels like a candidate — and how I should say so without making it sound like an admission that the material is disreputable. Please do not recommend readings or supply a bibliography; the group works from what I present in the room, and I am interested only in the shape and sequence of the hour I have.
