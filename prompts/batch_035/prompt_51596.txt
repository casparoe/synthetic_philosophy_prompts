Write a dialogue between two philosophers stuck in a long layover at O'Hare, arguing about whether omissions can be causes. The occasion: one of them, call her Maya, just told the other, Dev, that she killed her neighbor's orchid over the winter by forgetting to water it while the neighbor was away -- she says it plainly, "my not watering it caused it to die." Dev thinks this is sloppy talk and can't believe she, of all people, endorses it.

Make the case do some work. My neighbor didn't just forget to ask anyone -- she specifically asked Maya, and Maya agreed. Maya also had a key, the watering can was on the porch, etc. But also: in principle, anyone else on the street could have walked up and watered the orchid, and nobody says *they* killed it, and the same goes for literally every other person on earth, including people who didn't know the orchid existed. Dev should press hard on this (the profligate causes worry) and also on the deeper point that "not watering" doesn't seem to be an event or a process at all -- there's no spatiotemporally continuous connection, no physical quantity being exchanged, just... nothing happening, plus a dead plant. Maya shouldn't be a pushover: she should argue that the counterfactuals come out exactly right, that preemption and double prevention cases already force causal work on absences, and that our causal intuitions here track something real. Dev should come back at her with the observation that what seems to separate Maya from all the other non-waterers is her obligation -- and he should push on whether that means causation depends on norms, which strikes him as backwards, since we're supposed to figure out what caused the death *before* deciding who's responsibile, not after. Let Maya try to give a real answer to that (norms or defaults selecting among the non-waterers, contrastivism about "caused X rather than Y", maybe distinguishing omissions from mere absences) rather than just reasserting the counterfactual.

They should be equally matched -- Dev is not a fuddy-duddy and Maya is not a sophist. Have them actually respond to each other's moves, occasionally concede small points, and get genuinely a bit annoyed at one another in a way old friends do. End the dialogue unresolved, but with both of them having paid some visible price for their position (Maya owning up to what's weird about norm-relative causation, Dev owning up to how he'd explain why we hold Maya, and not the queen, responsible). Keep it casual, plain talk, no lecture-monologues.
