I've been keeping a list of my gut verdicts on questions about individual contributions to collective harms — the whole "my one act makes no difference, so how could it be wrong?" cluster — and I've concluded that I don't have a coherent position, only vibes. My partner, who is a straightforward consequentialist about expected value, claims all my verdicts fall out of multiplying tiny probabilities by large stakes, but I don't think that's true, and I'd like an actual principle, if one exists. Here are the cases and my verdicts. I vote in national elections even in a safely decided district, and I think people who don't are doing something at least mildly wrong — when someone says one vote never matters, I think they're mistaken, though I can't say how. I think buying factory-farmed chicken is wrong even though my skipping it won't change the number of birds raised — the chain from my checkout to a production decision is too long and slack. But I heat my house to a comfortable temperature all winter and feel no guilt at all, even though my annual emissions from that alone exceed the emissions of a flight I did feel guilty about taking for a friend's birthday. I blame each of the forty mill owners who individually dump legally diluted, individually harmless effluent into a river that collectively dies from it. And while I think an emergency drive to the hospital is obviously fine despite its emissions, I extend that excuse to surprisingly trivial errands, which suggests my necessity exception is really just an excuse generator.

Two pairs in particular bother me. The voting case and the heating case look structurally identical — a contribution so small that it makes no difference to the outcome, one to a good cause and one to a bad one — yet I feel obligatory pull in the first and nothing in the second, so whatever is doing the work can't just be the arithmetic of the collective harm. And I think failing to vote is a minor wrong while failing to donate blood, where a single donation near-certainly does some concrete good, seems supererogatory rather than required — which is backwards from any view on which the value of acting is what matters. So: is there one principle that accommodates all or nearly all of these (threshold effects, expected utility with tiny probabilities and huge stakes, complicity in a practice, something about the visibility of the causal chain), or does any workable principle force me to give some verdict up? If the principle exists, state it precisely, tie each clause to the case that motivates it, and tell me its verdicts on cases I haven't listed: a referendum in my town of four thousand voters where I'm genuinely unsure which way it will go; buying eggs at a farmers' market stall where my weekly purchase plausibly keeps the farmer afloat; paying a carbon-removal company to offset that flight after taking it; and being one of twenty people who each write a letter to the planning office, no single letter decisive, that together get a development blocked. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts your best principle cannot save, and why that verdict rather than the principle should go.
