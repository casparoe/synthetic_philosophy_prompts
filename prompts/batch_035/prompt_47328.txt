I am writing a novel whose hinge is a question I want to get right before I trust my narrator to think it. The plot: a woman learns, decades after the fact, that the sperm used at her fertility clinic was not her husband's — a substitution revealed by her son's ancestry test. The question she circles for the remainder of the book is not whether her son is really hers in the genetic sense; that she settles quickly. It is whether the child she calls Daniel could have existed had the substitution not occurred: same marriage, same appointment, the same ovum, but her husband's sperm rather than the donor's. Is that counterfactual child Daniel himself, or a sibling of Daniel who never was? I want her eventual position to read as considered rather than as a mood, and to manage that I first need someone else's considered position to check against.

So give me your credence — an actual number, in your first sentence — that origin essentialism is false for human persons: that a given person could have developed from at least some sperm-and-egg pair other than the one they actually developed from. Then unpack the number. I want the decomposition, with a rough probability and a line of justification for each term, and I want the following threads taken seriously, though you may add, discard, or restructure as you see fit:

1. Whether persons are essentially organisms, so that identity tracks the biological continuum from zygote onward, or essentially psychological continuants, in which case the origin question may be underdetermined by the metaphysics of the thing that matters.
2. The nearest-sibling problem: the sperm adjacent to the actual one carries a different genome, and the child it would produce looks, on any ordinary way of talking, like Daniel's full sibling rather than Daniel. But suppose we hold the parents fixed and vary only which gametes met — how much of the question survives that, and is the "origin" that matters genetic, gametic, zygotic, or something coarser?
3. Monozygotic twins and chimeras: two persons from a single zygote, and occasionally one organism from two. What do these do to the thought that the zygote is the origin of the person?
4. Whether the de re modal claim is even fully determinate — whether on a counterpart-theoretic reading of what Daniel could have been, the question might partly dissolve rather than answer.
5. A Davidson-style Swampman: a lightning-assembled duplicate of a person, with no origin in gametes at all. Do our intuitions about gamete-substitution carry over, or does Swampman simply prove that origins and duplication are different questions?

I should say where I stand: my own credence that Daniel could have existed from different gametes sits at about 0.3, and my editor — who read Kripke too young and never recovered — is at 0.05. For my own credence, at least, tell me which single term in your decomposition is doing the most work, and what argument or thought experiment, were my character to be persuaded by it, would most plausibly move her from your number to a confident denial. I don't expect the question to be empirically resolvable; I expect the reasoning to be. And please don't pad it with the history of the debate unless a term of your decomposition genuinely needs it — my narrator thinks in problems, not in literature reviews.
