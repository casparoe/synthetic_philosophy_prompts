Bit of an odd one, but here goes. My younger brother had a stroke last year, at 31 -- bad luck, no underlying cause, just one of those things. He's recovering, but he'll need support indefinately, and because I'm the one in the family with the spare time I've somehow ended up helping him manage it all. This has dropped me straight into the deep end of what I gather philosphers call the "equality of what?" debate, and I want your help figuring out which parts of it actually matter.

Concretely: he gets a personal budget from the council, and we get real choices about where it goes. We can use it to top up his income -- more money in his pocket, resources in the obvious sense. We can spend it on things that just make his day-to-day life nicer: a better flat, days out, whatever makes him happier (welfare, roughly). Or we can spend it on the stuff that builds back his capacity to do things he actually cares about -- transport, an adapted kitchen, ongoing physio and speech therapy (capabilities, I suppose). And the budget is fixed, so every pound towards one of these is a pound away from the others. It's not a hypothetical for us; we're filling in the actual forms this autumn.

I know the standard positions exist -- Dworkin on resources versus welfare, Sen's capabilities, the expensive-tastes objection, Anderson's line that luck egalitarianism is demeaning to the people it's supposed to help, the relational-equality crowd saying the whole "what do we distribute" framing is off. What I want from you is a ranking of the open questions in this literature by how much they actually do work in a decision like ours, with the ranking defended -- not a survey. For each question that earns its place, tell me what it would genuinely change about how we allocate the money. For each that doesn't, tell me why: is it effectively settled, or is it just irrelevant at this scale, or does it only bite at the level of designing the whole system rather than one family's budget? And if the honest answer is that the theory is mostly silent and we're going to have to muddle through on judgement, say that plainly -- I'd much rather know it than be handed a framework that pretends to decide for us.
