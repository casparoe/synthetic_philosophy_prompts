I'd like a prediction about Henry Sidgwick — the utilitarian philosopher of The Methods of Ethics, but in his other capacity: founding president of the Society for Psychical Research, a post from which he directed its investigations for nearly two decades.

First, the track record, as I read it.

In the Census of Hallucinations (1889–1892), a committee under Sidgwick's chairmanship canvassed some 17,000 people. Roughly one in ten reported having had a hallucination, and a small subset reported seeing or hearing a person within twelve hours of that person's death, in cases where the percipient had no foreknowledge of any illness. The committee's published conclusion was that "between deaths and apparitions of the dying person a connexion exists which is not due to chance alone" — a conclusion carefully limited to the existence of a connexion, with no commitment whatever as to what kind of connexion it might be.

Then Eusapia Palladino, Cambridge, 1895: a paid Italian physical medium invited to Myers's house. In the sittings she was caught freeing a hand — the two controllers discovered they had been holding each other's hands rather than hers — and Hodgson watched her use her feet on the furniture. On that basis Sidgwick and his circle judged her mediumship permanently discredited and barred her from further sittings. When the British Medical Journal afterward taunted the Society for having wasted its time on such a person, Sidgwick's public defense was that the Society had spent its whole existence combating and exposing the frauds of professional mediums, and that in all that time it had not once, in its Proceedings, published a report favoring the performances of any of them.

Then Leonora Piper: a paid American trance medium investigated by the Society for years. Hodgson — the same man whose earlier report had done more than anyone's to expose Madame Blavatsky — ended up persuaded that Piper was in contact with the dead; Podmore maintained to the end that nothing in her sittings required any supernormal explanation at all, since he could not point to a single unambiguous piece of information that could not have come from the medium's own mind working on hints let drop. As far as I can tell, Sidgwick never committed himself on Piper in either direction.

And throughout, his general stance: agnostic about personal survival to the end of his life, while treating the question whether personality persists after death as among the most important that inquiry could address, and insisting that the Society's method was to exclude fraud, chance, and ordinary information leakage one by one, and then publish whatever remained, whatever it was.

Now the new case. Suppose it is 1897, and a medium arrives in Cambridge. She is a professional — she charges for sittings and is her family's main support — but her phenomena are entirely of the mental kind: nothing physical, no levitating tables. Hodgson designs the protocol: sitters drawn by lot and introduced under invented names; each sitter instructed to volunteer nothing and to answer only yes or no; a stenographic record kept and checked afterwards for fishing and for anything the medium could have picked up from the sitter. Across dozens of sittings she produces, unprompted, names and circumstances of the sitters' dead relatives; and in a minority of sittings she states facts about the deceased that the sitter did not know and only afterwards verified in family papers or by writing to relatives — the sort of fact (an unmentioned half-brother who died in an asylum, say) that nobody in the room possessed.

Predict Sidgwick's verdict. Does he sign a favorable report — which, on his own statement to the BMJ, would be the first ever for a professional medium — or does he publish the material while suspending judgment on its import, in the manner of the Census, or does he hold that the Palladino lesson applies, and that clean evidence simply cannot be had from a paid medium at all?

I'm less interested in the answer than in the rules that fit his track record. I can see at least three: a never-a-favorable-word-for-a-professional rule; a quantify-chance-and-stop-there rule, under which he reports the excess over what leakage and guessing could produce but, as in the Census, declines to say what carries it; and a one-detected-fraud-discredits-all rule, under which the Palladino treatment transfers to any medium whose controls admit any gap. Say what each rule predicts about this case, where they diverge, and give your best guess with reasons. Two questions matter especially to me: (a) does it matter to his verdict whether the veridical facts were known to the sitter, or does telepathy from the living keep the spirit hypothesis underdetermined either way — and is that underdetermination something his Census habits would let him live with indefinitely, or would it frustrate him given what he wanted the inquiry to settle? and (b) if, in the hundredth sitting, a single instance of fishing were detected, would the Palladino rule kick in and retroactively discredit the whole record, or would the Census habit — tolerate some contamination, conclude that a connexion exists — carry the day? Finally, describe for me the further case, constructed to the same level of detail, that would best tell the candidate rules apart.
