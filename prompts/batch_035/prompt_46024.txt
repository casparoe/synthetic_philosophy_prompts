My flatmate, who is writing a thesis on truth and verification, has an argument he claims demolishes the anti-realist idea that truth is what is knowable. The conclusion seems to me to prove far too much, yet each individual step sounds like common sense, and I cannot isolate the one that is rotten.

(1) Every truth is knowable in principle: for any proposition, if it is true, it could come to be known.
(2) Suppose, as seems obvious, that there is at least one truth p that nobody in fact knows.
(3) Then "nobody knows p" is itself a truth, and truths about the state of human knowledge are precisely the sort of thing we can come to establish — historians of science do it all the time. So p is knowable, and so is "nobody knows p."
(4) Whatever is knowable separately is knowable together: someone who could come to know each of two things could come to know both. So "p, and nobody knows p" is knowable.
(5) Yet it cannot be known. Anyone who knew it would know that nobody knows p; and since what is known is so, nobody would know p — while that very person, knowing p, would know p. So no one can ever come to know "p, and nobody knows p."
(6) The conjunction is therefore both knowable and unknowable, which is impossible. Hence (2) is false: there is no truth that nobody knows. Every truth is already known.

He concludes that anyone committed to (1) — the verificationist, the anti-realist, anyone who ties truth to our capacity for inquiry — must swallow the absurdity that all truths are already known. Even he admits this sounds insane, but he insists the logic forces it, and he challenges me to point to the step that fails, since each one looks like ordinary epistemology. For my part, I would like to keep both (1) and (2) if I honestly can; (2) in particular strikes me as simple humility about the unfinished state of human inquiry.

Walk me through the argument carefully. Which step should I reject, why did that step look unobjectionable when I first read it, and what does rejecting it cost — for me, and for his conclusion? And if he is right that the conclusion somehow survives, I want to know exactly how it survives, not merely that it does.
