I'm writing a short paper for a graduate seminar that straddles philosophy of mind and value theory, on the question of what makes pain bad. The centerpiece of my paper is pain asymbolia, so let me first say how I understand it, since I'd want your comments to be on my package of views rather than on the clinical details: asymbolia is a rare condition, typically arising from lesions in the anterior cingulate cortex or nearby regions, in which patients respond normally to noxious stimuli in one respect — they identify them as painful, locate them, and can rate their intensity — but are strikingly undisturbed by them: they do not withdraw, do not grimace, and are sometimes reported to smile when pricked with a pin. A similar dissociation has been described in patients who underwent cingulotomy for intractable pain, and in patients on morphine, who say things like: the pain is still there, exactly as intense, but it no longer bothers me. I take this to be the crucial test case for any theory of pain's badness, and in preparing the paper I've settled on the following set of commitments, which I'd like you to audit:

(1) Pain is not the detection of bodily damage. Phantom-limb pain is genuine pain without any tissue damage at the felt location, so pain must be a phenomenal kind, individuated by how it feels. (2) Ordinary pain has two separable components: a sensory-discriminative component — where it is, how intense, whether it is stabbing or burning — and an affective-motivational component, its awfulness and the pull to make it stop. I think asymbolia straightforwardly proves that these can come apart. (3) Asymbolic patients genuinely feel pain: their sincere reports should be taken at face value, and what is intact in them is the pain itself, not some pain-analogue. (4) Asymbolic pain is not intrinsically bad for the person who feels it. A pinprick to an asymbolic patient causes tissue damage, and that is bad, but there is nothing further that is bad, over and above that, in the way there would be for you or me. (5) Pain is intrinsically bad: if you are genuinely in pain, something bad is happening to you regardless of your desires, attitudes, or the state of your body — it would be bad for you even if you had somehow stopped minding it. (6) Unpleasantness is not a usual accompaniment of pain but essential to it: an experience that was in no way disagreeable, however intense, would simply not count as pain — it would be mere intense sensation. (7) I am broadly hedonistic about well-being: how well your life is going is fixed by the felt quality of your experiences. (8) As a corollary, I hold two verdicts: a physician who fully treats an asymbolic burn patient's wound but withholds analgesia does nothing wrong, and a hypothetical perfectly safe drug that induced lifelong asymbolia in an infant would be a benefit to the child rather than a harm.

My question is whether this hangs together. Please go through the commitments and tell me which pairs or clusters are in genuine tension and how badly, which tensions are only apparent and dissolve on closer inspection, and — for each real tension — what would have to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision, if any, would clean up the most. I'll confess that when I sketched this in a seminar a classmate said I seemed to be "granting the separability of pain from distress and then refusing its consequences," but she couldn't say exactly where I'd gone wrong, and I haven't been able to locate the fault myself, so don't spare me — just make sure you distinguish the real problems from the ones that only look like problems.
