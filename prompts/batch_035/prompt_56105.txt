I've been reading Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness with a small reading group, and we've ended up arguing about what she would make of the modern happiness industry. To make the discussion concrete, suppose a government unveils a three-part package. First, a programme of "hedonic accounting": moment-to-moment experience sampling through citizens' phones, aggregated into a national wellbeing index that steers subsidies towards whatever activities measurably raise it. Second, a commercial ecosystem of life-optimisation apps that A/B-test your routines — sleep, hobbies, even friendships — and gently nudge you towards whatever maximises your own logged affect. Third, a cultural movement, call it the contentment movement, which teaches detachment from ambition and status-seeking, treats passionate attachment as immature, and recommends mild, low-variance contentment as the rational goal of life.

What I want to know is which of the three she would find least objectionable, and I'd like the reasoning to run through her actual doctrines rather than a generic Enlightenment gloss. My understanding of her position is roughly this: happiness depends on the conduct of the mind rather than on circumstance; one should be indifferent to gossip and the opinions of fools, since reputation is never worth the anxiety it costs; one should deliberately cultivate one's illusions and passions rather than try to see through all of them, because the perfectly disenchanted person is miserable; study is among the surest resources against sorrow; and even gambling, of all things, gets a qualified defence as a distraction from distressing thoughts. If I've misremembered any of this, say so and correct me before proceeding. Then run each initiative past her: the hedonic ledger treats happiness as something to be measured from outside and administered by the state; the apps mechanise a cultivation of illusion she wants done reflectively and for oneself; the contentment movement seems to deny precisely her claim that passionate love and demanding ambition are worth the risk of real misery. Where would she locate the deepest error — in the measurement, the mechanisation, or the detachment?

One further request: be honest about which parts of your answer are grounded in her documented views and which are extrapolation from temperament and biography — she was, after all, an aristocratic woman who ran her own laboratory, translated and argued about Newton, and chose risky love affairs with open eyes, and I'd like to know how far you think that biography entitles us to fill gaps the text leaves open. Please write in British English throughout, and end your reply with a single line of the form \stance{...} naming, in five words or fewer, the initiative she would find least objectionable — or "none, all equally" if you think that is the honest answer.
