So I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size SaaS company, and every year for april fools a few of us write fake internal documents for the company wiki (last year someone did a full RFC for migrating the org chart to a blockchain, and people are still mad about how plausible it looked). This year is my turn and I want to do one about something I've been half-obsessed with since a college roommate who was a philosophy major made me read Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" — I understood maybe 40% of it but the vibes have definately stuck with me for a decade. The setup: the company ships a new claim-classification microservice (call it VERITAS or FACT-TAGGER v0.9) whose whole job is to tag every sentence in product copy, docs, and changelogs as either ANALYTIC (true by definition, resolved instantly from a dictionary config, never needs to touch the outside world) or SYNTHETIC (requires an actual runtime lookup at reality). The joke is that over the course of the RFC's numbered sections — v1 design, a couple of incident postmortems, then the v2 proposal — the architecture quietly falls apart exactly the way the two dogmas do: the definition pipeline turns out to be validated by the same dictionary it's supposed to validate (a circular dependency, flagged by some poor engineer at 2am), the term-swap synonymy check keeps requiring a "meaning" service that itself depends on the analytic/synthetic tags, legal keeps sneaking facts into dictionary entries so they don't have to pay for runtime verification, and every incident ends up paging literally the whole on-call rotation because no single claim can be traced back to one clean sense-datum event (the original epic to decompose every claim into raw experience events gets cancelled mid-quarter). By the final section the RFC is just describing one big dependency graph where "analytic" claims are merely the ones with an enormous blast radius when you try to revise them, and the deprecation notice for the true-by-definition tag is written completely straight.

Constraints: about 700 words, RFC format with numbered sections, deadpan corporate engineering voice throughout — no winking, no "as the philosophers say", the document never admits it's about philosophy. Someone who actually knows Two Dogmas should laugh at specific things (the circularity of the definitional moves, holism showing up as paging the entire on-call rotation, the idea that even the core claims are revisable if you're willing to eat enough collateral changes, the pragmatic tradeoffs when deciding what to sacrifice during an incident) rather than at generic corporate-speak, and someone who's never heard of Quine should still read it as a plausible engineering horror story. Keep the engineering details realistic too — the kind of stuff that could actually appear in a real RFC (SLOs, config schema validation, deprecation timelines, a migration appendix). The fail state I want to avoid is a document that's just Dilbert-style office satire with an analytic/synthetic paint job — the epistemology has to do the actual comedic work. If it helps, end with a vague next-steps section proposing to revisit the boundary question in Q3, which everyone reading will understand is never going to happen.
