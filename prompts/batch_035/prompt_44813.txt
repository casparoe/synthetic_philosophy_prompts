I'm a retired pharmacologist (35 years running self-control experiments, first in pigeons and rats, later with human subjects) who got seriously into philosophy after retiring, mostly through my daughter who is doing a PhD in it. Somehow I talked my way onto the schedule of the philosophy department's works-in-progress seminar, and next month I'm presenting the view that what philosophers call akrasia is mostly not a puzzle about rationality at all, but an artifact of hyperbolic temporal discounting. The preference reversals we saw in the lab pigeons — smaller-sooner reward chosen over larger-later when the choice is immediate, but the preference flips once both options are pushed equally far into the future — show up in human behavior just as reliably, and I want to argue that if you take that seriously, weakness of will mostly dissolves into ordinary intertemporal preference, plus maybe some failures of what Ainslie called bundling future choices into commitment devices.

The audience is a philosophy department, not a bunch of economists: a couple of action theorists who take Davidson's treatment of akrasia seriously, a Kantian ethicist, someone into experimental philosophy, some grad students, and a visiting scholar working on free will and addiction who I suspect knows the neuroscience better than I do. A bit more seeding in case it helps: I plan to say that the philosophical literature treats akrasia as a puzzle precisely because it's described in the language of practical judgment — judging it better, all things considered, to do one thing while doing another — whereas on my picture what flips over time isn't the judgment but the valuation, and valuations flipping as a function of delay is just what discount curves do, no faculty of will required. I also want to claim that consistent exponential discounting was never an empirical finding, it was an axiom borrowed from economics, so philosophers who treat hyperbolic preference reversals as somehow pathological have it backwards.
