I'm taking a course on interwar history and something about the Treaty of Versailles keeps bugging me and my study group couldn't sort it out, so I want to try it on you. So there's the famous "war guilt" clause, Article 231, which pins the responsibility for the war's losses and damage on Germany and its allies, and that clause was basically the legal peg the victors hung the reparations bill on. But here's the thing: the actual amount Germany owed wasn't fixed in the treaty at all — it was left to commissions to work out later, and the number they eventually settled on had a lot to do with what Germany was judged able to pay, not with how guilty anyone was. Meanwhile, over on the criminal side, the same treaty demanded trials for individual war criminals, the Allies handed over a list of ~900 names, the Dutch refused to give up the ex-Kaiser, and the whole thing got whittled down until about a dozen low-ranking defendants were tried in Leipzig in 1921, for pretty minor offenses. So you end up with a strange patchwork: a whole nation assigned responsibility as a nation, a handful of individuals prosecuted as individuals, and a bill whose size seems to track the debtor's pocketbook more than anyone's culpability.

What theory of responsibility does this whole setup actually presuppose? Like — does holding "Germany" responsible only make sense if you think a nation can itself be an agent, with intentions and blameworthiness over and above the intentions of its leaders and voters, or is it something weaker, more like each citizen owing a share of a collective debt regardless of what they did or supported in 1914? Can it coherently be both at once? And what do I say about the fact that a lot of the people actually paying through the 1920s, in taxes and inflation, were born after the war started or were kids who never voted for anyone — is that supposed to be liability transferring without guilt, sort of like how a company's debts survive when shareholders change? Is that a principled distinction or just a fudge the victors needed? I don't know the philosophy of collective responsibility literature at all, so feel free to hand me the vocabulary (whatever the standard terms are for the different views here), but I also want an honest verdict: was the Versailles arrangement philosophically incoherent underneath, or does it track a real distinction between guilt and liability that we're still quietly using today whenever a state pays reparations for something none of its current citizens did?
