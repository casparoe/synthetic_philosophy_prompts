i'm a phd student in decision theory and my reading group has spent a month on money pumps, and last week someone brought up a version of the argument that I can't shake. here it is as he gave it to me:

(1) If you're rational, no one should be able to money pump you: there shouldn't exist a sequence of trades, each of which you knowingly accept because it makes you better off by your own lights at the time, that leaves you strictly worse off than doing nothing.

(2) The classic pump works because your preferences are cyclic right now — the trader only needs to read off your current preference state. Fine, that part I accept. But notice the pump never really needed the cycle. Suppose instead that your preferences are perfectly transitive at every single moment, but they change over time: today you prefer A to B, and by next month — through no one's trickery — you'll prefer B to A, because you'll have gotten tired of A, or learned something, or just changed.

(3) If a trader knows this will happen — and nothing in (2) forbids her knowing it, lots of preference changes are completely predictable, ask anyone who's watched a friend go through a breakup — she can run the same pump: today take your B and hand you A plus a small fee (you accept, you prefer A), then next month offer to take A back for B plus another small fee (you accept, you now prefer B). You end up exactly where you started, minus two fees, having improved your position at every step.

(4) So being pump-proof requires more than transitivity at each moment. It requires that you never accept a trade your future self will want to undo.

(5) The only way to guarantee that, in advance and against all traders, is to have preferences that never change — or to refuse every trade that anticipates a future reversal.

(6) But changing preferences is what learning is. Getting over things. Recovering from grief, or from an addiction, or from being twenty-two. If (5) is right, all of it is a standing liability to exploitation, hence irrational.

(7) And if (6) is too much to swallow, then the money-pump project is in worse trouble than it looks: the standard defense of transitivity was 'be transitive or get pumped', and the same reasoning, run diachronically, delivers 'never change or get pumped'. So the defense proves too much.

He thinks (7) is a refutation of the money-pump argument for transitivity. I think something in there proves too much, but I can't find the seam. My instinct is that the trader in (3) is doing something the trader in the classic pump doesn't have to do — something about what she has to know, since reading off a current cycle is cheap and predicting a preference change is not — but when I said that he replied that knowledge is knowledge and an exploitable agent is exploitable, and honestly I don't have a crisp comeback. There's also something about how I could just refuse the first trade, the way you'd refuse to buy the sports car you already know you'll regret, some resolute-choice type move. But he says that's just another way of conceding (5), because refusing trades you'd enjoy now, on behalf of your future self, is exactly the never-change discipline the argument demands.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep (1) in some form — I don't want to give up on exploitability mattering — and I'd like to keep (6) obviously. If you think (4) is actually fine and diachronic exploitability just is a rationality requirement, tell me, because that seems to imply agents are only rational viewed from outside of time, which can't be right but I'd like to see the bill for saying so. And if you think I've misdescribed the classic pump, or that the commitment reply is doing something I haven't noticed, just say that plainly — I'd much rather be told the question is badly posed than get a polite tour of positions.
