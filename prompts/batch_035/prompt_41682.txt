Next month I'm defending the following thesis at a departmental colloquium: that the citizens of a state can inherit a distinctly political responsibility for wrongs their state committed before they were born, and that this responsibility is neither moral guilt nor a dressed-up metaphor for ordinary duties of justice. My positive story takes Jaspers's taxonomy of criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt from The Question of German Guilt and presses the political strand much harder than he did: the state persists as a corporate agent across generational turnover, so the liabilities attached to it persist too; the responsibility attaches to citizens qua participants in a continuing institution rather than as wrongdoers; it is defeasible in a way personal guilt is not — I claim it weakens with the realistic possibility of exit, with the fading of the institutional advantages that the historical wrong made possible, and with repair already undertaken; and it licenses demands for material repair and truthful remembrance but not demands for the remorse or self-reproflection appropriate to a culprit.

The audience is a mid-sized department, and I can tell you who is in the room. There is a Kantian who thinks responsibility talk without voluntary wrongdoing is a category error and who will want to know whether my corporate agent is anything more than a syllable-saving fiction for a lot of organized individuals. There is a historian of twentieth-century Europe who distrusts philosophers using postwar Germany as a laboratory and will press me on whether anything in my account actually depends on the historical record, or whether the historical example is just set dressing. There is a legal theorist working on reparations who will ask what inherited political responsibility adds to the straightforward claim that unjust enrichment tracks forward through a corporate entity's assets and could have been secured by ordinary legal means. There is a grad student working on structural injustice who will suspect my account is the social-connection model relabeled and will want to know why I need "the nation" at all rather than dispersed structures. And there is a scattering of metaethicists who will ask whether a non-culpatible responsibility is really a responsibility or just a duty in costume.

Prepare my Q&A sheet accordingly: the objections I'll actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they would do, phrased the way the person I've described would put them, each with a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, and what I should concede up front rather than fight for. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. For calibration, I'll tell you the move I'm least sure of myself: I suspect the exit condition is the weakest plank, since exit from citizenship is typically expensive enough that "defeasible upon exit" may be vacuous. And hold yourself to the same standard: tell me how confident you are that each objection will really come from the corner I've assigned it to rather than from someone else, and single out the reply you are least confident survives its strongest form.
