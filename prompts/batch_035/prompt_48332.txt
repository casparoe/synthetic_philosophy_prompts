In my circle of friends and family there is a rule nobody has ever stated but everyone enforces: you may state something as a fact if you've checked it yourself, or if it came to you in a way that gave you every right to be sure. Last year a friend of mine announced at dinner that our neighbor was selling her house. He had it from his brother-in-law, who turned out to have invented it — and then, by pure coincidence, the house did go on the market two months later. Everyone at the table agreed he had been out of line in saying it, even though what he said was true and no harm came of it. I've been trying to work out what the rule we were applying actually is. It can't be "only say what's true," since he did say what was true. It can't be "only say what you sincerely believe," since he believed it. My own guess is that the rule is something like "only say what you know," but I can't spell out what that amounts to, and I'm bothered by the fact that the same people happily pronounce, with no checking at all, that it always rains here in June, and nobody objects. So I have two questions. What does this everyday enforcement presuppose about when a person is entitled to state something as fact — and is it really one consistent rule, or several rules for different kinds of talk, some stricter and some looser? And what would our conversations look like if we openly adopted one of the rival rules instead — only truth, only sincere belief, only knowledge — including which of the three comes closest to how we actually live?
