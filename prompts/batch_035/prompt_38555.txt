I lead a monthly meditation group at a men's medium-security prison, and I've been asked to give the big talk at the year-end gathering -- the one event where everybody shows up: lifers, guys finishing short bids, the unit manager, the mostly-Christian chaplaincy volunteers, and a dozen serious practicioners who will absolutely notice if I fudge the doctrine.

Write the talk for me. Here's the knot I want it to actually untangle, not just decorate with.

A while back one of the guys -- call him D, he's doing 25-to-life for something he did at nineteen -- asked me whether he could transfer merit to his victim's family, like karmic carbon offsets: do enough good, send it in their direction, square the books. Part of me wanted to just say yes, because it's moving and it's keeping him going. But it sits weird with me. On the one hand, Buddhist karma talk can sound totally consequentialist -- acts have consequences that land on you later, so D's plan looks like a straightforward scheme of generating good consequences now to cancel out bad ones then. On the other hand, everything I actually teach says intention is the whole game: the precepts are about the mind you act from, and the tradition's favorite reformed murderer, Angulimala, gets told by the Buddha, basically, that the debt is already paid -- precisely because his mind changed. So which is it? Is merit-transfer bookkeeping or is it something else entirely? If intentions are what count, what exactly is D accumulating, and is the whole ledger metaphor the thing I should be pushing back on?

I don't want the talk to resolve into it's-both-paradox-wise-smile. The guys in that room can smell a dodge a mile away. Take an actual position on whether karma talk is consequentialist bookkeeping or intention-tracking (or a third thing), explain what that means for whether D can really give anything to his victims, and be honest about what a talk cannot fix -- some of the men in that room did things nobody in the room thinks can be squared.

A few practical notes: no sutra numbers or citations, the audience can't check them and I don't want to sound like a grad student (I am one, don't let it leak). Keep the chaplaincy volunteers comfortable -- it should read as Buddhist teaching, not as an argument that their thing is wrong. And don't use the phrase spiritual bypassing, the guys have heard it from me too many times already. About fifteen minutes spoken length.
