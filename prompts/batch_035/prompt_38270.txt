Nearly every wealthy country now runs its climate policy on a three-part mixture, and the parts sit together uneasily. First, emissions are priced — carbon taxes, cap-and-trade with auctioned permits, fuel duties — on the model of an externality to be internalized: raise the price and agents will substitute their way to less harm. Second, the state bypasses prices and issues direct commands: efficiency standards that ban products rather than making them expensive, subsidies for particular technologies (heat pumps, electric vehicles, solar), and outright prohibitions on practices regardless of anyone's willingness to pay. Third, and throughout, growth in GDP is treated as a constraint climate policy must respect: the official promise of nearly every national strategy is that decarbonization and continued growth are compatible, and a policy that visibly tanked growth would be abandoned no matter how much carbon it saved. Critics from the degrowth camp complain that this edifice quietly presupposes that climate change is a problem of relative prices and technology, not of aggregate throughput — the total scale of energy and material the economy moves — which no price signal and no efficiency gain can reduce while output grows.

Reconstruct the implicit theory. What view of value, and of the way environmental harm enters value, does this policy mixture presuppose? Does the pricing component presuppose that climate harms are commensurable with other goods in a common money-metric, that consumers can be left sovereign over substitutions, and that the relevant remedial logic is compensation-or-price rather than prohibition? And does the regulatory component presuppose the opposite — that some harms are not to be priced at all, that there are thresholds or incommensurabilities the externality framework cannot express? Consider whether the three parts are actually consistent with one underlying view (for instance, a sophisticated welfare economics that prices marginal harms but regulates where substitution fails or where harms are non-marginal), or whether the mixture reveals that the state does not really believe its own externality story and regulates where it distrusts the substitutions that prices would produce. Then say whether the growth target is an independent presupposition — that aggregate throughput is not itself the problem, only its carbon intensity — or merely a political constraint that no philosophical theory is committed to either way. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with: in particular, could a theory on which sustainability requires reduced throughput, not cleaner throughput, still license carbon pricing and technology subsidies as transitional instruments, so that the practice underdetermines the choice between green-growth and degrowth worldviews? Finally, describe what a policy built from the degrowth premise would have to change — which of the three components would be dismantled, which would survive in altered form, and what would have to be true empirically about decoupling for the change to be justified.
