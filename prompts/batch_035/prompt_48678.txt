I'm a retired chemist who got into philosophy of logic embarrassingly late in life, mostly via my son-in-law, who is a professional logician and a committed dialetheist -- he thinks the Liar sentence is both true and false, full stop. Two years of holiday dinners later I've dug myself into the paracomplete/gaps camp: my instinct is that the Liar is neither true nor false. We keep arguing past each other, and last month I read about adversarial collaborations in psychology, where two researchers on opposite sides write up what they actually agree on before designing tests. That's what I want from the two of us. So: draft the joint statement we should both be able to sign.

We agree on more than either of us admits at the table, I think. Neither of us accepts Tarski's hierachy of languages as an answer to the natural-language Liar (fine for formal arithmetic, no answer to what we say every day); both of us want to keep something like the T-schema -- the equivalence between p and the claim that p is true -- rather than quietly gutting the truth predicate; both of us think the move of just declaring truth not self-applicable is a cop-out. But I might be wrong about exactly what a dialetheist can sign onto, and that's part of your job: find the formulations each camp would accept the wording of, not the versions that flatter one side.

Then the disagreements, stated precisely: he says gluts, I say gaps, and I want it spelled out what exactly each camp says the Liar sentence does -- whether "not true" and "false" come apart the same way on both sides, and whether the strengthened Liar (this sentence is not true) is a serious problem for my camp or just for any single fixed-point treatment. Then the cruxes, since I want to know what would actually move one of us. I suspect one is Curry's paradox, which I've never fully digested: I understand his side has to give up or restrict modus ponens or contraction somewhere, and I honestly don't know whether my side faces a parallel cost -- write down what each of us should predict about how much classical reasoning survives in ordinary mathematical contexts, and what argument or case both of us would accept as bearing on it. Another crux is the revenge arguments: whether a neutral observer should conclude that each camp needs its own negation-ish operator that the other camp can't interpret, and what each of us would predict an impartial logician would say about that. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table (issue | his camp's wording | my camp's wording), then cruxes with each side's predictions recorded in advance, so we can stop rehashing the same ground at Thanksgiving. And if you think a chunk of our dispute is verbal rather than real, say which chunk and why. Keep it aimed at an interested amateur though: I know roughly my K3 from my LP at this point, but no proof sketches of structural rules please.
