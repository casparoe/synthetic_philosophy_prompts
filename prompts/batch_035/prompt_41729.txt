I help run a philosophy-of-language reading group for friends I originally met through the rationalist blogosphere — fifteen of us, mostly people who've internalized the standard material on scope errors, type/token distinctions, and semantics-vs-pragmatics, and who are therefore a bit smug about how easily we can dissolve puzzles. Next month's text is the School of Names, and I want to open with the white horse dialogue without letting anyone simply pattern-match it to "obvious equivocation, sophists gonna soph." Design a 75-minute activity that makes the group feel why the argument is harder to dismiss than it looks before they see the actual text.

Here's the idea I want the activity built around, though improve on it freely: a customs-gate role-play in an fictional empire. Travelers carry cards for animals — a black horse, a yellow horse, a white horse, a white ox, a mule, a bronze horse — and must pass a gate where an edict is posted: no horse may cross. Border clerks rule pass or impound and must give a one-line justification; rulings can be appealed to a judge, and inconsistent rulings cost the clerks points while successful smugglings pay the travelers. A second round should then change the stakes — say the edict is replaced by a tax of ten coins per horse, and the tax office simultaneously publishes a tariff listing a separate rate for white horses — so that whether a white horse is a horse stops being a matter of stipulation and becomes a matter of which principle the officials are applying: the extension of a term versus the conditions under which it correctly applies, shape versus color, what a request for a horse satisfies versus what a request for a white horse does.

The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the actual moves in the dialogue — the argument that "horse" picks out shape while "white" picks out color, and the argument that someone asking for a horse will accept a yellow or black one while someone asking for a white horse will not — and then say where the analogy between our game's legal fictions and Gongsun Long's terms holds and where it quietly cheats. When you discuss how the group should read the text afterwards, please keep two registers apart: flag which points about the dialogue are broadly settled among people who know the literature, and which are genuinely contested interpretive claims — for instance, how much work the grammar of classical Chinese terms is doing, and whether the piece is a serious contribution to a logic of language or a virtuoso display for court debaters — and don't launder one scholarly reading into the consensus. Also include your honest prediction of the two or three ways this predictably goes wrong the first time, and one thing about the original context — the adversarial disputers' culture, or whatever Gongsun Long was actually up to — that no classroom game can reproduce and I'll have to supply myself.
