I retired last year from a career in insurance adjusting, and to keep myself occupied I have been reading popular physics books, mostly ones about quantum mechanics. Somewhere along the way I kept encountering the claim that Schrödinger was deeply influenced by the Upanishads, so when I found a battered paperback of his little book My View of the World at an estate sale, I bought it on impulse. I have no training in philosophy and only the haziest sense of what Vedanta is, but the book has unsettled me more than anything I have read in years, and I want to make sure I am understanding one particular stretch of it correctly rather than fooling myself.

The part I keep returning to comes early, where he has you imagine sitting on a mountainside at dusk watching the light fade from the glaciers, and he asks, "What is it that has called you so suddenly out of nothingness to enjoy for a brief while a spectacle which remains quite indifferent to you?" He then goes on: "The conditions for your existence are almost as old as the rocks. For thousands of years men have striven and suffered and begotten and women have brought forth in pain. A hundred years ago, perhaps, another man sat on this spot; like you he gazed with awe and yearning in his heart at the dying light on the glaciers. Like you he was begotten of man and born of woman. He felt pain and brief joy as you do. Was he someone else? Was it not you yourself? What is this Self of yours? What was the necessary condition for making the thing conceived this time into you, just you and not someone else?" My problem is that I can read this two ways and I cannot tell which one Schrödinger intends. On one reading it is a piece of elevated rhetoric about how human experience repeats itself: someone sat there before me, someone will sit there after, and contemplating this stirs a feeling of kinship, but nothing more. On the other reading it is a serious, literal claim connected to the Upanishadic formula he invokes elsewhere in the book: that there is only one consciousness, that what we perceive as a plurality of separate minds is, as he puts it in another essay, "only an appearance; it is not real," and that the man on the mountainside a century ago literally was me, with the separateness of persons being a kind of mirage. I also cannot see what work the sentence about my "conditions" being almost as old as the rocks is supposed to do. Is the thought that because the causal chain producing me stretches back through countless generations, "I" cannot be pinned to one particular body, and if I cannot be pinned down, then the question of who was sitting there a hundred years ago simply loses its grip? That feels like it might be a sleight of hand — the fact that a chain of causes is very old does not obviously make its latest product identical to its earlier ones, any more than the age of a river makes two drops of water the same drop.

Could you help me work through this passage properly? What I would like is, first, a clear account of what claim Schrödinger is actually making and how the pieces are supposed to fit together — the old conditions, the man on the mountain, the question about the "necessary condition" for being me rather than someone else. Second, I would like to know whether the passage is meant to rest on anything mystical, or whether the whole thing can be reconstructed as a plain philosophical argument that a skeptical reader could evaluate step by step, and if so, where its weakest link is. Third, I am curious what an answer to his final question would even look like: is he suggesting that there simply is no fact of the matter about what makes this particular person me rather than someone else, and would that make the question dissolve rather than get answered? I should say up front that I do not want a reading list or references to look up — I have the book in hand and no appetite for scholarship. What I want is to come away understanding the passage well enough that I could explain it, and its possible weaknesses, to my brother-in-law over coffee.
