i'm writing a term paper on bounded rationality and i think i've managed to prove way too much, which usually means i've made a mess somewhere, but i can't see where. here's the argument, please actually engage with the steps and not just vibes.

(1) Satisficing, roughly in Simon's sense, says: don't search over all options and pick the best, search until you find one that clears your aspiration level, then take it and stop. the aspiration level is supposed to adjust to how easy or hard satisfaction turns out to be.

(2) now consider how that aspiration level gets set. either it's set by some kind of rational trade-off — weighing the expected gains of more search against the costs of search — or it isn't.

(3) if it is set by that trade-off, then choosing your aspiration level is just choosing the stopping rule that maximizes expected utility net of deliberation costs. a satisficer with a well-chosen aspiration level is therefore a maximizer wearing a costume, and the difference between satisficing and maximizing is a modeling convenience for us theorists, not a difference in the agent's rationality.

(4) if it isn't set by a trade-off, then it's basically arbitrary, and for literally any action you take there exists an aspiration level under which that action counts as satisficing, so satisficing imposes no constraint at all — it can rationalize anything after the fact.

(5) so satisficing is either disguised maximizing or empty. either way there's no third theory of rational choice sitting between full optimization and mere description of what limited agents happen to do, and the whole normative program of bounded rationality is a confusion. my advisor is very into this program, so, awkward.

i'm confident the conclusion is too strong — bounded rationality people clearly have real content in mind, stuff like ecological rationality and the argument that the optimization ideal is not even well defined for an agent who can't survey all options. but every time i try to poke my own argument i end up just re-asserting that the dilemma is false without showing which step is bad. my instinct says the trouble is somewhere around (2) through (4), the phrase "set by a trade-off" is quietly doing a lot of work, but i cant pin down the equivocation.
