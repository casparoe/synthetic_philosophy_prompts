Two policy researchers — one an advocate of the Nordic model of criminalizing the purchase but not the sale of sex, one an advocate of the New Zealand model of full decriminalization with occupational safety regulation — have been locked in the same argument for years: mostly about Sweden since 1999, Germany's 2002 legalization, the Prostitution Reform Act in New Zealand in 2003, and France's 2016 move to criminalize clients. Write the joint statement they should be able to produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both can sign, covering both the empirical ground — what these four regimes plausibly do and do not show, why cross-jurisdiction comparison is so hard (selection of which countries adopt which regime, concurrent changes like the rise of online platforms, the near-impossibility of measuring trafficking and hidden-market violence), and what advocates on both sides tend to overclaim — and the normative ground, including whatever moral claims both genuinely share about coercion, exit options, and the state's duties to sellers; (2) a disagreements table in which each disagreement is stated twice, once in wording the Nordic-model supporter would accept as fair to their view and once in wording the decriminalization supporter would accept as fair to theirs — including whether prostitution should be understood as violence grounded in gendered domination or as labor under economically constrained choice, and whether that framing is itself part of the dispute; (3) the cruxes: questions such that settling them would move at least one side, with at least one crux about what reduced client demand does to coerced and non-coerced sellers alike, and at least one about whether sellers' own risk-management practices (screening, working indoors, sharing flats) are protective adaptations that law should accommodate or symptoms that law should bypass; and (4) for each crux, a concrete comparison, development, or study both agree would bear on it — e.g., what each predicts sellers reported about violence, police treatment, and ability to refuse clients in the years after New Zealand's reform, and after France's 2016 law, with the predictions recorded so they could in principle be shown wrong. Finally, where any part of their dispute strikes you as verbal — definitional disagreements over "exploitation," "choice," or "demand" that dissolve once terms are fixed — say which part and why, and separate it from the substantive residue.
