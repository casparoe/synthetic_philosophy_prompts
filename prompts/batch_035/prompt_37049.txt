I'm a backend engineer, been working with ML infra for the last few years, and I've gotten a bit obsessed with a question I can't shake: if some computations matter morally (because they're, like, suffering or whatever) and others don't, what decides which ones? A rock running a physics sim of a brain presumably doesn't matter morally. An actual brain does. A trained neural net is somewhere in the middle and nobody agrees where. I wrote up my thoughts below as a draft essay-ish thing, and I'd love comments. I have no philosophy background at all so please be blunt about where I'm reinventing wheels badly or just confusing myself.

Draft (rough notes, not polished):

Title: "Moral weight is a distributed systems problem"

The core move I want to make: treat the question "which computations matter morally?" the way engineers treat blame assignment in a distributed system. When a request fails in a microservices setup, no single service "caused" it — we trace the request and assign weights across services based on counterfactuals: if this service had responded differently, would the outcome have changed? I want to say moral status attribution works the same way, except the "outcome" isn't a failed request, it's the instantiation of a valenced state (pain, distress, whatever), and the "services" are subcomputations inside a larger system.

Concretely: a mouse brain runs lots of subcomputations — motor control, olfaction, some predictive modeling, plus (we assume) states with negative valence when things go badly. The usual framing asks "is the mouse sentient?" — a binary question at the level of the whole animal. I think that's the wrong level, the same way asking "is the system healthy?" is the wrong level when what you want is which component is misbehaving. My claim: moral status isn't a property of systems but of subcomputations, and a system matters to the degree that it hosts computations that individually matter.

Where this gets interesting is what I call the "distillation problem" (open to better names). Suppose you take a system that definitely instantiates morally relevant computations and you gradually compress it — like model distillation, where a big teacher model is replaced by a smaller student that matches its behavior. At every step the behavior is preserved and the system is smaller. Was moral weight lost? If you say no, you're committed to rocks mattering (a rock can be *arranged* to preserve the behavior eventually, and certainly a lookup table can). If you say yes, it was lost at some point, then either there's a sharp cutoff in a smooth process — which seems insane — or moral weight is being diluted gradually, in which case it comes in continuous amounts attached to subcomputations, which is the view I'm pushing.

The objection I keep running into from myself: attribution via counterfactuals is a mess even in software. Counterfactual traces depend on what you hold fixed. If my service would have failed anyway because a downstream service times out regardless, does my bug "matter"? Similarly: if I ablate one of two redundant pain-realizing subcomputations in a brain and the animal shows no distress because the twin picks up the slack, does that mean neither mattered, or that each mattered conditionally on the other? Dependency structures that are redundant seem to break naive counterfactual attribution, and redundancy is everywhere in biological and artificial systems. So my "moral trace analysis" needs to say something about redundancy, and I genuinely don't know what it should say.

Second self-objection: this smells like it just relocates the hard problem. Instead of "which systems are sentient" we now ask "which subcomputations matter" — but with even less grip, since subcomputations don't come pre-carved (where does one end?). I have a hunch the response is that this is actually fine, that the boundary-drawing problem is an engineering problem we solve pragmatically all the time (module boundaries in code are also arbitrary at some level but we still ship), but I can't make that response feel satisfying rather than dismissive.

What I'd like from you:

1. Is the "attribution level" move (system-level question → subcomputation-level question) actually novel, or is this just functionalism stated in worse vocabulary? If the latter, where exactly did I go off the rails?
2. The distillation argument — do you buy that gradual compression forces either the rock conclusion or continuous moral weight? I feel like there's a version of this that's basically a slippery slope complaint dressed up, and I can't tell if it's me.
3. The redundancy problem is the part I care most about. Any principled way a counterfactual-attribution story handles redundant components? Doesn't have to be from the philosophy literature — I'd even take an analogy from fault-tolerance engineering if it does real work.
4. Be honest about whether the essay idea is interesting enough to write up properly or whether it collapses into something a competent philosopher would find trivial. I can take it.
