I should say up front that I have no philosophical training -- I'm a retired pharmacist, and everything I know about this comes from library books and long walks. But a personal experience has put a question to me that I can't set down, and I'd like you to answer it with an actual number rather than a balanced overview.

Some years ago I was on a medication that produced waking hallucinations, and at least once the hallucination was perfect: I watched a nurse enter my room, look at my chart, say a few words to me, and leave, and only later discovered that no nurse had come in at all. From the inside, at the time, I could find no difference whatsoever between that and ordinary seeing. Since then I've read what I can about the philosophy of perception, and I understand that my episode is exactly what fuels the argument from illusion: because a hallucination and a genuine perception can be subjectively indistinguishable, the two must share some common mental ingredient -- an inner picture, say -- and it is that ingredient, not the external world, that we are directly aware of even when things go well. I've also read that one reply, disjunctivism, denies precisely this: it holds that a veridical perception and a perfect hallucination, however alike they feel from the inside, share no fundamental mental component; they are simply different kinds of event, and the felt similarity doesn't go very deep.

Here is what I want: your credence that disjunctivism, so understood, is true. Please decompose it into the sub-questions it turns on, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each. Off the top of my head the terms would include at least: the probability that introspective indistinguishability does not guarantee a shared mental factor (i.e., that two things can feel exactly alike from inside yet differ fundamentally in mental kind); the probability that hallucination is a derivative or defective case whose nature depends on perception succeeding, rather than perception being a mere add-on to a mental state hallucination already exemplifies; the probability that treating the two as fundamentally alike leaves perceptual consciousness or its relation to the world harder rather than easier to explain; and any other terms you think genuinely belong. Then combine them into your overall number, and tell me which single term the final figure is most sensitive to, and what argument or consideration would move it most.

I also want you to weigh an observation of my own that I haven't found discussed anywhere, and which I suspect I'm reasoning about badly. My memories of the hallucinated nurse feel different from my memories of real events from the same period -- thinner, less connected to other memories, like a stage set rather than a street. I can see three readings: the difference was present in the experience itself and introspection simply missed it; the difference was manufactured afterwards, when I learned the event hadn't happened; or the difference belongs to memory rather than to perception. My instinct is that the first reading would support the disjunctivist -- if there was an accessible difference all along, the indistinguishability premise looks false -- but I can also see someone replying that a mere difference in memory or in underlying brain activity is something every theory already grants, and that the question was never about that level. Please tell me which of the three readings you find most likely, and whether your answer, under each reading, would move your credence on disjunctivism at all, and by roughly how much.
