At a recent meeting of my reading group, we spent the evening on John von Neumann's 1955 Fortune essay "Can We Survive Technology?" and two members, Dev and Maya, got into a dispute about what kind of document it actually is. I'll recount their exchange as faithfully as I can.

Dev opened by calling the essay straightforward technological fatalism. Von Neumann, he said, announces that the globe is in a rapidly maturing crisis because the environment in which technological progress must occur has become "undersized and underorganized," declares that we are, "literally and figuratively," "running out of room," and then concedes that the crisis "does not arise from accidental events or human errors" but is inherent in technology's relation to geography and political organization — and that when or how it ends, "nobody can say." By Dev's lights, this is a prophecy of inevitable catastrophe: if the crisis is structural, not a matter of accident or error, then no amount of wisdom averts it, and the modern debate about powerful AI is simply the latest act of the tragedy von Neumann already scripted.

Maya replied that this misreads the essay's logic. Von Neumann's diagnosis, she said, is a mismatch between two things that evolve at different rates: our technical capabilities, which accelerate, and our political organization, which lags. That is why he calls the environment "undersized and underorganized" — the deficiency is on the organizational side of the ledger, which is a fact about us, not a fact about technology that dooms us. And his prescription follows from the diagnosis: he is skeptical of restraining progress, and his counsel is to proceed with deliberateness, flexibility, and judgment. Whatever that is, Maya said, it isn't fatalism; the title poses a genuine question, not a verdict.

Dev pushed back that Maya can't have it both ways. If the crisis is inherent — if it is structural rather than a matter of "accidental events or human errors," as von Neumann puts it — then in what sense is the situation steerable at all? Structural crises end when their structure ends, and von Neumann offers no account of how the political side catches up. Worse, Dev continued, the advice to steer with judgment is vacuous: telling statesmen to be flexible and patient is what every cautionary essay since Plato has said, and it distinguishes von Neumann's position from nothing.

Maya answered that the advice is not vacuous if it rules things out, and she claimed it rules out two specific reflexes: the impulse to halt or throttle the disruptive technology, and the impulse to act precipitously out of panic — the belief, as she put it, that when the pace of change outruns our institutions, the answer is to slow the change rather than to grow the institutions. Whether von Neumann's confidence that we can do the latter was warranted is a fair question, she conceded; but that is a question about whether he was right, not about whether he was a fatalist, and on the second question the text is unambiguous.

They eventually asked me to referee, and I found I couldn't. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, and justify the rating. Then tell me who is more right overall, and whether there is anything of consequence that both of them miss about the essay.
