A friend of mine, Devan, has spent eleven years as a quality-control taster at a coffee roastery. When he was hired he genuinely loved the flagship dark roast; today the same coffee strikes him as flat and ashy, and he insists the enjoyment he used to take in it is simply gone. Last week two of his colleagues — a sensory scientist named Priya and a philosophy-trained lab manager named Marcus — argued over what exactly happened to Devan, and I couldn't follow all of it.

(1) Priya opened by insisting that only two things could have changed: Devan's private experiences themselves — the taste qualia — or his standards, the criteria he applies in judging an unchanged experience. Qualia-change or standard-change; those are the options.

(2) Marcus replied that the dichotomy is broken before anyone chooses between its horns: it treats experiences as inner objects with identity conditions of their own, and no one has ever specified what it takes for one of these objects to count as the same experience had on two occasions, or as one experience changing rather than one ending and another beginning. Until that is fixed, the question whether Devan's qualia changed is not open or closed but ill-posed — and the fact that we cannot answer it is itself some evidence that qualia were a bad posit in the first place.

(3) Priya said there is an obvious test: Devan remembers liking the coffee, so let him compare the present taste with the memory. If the two differ, the qualia changed; if they match and he now judges the coffee inferior anyway, the standards changed.

(4) Marcus said this relocates the problem rather than solving it: the memory is itself a present-tense representation, plausibly contaminated by whatever process altered his standards, so it cannot serve as an independent benchmark. He added that the dichotomy also ignores a third possibility: Devan's discrimination may have sharpened. Perhaps the ashy note was always in the cup and the young Devan simply could not detect it — no change in qualia, no change in standards, just better resolution.

(5) Priya countered that at least that third option is empirically tractable: run Devan through forced-choice discrimination tests against similar roasts he used to confuse. If he now sorts them reliably, something real and measurable changed.

(6) Marcus agreed the tests were worth running, but held that they would show only what his capacities are; the question whether the experiences themselves changed would sit exactly where it sat before, which, he said, was his point all along.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is sound, confused, or somewhere in between, and justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything both parties are missing about what it would take for their disagreement to be settled — or whether, as Marcus claims, it was never a well-formed disagreement to begin with.
