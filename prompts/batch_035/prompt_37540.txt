I help run a little philosophy-of-literature reading group — mostly grad students plus a couple of published fiction writers who enjoy fighting about this stuff — and next session is on authorial intention and the meaning of a text. I want to open with a case I've made up, but made up properly: engineered so the theories actually come apart, instead of me just stipulating that they do. Build me a thought experiment to these specs.

The scenario itself: one paragraph, realistic, contemporary literary fiction — no impossible Borgesian texts, no authors conveniently dying mid-sentence, no manuscript that reads differently to different people. Somewhere in it there should be one interpretively loaded detail (an image, a repeated phrase, a structural choice — your call) that is not ambiguous as written; the disagreement has to come from around the text, not from the text being fuzzy.

The verdict pattern I want: strict actual intentionalism (the author's actual intention, however we got hold of it, fixes the meaning) and value-maximization (the reading that makes the work best fixes it) should land on the same meaning, while hypothetical intentionalism (what an ideal reader working from the public record would attribute to a hypothetical author) and a moderate intentionalism on which only intentions successfully recoverable from the work and its context confer meaning (usually associated with Levinson — look, I might be misremembering, describe it however is accurate) should land on a different one. So a clean 2-2 split — but with each pair agreeing for its own reason, not by accident. I'll notice if the value-maximizer and the strict intentionalist only agree because I rigged the case so the author's intention happens to be brilliant.

For the split to be generated rather than smuggled in: the actual intention must be recoverable only through private evidence (notebooks, letters, an abandoned draft — invent the specifics), while everything public points the other way. So: author is alive but a notorious interview-misleader who flatly refuses to discuss this detail; the detail has a buried history (cannibalized from an abandoned project where it meant something else, and the author only later noticed the new resonance and quietly went with it). You'll have to decide whether that "going with it" counts as a fresh intention, and tell me where in the timeline each theory locates the meaning-fixing moment — I suspect that timing question is doing more work than people assume, but check.

Then the walkthrough: what each of the four theories says about the case, why, and exactly which feature of the design each verdict gets its grip on. After that, name the single feature doing the work — is it the private/public evidence divide, or actually the timing question, or something else I haven't spotted? Then two stress tests: what happens to the split if the notebooks get published posthumously and the hypothetical reader's evidence set expands to include them; and how the moderate intentionalist would argue the case is rigged against them — in particular whether "recoverable in context" should mean recoverable by the actual flesh-and-blood audience or by an idealized one, and how that rereading redraws the 2-2 into something else. Same question mirrored for the hypothetical intentionalist: is their ideal reader allowed to weigh how misleading authors tend to be in interviews, and if so does that fix the case or break the theory's whole epistemic setup?

Honesty clause: at the end, tell me which verdict assignment you're least confident in and what would change your mind about it — what a defender of that theory would have to say before you'd concede they were never really on the wrong side of the split. And flag anywhere the case cheats: any spot where I've smuggled a verdict into the setup instead of earning it from the theories, or where two of my four theories secretly collapse into each other given the details you chose. If they do collapse, say so and tell me what detail would have kept them apart.
