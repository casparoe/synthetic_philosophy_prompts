I've been reading rationality blogs and forums for the better part of a decade, and one slogan that keeps resurfacing in arguments there is 'language shapes thought'. Most recently it turned up in a thread about whether English, with its ambiguity and idiom-riddled grammar, makes people systematically worse at clear reasoning than they would be in some logically engineered alternative. One commenter was quite serious about eventually doing his thinking in a constructed language like Lojban precisely on these grounds, and treated the slogan as though it were an established scientific result that settled the matter. Others dismissed it as postmodern nonsense. I have the suspicion that both camps were arguing about different claims while using the same five words, and I'd like to untangle them before I wade in.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that 'language shapes thought' could stand for, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical? I can dimly see that some readings are platitudes no one would deny — presumably any language lets you communicate, and vocabulary affects what's easy to say — while others are substantive and maybe empirically supported, and at least one reading is the old strong determinism claim on which, if your language has no word for a concept, you literally cannot entertain it. For each reading, tell me whether it's plausible, whether the actual psycholinguistic evidence (colour terminology, spatial reasoning, grammatical gender, that sort of thing) bears on it at all, and — crucially — which reading is being invoked when someone uses the slogan to argue that switching to a logically regular language would improve one's everyday reasoning.

I'd also like to know what the slogan would have to mean for my fellow forum-goer's project to make sense. If language merely nudges attention and habitual categories, it's unclear why Lojban would help with, say, noticing when an argument equivocates, rather than just making certain sentences harder to state sloppily. And if some strong reading is true, does that cut against his project in unexpected ways — for instance by making English-thought and Lojban-thought partly incommensurable, so that he couldn't recognise the improvement? Please be explicit about which readings would support the slogan being used as an argument-ender, and which would leave the interesting questions exactly where they were.
