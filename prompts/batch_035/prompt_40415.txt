My friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about whether a nation, as opposed to its individual members, can be guilty, and whether such guilt can reach people born long after the crimes in question. I'll try to state each move as its proponent actually made it, not as I now judge it.

(1) I opened from ordinary practice: Germany is routinely said to carry a burden for the Holocaust, and its institutions keep apologizing for it — official statements, reparations, duties of remembrance. If everyone accepts that a polity can owe something for crimes committed before most of its current members were born, it looks as though guilt attaches to the collective itself, not merely to dead individuals. (2) He replied that I was confusing guilt with responsibility: being guilty is a predicate that requires a wrongful act by an agent who could have done otherwise, and no one born in 1970 could have done otherwise about 1943. What the later-born inherit are duties of remembrance and repair, which are real, but they are not guilt — calling them guilt is a category error that debases a word reserved for wrongdoers. (3) I answered that the cases pull apart: we already make sense of liabilities inherited by people who did nothing to incur them, since an estate can be answerable for the debts of the deceased; if heirs can owe money they never borrowed, it is not obvious why heirs of a polity cannot owe remorse and repair they never earned. (4) He said the analogy fails exactly where it matters: debt is transferable because money is impersonal, whereas guilt is essentially indexed to the agent's own will, so inherited guilt can only be a metaphor — and taking it literally produces the outcome Jaspers most feared in 1946, namely that once all are said to be guilty in the same way, the distinctions that matter vanish and the genuinely culpable hide in the crowd. (5) I conceded the dilution worry but offered a counterexample: a polity that deliberately perpetuates itself as the same agent — teaching its children a sanitized history, retaining the victims' property, defining membership by descent from the perpetrator generation — has made itself a continuing accomplice after the fact, so at that point the later-born members are not inheriting guilt at all; they are incurring it. (6) He closed by observing that in my own example it is again particular people — curriculum writers, officials, property holders — who incur guilt, and that the nation never shows up as a distinct wrongdoer: the collective is just the site where individual wrongs accumulate, and inflating it into a guilty super-person is one of the ways states launder their members' blame.

We ran out of evening rather than arguments. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating. Then assess whether there is a position neither of us occupied that would have dissolved the dispute, and if so, what it costs to hold it.
