I came across a book review, in a popular science magazine, of a philosophy book about decision theory, and it contained a paragraph summarizing what it called risk-weighted expected utility theory, associated with the philosopher Lara Buchak. It went roughly like this:

"Classical expected utility theory says that a rational agent chooses among gambles by multiplying the utility of each outcome by its probability and summing. Buchak's theory was built to make room for risk-aversion, and her key innovation is to replace the ordinary probability weights with a risk function that downweights improbable good outcomes while leaving improbable bad ones at full weight. Because the risk function is a matter of individual taste, like a fondness for jazz, the theory ends up permitting any risk attitude at all: there is no longer any principled way to criticize someone who buys both lottery tickets and insurance. Her central evidence is the Allais paradox, where people systematically violate the independence axiom, which she takes to show that the axiom is simply false and that anyone who maximizes expected utility is making an error. And in the end, since the risk function can absorb all of a person's cautiousness, the theory quietly reduces risk-aversion back to the old idea of diminishing marginal utility of money."

Something about the last sentence struck me as odd, because I had the impression that separating risk-attitudes from the shape of the utility curve was supposed to be the whole point of the view. But the reviewer writes with great confidence, and I don't know decision theory well enough to audit the paragraph myself.

Could you go through it clause by clause and tell me which parts a defender of the theory would accept, which are defensible simplifications of the technical machinery, and which are genuine misrepresentations? In particular, I would like to know: (1) whether the description of the risk function as downweighting improbable good outcomes while leaving improbable bad ones untouched is a fair rendering, or whether the risk function operates on something other than raw probabilities of individual outcomes; (2) whether the theory really holds that any risk attitude is beyond criticism, or whether it imposes structural constraints of its own that rule out some patterns of preference; (3) what her actual stance is on the independence axiom, and whether she thinks agents who satisfy ordinary expected utility maximization are irrational; and (4) whether the reviewer's final claim, that the theory collapses back into diminishing marginal utility, follows from anything accurate earlier in the paragraph or instead contradicts the theory's motivation. If possible, close by giving a corrected version of the paragraph, in roughly the same number of words, that a theorist in this tradition would sign off on.
