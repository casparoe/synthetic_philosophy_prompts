I'm taking a historiography seminar built around Braudel's Mediterranean, and my professor leans constantly on his image of the sea to structure the whole course: events are the foam and spray on the surface, conjunctures are the tides and swells, and the longue durée — geography, climate, the deep structure of trade routes and settled land — is the sea itself. The metaphor isn't decorative in her hands; it does real argumentative work. Because foam is thrown up by the sea but contributes nothing back to the currents, she concludes that event-history and the history of individuals are epiphenomenal: explaining the Dutch Revolt by William of Orange, or the Second World War by Hitler's decisions, is like explaining the shape of a wave by pointing at the spray. The deep layer explains everything and is explained by nothing the surface does. She's even extended it to the present, arguing that whatever we think of as recent political upheavals is surface noise on slow structural currents — demography, energy regimes, sea lanes — and that a historian who spends her career on events is doing meteorology, not oceanography.

I'm supposed to write my term paper on whether the surface really is causal in Braudel, and I've come to suspect the sea image is smuggling in the very conclusion it's supposed to support. For one thing, a real sea's depths don't respond to what happens at the surface, but Braudel's deep layer demonstrably does get reworked by "events": the Suez Canal and Panama Canal rewrote his beloved maritime routes in an afternoon of engineering; the printing press, nuclear weapons, and the internet seem like short-run happenings with longue-durée consequences; climate itself is now being altered by decisions taken within a single human lifetime. For another, the metaphor suggests the layers have different physical status — the deep layer is a thing, the surface merely its disturbance — whereas one might argue they're just different zoom levels of the same causal fabric, with no ontological depth charge at all. And there's a normative edge to it too: calling individual actors "foam" quietly demotes them, which the historical facts alone don't do.

So I'd like a proper audit of the analogy rather than the rallying-cry version we get in seminar. Please give me an explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the sea, the currents, the swell, the wave, the foam, the swimmer or the ship, and whether anything in the picture plays the role of a storm, which after all is neither surface nor depth but a third thing passing through both. Then the disanalogies: which of them are harmless (irrelevant to the use the metaphor is being put to) and which are load-bearing for the claim that events are causally idle? In particular, I want to know whether the cases where events demonstrably rewrite the deep layer — canals, epidemics, technological ruptures — count as counterexamples to Braudel's picture or can be absorbed as boundary phenomena, and what it costs the framework to absorb them. Finally: does my professor's conclusion — that historians of events are doing something epistemically lesser — survive once the metaphor is audited, or was the metaphor carrying it the whole time? And if the sea is the wrong picture, is there a better analogy for multiple interacting timescales where fast processes can reconfigure slow ones (I've been toying with erosion and earthquakes, or a river cutting a new channel, but I can't tell if these improve anything or just relocate the problem)?
