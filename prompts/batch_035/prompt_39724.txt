I'm a second-year PhD student in combinatorics, and I'm weighing a switch into alignment-flavored theory work — specifically the project, pushed by a small community of mathematically inclined researchers over the past few years, of formalizing "heuristic arguments" and the "presumption of independence." Since you may know this area only loosely, here is my understanding of the project, and I'd be grateful if you took this framing as given rather than reconstructing it from scratch. We can't hope to prove that a powerful AI system is safe in full generality; any such proof would need to rule out every bad strategy, including ones we can't enumerate. The fallback hope is instead to exhibit, for each specific dangerous strategy, a *heuristic argument* that the system won't pursue it — an argument weaker than a proof but stronger than an intuition, something that filters possibilities down using reasoning we can check even if the system being analyzed is smarter than us. The presumption of independence is the default assumption that a group of AI systems won't stumble on some joint strategy — a way of coordinating or colluding that produces a catastrophe — that our best analysis of the individual systems fails to anticipate. The dream is a formal object, something like a restricted argument system or a short piece of dominating evidence, such that we can find it, verify it, and know in a precise sense that the system's actual behavior is constrained by it.

The open questions I've managed to map out from the margins are roughly these. (1) What formal object *is* a heuristic argument? Candidates include circuits computing conditional expectations of arbitrary functions, probabilistic programs whose inferences dominate the analyzed system's inferences, and proofs in deliberately weak theories — each of which seems to capture part of the intuition and fail on another part. (2) The adversarial problem: a misaligned system can produce arguments that look heuristic to us, so the account needs a non-arbitrary fact of the matter about which arguments genuinely constrain the system, and it's not obvious where that fact could live. (3) The gap between toy domains — reasoning about a SAT solver's outputs, or finite boolean functions where "heuristic argument" can be made crisp — and anything resembling deep learning. (4) Whether the presumption of independence is even a sensible default, or whether boundedly-rational systems are better than any bounded analysis at finding coordination strategies. (5) Tractability: a heuristic argument is only useful if it's vastly cheaper to find and check than the behavior it's about, or we could just run the model. (6) The enumeration problem: even if we can handle each bad strategy piecemeal, does anything ensure the strategies compose into a manageable list?

Assume I have two to three years, strong training in combinatorics and complexity theory, essentially no ML experience, and a willingness to learn only what the work demands. Which of these problems — or something I haven't listed — should I actually work on, in what order, and why? I want a defended ranking, not a menu: say which of these is upstream of which, which are PhD-scale tractable versus decade projects in disguise, which are neglected enough that a single person moves them, and what developments over the next few years would reshuffle the list. Please commit to a definite ordering and, if you'd cut one of my six framings entirely as confused or misplaced, say so and explain what should replace it.
