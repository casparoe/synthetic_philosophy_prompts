I'm putting together a first-year seminar on social epistemology, and next term I want to open the herding unit with "The Emperor's New Clothes" before we go anywhere near the formal models of information cascades. The obvious reading is that Andersen gives you a picture-book version of a Bayesian cascade: each courtier looks at the loom, recieves a private signal — nothing there — but conditions on everyone before him having publicly admired the cloth, and concludes that the rational move is to say he can see it too. So the whole court ends up endorsing a falsehood, and no one in the chain is actually being stupid — that last bit matters to me, I don't want a handout that reduces the story to "don't follow the crowd", because individually the courtiers' reasoning is arguably fine.

What I'd like is a proper critical reading that pushes on whether the tale is even about an informational cascade, or something else that the models have to be bent to fit. The tailors' setup — the cloth being invisible to anyone unfit for his office or unusually stupid — means the courtiers aren't purely inferring from others' behaviour; they also face a cost for deviating that has nothing to do with what they believe. So is the story better modelled as reputational herding, or even as pluralistic ignorance (everyone privately sees nothing, publicly conforms because each assumes he'd be alone in dissent)? And then the child: in the cascade literature cascades are famously fragile — a few publicly observed deviants can topple them — so it's tempting to read the child as just that mechanism in miniature. But he isn't really a brave deviant who privately disbelieves and speaks up anyway; he plausibly never runs the social inference at all, doesn't know enough to be afraid of looking dim, and reports the naked truth as though there were no audience whose opinions he should be weighting. Is that epistemic innocence or just a different information structure, and can something that functions as a failure of rationality be the thing that rescues everyone else's?

Two things I want the reading to take seriously rather than paper over. First, where the tale resists the model: at the end the emperor himself suspects he's been had and keeps walking anyway, head held higher, chamberlains holding up the train that isn't there — a persistence of conformity among the principals that the crowd's cascade-breaking apparently can't touch, and which I don't think the standard herding machinery says much about, since his payoff is political rather than epistemic. Second, whether Andersen adds something to the debate that the formal papers don't give you — maybe something about the asymmetry between cracking a cascade among bystanders and cracking it among the people with their necks on the line. It's for a seminar handout so no citations or quotes needed, just the reading, and I'd like it honest about the points where the story and the economics genuinely pull against each other.
