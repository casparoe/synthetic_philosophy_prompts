I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, but I listen to philosophy podcasts on my commute and one of them talked about something called the pessimistic meta-induction: that because pretty much every past scientific theory eventually got replaced, we should expect our current ones to be replaced too, and so shouldn't believe them true. The standard realist comeback, as I understand it, is that some theories were "approximately right" or "got the structure right" even though the entities or mechanisms got tossed. The problem is that when I try to sort particular historical theories into "on to something" versus "merely predicting well," my own verdicts don't look like they come from one rule, and I'd like someone to tell me whether they do.

Here are my verdicts, from thinking about this the way I think about judging whether a deprecated system's design is worth salvaging before a rewrite:

1. Fresnel's wave theory of light: the luminiferous ether it presupposed turned out not to exist, yet Fresnel's equations for diffraction basically survived into modern optics. Verdict: on to something important.

2. Caloric theory: there is no fluid of heat, but Fourier derived his heat equation while working within caloric theory, and we still use that equation daily. Verdict: on to something, and it feels like the same "something" as in case 1 — the math survived even though the ontology died.

3. Ptolemaic astronomy: the epicycle machinery predicted planetary positions well for centuries, and Copernicus himself kept small epicycles. Verdict: NOT on to something — it was sophisticated curve-fitting, and the fact that its predictions were good for its day doesn't make it even approximately true.

4. Phlogiston theory: I'm honestly sympathetic to it in a way I'm not to Ptolemy — phlogiston was a real attempt at a conservation-style story about combustion, and something in that neighborhood survived. Verdict: on to something, at least a little, even though essentially no equation or quantitative structure survived from it.

5. The Bohr atom: there are no electrons hopping between fixed circular orbits, and the picture is strictly false. Verdict: not on to something in the truth sense, but a genuinely useful ladder — worth teaching, not worth believing.

6. Mendel's theory of the gene: the particulate inheritance story was basically right, and the gene turned out to be a real thing, even though Mendel himself had the wrong picture of what genes were physically made of and where they lived. Verdict: on to something in a stronger way than any case above.

7. Darwin's original theory: blending inheritance had to be thrown out, the picture of heredity was wrong, and the modern synthesis changed a lot — yet I can't make myself say natural selection was "approximately true" the way the ether equations were "approximately right"; it feels true, full stop, from the beginning. Verdict: on to something in a way that doesn't fit neatly with cases 1 and 2 at all.

So here is what I want. First, tell me whether there is a single principle about what a theory "gets right" that delivers all seven verdicts — I have in mind candidates like "retention of mathematical structure," "retention of the central entity or mechanism," "novel predictive success," or some combination — and be concrete about which clause handles which case. My suspicion is that no clean principle works, and I can already see the trouble: cases 1–2 suggest the rule is "the equations survived," but that rule condemns phlogiston (case 4), which I want to save, and it would seem to elevate Ptolemy's predictive machinery too, since some of the computational apparatus carried over to Copernicus — which I absolutely refuse to accept. Second, tell me exactly which of my verdicts has to go if we insist on a simple principle, and which principle that would be. Third, apply whatever principle you settle on to two cases I haven't listed: the current standard model of particle physics (suppose it gets superseded the way everyone expects — was it on to something?), and, harder, dark matter — where we have eighty-odd years of gravitational anomaly-fitting with the entity still unidentified, which looks uncomfortably like either phlogiston or Ptolemy depending on how the story ends. And if your principle gives different verdicts on dark matter depending on whether the entity is eventually found versus the theory being rewritten without it, say so, because that bears on whether the principle is about our current theories or only about how history will have gone.

One constraint on framing: I don't want the answer to be "verdict 3 is just prejudice against geocentrism." Maybe it is, but if so, show me what principled distinction between prediction-machine theories and structure-getting theories my Ptolemy verdict could be recovering, and whether the phlogiston verdict can be saved by the same move. I'd rather repair the verdicts into a consistent set than be told they're arbitrary — but tell me honestly if some of them are.
