I am preparing a seminar session for our epistemology graduate course on the norms of assertion, and I would like your help with a thought experiment we will run as part of it. Suppose we could bring J. L. Austin back, brief him thoroughly on the last six decades of philosophy of language, and then put him in front of the contemporary debate about which norm governs assertion: the knowledge norm defended in Williamson's Knowing and Asserting (Philosophical Review, 1996), the truth norm, or the various justification- and belief-based alternatives. Austin died in 1960, before this debate took shape, but he seems to me a uniquely interesting figure to project into it, given that he both theorized about assertion as a speech act and, in his 1946 paper Other Minds, argued that an utterance of "I know" functions rather like a performative — that in saying it, a speaker gives something like a guarantee, in the way that saying "I promise" places one under an obligation, rather than merely reporting an inner condition. My question, then: what position do you think Austin would take on the norm-of-assertion debate, and why?

In developing your answer, I would like you to engage with at least the following considerations. First, Williamson conceives of the norm of assertion as a constitutive rule of the speech act, analogous to the rules of a game, and holds that knowledge is the rule that best explains the full range of our critical practices — for instance, why it is peculiarly fitting to respond to an assertion by asking how the speaker knows, and why assertions that turn out false are retrospectively criticized even when reasonable when made. Would Austin's much-discussed discomfort with the idea of a sharp constative/performative distinction make him sympathetic to the thought that assertion has a constitutive rule at all, or would he instead treat the demand for a single norm as an instance of the philosophical pathology he spent his career diagnosing — the insistence that, because we have a single noun, there must be a single unified act or phenomenon for it to name? Second, how would Austin react to the stock counterexamples to the knowledge norm, for example the physician who asserts the test result recorded on a chart while privately doubting it, or the lottery cases where it seems one may truthfully assert that a ticket will lose while failing to know it? Would he see these as principled counterexamples, or as artifacts of philosophers' tendencies to describe utterances in artificial contexts stripped of the ordinary settings that give them their point? Third, I want to press you on a somewhat unusual angle my co-teacher and I have been toying with: there is a case to be made that Austin's performative treatment of "I know" would support a knowledge norm, but from an entirely different direction than Williamson's — not as a constitutive rule of the act of assertion, but as something undertaken by the speaker from within the act, on particular occasions, rather than imposed on all asserters from outside. In other words, the guarantee reading might make the knowledge connection something a speaker incurs rather than a rule the speech act carries, and this would locate Austin closer to the view that norms are敏感 to what a speaker does with an utterance in context than to the view that they are fixed by the act type itself. I would appreciate your assessment of whether this Austinian route to a knowledge-like norm is textually plausible, merely anachronistic, or both — and, if you think he would reject the whole framing of the debate, what his alternative would look like and what it would predict about the standard cases. Please be explicit about where you are extrapolating beyond what Austin actually wrote and about the degree of confidence you would attach to each conjecture.
