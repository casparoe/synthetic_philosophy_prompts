I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and for most of that time my closest friend from that community has been slowly converging, over shared dinners and long comment threads, on a stable set of verdicts about when an individual's contribution to a collective harm is wrong. I now want to predict her verdict on a new case, and I'd like your help thinking it through.

Her verdicts so far, with the reasons she gave where she gave reasons:

1. Buying one chicken breast at a large supermarket: not wrong. Her purchase does not change how many birds are raised or slaughtered; the store reorders by the pallet.
2. Buying chicken from the small butcher near her, who adjusts each day's order to the previous day's sales: wrong. There her single purchase plausibly shifts the number of birds ordered by one.
3. Dropping a single bottle in a park that is already visibly littered: wrong. She insists this has nothing to do with whether her bottle changes whether the park is trashed; it is wrong because it free-rides on everyone else's restraint.
4. Skipping a national election when she has only a mild preference: fine. Voting third party in a national election when she genuinely prefers the third-party candidate and only slightly prefers one major candidate to the other: also fine — she holds that a mild preference between the majors creates no strategic obligation, and she would tell anyone similarly situated to vote their conscience.
5. Driving to work instead of taking a train twenty minutes slower: fine, on the same reasoning as the supermarket chicken.

The new case is her town's council race. Polling and the margins in comparable races suggest the two major candidates will finish within a few dozen votes of each other. The third-party candidate she genuinely prefers polls around eight percent, and she slightly prefers one of the majors over the other. Will she vote third party with a clean conscience, or has her own pattern of verdicts trapped her into calling that vote wrong?

Please do four things. First, lay out the candidate rules that fit her track record equally well — I can see at least a marginal-causation rule, on which only plausibly pivotal contributions are wrong, and a free-riding rule, on which contributing to a collective harm is wrong only when it exploits others' restraint — and consider whether some hybrid that treats elections differently from markets also fits. Second, say what each rule predicts about the council race. Third, give your best guess at her actual verdict, with your reasons for preferring that reading of her. Fourth, name the further case — the one you would pose to her over dinner — that would most cleanly separate the rules that currently fit her record, and state what each rule would predict about it.
