I run a philosophy club at my high school — about fourteen students, one-hour meetings, no philosophy background required — and next week I want to build a session around a problem I came across called the Sleeping Beauty problem. Here is the setup as I understand it: Beauty agrees to an experiment. On Sunday night a fair coin is flipped while she sleeps. If it lands heads, she is woken once, on Monday. If it lands tails, she is woken on Monday and again on Tuesday, and after each waking she is given a drug that erases her memory of it, so each waking feels to her exactly like the first. Whenever she wakes, she's asked: what is your credence that the coin landed heads? Some people say one half, because she knew the coin was fair and learned nothing she didn't already expect; some say one third, because there are three wakings she could be in (heads-Monday, tails-Monday, tails-Tuesday) and only one of them involves heads.

I'd like you to design an in-class game that makes my club members feel why this is genuinely hard, before anyone tells them the standard positions. The trouble I keep hitting: I can't actually drug anyone, so how do I reproduce the amnesia? My idea was something like cards and envelopes — students take turns being "Beauty," the coin is flipped out of their sight, and a facilitator hands them an envelope telling them which day it is, with the setup arranged so they genuinely can't tell Monday from Tuesday. But I'm not sure how to do the payoffs. Should each Beauty wager on heads at each waking, with the payoff per waking (which seems to favor one answer) or per experiment (which seems to favor the other)? I suspect the betting structure changes what the "right" answer looks like, and I'd like the game to expose that rather than bury it.

So please give me: concrete rules and materials I can actually prepare in an evening, a payoff scheme that works without real money, the debrief that maps what happened onto the one-half and one-third positions, and an honest note on what my card-and-envelope version fails to reproduce about the original problem — especially whether the "wakings" being distinct people in a room, who can watch each other and compare notes afterwards, quietly breaks the self-locating part of the puzzle. Also, please predict what will go wrong the first time I run it. I'd rather not hear the terms "halfer" and "thirder" until the debrief, and neither should they.
