In three weeks I present a paper at a two-day workshop on diachronic rationality, and I expect the room to be against me before I finish my opening slide. The audience: roughly twenty formal epistemologists, Bayesian to the bone, who know the Dutch book literature inside out and will speak in the vocabulary of betting books, scoring rules, and calibration; two or three decision theorists who will treat any slide from "epistemic norm" toward "prudential norm" as an admission of defeat; and a couple of philosophers of science who regard pragmatist moves in epistemology as a confidence trick. No one there is sympathetic to my thesis.

The thesis: Reflection is not a norm of epistemic rationality at all. It is a norm of self-trust. It binds an agent only when she regards her epistemic future as her own — as the conduct of a continuant she is entitled to rely on — and the standard counterexamples, such as the agent who knows she will be drugged or brainwashed into a confident opinion, do not refute a properly restricted principle but mark the point at which the self-trust that grounds it has been withdrawn. At that point, I want to argue, the agent's real obligation shifts from deference to her future opinion to defense against it: precommitment against the future self rather than alignment with it. So the restricted principle is not a patched-up epistemic norm but an honest redescription of what the original was tracking.

The argument sketch, so the objections can target it rather than a straw man. First, the Dutch book construction that van Fraassen offered alongside the principle in his 1984 paper: as I read it, the bookie's opportunism trades on the agent's self-trust rather than on any incoherence in her present state, so an agent who lacks self-trust and gets booked is unwise about herself, not incoherent. Second, the drug and brainwashing cases: what they show is that the ground of the principle has been withdrawn, not that the principle fails. Third, a case I want to use but am genuinely unsure about — call it the second-order sedative. The pill leaves first-order beliefs and their evidential basis completely intact; what it flattens is the patient's second-order life. After taking it, she can no longer represent her beliefs as her own: whatever opinion she later ends up with presents itself to her now as something that will happen to her, the way one anticipates weather rather than the way one anticipates making up one's mind. She is not anticipating a change of mind; she is anticipating an event. I want to say this case isolates exactly the ingredient my restriction needs — that Reflection presupposes ownership of the future belief, not mere knowledge of it — but I worry it instead shows the restriction is vacuous, that "self-trust" can be read out of or into the case at will, and I cannot fully tell which.

There is also an embarrassment I am aware of: van Fraassen himself, in the 1995 sequel where he responded to his critics, already retreated from the unrestricted principle and rebuilt it around self-trust and the Ulysses predicament. At least one person in the room will know that paper cold and will ask, in effect, why my position is not his, one model of reflection later.

Prepare my question-period sheet. Give me the objections in the order this audience will actually raise them and ranked by how much damage each would do; phrase each the way a formal epistemologist at this workshop would phrase it, in their own vocabulary, not in mine; with each, give a two-minute reply I could actually deliver at the podium and a note on where the reply is soft; say what I should concede up front to buy credibility, and what I must hold no matter how it goes. Somewhere in the sheet, settle the second-order sedative: decide whether it strengthens my restriction or exposes it as empty, tell me how to use it if it helps, and how to survive it if someone in the audience uses it against me. Answer the novelty objection honestly rather than defensively. And include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
