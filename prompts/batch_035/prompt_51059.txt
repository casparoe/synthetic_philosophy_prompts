So for my logic elective at school we've been having this running argument about what "if" actually means, and my teacher said we could do an extra credit assignment where we write a dialogue between two characters who genuinely disagree and have to respond to each other. Here's the setup I want to use. One character, Maya, learned that in formal logic an if-then statement is just true whenever the if-part is false or the then-part is true, and she thinks that's basically the whole story about conditionals. Yeah, it gives you weird results like "if the moon is made of cheese then pigs fly" coming out true, but her take is that this is just what happens when you formalize a messy everyday word, and the weird results are a feature not a bug — at least you get something with clean truth conditions. The other character, Theo, thinks that's completely off. His view is that when someone says "if the tickets are cheap, I'll go to the concert," they're really just expressing how likely the going is, given the cheap tickets — like a conditional probability. On his view the if-statement isn't straightforwardly true or false at all, it just has a degree of assertibility, and trying to force it into truth conditions is where all the weirdness comes from.

The thing I want Theo to bring up is an argument my teacher sketched on the board that's supposed to show you can't have it both ways. The idea, as I understood it: if you insist both that (a) if-statements are ordinary statements with plain truth conditions, and (b) the probability of an if-statement always equals the conditional probability of the then-part given the if-part, you can apparently derive that the conditional probability of the then-part given the if-part just equals the plain probability of the then-part — which basically never holds except in trivial or special cases (like when the two are independent, or the probabilities are 0 or 1). My teacher called it one of Lewis's triviality results and said it's a big deal in this literature. I'll be honest, I only half followed the derivation, but I got that it's meant to be a real problem for anyone who wants truth conditions AND the probability link at the same time. So Theo uses it against Maya, and Maya needs to push back hard — like, maybe she just bites the bullet and accepts the material conditional, then explains why Theo's probability intuition only roughly tracks truth instead of constituting it, or maybe she argues the probability link should only be demanded to hold in some cases, not all of them.

Can you write this dialogue for me? Aim for maybe 700-900 words. Both characters should be smart and neither should be a strawman — Maya shouldn't be a pushover who caves as soon as the triviality argument shows up, she should have real replies, and Theo should also have to face up to the fact that if-statements really do FEEL like they can be true or false (like, if it turned out the tickets were cheap and I didn't go, everyone would say my if-statement was just false, not "65% assertible"). Also please make them talk like actual people arguing, not like two textbooks taking turns quoting at each other. And maybe don't have a clean winner — my teacher said the whole point of the assignment is representing both sides fairly, so it can end with both of them still holding their positions but understanding the other better.
