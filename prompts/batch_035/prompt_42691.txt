So I've been on a care ethics bender for the last couple months — Gilligan's In a Different Voice, then Noddings' Caring, then a bunch of SEP wandering — mostly because I realized that after a decade in the rationalist blogosphere I can recite the consequentialism-vs-deontology-vs-virtue-ethics taxonomy in my sleep, but the whole ethics-of-care tradition barely registers there, and I wanted to fix that. Then I rewatched Remains of the Day and realized I might have the perfect object for it, but I want to sanity check the reading before I write it up.

The pitch: Stevens is basically a stress test for the justice/care distinction. He runs his entire life on dignity, role-duty, and loyalty to a great man — and when that produces disaster (Darlington's appeasement arc, the sacking of the two Jewish maids that Stevens relays because it's not his place to have views), the standard reading is "rule-following morality, unchecked, curdles into complicity." The care-ethics reading cuts differently though, and here's the part I keep going back and forth on: Stevens *does* care, in the thick relational sense — about his father, about Miss Kenton, arguably about Darlington — but the caring is entirely routed through the role, and the role flattens it into "caring-about" rather than "caring-for." The scene where Miss Kenton gets the news of her aunt's death and Stevens can only respond by adjusting the staff schedule is the exact moment where the caring response is right there and something in him refuses it. So maybe the novel dramatizes a real hole in the theory: care ethicists famously have trouble criticizing caring that's misdirected or trapped inside a bad practice, and Stevens's whole life is misdirected caring. Meanwhile Miss Kenton, who has all the actual engrossment, pays for it — her caring labor gets her a marriage to a man she doesn't love, and the book doesn't exactly hand her a win either.

What I'd love from you: (1) does the reading hold up, or does care ethics just relabel what we already knew about Stevens and his repression; (2) which scenes should I actually anchor the essay on — I'm thinking the aunt's-death scene, Stevens's father dying upstairs while he keeps serving during the 1936 conference, and the last meeting between Stevens and Miss Kenton by the bus stop in the rain, but I worry I'm just picking the obvious ones; and (3) is there an honest version of this essay that ends with care ethics being *insufficient* here — like, the novel needs the justice side to say the butler's servility is bad, and care alone can only say it's sad? If a philosopher of care ethics read my post, where would they say I cheated?
