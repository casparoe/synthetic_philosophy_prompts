I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, working mostly on spacetime ontology, and my department has this running seminar where two faculty have been going at each other for years about the hole argument in general relativity. One is a substantivalist — he thinks spacetime points are real in some robust sense and the manifold isn't just bookkeeping — and the other is a relationalist, whose line is basically that there is no container, only the fields and their relations, and that talk of "this point here" as opposed to "that point there" is pure gauge. The rest of us have finally convinced them to try an adversarial collaboration, and somehow I got tasked with drafting the joint statement they'd both sign. I want a solid draft to bring to the first meeting so we're staring from somewhere other than a blank page.

Please act as an expert in philosophy of physics who has facilitated adversarial collaborations before. Think carefully, and maybe do a quick brainstorm before committing to formulations — the whole difficulty is in the drafting: every agreed claim needs wording both would sign, and every disagreement needs wording that the side it describes would accept as a fair description of its own view, not a strawman. The document should have: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims, covering at minimum what both sides take the hole argument to establish formally — that in GR, given the general covariance of the field equations, applying an arbitrary smooth transformation (an active diffeomorphism) that scrambles the metric and matter fields inside some hole region yields a model that represents the very same physical situation, what's usually called Leibniz equivalence, and the corollary that only diffeomorphism-invariant quantities are physical observables; (2) a disagreements table. I expect the live divergences to include at least these two: whether the lesson of the argument is just that GR is a gauge theory and there's no further metaphysical moral to draw, versus that it's a direct refutation of taking bare manifold points as individuals; and whether the substantivalist's standard fallback — accept Leibniz equality of the models, give up on haecceity for points, but still insist spacetime itself exists (what the literature calls sophisticated substantivalism) — is a genuinely distinct position from relationalism or just relationalism with extra words attached.

Then (3) the cruxes — I suspect one is whether haecceitism about points was ever doing any real work in the substantivalist's view, and another is whether anything in physics as opposed to verbal preference could ever distinguish sophisticated substantivalism from relationalism, say in the different approaches to quantum gravity. For each crux, give a case, argument, or thought experiment both would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance, before the case is evaluated. And if you conclude that some part of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why — but don't declare the whole thing verbal unless the formulations you drafted really support that; the relationalist at least will want that conclusion earned, not asserted. One of them keeps dragging the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence into everything, which is fine where genuinely relevant, but the statement should stand on the GR argument itself, not on 18th-century theology. Format: numbered agreed claims, then a disagreements table, then cruxes with predictions. It should read as a document the two of them could actually put their names to, not an essay about them.
