I'm a software engineer on an ML infrastructure team; my last formal philosophy exposure was a first-year course a decade ago, so please keep any jargon you use either explained or replaced with engineering vocabulary. A recurring argument on my team never resolves because people use the same words for different positions. When someone from the interpretability side says a model "has a feature for code-switching" or "represents board state in layer 30," some of us mean that as a claim about something that genuinely exists inside the network, others as convenient predictive shorthand, and nobody notices the disagreement because we all nod along. I want to build a diagnostic instrument — a decision tree — that sorts my colleagues and me by what we actually believe, so the argument can at least be located rather than repeated.

Please construct the tree as follows:

1. Build it from questions, not from the names of philosophical schools. Every internal node should be a question that a working engineer can answer from concrete cases about neural networks, without any philosophy background and without being able to tell which position their answer leads to. To give you the flavor of branch points I have in mind (use, adapt, or replace these as you see fit):
   - If two different decompositions of the same model's activations predict its behavior equally well but carve up the internal space differently, is one of them wrong, are both acceptable, or does the fact that both work show there was never a fact of the matter to be right about?
   - When an interpretability technique — probing classifiers, activation steering, sparse autoencoders trained to find "features" — reliably works, does its success show that the things it found were in the model all along, or only that the tool is useful, the way a good compression scheme is useful without the codebook being somehow "in" the data?
   - Does it matter whether a discovered feature is causally load-bearing (ablate it and the behavior degrades) versus merely correlated with behavior?
   - If a model packs more features than it has neurons by storing them in overlapping, not-quite-orthogonal directions, does that strengthen the case that the features are real, or does it reveal that "feature" is doing suspicious work?
   - If two networks with entirely different internal wiring implement the same input-output behavior, is the internal structure part of what we mean by "representation," or irrelevant to it?
2. The leaves should be able to locate at least these positions, described in plain language rather than assumed by name: the full-realist view that networks have representations in roughly the robust way maps represent a territory; an instrumentalist view on which representation-talk is a useful fiction that makes prediction and control easier; a middle view in the spirit of "real patterns," where patterns count as real when they support prediction and compression, and that is all the reality there needs to be; and an eliminativist corner holding that nothing inside a network deserves the word. If the logical space your questions generate contains combinations nobody plausibly holds, note them and say briefly whether that's because they're incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
3. Be honest about the tree's resolution limits: if your questions cannot actually separate, say, the real-patterns view from the instrumentalist one — if any answer pattern consistent with one is consistent with the other — flag that pair explicitly rather than smuggling in a question that only appears to divide them.
4. Aim for five to seven internal questions and at most eight leaves. One page of decision logic, not a monograph.

Then apply the tree to me. My own answers to the flavor questions above: I think superposition makes features more real, not less, because the model is paying a real cost (interference, error) to keep them separable; I'm queasy about counting merely-correlated probes as finding anything; but I also can't bring myself to say that one of two equally predictive decompositions is simply the wrong one, which I suspect undercuts my realism more than I'd like.

End with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing only the label of the leaf my stated answers place me on (three words or fewer), or, if I land between leaves, the two labels joined by "vs.".
