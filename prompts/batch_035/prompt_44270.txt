Two of my friends from the math department got into it after a seminar and dragged me in, and honestly I lost the thread halfway through, so I need someone to referee. The topic was whether mathematicians should seriously worry that the axioms they use (like ZFC, or even just basic arithmetic) are actually inconsistent.

It started when Dan said the whole thing should keep us up at night: Gödel's second incompleteness theorem means ZFC can never prove its own consistency, so if it is consistent we'll never know, and for all we know there's a contradiction sitting in there right now. And since classical logic explodes from any contradiction, one bad axiom takes down everything at once. Maya said that's a misreading: Gödel just says the system can't certify *itself*, but we can prove consistency from the outside — Gentzen proved Peano arithmetic consistent using transfinite induction up to ε₀. Plus, she said, if arithmetic were inconsistent, we'd have found it by now; humans have been doing math for millennia and proofs get checked and re-checked by thousands of people.

Dan said the Gentzen proof is a shell game: you've just moved the trust from arithmetic to transfinite induction up to some ordinal nobody can picture, so nothing was gained. And the "we'd have found it by now" argument only works if the shortest contradiction is short enough to stumble on — a contradiction could be a million pages long, and nobody ever finds those by accident. Maya answered that this cuts against him: if the only contradictions out there are a million pages long, they can't actually hurt anyone, and if the axioms did blow up we'd switch to some paraconsistent logic or just keep using the fragment that works — the way geometry survived finding out the parallel postulate isn't true of actual space.

Dan said that's way too breezy, because mathematicians lean on consistency assumptions constantly — relative consistency results, model existence, half of set theory is conditional on Con(ZFC) — so an actual inconsistency wouldn't be a harmless cosmetic problem, it would mean decades of results are void. And set theorists are making it worse by adding large cardinal axioms, actively climbing toward wherever the contradiction probably lives. Maya said the paradoxes already happened once — Russell's paradox blew up naive set theory, people patched it, and the system has run a century without a fault — so the sensible attitude is inductive confidence, the same reason you trust the sun to come up, not lying awake over it.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? And tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing.
