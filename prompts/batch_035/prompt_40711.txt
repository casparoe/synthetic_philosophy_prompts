I'm taking a Thucydudes seminar (sorry, Thucydides — long day) and my whole seminar grade rides on a mid-term debate where I got assigned Cleon's side in the Mytilenean debate, the position literally everyone in the room is rooting against. My professor warned us she'll dock anyone who plays him as the cartoon thug — you know, all roar, no argument — and says his speech in Book 3 is actually one of the sharpest cases in the whole history for why an empire cannot afford mercy. So before I have to defend him next week, I want to practice the OTHER side, Diodotus, against the strongest Cleon I can get. That's you.

Set the scene like this: it's the second assembly in 427. The first trireme with the death decree has already sailed for Mytilene and the vote to reopen the question barely passed, so you're furious before you even open your mouth — yesterday's panic has softened into pity and you intend to whip it back into shape. Play Cleon for real: use his actual arguments wherever he has them (an empire held by force can't be governed by consent and shouldn't apologize for it; the Mytileneans weren't wronged subjects but autonomous, privileged allies who coldly calculated that Athens was weak and would stay weak; a penalty that only follows actual wrongdoing just invites every city to gamble and hope Athens forgets; and the real disease is the assembly's love of displays of cleverness and its willingness to be talked out of its own decisions). But you can improvise beyond the text too, as long as it fits who he is. Don't soften him — he's contemptuous of intellectuals, flatters the ordinary farmer-soldier over the saloon arguer, and thinks pity is a luxury of people who don't have to run an empire. Stay in character until I say "stop", and keep each reply to one meaty paragraph so it feels like an actual exchange on the pnyx.

Here's my opening as Diodotus: Cleon, your penalty defeats itself, and you of all people should see it, since you're the one always going on about interest while the others babble about justice. The next city that revolts, knowing your blanket death decree is certain, has nothing left to lose and will fight us to the last brick — we'll spend our ships and our men conquering rubble, then lose the tribute a living Mytilene could have paid for decades. People in that position don't respond to deterrence the way your arithmetic assumes, because hope outweighs fear exactly when things are desperate, and you've just made hope the only thing left. And look who your net catches: the oligarchs sent the fleet, yes, but it was the commons of Mytilene who mutinied and handed us the city — and you're proposing to execute the one faction that actually surrendered to us. Take their walls, take their fleet, take their land and put our allies on it, punish the guilty men by name, and the rest of Greece concludes revolt is ruinous but surrender survivable. Kill everyone and you've merely taught every future rebel to hold out forever. There — convince me I shouldn't vote with you anyway.
