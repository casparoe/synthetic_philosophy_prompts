I'm a civil engineer sitting in on a philosophy of science course, and I keep noticing something about my own profession that I can't quite articulate. In flood-risk practice, regulators designate the 1% annual chance floodplain — the so-called hundred-year flood — and that single number carries enormous legal weight: it determines where flood insurance is mandatory, where buildings must be elevated, and whether land can be developed at all. Everyone involved knows the underlying situation isn't really like that. The hydrologic models disagree with one another; the historical record is short; the climate is changing in ways that break the stationarity assumptions the models rest on; and experts openly dispute which model to trust. Yet all of that mess is funneled into one crisp exceedance probability, which is then compared to a sharp threshold, so a parcel of land either is or is not in the zone. A related habit shows up in structural codes, where design is calibrated to a target reliability — effectively a point probability of failure over a structure's lifetime — even though the whole reason engineers use blanket safety factors rather than direct probabilistic calculation is that they don't trust any single probability model enough to commit to its output.

What does this practice presuppose about uncertainty? I can see a few candidate readings and don't know how to choose between them. Perhaps it presupposes that there is a fact of the matter about the true exceedance probability, with the rival models functioning as noisy measurements of one underlying quantity. Perhaps it needs nothing that strong — maybe a single number is just a convention that lets a regulatory system act, so it works less like an estimate and more like a decision rule under ambiguity, a precautionary threshold dressed up as a measurement. Or perhaps the practice is genuinely incoherent: a pretense of precision the science doesn't support, which manufactures real injustice at the boundary, where a parcel at 1.01% annual chance gets no protection at all and its neighbor at 0.99% gets the full apparatus of building codes and insurance requirements.

Please reconstruct the implicit epistemology of the flood-zone practice. Say which rival views of probability and of ambiguity it quietly rules out — for instance, whether it is compatible with treating the hydrologic uncertainty as model-based ambiguity rather than as mere spread around a true value — and whether its parts presuppose consistent things, given that the same agencies elsewhere insist the numbers are provisional while enforcing them as if they were exact. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined, such that several theories of uncertainty would license it unchanged. Finally, tell me what would actually have to change if regulators took imprecise probabilities seriously: what interval-valued flood zones, or building standards triggered by the upper bound of the interval rather than the midpoint, would do to the insurance, zoning, and mortgage systems that currently run on the binary in-or-out designation.
