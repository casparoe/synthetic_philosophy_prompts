My friend Kavita and I spent the better part of last night arguing about indirect normativity — the family of proposals, familiar from coherent extrapolated volition and its many cousins, according to which we specify an AI's goal not directly but by reference to what some idealised version of us would want or endorse under improved conditions. The argument deadlocked over a specific structural question, and I'd like you to build me a thought experiment that isolates it. Please act as a professional philosopher who works on both idealisation in metaethics and goal specification for advanced AI, and take the construction brief below seriously as a set of engineering tolerances: a case that meets the letter of the brief by stipulating its verdicts rather than generating them fails.

Here is the deadlock. We distinguished two dimensions along which an indirect-normativity procedure can idealise a person. The first is epistemic: supply all the true factual information, remove confusions and ignorance, and let the person deliberate fully informed — but leave her evaluative dispositions exactly as they stand, cravenness, squeamishness, akrasia and all. The second is procedural or rational: run her actual dispositions forward under better conditions, letting them revise themselves as they would if she were at her reflective best, so that flaws she would herself come to disavow get washed out. Kavita thinks that once the factual errors are cleared away, the two procedures converge, because a person's dispositions plus the truth just are her reflective equilibrium. I suspect they can come apart in a way that matters, but I kept failing to construct a case that didn't cheat. So the brief:

Construct a scenario featuring a single, realistic person facing a single practical decision, such that epistemic-only idealisation delivers one verdict and procedural idealisation delivers the opposite, subject to the following constraints. First, the divergence must not rest on any false factual belief; both idealisations see exactly the same facts. Second, it must not rest on a value or disposition the person would disavow the moment it were pointed out to her — the divergence has to survive her considering the disposition head-on and, at the object level, standing by it. Third, no exotic cognitive technology: the idealisations must be describable without science fiction — think of something like a year of unpressured reflection in good company, not mind editing. Fourth, the person must be sympathetic and recognisable; a reader should be able to imagine being her, and neither verdict should make her out to be a fool or a saint.

Before committing to a scenario, please brainstorm a few candidates and say briefly which ones you discarded for cheating. Then, for your chosen case: (a) walk through what each idealisation delivers and why, making clear the verdicts fall out of the two procedures rather than being written into the setup; (b) identify which single feature of the case is doing the work, and say what happens to the divergence if that feature is removed; and (c) give the strongest reply available to someone on Kavita's side — someone who holds that full information plus stable dispositions simply constitute the person's reflective equilibrium — and assess honestly whether that reply dissolves the case or merely relocates the disagreement. The purpose of the case, if it matters, is to help us see which kind of idealisation we would actually want written into an AI's goal specification, so treat 'neither procedure would be used in practice' as an evasion, not an answer.

Please reason carefully and in prose rather than bullet points, and write your reply in British English throughout.
