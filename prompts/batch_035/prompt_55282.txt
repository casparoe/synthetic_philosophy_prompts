I'm a PhD student working on bargaining and conflict-avoidance stuff, mostly with an eye on AI negotiations and treaty design, and somewhere over the last year I've drifted into the view that safe Pareto improvements are basically a free lunch: whenever two agents face a standoff where destructive conflict is a live possibility, they can jointly commit in advance to playing a modified game that, whatever they would have done in the original, leaves everyone weakly better off — two states in a crisis agreeing that if it would have come to war, the matter gets settled by some rule or lottery both prefer to the risk of fighting. If that's right, a lot of the pessimism in bargaining theory is overstated and there's a real practical agenda built on it, which is more or less what I want to write my thesis around.

Before I commit to that, I want the view stress-tested. What would count against "basically a free lunch"? I'm half-aware of some candidate defeaters but havent worked them through:

- Safety is only defined relative to the original game, and it's not obvious what fixes the original game or the counterfactual "what would have happened" when the agents themselves disagree about the baseline — an improvement over a contested baseline can quietly be a rip-off.
- Per-game safety says nothing about linked or repeated games, where adopting these commitments signals something about you and can change which games others choose to put you in.
- Pareto-improving isn't fair: a "safe" improvement can bake in whatever bargaining power existed at commitment time and then launder it as neutral.
- It only counts the two parties, so third parties can eat external costs and it still counts as safe.

So: which of these, if any, are fatal rather than merely awkward? Are some of them already handled somewhere in the literature — if there even is a literature beyond the one paper that introduced the idea, I honestly dont know? And separately: my statement of the view leans hard on "in principle they can commit," and I'd like an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that's a bug or just inherent to a priori claim of this type. If you think some other defeater I haven't listed is more dangerous than any of mine, lead with that.
