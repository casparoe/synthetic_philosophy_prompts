I write about science and ideas for a newspaper — mostly physics stories, the odd book review — and something I keep bumping into has started to bug me, so I'm hoping a philosopher can untangle it.

Whenever a big result drops, say a new measurement that disagrees slightly with the standard model of particle physics, the physicists I ring up all say some version of the same thing: this doesn't mean the old theory is wrong, it means we're converging, the old theory was always known to be incomplete, this is what progress looks like. Then in the next breath they'll describe some rival framework somebody proposed in the nineties as just wrong — not incomplete, wrong. And the whole machinery of the field runs on the same assumption: exam boards teach teenagers Newtonian mechanics as if it were simply true, then reveal at A-level or uni that it's an approximaton that breaks down at high speeds and small scales; a new theory counts as promising partly because it reduces to the old one in familiar regimes; a small anomaly is business as usual while a big one is a crisis.

Here's what I want to understand. What view of truth — or of what a theory even is — does all of that presuppose? The practice seems to need some notion of distance from the truth: Newton was false but nearly right, the luminiferous aether was just false, and physicists act as though you can rank these things even while every theory in the pile is strictly speaking untrue. But I once interviewed a historian of science who flatly denied that successive theories get closer to anything at all — he said the change from one to the next just isn't that kind of thing, and he talked as though everyone in his field had settled this decades ago. So the way physics talks about itself looks like it quietly commits to something that at least some people who study science for a living think is incoherent.

So please reconstruct the implicit picture: what does "teach the false one first, mark the anomalies by size, treat reducing to the old theory as a virtue" actually require? Does it strictly presuppose that theories are true-or-false descriptions of one fixed reality that we gradually approach, or could an anti-realist — someone who thinks theories are instruments, useful or not useful — run the very same practice just as comfortably? Where is the practice genuinely forcing one metaphysics over a rival, and where is it underdetermined, such that several pictures of what theories are would licence it equally well? And does the actual comparison (Newton nearer the mark than Descartes's vortices, say) do any real work, or is it decoration?

Since a version of this might end up in print, don't give me a confident essay. Tell me where your reconstruction is most likely to go wrong, and what consideration or argument would genuinely change your mind about it — I'd rather print a hedged thing that survives an expert reading it crossly than a tidy thing that falls apart. And if philosophers do have a name for the "false but nearly right" idea I'd like to hear it, but don't just hand me the label and stop; I want to know whether it's survived the objections.
