I run a little film-and-philosophy discussion group, and next month we're watching Adaptation, the 2002 Spike Jonze film where the real screenwriter Charlie Kaufman is played by Nicolas Cage, sweating over adapting a real book (Susan Orlean's The Orchid Thief) and eventually writing the very movie we're watching into his own screenplay, helped along by a fictional twin brother, Donald, who doesn't exist. The last act famously delivers everything Kaufman said he'd refuse to deliver: a car crash, drug bust, alligators, a death — the Hollywood formula, delivered straight, after a screenwriting guru had told him the ending is where you wow them.

I want to read it through Hofstadter's strange loops and tangled hierarchies, and I'd like you to take both the film and the GEB framework seriously rather than using one to decorate the other. Escher's Drawing Hands has two hands drawing each other, but Hofstadter's point is that the picture only works because of an inviolate level: the paper the hands are drawn on stays flat and gets drawn by neither hand, and in a sense the whole thing is really drawn by Escher, whose hand never appears in the picture. So my questions are roughly: where is the inviolate level in Adaptation? The obvious candidate is the real Charlie Kaufman who actually wrote the script — but the film keeps dragging him on screen (it opens on the set of Being John Malkovich, it constantly references his real life), so the escape hatch is in plain view the whole time instead of hidden. Does a strange loop still produce the vertigo Hofstadter describes when the level that's supposed to be outside the loop is also a character in it? And is the clichéd ending inside the loop (Donald, a fiction, "writes" it) or is it the inviolate level breaking through — the studio movie as the paper the whole thing is drawn on, which finally draws on itself?

Please also be honest about where the film resists this framing. My group will want to know whether the ending is a genuine tangled hierarchy or just collapses into irony — Kaufman gets to have his clichés and disavow them too, and some people in my group will say that's not a strange loop at all, it's just having it both ways, dont let the Escher framing make it look deeper than it is. If that's right, say so, and tell me what the film adds to the strange-loop idea that the GEB discussion alone doesnt — and whether there's a scene or two I should queue up for the group to argue about.
