I am preparing for a seminar discussion of Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals" (Book I, chapter XXX of the *Essays*, read in Charles Cotton's seventeenth-century English translation). The essay is usually treated as an early argument for cultural relativism: Montaigne contrasts the supposed savagery of the Tupinambá of Brazil with the cruelty of religious wars in France and suggests that Europeans simply call barbarous whatever is unfamiliar to them. What I am having trouble with is the essay's long opening, which never mentions cannibals at all and instead moves from a classical anecdote through a digression on Atlantis, shifting riverbanks, and encroaching seas. I would be grateful for help understanding how this opening is supposed to work.

Here is the passage (from Cotton's translation, with my ellipses and bracketed summary where indicated):

> When King Pyrrhus invaded Italy, having viewed and considered the order of the army the Romans sent out to meet him; "I know not," said he, "what kind of barbarians" (for so the Greeks called all other nations) "these may be; but the disposition of this army that I see has nothing of barbarism in it." ... By which it appears how cautious men ought to be of taking things upon trust from vulgar opinion, and that we are to judge by the eye of reason, and not from common report.
>
> I long had a man in my house that lived ten or twelve years in the New World, discovered in these latter days ... I am afraid our eyes are bigger than our bellies, and that we have more curiosity than capacity; for we grasp at all, but catch nothing but wind.

[The essay then digresses at length. Montaigne retells the story, which he attributes to Plato's account of Solon and the priests of Sais, of the lost island of Atlantis, and weighs — only to doubt — whether the newly discovered New World could be that island. He supports the doubt with natural examples of geographical change: the river Dordogne shifting its banks and undermining houses within living memory, and his brother's estate in Medoc being buried under advancing sands as the sea claims several leagues of land. He closes the digression with the report, from a work then attributed to Aristotle, of Carthaginians who crossed the Atlantic and began a colony on a fertile island before the Carthaginian senate forbade further emigration on pain of death.]

My questions:

1. First, the framing anecdote. Who is King Pyrrhus, why did the Greeks call all other nations "barbarians," and what exactly is the anecdote doing as the opening move of an essay about Brazilian cannibals? How does a Greek misjudging a Roman army map onto Montaigne's subject?

2. Second, and this is what puzzles me most: what is the argumentative function of the Atlantis digression? It delays the announced subject by many hundreds of words and consists of what looks like geographical and antiquarian trivia. Is Montaigne rehearsing a lesson in the unreliability of inherited testimony — ancient accounts of Atlantis, misattributed Aristotelian texts, secondhand travel reports — before asking his reader to trust a *different* kind of testimony (his own houseguest's account of Brazil)? Or is the digression ironic, a deliberate display of the learned pedantry the essay elsewhere distrusts? Or is it doing something else entirely — for instance, establishing the New World as genuinely new, cut off from the textual tradition Europe would otherwise use to absorb it?

3. Third, how does the complaint that our eyes are bigger than our bellies and that we grasp at all but catch nothing but wind bear on the digression it interrupts? Is this self-mockery, aimed at Montaigne's own curiosity about the New World, or a jab at Europe's colonial appetite, or both?

4. Finally, the connective question for my seminar: if the essay's later relativist claims depend on Montaigne's confidence in his informants' reports about Tupinambá life, how does the opening's skeptical treatment of ancient and secondhand reports cohere with that confidence? What, if anything, does Montaigne signal about the epistemic standing of his New World source that distinguishes it from the Atlantis and Carthaginian stories he distrusts?

I would especially value an answer that treats the opening as an integrated part of the essay's argument rather than as throat-clearing, and that is explicit about where the text supports a reading and where the reading must be reconstructed rather than quoted.
