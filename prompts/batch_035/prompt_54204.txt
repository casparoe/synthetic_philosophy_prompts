So I'm a third-year phd student in ML, working on reward/preference learning from human behavior (inverse RL, RLHF-adjacent stuff), and I keep smacking into the same foundational fight wearing different costumes. I want you to write a dialogue between the two sides. Please read the requirements at the bottom, they matter for grading.

The fight: when we infer preferences from behavior, are we recovering a latent fact about the person, or making a normative decision disguised as measurement?

Side A is the model-based view — basically the working assumption of most of my field. The human has stable latent preferences; behavior is a noisy function of those; you posit some noise model (softmax/Boltzmann rationality, rational inattention, whatever) and invert it. Deviations from model-optimal behavior are mistakes, and explaining them away is part of the point. On this view there is a fact of the matter about what the person really wants, and better models are more accurate about it.

Side B is the behaviorist / choice-only view. On this view there is no fact of the matter beyond choice patterns: the person's true preference is not a latent variable you can be more or less accurate about, it is a construct, and any procedure that purifies the data — downweighting some choices, treating others as noise — is just the modeler's normative judgment in a lab coat. The sophisticated version is the Bernheim–Rangel program in behavioral welfare economics (the Beyond Revealed Preference paper), which does not say just take choices at face value; it says welfare conclusions may only be drawn from choices that are robust: an option counts as preferred only if the alternative is never chosen when it's available, across all the ancillary conditions — frames, states, contexts — in which the decision arises.

Have them fight over a concrete case. Use this one, or something in the same spirit if you think a different case separates the views better:

Maya sets a 6am alarm every night, visibly plans her mornings around waking early, tells anyone who asks that she's a morning person who hates losing her evenings to snoozing — and nearly every morning she snoozes until 7:30 and misses the stuff she planned. Scattered in, there are mornings where she does get up at 6, and afterwards reports the day went better for it. So the alarm-setting and the stated desire point one way, the snoozing points the other, and the occasional successful morning gives each side a little ammunition.

A wants to say: latent preference for early rising, but the action channel is very noisy in the tired state — maybe a state-dependent temperature, maybe the deliberation-vs-execution gap — so the inference problem is interesting but soluble in principle, and the stated desire is evidence about the latent variable like anything else. B wants to say: you have two behavioral regularities pointing in opposite directions, and whichever one you call the preference is a choice you make. Also, B should notice that the stated desire is itself behavioral data, not a peek behind the curtain — so A doesnt get to treat it as the gold standard by fiat.

Requirements:

- Both characters genuinely competent, no strawman. The strongest thing each side has going for it should actually show up in the dialogue.
- Real back-and-forth — they respond to each other's actual arguments. In particular I want to see:
  - A pressing B on the fact that robustness-across-contexts is itself a modeling decision — which conditions count as ancillary, which choices get grouped as the same choice, where the line on contexts gets drawn — so the choice-only view doesn't escape model-dependence, it just relocates it. Plus: the robustness requirement is nearly impossible to satisfy in practice (almost nothing survives across all frames), which threatens to leave the view with nothing to say, and B has to have a reply to that.
  - B pressing A on misspecification: the recovered preference is a function of the noise model you posited — change the temperature, make it state-dependent, add habit formation, and the recovered preference changes — so what does accuracy even mean when there's no ground truth to validate against? This is not meant as a rhetorical question; A should seriously try to answer it (e.g., via prediction of future behavior under interventions) and B should push on whether predictive success under intervention actually validates the preference claim or just the model.
  - At some point they should notice they may be using preference for two different things — a latent cause of behavior vs. a welfare standard for judging behavior — and briefly fight about whether that distinction dissolves the dispute or just relocates it (again with the relocating, i know).
- Don't have them agree at the end. But let them pin down exactly where the crux is — I want to come away knowing what empirical result, if any, would count as settling it.

Length: whatever it takes for the above to be actual argument rather than positions being recited, but no filler — no throat-clearing, no summarizing the debate back to each other for my benefit.
