I sit on the board of a small housing cooperative — about forty units, mostly longtime residents, governed by bylaws we amend by a two-thirds vote. A few weeks ago it came out that one member, a widower in his fifties who has lived here longer than almost anyone and is generally well liked, hosts a regular gathering in his soundproofed basement: consenting adults, nobody paid, everyone of age, and he showed the board the elaborate consent rules he runs it by, apparently having anticipated exactly this discovery. There's no noise, no nuisance, nothing visible from the street; we only learned about it because a neighbor's teenager noticed guests in unusual dress using the side entrance, and gossip spread from there. Now a faction of residents, led by a friend of mine on the board, is circulating an amendment that would let the co-op fine, and eventually evict, members for "private conduct contrary to the community's shared moral standards" — even where that conduct breaks no law and disturbs no one. My friend's argument isn't mere personal disgust, and I want to be fair to it: he says a co-op is more than a bundle of leases, that it's a community held together by a shared moral life, and that if conduct most members regard as shameful becomes quietly normalized among us, something that makes this place what it is will erode — the same way a town whose shared moral expectations decay stops being the town it was, even if no individual act of decay ever harms anyone. Another board member says flatly that what people do in their own homes, legally and silently, is none of our business, full stop, and that any other standard just enshrines majority taste with teeth. I have to vote in ten days and keep going back and forth. I half-remember from an undergraduate law course that there was some famous mid-century debate about exactly this question — whether a community may punish conduct purely for its immorality, absent any harm — but I can't reconstruct the arguments well enough to know which side had the better of it, or whether those arguments even transfer from national lawmaking to a voluntary association whose members chose to join and can choose to leave.

Help me think this through. Please structure your response in four sections, in this order: "What's really being decided" (one short paragraph, in plain language, on the underlying issue my board is actually voting on); "The strongest case for the amendment" (two to three paragraphs — give the position its best form, not a caricature); "The strongest case against" (two to three paragraphs, likewise); and "How I should vote" (one to two paragraphs, in which you may take a position, but tell me what would have to be true for the other side to win, and address specifically whether the fact that we're a voluntary cooperative rather than a state strengthens the case for moral enforcement, weakens it, or cuts both ways).
