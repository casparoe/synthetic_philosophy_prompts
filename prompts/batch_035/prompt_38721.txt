I'm writing a profile of one of my graduate instructors for our departmental magazine, and I need help predicting her next ruling. She teaches a seminar on judgment and forecasting, and her standing role in it is to settle what we have come to call "reference-class disputes": whenever two of us back a forecast with base rates drawn from different classes of past cases, she issues a ruling, and she keeps a ledger of every entry. The rulings so far: for her uncle's cancer she used five-year survival for that cancer at his age and stage, setting aside the oncology team's more favorable read of his particular case; for a classmate leaving academia for a startup she used the failure rate for seed-funded startups in that funding cohort, and declined to hear the argument that these founders are exceptional; for how long the department's new master's program would survive she used the fates of comparable programs at peer institutions, waving off both the dean's narrative about this program's special demand and, on the other side, all new academic initiatives as too mixed a bag; and — the ruling that surprised the room — for whether her friend's long-distance relationship would survive the friend's year abroad she used outcomes for long-distance relationships as a whole, refusing to weigh any evidence about how committed this particular couple happens to be.

Two of us have tried to reconstruct the rule she is running, and we have come up with different ones, each of which fits every entry so far given suitable auxiliary assumptions. On my reconstruction, she always replaces the case's own story — the hospital's optimism, the founder's confidence, the couple's commitment — with the statistics of a broad class of comparable cases, and she requires the class to be wide enough for the base rate to be stable. On my classmate's, she matches the class on whichever features causally drive the outcome and lets the width fall where it may; on that reading, the cancer entry reflects that stage and age drive survival, and the relationship entry reflects her belief that felt commitment does not. Both rules still fit because the ledger alone cannot tell us whether the startup ruling came from the thought that founder talent doesn't move failure rates or simply from the thought that any seed-funded startup is the right kind of thing to count.

Her next ruling is the one the whole seminar is waiting for: she has agreed to settle, in front of us, the right reference class for forecasting how much longer the United States will remain the world's leading economic and military power. The contending classes are all empires or hegemons in recorded history (a large membership, most of it long ended, with a median lifespan of some centuries on the usual datasets); nuclear-armed great powers since 1945 (a handful of members, one of which, the Soviet Union, came apart rather quickly); and continental-scale states with durable written constitutions (arguably a class of one, or two if Rome is allowed at a stretch). Before she rules, I would like three things from you. First, which rules could still fit her entire ledger once this entry is in, and what each surviving rule predicts here — the classes above have visibly different sizes and medians, so genuine rules should diverge on them. Second, your best guess at her ruling, with the reason it is better than a coin flip; one consideration worth weighing is that nothing in her ledger so far has let her use a class with fewer than a few dozen members. Third, since I suspect the ledger genuinely underdetermines her: the further dispute we should save for last, the one new case that would best split the surviving rules. My own candidate is a forecast with only a couple of precedents, such as the remaining lifespan of the European Union, but tell me if there is a cleaner one.
