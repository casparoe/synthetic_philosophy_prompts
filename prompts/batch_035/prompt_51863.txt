My roommate has been on an Aristotle kick and he's adapted the sea battle argument into something he applies to literally everything now, and I keep feeling like it proves way too much but I can't find the broken rung. Here's his version, from a notes app he made me read out loud:

(1) Either I'll bring my umbrella to work tomorrow or I won't. That's just excluded middle, and it holds no matter what the weather does.
(2) If I will bring it, then it's true right now, on Tuesday evening, that I'll bring it Wednesday.
(3) Whatever is true now is settled now, in the same way the past is settled: nothing I do on Wednesday can reach back and make a Tuesday-evening truth false.
(4) So if it's already true that I'll bring it, my not bringing it isn't really an open option, and if it's already true that I won't, my bringing it isn't either. One or the other is true, so in every case exactly one course is open to me.
(5) He then generalizes: nothing in (1)-(4) depended on the umbrella. It works for the storm itself, for whether my sister has kids, for whether there's a sea battle.
(6) Conclusion: deliberating about the future is theater. Everything I "decide" was settled before I started deciding. He says Aristotle saw this, which is why Aristotle was driven to deny bivalence for future contingents — if tomorrow's battle is neither true-now nor false-now, the trap in (2) never gets set.

He also has an add-on he's very proud of: suppose a weather model, deterministic in the physics sense, says I bring the umbrella. He claims that with the model in play the fatalist conclusion gets stronger, since now there's an actual mechanism doing the settling, not just truth-values hanging around.

Here's my problem: the argument looks valid and every premise looks like something I believe, but the conclusion is that I've never made a decision in my life, which I find hard to swallow even before breakfast. And the escape he offers (dropping bivalence for future statements) seems to cost a lot too — it apparently makes some predictions neither right nor wrong ahead of time, and I'd like to know if that's actually a price or just a weirdness.
