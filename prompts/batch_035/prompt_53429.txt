I sit on the standards and verification committee of a long-form investigative magazine. Our current policy states that a story may run as "fully fact-checked" when every checkable claim in it has been individually verified, where verification means the fact-checker has reached a high but explicitly fallible confidence standard — call it being at least 95% sure the claim is true. Last month our stats-minded deputy editor flagged a problem with a 6,000-word feature we'd just approved: it contains roughly 140 discrete factual claims, and if each is verified only to 95% confidence, then even under the optimistic assumption of independence, the probability that every single one is true is well under 10%. In other words, by our own lights we are near-certain that every story we label "fully fact-checked" contains at least one error, and we'd rationally bet that it does. She pointed out that this is structurally the same bind as the lottery paradox — where every ticket is probably a loser, and probably every ticket is a loser, yet they can't all be — and the preface paradox, where an author can rationally believe each claim in her own book while also rationally believing the book contains at least one falsehood. The committee is now split three ways: one faction says the per-claim standard is fine and the aggregation worry is a philosopher's puzzle with no journalistic significance; a second wants a blanket disclaimer added to long features, something like "in a story of this length, the probability that at least one statement is wrong is substantial"; and a third wants the confidence threshold raised for longer stories, which fact-checkers warn is unworkable and would effectively kill ambitious reporting.

I need to draft the written opinion I'll circulate before our quarterly meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action — keep the standard, add a disclaimer, revise the threshold, or something else the committee could actually vote on and implement — and it should defend the recommendation honestly, which means engaging the strongest version of the faction that loses, not a strawman of it. In particular, I'd want the memo to get clear on whether the problem is really about the aggregation of fallible confidence (in which case the disclaimer faction has a point), about a confusion between the rationality of individual beliefs and the rationality of asserting a conjunction (in which case maybe nothing about our practice needs to change), or about what the word "verified" is doing in our policy language — whether it commits us to a claim about each assertion, or to something closer to a preface-style epistemic humility about the whole. We're journalists, not epistemologists, so please explain any technical apparatus you use rather than assuming we know it.

One caveat: if you think my deputy editor's argument is actually confused — that per-claim verification standards don't generate the problem she claims, or that the analogy to the lottery and preface cases breaks down somewhere important — please say so plainly and say where, rather than dutifully writing a memo around a premise that doesn't hold. I'd rather learn that our policy is fine than adopt a disclaimer we can't defend. But if the problem is real, I want the memo to treat it as real, including the uncomfortable possibility that no label we can put on the story fully captures our epistemic position.
