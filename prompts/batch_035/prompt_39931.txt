The exam question:

"Clark and Chalmers argue that Otto's notebook, in which he records information to compensate for memory loss, can be literally part of his mind. Adams and Aizawa reply that causal coupling with a cognitive process is not the same thing as constitution of that process: the fact that a painter depends on her brushes does not make the brushes part of the painter. Explain how the parity principle is supposed to answer this objection, and assess whether the 'glue and trust' conditions do any further work."

The student's answer:

"In Clark and Chalmers's case, Otto suffers from memory loss and carries a notebook everywhere. On learning that the Museum of Modern Art is on 53rd Street, he writes the fact down; when he later wants to visit, he consults the notebook and walks to 53rd Street. Inga, who has intact biological memory, heard the same fact at the same time and later walks to the museum without consulting anything. Clark and Chalmers hold that if Inga's stored belief counts as a belief, then so does Otto's notebook entry, since it plays the same role in reasoning and action: Otto consults the notebook as routinely and as trustingly as Inga consults memory, and he would hesitate just as little to act on what it says.

The parity principle is meant to secure this. It says that if a process were done in the head, we would have no hesitation in counting it as part of cognition; so when the same process is in fact done in the world, parity requires that we count it as cognitive there too. Adams and Aizawa reply that this trades on an ambiguity: being causally coupled to cognition is not the same as being constitutive of it, and nothing in the parity principle converts the one into the other. On the face of it this is a strong objection, since parity merely stipulates equal treatment and cannot by itself say which treatment is the correct one.

The objection is nonetheless less damaging than it looks, because the parity comparison is hostage to the mechanisms. For the internal and external cases to be treated on a par, the processes must be relevantly similar in their mechanism, not merely in their effects, and whether they are similar is an empirical question for cognitive science rather than a conceptual one. Since the extended mind thesis therefore rests on whether notebook use and biological memory turn out to share their mechanisms, it is best understood as an empirical hypothesis, and the findings of embodied and situated cognition give reasonable grounds for thinking it has largely been confirmed.

The internalist is in any case not well placed to press the point, since even Rupert, a sympathetic critic who has done much to refine the extended view, concedes that no principled mark of the cognitive can be specified. If the internalist cannot say where cognition ends, the boundary falls wherever the parity principle puts it.

The glue and trust conditions do further work at exactly this point. The notebook is reliably available and typically invoked, the information in it is more or less automatically endorsed rather than re-checked, and it is easily accessible. A resource meeting these conditions is used without deliberation, and that is what makes the connection to it constitutive of belief rather than a mere causal aid: because Otto does not pause to weigh up the notebook, the notebook functions the way memory functions, and what functions as memory is memory.

So the parity principle answers the coupling–constitution objection, though on empirical rather than conceptual grounds, and the glue and trust conditions explain why the notebook, unlike an aide-mémoire a stranger might hand him on the way, is part of Otto's mind."
