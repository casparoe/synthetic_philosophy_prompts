In my philosophy of mathematics seminar we've spent the term on Benacerraf's dilemma and the deadlock it produces, and I've come away with a suspicion I'd like tested. The platonists concede that a causal epistemology leaves numbers unknowable and then propose non-causal substitutes — indispensability to our best scientific theories, set perception, or structural constraints on reference — while the nominalists concede that mathematics is indispensable and then either reinterpret its truths (fictionalism, if-thenism) or attempt to reformulate science without quantifying over abstract objects, and each side treats the other's concession as fatal rather than merely awkward. Meanwhile Benacerraf's own earlier observation that the many rival set-theoretic identifications of the numbers all work equally well gets invoked by both sides, as if it helped each. My hypothesis is that the two camps are not really disagreeing about abstract objects at all but about what an epistemology owes us: one side demands some positive story about the reliability of the link between our beliefs and causally inert subject matter, the other suspects that demand smuggles in a causal theory of reference or a standard so demanding it would also doom knowledge of the past, of other minds, and of modality — a companions-in-guilt point that cuts against mathematical nominalism's own allies. Diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says: is it substantive, merely verbal, or two questions — what makes mathematical statements true, and how we manage to know them — run together under one heading? Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a worked-out non-causal account of mathematical knowledge, a completed nominalization of science, or a distinction that dissolves the dilemma. And if you think nothing should be expected to move the debate, say so and argue it.
