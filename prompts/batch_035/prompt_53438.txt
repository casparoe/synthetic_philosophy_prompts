In a seminar on transitional justice, our instructor kept reaching for a medical analogy to explain what South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission was for: a nation after atrocity, she said, is like a patient with an untreated wound; the public hearings are like a surgical procedure that opens the wound to clean it out; testifying is like the draining of an abscess, painful at the time but necessary for healing; and amnesty is like the anesthetic that makes patients willing to undergo the procedure at all. She used this to draw a substantive conclusion: since the point of the commission was therapeutic, its success should be measured by whether victims and the nation achieved something like recovery, not by whether perpetrators were punished or institutional reforms followed -- and a commission that produced testimony but no healing would have failed at its core mission.

I'd like a careful audit of this analogy. Please set out explicitly what maps onto what -- patient, wound, procedure, anesthesia, surgeon, recovery -- and note who or what plays each role (individual victims, perpetrators, the "nation," the commissioners). Then identify where the mapping breaks: which disanalogies are load-bearing for her conclusion and which are incidental? I'm particularly interested in whether the analogy quietly imports a conception of the patient as a single unified body when the actual "wound" is distributed across victims with divergent interests, whether "healing" and "reconciliation" can be treated as the same target, whether the anesthesia-amnesty mapping distorts what amnesty demanded (e.g., full disclosure as a condition), and whether a therapy that a patient can refuse maps onto a process whose beneficiaries and subjects were largely not consulted. Finally, tell me whether her conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table -- whether one could defend "measure success by healing, not punishment" without the medical scaffolding -- and, if the analogy fails, what would serve better as a comparison.
