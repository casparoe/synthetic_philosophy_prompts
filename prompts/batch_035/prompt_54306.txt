I have been keeping a private list of my gut reactions to unrealistic assumptions in scientific models, in the hope of finding a rule behind them, and so far the list has rules that apply to some of my cases. Treating a planet as a point mass when computing orbits: fine. Ignoring friction on an inclined plane: fine, and I notice I don't even object when the friction is quietly dropped rather than added back in later. Assuming consumers are perfectly rational and always pick what best serves their interests: fine, even though almost no consumer is like that. Assuming a market is perfectly competitive, with enough buyers and sellers that no single one can move the price: fine. But a model of a whole economy that assumes the government always acts in the public interest: not fine, I dismiss it straightaway. An epidemic model that assumes everyone complies with quarantine rules perfectly: not fine, it seems to have quietly assumed away the thing it was supposed to explain. And then the pair I cannot reconcile: a traffic model in which every driver always takes the fastest route and never hesitates, detours, or gets lost — fine, even though it is wildly false about people; while a model of elections in which voters are perfectly informed about the candidates — not fine, somehow, or at least much less fine. My tentative thought is that an assumption becomes objectionable when it smuggles in the model's answer rather than merely clearing the way for the question, but I cannot make that precise enough to tell me which side of the line the traffic model and the election model really fall on. Is there a principle that captures all of these verdicts? If so, please state it as precisely as you can, show what it says about the two cases I flagged as difficult, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to give up.
