I've spent enough years reading LessWrong and adjacent economics blogs that "markets remain undefeated" is close to an identity for me: I've argued for legal kidney sales, defended post-hurricane price gouging, and treated Sandel-style commodification worries as status quo bias dressed up as philosophy. But the arguments I actually reach for are a grab bag — revealed preference, efficiency gains, autonomy, "we already allow X which is worse" — and nothing in the grab bag tells me when it runs out, or why my own intuitions defect in certain cases (prediction markets on whether a public figure dies this year, buying the right to name someone else's child, surrogacy arranged across steep global wealth gradients, vote markets in the sharp sense rather than the diffuse lobbying sense). So before I read another paper taking a side, I want the spec first: write the desiderata that any adequate theory of repugnant markets — a theory of when trading something for money is a genuine mistake, not merely when a trade happens to be unpopular — has to meet, where adequacy is fixed by a concrete purpose: settling hard cases about which trades to permit, ban, or restructure, at the level of policy rather than etiquette.

Requirements I'd expect a serious list to confront, though you should treat this as a sketch, not a script: explaining the observed texture of repugnance rather than one or two cherry-picked examples (why unpaid blood donation and kidney sale feel so different when plasma is routinely paid for; why life insurance on your own life is banal while a side bet on a stranger's death is obscene; why the vote is treated as more sacred than the kidney); delivering verdicts on contested cases without either deferring wholesale to intuition or dismissing it wholesale, since both deference and dismissal make the theory useless for the purpose I named; surviving the historical record that repugnance moves (usury bans, the old suspicion that life insurance was profiting from death, the fading stigma of life insurance generally, changing views on lending and on organ donorhood); and staying consistent with what market design has learned from Alvin Roth's work — that repugnance is an actual constraint that real institutions route around, which cuts against both "repugnance is pure noise" and "repugnance is self-vindicating."

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are camp prejudices smuggled in as neutrality — I want to know if my welfare-and-autonomy starting point is smuggling anything in, not just Sandel. Then, the part I most want: say whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, whether a theory can both treat repugnance as genuine evidence about value corruption and forbid capitulation to status quo bias, given that the two are hard to distinguish in practice. Finally, briefly and without gentleness, score three candidates against the list: the corruption-and-fairness framing associated with Sandel, Satz's conditions on noxious markets, and the simple welfare-plus-autonomy view I walked in holding. The criteria are the deliverable; the scorecard should be a paragraph each.
