In my philosophy of language seminar, the instructor keeps leaning on Wittgenstein's rails picture for Kripke's skeptical paradox: to grasp a rule, the picture says, is to have rails laid out to infinity — the rule already determines every future application before you reach it, so if nothing rail-like is there (no fact about my finite history that settles plus rather than quus), there is no fact about what I meant. My friend, who works in machine learning, says the picture deserves a second life as a rebuttal instead: training a model is exactly rule-following from a finite sample, models generalize to unseen inputs all the time, and no one concludes from the underdetermination of extrapolation that there's no fact of the matter about what a model learned — so maybe rule-following is just disposition plus best extrapolation once you give up on rails. Audit the analogy rather than picking sides between the slogans. First give the correspondence table: what plays the role of the rails, of the laying of the rails, of the train, and of the stretch of track not yet laid. Then say where the picture breaks: which features it imports that meaning lacks — rails are laid in advance of travel, they're surveyable all at once, they constrain by physical contact rather than by interpretation — and which of those disanalogies actually matter for the skeptical conclusion, as opposed to being harmless scenery. Then the two verdicts I want: does the skeptical conclusion depend on the rails picture (could a skeptic who drops it still get the argument to go), and is my friend's ML rebuttal a genuine escape or a dispositionalism that runs straight into its own quus-style problem — a model's past behavior plus the training data is compatible with indefinitely many extrapolations, so hasn't he just moved the rails into the optimizer? If neither picture can carry the weight, say what would.
