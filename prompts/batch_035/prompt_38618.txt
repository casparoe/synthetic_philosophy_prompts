I write for a general-interest magazine about science and ideas, and I'm at work on a long essay about epiphenomenalism: the view that experience is real but causally idle, a shadow cast by the brain that casts nothing back. The essay's pivot is the problem philosophers call the paradox of phenomenal judgment — if qualia make no causal difference to the physical world, then when someone winces and says that the burn hurt, the hurt itself contributed nothing to the wince or the words. I want a thought experiment that turns this from a curiosity into a genuine epistemological cost, and I want it built to the following specification, because the stock devices have gone stale.

The scenario should be ordinary life — a kitchen, a rehearsal room, a hospital bedside — no laboratories, and none of the usual apparatus: no zombies, no Mary, no inverted spectrum as traditionally run. In it, a subject makes a fairly detailed first-person judgment about her own pain, the kind she would express the same way in speech or on a form. The task is then to show that, given epiphenomenalism, this judgment is unsafe in the epistemologist's sense: in the nearest worlds in which she forms the same judgment by the same method, it is false. The nearby worlds must be built under two constraints. First, every one of them must be a world in which she remains conscious — none may be a world from which experience has been subtracted, so the case cannot lean on the conceivability of zombies. Second, each must share our physical laws and her complete physical history, so that the only thing allowed to vary is the psychophysical pairing — which quale rides on which brain state.

After the scenario, do the demonstration. Explain why causal closure makes her utterances identical across these worlds while the truth-values of her judgments shift. Then show that the mispairings lie in nearby worlds only because, on epiphenomenalism, qualia are distinct from the physical and connected to it by contingent laws rather than identity; the type-identity physicalist, for whom the pairing is necessary, should find these worlds remote — and you should say why, since otherwise my case is just the zombie argument wearing a new coat. Third, set out the horns: the epiphenomenalist must either allow that the subject does not know she is in pain, deny that knowledge requires safety, or insist the psychophysical pairing is necessary after all — and you should note what that last option costs her. Finish by identifying which single feature of the setup is doing the work, what happens to the case if that feature is deleted, and the strongest reply an epiphenomenalist could give to defuse it — for instance, by restricting which worlds count as nearby or by denying that safety governs knowledge grounded in acquaintance.

One caution: the case must generate its verdicts from the theory, not stipulate them. My readers are laypeople, but philosophers of mind will read the essay too, and if any step quietly assumes what it is supposed to establish, they will write letters — and the letters page is only so long.
