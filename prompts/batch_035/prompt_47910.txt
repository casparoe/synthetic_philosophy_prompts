so I run a small newsletter about decision theory, mostly read by people who encountered Newcomb's problem once, picked a side in the comments, and never stress-tested that side again. I want to build a "which decision theory do you actually hold?" quiz for it. The point is to sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they like, so no question can just ask about the theory directly.

Here's what I need:

- 8 to 10 questions, each a concrete case with 2 or 3 answer options. No question may use the words evidential, causal, updateless, functional, timeless, or precommit, and no question can ask what view the reader holds. The verdicts have to do all the sorting work.

- The case set has to include, in whatever wording you like: (1) original-flavor Newcomb with a predictor who's been right basically every time before; (2) a transparent version where you can literally see the opaque box is empty before you decide; (3) the smoking lesion setup, where a gene independently causes both a taste for smoking and cancer, and smoking itself does nothing either way; (4) the case where before a fair coin flip a very reliable predictor promises to put $10,000 in your bank account if it predicted you'd pay it $100 after seeing tails -- the coin comes up tails, do you pay; (5) a hitchhiker case where you only got the ride because the driver predicted you'd pay up at the destination; and (6) an extortion case where the blackmailer read your diary in advance and only targeted people he predicted would pay up. You can reword or extend these, but the verdicts have to split the theories the way the originals do.

- Also invent at least one case that isn't from the literature. My draft attempt: before the predictor does its thing, you can hire a friend who will secretly and irrevocably bind you to one-box, and you'll never know whether he actually did it. Do you hire him, and does it matter whether the predictor can detect that you hired him? Feel free to replace that with something better -- the requirement is just that at least one question makes someone who's read all the standard cases actually stop and think.

- A scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions: plain two-boxing, plain one-boxing, the family that one-boxes AND pays in the tails case AND refuses the extortion, the just-bind-yourself-early-and-stop-asking pragmatist position, and the deflationist who thinks some of these cases are underspecified or that the predictor setup smuggles in something weird about time. Where two positions get the same leaf, say so explicitly -- I'd rather the quiz admit what it can't separate than pretend otherwise.

- For combinations of answers that no known position occupies, name them and say whether you think nobody's there because the combination is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody got around to it.

- One short diagnostics note at the end: which single question does the most work, and which two questions could I cut with the least loss of sorting power?

Keep each case description to 2-4 sentences and write casual, like you're talking to a smart friend who's new to this. Dont worry about attribution, I just need the logic of the cases, not who came up with them. American spelling please.
