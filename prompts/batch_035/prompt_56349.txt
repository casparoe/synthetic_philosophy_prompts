I'm a third-year student taking an upper-level ethics seminar on agent-relative constraints, where we've spent half the term on the paradox of deontology, and I also coordinate weekend deliveries for a student-run mutual-aid pantry. Every couple of weekends I face a version of the same decision: whether we may break a specific commitment we've made to one household — a box we reserved and promised, a delivery time we guaranteed, information we said we'd keep confidential — in order to prevent what looks like a clearly larger total harm to several other households. These decisions usually have to be made on the spot, in about ten minutes, with two first-year volunteers watching me make the call, and there's no supervisor to escalate to. Please take the role of a professional moral philosopher who is experienced at turning theory into usable procedure, and think carefully before answering: begin with a short brainstorm about which distinctions from the constraint literature actually matter for decisions like mine and which can be dropped without losing the point. Then write me a decision procedure I can run in ten minutes: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure outputs a firm no; and where it explicitly hands the decision back to me because the question is one of judgment rather than something the theory settles. The procedure should take a defensible position on whether our constraint against breaking commitments is absolute, threshold-governed, or grounded in systemic effects like volunteers and clients losing trust in our rules, and it should explain how it handles the difference between harms we intend and harms we merely foresee. Finally, run it on three cases: (1) we promised a household the last box of baby formula, and a household with twice as many infants arrives an hour later; (2) our van can make only one more run tonight, so keeping the promised delivery time to one family means three other families go without food until Monday — I want the procedure to be honest if it tells me to keep the promise anyway, since that will be an answer I don't like; and (3) a client shared something with us in confidence that a city agency would need to know in order to house them sooner. Close by stating plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
