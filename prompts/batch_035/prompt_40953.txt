I'm preparing a discussion-section handout for an introductory cognitive science course on whether the language you speak shapes the way you think. Please write a decision tree that takes a student from the starting point "I grant that controlled experiments have found cross-linguistic differences on tasks that don't require producing or comprehending any language" to a position on linguistic relativity. Each internal node must be a question the student can answer from her own judgment about concrete cases, without knowing which position it leads to — no node may ask her to endorse or reject a school by name. Each leaf should carry the name of the position that occupies it (or "unnamed" if nothing sits there), plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Close with a short section flagging any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate, and any unoccupied combination you suspect is empty for an interesting reason rather than mere neglect — say which, and why.

The tree must be built from the following four questions, in an order of your choosing, plus exactly one further diagnostic question of your own invention (mark it as yours):

1. Persistence under load: do the reported differences survive when the subject must carry out a concurrent verbal task, or do they wash out?
2. Grammar or vocabulary: do the differences show up only where the two languages differ in obligatory grammar (forced marking of number on nouns, obligatory tense or evidential marking), or also where they differ only in habitual vocabulary (how they carve up the blues, which spatial metaphors are usual)?
3. Direction of causation: is the language doing the shaping, or do language and the measured cognitive habit jointly track a third factor — the surrounding culture, the subsistence practice, the schooling? Phrase the node so the student can take a stance on this from the cases rather than from methodological doctrine.
4. Modulation or constitution: does the linguistic resource merely make certain thoughts easier or harder, or does it make whole classes of thought — exact quantities beyond the small-number range, certain fine evidential distinctions — unavailable without it?

The leaf set should include at least: the strong-determinist view in its now-abandoned form; the thinking-for-speaking view on which the effects operate only while language is being planned or produced; the habit-level relativity familiar from the neo-Whorfian literature; the view of language as a cognitive tool or technology that restructures categorization even offline; and the universalist view on which the apparent effects are artifacts of translation or task design. Remaining combinations may be labeled "unnamed" rather than forced into a position.

The cases the student is asked about should be drawn from this battery — describe the phenomena concretely, and expect no knowledge of the underlying studies: a language whose everyday descriptions require an absolute, compass-like directional frame even when giving directions indoors; two languages that carve the blue region of the spectrum differently; a language that marks number on all nouns versus one that marks it on few; how speakers of a language with grammatical gender personify objects, compared to speakers of one without; and bilinguals tested in each of their languages, who shift with the language of the session. Do not cite or name any specific experiment, language, or researcher; the tree should run on the structure of the cases, not on pedigree.
