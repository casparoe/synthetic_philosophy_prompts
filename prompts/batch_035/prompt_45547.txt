In my Theory of Knowledge class we were debating whether a claim can be "true for one person but not for another," and a classmate ended her turn with what she treated as a knockdown point: that calling a proposition true adds nothing to the proposition — that saying "it is true that snow is white" amounts to no more than saying "snow is white" — so truth is a redundant word, and any argument about what truth "is" is an argument about nothing. Our teacher seemed impressed, but I had the feeling my classmate had slid between at least two different claims: something merely grammatical about when the word "true" can be dropped, and something much bigger about there being no genuine property of truth for anyone to be a relativist about.

Could you separate out the distinct precise theses her slogan might be expressing — arranged from the most trivial to the most radical — and, for each, say whether it is plausible and whether it actually supports either dismissing relativism or dismissing the whole question of what truth is? Where it helps, please test the readings against cases where the word clearly cannot just be dropped, such as "everything the witness said is true" or agreeing with a sentence in a language one doesn't speak. I would like the answer as a numbered menu of readings with a verdict on each, so that I can bring it to our next class and show which claim my classmate actually needed for her conclusion.
