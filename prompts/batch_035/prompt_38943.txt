I've been spending a lot of time lately with the idea of Moloch — the thought, laid out memorably in Scott Alexander's 2014 essay "Meditations on Moloch," that competition itself can become a kind of god that devours everything we care about. Whether it's companies forced to cut corners because their rivals will, academics trapped on the publish-or-perish treadmill, or nations in an arms race, the story is the same: whenever an agent can gain an edge by sacrificing something it values, the pressure of competition grinds away everything except the single dimension being competed on, and the race bottoms out in a world where everyone is worse off in every way except relative standing. I believed this story for a long time, and in a sense I still do, but a worry has crept up on me that I can't shake, and I'd like you to argue a position about it rather than just soothe me.

The worry is this: a race to the bottom only makes sense relative to a bottom. To say the participants end up worse off, you need a standard of value that isn't itself being competed away. But people's values seem to adapt to their circumstances — if everyone on the treadmill eventually comes to want nothing except the next promotion, then in what sense has anyone lost anything? The Moloch story seems to need a fixed vantage point outside the race, some standard of value that the competition cannot touch, and I can't see where such a thing would come from. Everything I might appeal to — human flourishing, some authentic set of preferences from before the race set in, a moral law — looks like it could itself be eroded or at least reshaped by the very pressures it's supposed to be judging from a safe distance. Either Moloch's critique is relativism in disguise, or there's a fixed point I'm failing to see.

So here is the position I'd like you to argue for, as strongly and honestly as you can: the real philosophical content of the race-to-the-bottom idea is not the game theory but the identification of a vantage point that competition cannot erode, and, as a consequence, most apparent multipolar traps are better understood as unresolved disagreements about that vantage point than as cases where everyone already agrees on what's being lost. Please show what work the fixed standard actually does in the argument, say something serious about where it could plausibly come from without assuming it descends from nowhere, and — since I want a real argument and not a pep talk — confront the obvious objection that a standard outside all competition is either an illusion or just someone's local preference dressed up as a universal one. If you think the position only holds in a weakened form, argue for the weakened form, but tell me exactly what was given up to get there.
