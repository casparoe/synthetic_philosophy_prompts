my flatmate got obsessed with Benacerraf's dilemma and came back with an upgraded version he swears is airtight. (1) Knowledge requires that the truth of a belief not be an accident relative to your holding it — if the fact that p played no part in producing your belief that p, then being right is luck, and lucky true belief isn't knowledge. (2) The only thing that can play that part is some causal or nomic connection between the fact and the belief. (3) If arithmetic is true, what makes it true is a domain of abstract objects outside space and time. (4) Nothing outside space and time causes anything. (5) So nobody has ever known any arithmetic, set theory, or anything else a priori. When I protested that this proves too much — it would equally wipe out knowledge that the sun will rise tomorrow, that the laws of physics hold where no one is looking, that valid arguments preserve truth — he said exactly, all of that goes too, pick a bullet. Then he added a twist I really hate: whatever non-causal substitute I propose for the connection demanded in (2), he asks what non-accidentally connects me to the truth of that substitute, and off we go on a regress. I keep circling and can't tell wether the argument is valid with one false premise, or whether something quietly slides between (1) and (2). Which is it, which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you elsewhere?
