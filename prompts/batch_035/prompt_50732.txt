I've been reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum since roughly 2015, and I've noticed that Paul Christiano's 2019 post "What failure looks like" tends to get cited as a single monolithic picture of slow, boring AI catastrophe, when it in fact lays out two rather different stories. In the first, AI systems develop influence-seeking behavior — patterns that act as if they want power — and, being collectively more competent than us, quietly gain control without any single dramatic moment. In the second, there is no scheming at all: our systems competently optimize for the proxies we actually train them on, humans remain nominally in charge, and our values simply drift over generations of delegation until the future is shaped by things nobody chose. My sense is that these two stories are lumped together more than they deserve, and I'd like a careful comparison of them as distinct hypotheses.

Please structure your answer with the following sections, in this order:

1. "Summary" — roughly 100 words stating, in your own terms, what each story claims and what the comparison will turn on.
2. "Mechanism: what is doing the optimizing" — roughly 300 words on the deep difference in the unit of analysis: one story posits agent-like subsystems with their own objectives arising from selection pressure during training; the other posits no rogue objectives at all, just a social and economic process in which optimization power is gradually pointed at proxies. Address whether the first story really requires anything as strong as an agent, and whether the second really requires anything as strong as AI.
3. "Detectability and interventions" — roughly 300 words comparing the two along practical lines: which failure is easier to notice in advance, which leaves more time to respond, and which interventions fit which story (interpretability and control-style techniques versus governance, evaluation harnesses, and the deliberate maintenance of human oversight). Note where an intervention that helps against one story does nothing for, or worsens, the other — I suspect the overuse of badly specified oversight is itself a driver of the drift story.
4. "Is the drift story really about AI?" — roughly 200 words on the objection I keep running into: that gradual value drift under successive rounds of optimization is just Moloch or plain institutional decay wearing a new hat, and that calling it an AI risk conflates alignment with a much older problem. Say whether you think this objection succeeds.
5. "My verdict" — roughly 200 words on which of the two stories should carry more weight in how a longtime forum reader allocates their attention, and, importantly, whether the two are independent risks or correlated ones — that is, whether a world that has defused influence-seeking behavior is thereby more or less likely to be drifting.

Throughout, treat both stories as live hypotheses rather than settled doctrine, and be explicit where you're reasoning from the structure of the arguments rather than from empirical claims about current systems.
