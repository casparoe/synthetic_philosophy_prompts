So the Principal Principle debate has been going since the 80s and as far as I can tell everyone's positions are fixed and nobody moves. Quick setup of the stall as I see it: Lewis says rational credence in an outcome should equal the objective chance of that outcome, conditional on the chance and any admissible background. But he also gives a Humean analysis of chance — chances are whatever regularities in the total history of events would figure in the best systematization of that history, trading fit and simplicity. The bug everyone keeps circling: once chance is defined that way, the Principal Principle looks less like a norm of rationality and more like a partial definition of what gets to count as a chance function in the first place — a system that agents wouldn't defer to wouldn't have looked chancy, so the deference is, in a sense, baked into the analysis. Humeans say the two roles fit together fine and offer patched versions of the principle; anti-Humeans, who think of chance as a real tendency or power out in the world, say the Humean has quietly changed the subject and their patches just restate their starting intuitions. Nobody is convinced by anyone. My working hypothesis is that this is largely verbal: the two camps are answering different questions under one word — one asking what a rational agent should defer to, the other asking what structure the world actually has — and each side hears the other as botching its own question. Is that right? Or is there a genuine substantive disagreement in there, maybe over whether rational deference has any content at all independent of the pattern of events? And concretely, what would count as progress: a worked-out case where the guide-reading and the definition-reading actually come apart, a split of the concept into two, or is this debate just ill-posed and I should give up on it? Please say how confident you are in whatever diagnosis you give, and point to the specific step in it you're least sure about.
