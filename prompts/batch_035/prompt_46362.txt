I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, though I've read a fair amount of popular-level material on dynamical systems and chaos, and I once wrote a toy double-pendulum simulator that taught me more about floating-point precision than I wanted to know. At a recent team dinner the conversation drifted onto whether the future is "already written", and I found myself saying things that sounded fine individually but didn't obviously fit together as a package. Rather than keep asserting them one at a time, I'd like a proper consistency audit, and I'm told this is the sort of thing people who've studied philosophy are trained to do.

Here is the bundle. (1) The classical laws of physics are deterministic: given the exact state of a closed system at one instant, its entire future is fixed by the equations of motion. (2) Chaotic systems like the weather show that determinism and predictability come apart: such a system is deterministic but practically unpredictable, because any error in the measured initial conditions is amplified exponentially with time. (3) For that reason I believe a sufficiently funded forecasting effort — denser sensor networks, finer model grids, more compute — could in principle push useful weather forecasts out arbitrarily far: months or even years ahead, with the only obstacles being measurement precision and resources. (4) I take quantum measurement outcomes to be genuinely chancy, in the plain textbook sense, and so I believe the universe as a whole is not deterministic. (5) I'm a compatibilist about free will: even if my choices were fully determined in advance, that would not make my deliberating an illusion or my decisions any less mine. (6) And yet I've caught myself telling people that the unpredictability of chaotic systems is what "keeps the future open" and makes room for genuine choice — that without sensitive dependence on initial conditions, our futures would be fixed in a way that matters.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look that way, ordering the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad; for each real one, say what would have to give way and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the bundle. I want an audit of my position set rather than a survey of the literature, and I have no preference about which commitment should be the one to go — I genuinely don't know where the problem is. Finally, since I'll be repeating whatever I learn at the next dinner, please state how confident you are in each verdict you deliver, and explicitly single out the single point in your audit where you would trust your own answer least.
