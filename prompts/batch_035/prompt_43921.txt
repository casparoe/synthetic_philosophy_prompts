At a philosophy of biology reading group I help organize, a member who had been reading a popular book on teleology in biology distributed a one-paragraph summary of the debate over biological function, and I'd like to know how much of it to trust before we discuss it. Here is what he wrote:

"There are two big theories of what it means for a biological trait to have a function. The selected-effects theory says that a trait's function is whatever the trait does now that benefits the organism — the heart's function is to pump blood because pumping blood currently keeps us alive. The causal-role theory says that a function is just whatever a part contributes to the activities of some containing system: since the heart's beating contributes to the circulatory system's ability to move blood, pumping is its function; but on this view making heartbeat sounds is equally a function, since the heart causes that too. The two theories are straightforward rivals: they agree on the ordinary cases and come apart only in odd ones. Each has been refuted. The selected-effects theory is refuted by vestigial organs — the appendix does nothing useful for us, so the theory has to say it has no function, which is absurd. The causal-role theory is refuted by the observation that functions become relative to whatever system a researcher happens to choose for analysis, so they stop being biological facts at all. Given that both accounts collapse under standard objections, the sensible conclusion is that function talk should simply be eliminated from biology."

Go through the paragraph and tell me what a defender of each theory would accept, what each would object to, and where it runs two different claims together. In particular: does the selected-effects theory really define function in terms of current benefit, or is something historical doing the work, and does the answer affect how the vestigial-organ example lands? Do causal-role theorists actually have to grant that making sounds is a function, and if they have a principled way to deny it, what is it? Is the claim that the two theories give the same answers in the ordinary cases and diverge only in weird ones correct, or do the theories aim at different questions in the first place? And are the two "refutations" genuine refutations, or only boundary-marking observations that defenders can absorb? I'm somewhat drawn to the final sentence about eliminating function talk, but that may only be because this paragraph is all I've read on the topic. Please also flag where your corrections are most likely to be wrong — which of them rest on contested readings of the literature, and what an argument or example would have to show to make you abandon your verdict on any part of the summary.
