My flatmate thinks he's found a proof that Moore-paradox sentences are secretly contradictory, and that the whole "consistent but unassertable" line everyone takes is just wrong. It goes:

(1) Take "It's raining, but I don't believe it." There's clearly a possible situation where it's true — me asleep while it pours outside, or me confidently believing it's dry. So it's not a contradiction in the strict sense. He grants this for now, just to set up the trap.
(2) General principle: whatever could be true could be believed by someone. No truth is in principle unbelievable. (He says if you deny this you're stuck with truths that couldn't so much as be held in mind by anyone, and he doubts anyone wants to commit to that.)
(3) So it's possible that I believe that it's raining and I don't believe it.
(4) If you believe a conjunction you believe each conjunct. So in that situation I believe that it's raining, and I believe that I don't believe that it's raining.
(5) But someone who believes it's raining can't also believe that they don't believe it's raining. His gloss: a rational believer can't be that directly wrong about their own mind — believing p while believing you don't believe p would mean your beliefs about your beliefs are contradicted by the beliefs themselves.
(6) So the Moore sentence isn't just unassertable, it's unbelievable — no possible situation where anyone believes it.
(7) And now the principle from (2) runs the other way: a sentence that couldn't be believed by anyone in any possible situation can't be possibly true either. So (1) was wrong all along — the contradiction is real, just buried somewhere in the pragmatics, and the standard story ("it's consistent, it just can't be sincerely asserted, that's why it sounds paradoxical") collapses.

He's awfully pleased with himself and keeps saying this also proves nobody can believe "it's raining and nobody believes it's raining," which he takes as more evidence that the consistent-but-unbelievable category is bogus.

Part of me wants to reject (5), since actual people do seem capable of believing they lack beliefs they have — but saying that feels like just conceding humans are sloppy rather than diagnosing anything. Part of me suspects (2) of doing something fishy, but it looks obviously true for ordinary sentences like "there are seventeen trees in the park." And I genuinely can't tell whether his step from (6) to (7) is even the same principle as (2) or something stronger that (2) never licensed. Walk me through which step to reject, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs him — including whether any of this actually touches the standard pragmatic explanation of why the sentence sounds absurd when asserted.
