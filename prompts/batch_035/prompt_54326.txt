For roughly the past decade I've followed the consciousness wars on LessWrong and adjacent blogs, long enough to have watched the claim that phenomenal consciousness is some kind of conceptual confusion migrate from fringe heterodoxy to something like local orthodoxy — and somewhere along the way I became convinced of a version of it myself: what I'll defend is that phenomenal consciousness, as most people intuitively conceive it, is a user illusion. The introspective sense that there are intrinsic, private, ineffable qualities of experience — qualia over and above all the functional and representational facts — is an artifact of how the brain models its own information-processing, roughly as a desktop icon is a purpose-built simplification of circuitry rather than a faithful picture of it. My sketch of the argument: first, introspection is not inner observation but a perceptual interface delivering a compressed self-model tuned for usefulness rather than accuracy; second, the features that model attributes to experience — intrinsicness, privacy, ineffability, some nonfunctional residue — are interface features being misread as features of the thing modeled; third, once that misreading is diagnosed, the hard problem dissolves into a set of ordinary, tractable problems about how the self-model is implemented and why it takes the form it does; and fourth, crucially, the view does not deny that we seem to have phenomenal properties, since seeming is the phenomenon — it denies only that seeming requires being.
