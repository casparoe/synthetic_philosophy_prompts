My partner and I have until the end of the month to pick between two cities, and I've managed to turn it into a philosophy problem, which if I'm honest is probably just elaborate procrastination. Option A: stay put — kind of mediocre jobs for both of us, but we're ten minutes from my aging dad and basically everyone we know is here. Option B: move — we'd both get work we're actually excited about, more money, but we'd see my dad maybe twice a year and have to build a social life from scratch. Here's the thing that's been bugging me: neither option is better. But they don't feel equal either. "Equal" feels wrong in a way I can't quite put my finger on — like it would mean the values just cancel out, and they obviously don't, there's no common currency they're sitting on a scale in.

So naturally I went down the rabbit hole, and it turns out this exact thing is a live debate. Raz at one point suggested that hard choices are ones where the options are exactly equal in value, and Ruth Chang has spent her career arguing that's a mistake — that there's a third relation, parity, where two options are comparable, neither one is better, and yet they're not equal, and it's precisely because they're on a par that our choosing creates reasons instead of just passively tracking reasons that were already there.

What I want from you is an actual credence — a number, not "it depends" — that Chang's parity view is the right story about what's happening in choices like mine. The rivals are: Raz-style exact equality; the "no fact of the matter, it's just indeterminate" view; and the deflationary view that parity talk is just phenomenology, a description of how hard choices feel, with no real relation out there backing it up. Then decompose the number. Roughly: the probability that there's a determinate value relation between options like these at all; the probability that "neither is better" reflects genuine comparability rather than just the two of us being ignorant of some fact that would settle it; the probability that, conditional on all that, the relation is parity rather than exact equality; plus any other terms you think belong in there, each with a number and one line of justification. Then show me the combination. After that, tell me which term you're least confident in and what would actually move it. And if you had to locate the disagreement between someone at 0.8 for parity and someone at 0.2 — is it about whether value comparison is always ultimately quantitative, about whether reasons created by the choice are real reasons, or somewhere else entirely?

I'll admit the practical stakes are mostly my sanity at this point — I've noticed that whichever framing I wake up believing, I feel either relieved or trapped, so I'd also take one sentence at the end on whether the answer should even matter for how we pick. But mainly I want the number and it's decomposition.
