I am a retired physicist who has, over the past few years, taken up philosophy of perception in earnest. Next month I present to a mixed seminar: mostly philosophers of perception, including one who holds that the sight-restoration studies of the last two decades settled Molyneux's question empirically, plus several developmental vision scientists who have worked directly with patients whose sight was restored after congenital blindness, and a handful of graduate students with no stake in the fight.

My thesis is that those studies answered a question Molyneux never asked. Molyneux asked whether a person born blind, newly granted sight, could at first sight — before any opportunity to learn what things look like — tell a sphere from a cube by sight alone. The studies instead show that such patients learn to match seen objects to felt ones within minutes or days; that is a finding about the speed of cross-modal learning, not about whether the senses share a spatial vocabulary prior to experience. I will further claim that once "at first sight" is made precise — say, within the first few fixations, before any feedback — the answer to Molyneux's question as he posed it is no, and that the genuinely interesting question the studies answer is how fast the two senses converge.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections this audience will actually raise, in roughly the order I should expect them, each phrased as that audience would phrase it; for each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, what I should concede, and where the reply is soft. Identify the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss, and include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Please answer in American English.
