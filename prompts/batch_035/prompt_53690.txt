Last night a friend of mine — a software engineer with strong opinions and more patience than either of us should have had at that hour — argued his way from Hilbert's hotel to the conclusion that actually infinite collections are incoherent, and I ended up defending far more of Cantor than I had planned to. His case had two prongs, and I want to state them fairly, because I expect to hear them again: first, that the hotel's trick of shifting every guest to the next room only works because the guests form a finished, re-indexable totality, so the argument quietly presupposes the very thing it was supposed to make palatable; second, that "some infinities are bigger than others" is a bookkeeping convention dressed up as a discovery, since if every natural number has a partner among the evens, the evens can't be smaller in any sense that matters — they're the same collection with every other element struck out.

Next week I have to teach a fifty-minute first-year section on actual versus potential infinity. The students are philosophy majors with at most calculus behind them; they have never seen a proof by bijection, and most will arrive thinking of infinity as a very large number that keeps growing. I don't want the explanation itself — I want a teaching plan. Specifically, I'd like: the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what depends on what, so that the definitions feel inevitable rather than stipulative; which single example should carry the whole session, and what that example must contain and avoid — I am no longer confident Hilbert's hotel is the right carrier at all, since it so easily reads as a party trick that demonstrates nothing; the misconceptions the students will predictably bring or form, including the ones my friend articulated and the ones first-years generate on their own (that Cantor proved infinity exists; that potential infinity is just actual infinity with an escape clause; that equipotence means two collections are the same in every respect), each with the point in the sequence where I should head it off rather than let it calcify; guidance on keeping the distinction between mathematical coherence and physical instantiateability from collapsing, since that distinction was the entire argument with my friend; one closing question whose answer would show me whether they have genuinely grasped equipotence or merely enjoyed the parade; and what I should openly decline to explain at this level — the Continuum Hypothesis, large cardinals, whatever you judge — and how to decline it without implying it is unimportant. Please plan rather than expound.
