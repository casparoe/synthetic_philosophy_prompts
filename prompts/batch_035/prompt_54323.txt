For my final-year seminar on conditionals I must submit a short debate piece defending the following position, and I would like you to draft the argument for me: Lewis's triviality results are best read as a general proof that no proposition whatsoever can play the role that the Equation assigns to indicative conditionals, where the Equation is the thesis that the probability of "if A, B" equals the conditional probability of B given A; so we face a choice between denying the Equation and denying that indicative conditionals express propositions at all, and we should take the second horn, treating utterances of "if A, B" as conditional assertions — the speaker asserts B under the supposition that A. Please write the argument as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, in British English. One point should briefly reconstruct why the triviality argument forces this dilemma rather than merely embarrassing one analysis; one should answer the objection that we can intelligibly conjoin and negate conditionals (as in "it's not the case that if A then B, and also not the case that if A then C") by saying what such compounds amount to on a conditional-assertion reading; and one should develop the suggestion that the marginality of embedded conditionals in ordinary grammar — consider how strange "if if A then B, then C" sounds — is itself evidence for the view, since a language in which speakers never needed to embed a construction would be just what you would expect if that construction did not express a proposition. The remaining two points are yours to allocate to whatever supporting considerations you judge strongest.
