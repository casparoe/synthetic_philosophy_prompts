So here's my situation. I'm a journalist — I write about science and ideas, mostly explainers and essays for the weekend sections — and I've somehow got a 45-minute slot at a festival-of-ideas type event next month. My talk is on the Condorcet jury theorem, and my thesis is basically: this is the most misquoted result in popular social science. The TED-talk version says "big groups are wise, add more people, average out the noise." What the theorem actually gives you is a conditional: IF each voter is independently better than a coin flip, THEN the majority's reliability climbs toward certainty as you add voters. And the conditional is doing all the work. The part nobody quotes is the twin result, which comes out of the same math: if voters are below coin-flip accuracy, majorities get more reliably wrong as the group grows — bigger crowd, more confident mistake. Plus, real voters aren't independent: they read the same handful of outlets, talk to each other, get hit by the same mood. Once you allow correlated errors, adding voters stops being guaranteed to help, and the honest version of my talk is "wisdom of crowds is a conditional, not a law of nature, and we're terrible at checking whether the condition holds — seperate question, and usually the whole game."
