I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and I've ended up with a set of views about how to make decisions and give to charity. I'd like a consistency check. My views, roughly:

1. If the probabilities and payoffs of the options can be written down as numbers, the right choice is the one with the highest expected value, and this applies even to one-off decisions you'll never get to repeat.
2. Extraordinary claims should get very low prior probabilities, and updating on evidence is how you fix them; that's just good epistemics, not a bias.
3. If one charity averts a death for $3,000 and another for $5,000, giving to the cheaper one is morally required, not merely wise, because a life saved is a life saved and the rest is accounting.
4. The stranger who promises, without any evidence, to prevent the torture of an astronomically large number of people in a far-off simulation if and only if you hand over $5 today should be refused, and refusing him is not a failure of nerve.
5. I would not put my retirement savings on a fair coin flip that doubles them, but I think a donor choosing between charities should be effectively indifferent to risk.

Please reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, one per belief, each a sentence or two stating whether that belief is consistent with the rest, and, if not, which one it clashes with and what would have to give. No introduction or conclusion.
