So I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background at all, and I fell down a rabbit hole arguing with a friend about whether an LLM "has beliefs." Someone in the thread brought up Davidson's radical interpretation and summed it up as basically "you can only interpret a system by assuming it's mostly right," and now I can't let it go. From where I sit it sounds wierdly familiar: it's what we do when we train a model on a corpus. All you get is observable input-output behavior, and you have to hypothesize the machinery underneath without peeking inside. But apparently Davidson thinks this line of thinking gets you somewhere much stronger — like that most of a speaker's beliefs must be true, and that the very idea of a radically different conceptual scheme is incoherent? I'd like an explanation of what radical interpretation is actually doing. What exactly the interpreter is given in Davidson's setup (his version of "the data"), how the principle of charity is supposed to work, and how he gets from "I can't interpret you unless I take most of what you say to be true" to conclusions about belief being mostly true and conceptual schemes not existing.

The gears I most want to see: is charity supposed to be an epistemic constraint on interpreters (a practical fact about how translation/interpretation has to proceed) or a metaphysical claim about what belief and meaning are? Because those feel very different, and I can't tell which one Davidson needs for the big conclusions. Also, I've seen people complain that there's a slide in there somewhere — that even if successful interpretation requires me to interpret you as agreeing with me, that doesn't show you actually agree with me or that your beliefs are true — but I don't fully understand the complaint or whether it sticks. And is the anti-conceptual-schemes argument supposed to be a separate argument or the same one in a different hat?

Feel free to use engineering analogies (fitting a model, building a compiler, reverse-engineering a black box) if they actually carry weight, but don't dumb it down — I'd rather have the real structure with the analogies as scaffolding than a pile of analogies. Please write in American English. And one thing that matters to me: separate what's actually settled among people who know this stuff from what's genuinely contested — e.g. whether the argument against conceptual schemes works, whether charity is about truth or just about interpretability, how much of the standard reading of Davidson is later reconstruction — and don't hand me a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus. If the honest answer to "did the slide happen?" is "philosophers have been fighting about this for decades," say that and give me the shape of the fight.
