I run a small philosophy website, and my next interactive feature is a sorting quiz aimed at educated non-specialists: not "which ethical theory do you like?" but a diagnostic instrument for one narrow and surprisingly slippery question — what, if anything, all pleasures have in common, i.e., the heterogeneity problem for hedonism. The quiz should sort readers by their answers to concrete cases, and then the scoring key should tell them where those answers place them in the actual philosophical landscape.

Please design the following.

1. Ten questions. Each must present a concrete case with two or three answer options. The cases should be chosen so that the main positions genuinely come apart on them — for example, variants of: someone pleased about a friend's success that they never experience or think about consciously; the masochist whose pain is welcomed; the absorbed climber or chess player who reports no distinct feeling of pleasure until the activity ends; the pleasure of relief when an itch is scratched, versus a positive sensation like warmth; sensory pleasures of food and drink versus the quieter satisfaction of learning something one wanted to know; pleasure grounded in a false belief (being delighted that a friend won the lottery when she did not); and any other cases you judge to be more diagnostic than these. Do not include any question that simply asks readers to self-apply a label like "is pleasure a feeling or an attitude?" — every question must be answerable by someone who has never read a word of philosophy.

2. Derive the answer space before the names. That is, first set out the axes the questions probe — for instance, whether every pleasure must be phenomenally conscious, whether pleasure requires an object or propositional content, whether it requires a pro-attitude, whether the felt quality (if any) is common across cases or only locally so — and show which combinations of answers the ten questions can in principle distinguish. Then locate the known positions in that space as leaves: felt-quality or phenomenalist accounts; propositional-attitude or attitudinal accounts on which pleasure is being pleased that something is the case; desire-satisfaction accounts on which pleasure is the subjective satisfaction of desire; hybrid accounts that require both a feeling and an object-directed attitude; and deflationary or family-resemblance positions on which the heterogeneity is simply conceded and hedonism is reinterpreted as a claim about a loose cluster. Where you are confident about who defends a leaf, attach names; where you are not confident, say so rather than guessing.

3. Include a final branch for readers whose answers accept the heterogeneity across the board: state clearly whether their pattern amounts to abandoning hedonism about well-being, or merely to reforming it, and what separates those two outcomes.

4. Explicitly identify answer patterns that no one occupies in the literature — and say, briefly, whether each unoccupied combination is unoccupied because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive for some identifiable reason, or simply because it has not been explored.

5. Flag honestly which pairs of positions your ten questions cannot separate. In particular, consider whether a case-based quiz can distinguish an attitudinal account from a desire-satisfaction account at all, given that any case involving a pleasing object will involve both an attitude toward that object and a desire concerning it; if they cannot be separated by cases, say so and note what would be needed to separate them instead, rather than inventing a fake diagnostic question.

6. In the scoring key, for each leaf give one sentence on the leaf's most awkward commitment — the case that costs it the most — so that readers finish the quiz knowing not just which position they hold but where it is most likely to hurt.

Keep the tone polished but accessible: readers should never need a technical term to answer a question, though the scoring key may use the technical vocabulary and define it on first use. Do not include citations or a reading list.
