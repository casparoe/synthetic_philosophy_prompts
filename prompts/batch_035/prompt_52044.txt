I am a novelist midway through a book narrated by a neuroscientist who has come to hold that phenomenal consciousness is an introspective illusion — that there is nothing it is like to be her, only a powerful and systematically misleading appearance that there is. Two chapters turn on her working out, in her notebook, what would actually make her give the view up, and I refuse to fake those scenes. So write me the specification she would draft: the desiderata any adequate theory of consciousness must meet before she would concede that the phenomenon is real and explained — the cases it has to handle (the explanatory gap, Mary and the color scientist, blindsight and kindred dissociations, dreams, pain, the asymmetry between how experience seems from inside and what third-person science can reach), the distinctions it must not collapse (phenomenal character from access and reportability, eliminativism about qualia from reduction of them, the illusion of experience from the experience of illusion), and the empirical results it cannot contradict — with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Then mark which of these are genuine desiderata and which are prejudices of one camp smuggled in as standards, and tell me whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable: my suspicion is that the trouble lives in the pair 'must explain why it seems there is something it is like' and 'must not help itself to unexplained seemings in the explanation,' and I want to know if that is a real tension or a bad reading. Finally, in a paragraph, score illusionism, global workspace theory, and higher-order thought against the list — my character should be defeated or vindicated by real standards, not ones I invent for convenience.
