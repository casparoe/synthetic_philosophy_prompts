I'm finishing a graduate seminar that straddles formal epistemology and market microstructure, and for our last session each of us has to submit a written "market epistemology profile" — a plain statement of our considered views, which the rest of the group will then try to pull apart. Mine is below, and before I hand it over I'd like an honest audit of it, because I suspect there are problems in the set but I can't tell where. The commitments are: (1) I take the no-trade result to be basically sound: among agents who share a common prior, are rational, and have those facts common knowledge, there should be no purely speculative trade — trade should trace to liquidity needs, hedging, or differences in risk tolerance, never to one side simply knowing better. (2) I think the common prior assumption is a reasonable idealization, at least for sophisticated agents operating in the same market with access to the same public information; where priors differ wildly, I take that as evidence that at least one party formed its prior through some defective process. (3) Following Aumann, I think rational agents with common priors cannot agree to disagree, so persistent, unresolvable disagreement among informed experts is a symptom of irrationality somewhere in the pool. (4) I think actual markets are impressively informationally efficient: prices aggregate dispersed private information to a degree that no individual analyst can hope to match. (5) I nonetheless spend a great deal of time doing fundamental research on individual stocks and trade actively on it, in the sincere expectation of earning modest excess returns. (6) I think the bulk of trading volume is generated by noise traders with behavioral biases, and that part of what makes my research profitable is trading against them. (7) I think some bubbles are identifiable from the inside — I exited crypto in late 2017, not on the basis of anything in the price data, but because it seemed obvious to me that prices had detached from any plausible story about fundamentals. (8) And in general I think that when my judgment conflicts with a market price, I should usually defer to the price, since the price reflects far more information than I have.

Two friends have already given me preliminary feedback and I can't tell whether either has located a real problem. One says (1) and (5) flatly contradict each other — that I can't believe the no-trade theorem and also be a deliberate speculative trader. The other says the real trouble is (4) and (6), because if volume is dominated by noise traders pushing prices around for behavioral reasons, it is mysterious why prices should remain informationally efficient at all. My own hunch, which I distrust, is that the trouble involves (7) and (3) somehow — that calling a bubble from the inside, on the basis of what seems obvious to me, is exactly the kind of confident private conviction that (3) and (8) tell me to discount when it puts me against a crowd. But maybe that dissolves too, since (6) gives me a story about why the crowd on the other side of my crypto exit wasn't fully rational.

So here is what I'd like from you. Go through the eight commitments and tell me which pairs or clusters genuinely conflict, which apparent conflicts dissolve on inspection, and rank the genuine tensions by severity — don't just flag every pair that could be made to look problematic. For each real tension, say precisely which commitment is doing the damage, what the cheapest consistent revision would be, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most. I'd also like a verdict on my two friends' diagnoses: was either of them right, or did they both miss the worst of it? I honestly don't know which of these views I'd give up first, so please don't assume you know either — I want the audit to decide that, not my preferences.
