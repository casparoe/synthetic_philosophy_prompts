I sit on the board of a mid-sized industrial company that has spent the past year in a bidding war with a rival to acquire a smaller software firm. Between advisor fees, financing commitments with ticking fees, a dedicated integration team, and repeated rounds of due diligence, we are roughly $40 million into pursuit costs, and the target's board keeps running ascending rounds. At our last meeting we had to decide whether to submit one final raise or withdraw, and the argument was carried by our CFO, Delia, and Marcus, our head of corporate development. I couldn't tell afterwards whether they were disagreeing about the sunk-cost fallacy, about what psychologists call escalation of commitment, or about whether those are even different things. Here is the exchange as I recorded it.

1. Delia argued for withdrawal. The $40 million is spent; whether to continue depends only on whether the remaining costs beat the remaining benefits, and on our best projection of where the rival's limit lies, the price we would now have to go to makes continuing a losing proposition. Continuing out of deference to money already spent is the sunk-cost fallacy — what biologists call the Concorde fallacy — and the fact that we have already paid so much is precisely what that principle forbids counting.

2. Marcus replied that he wasn't counting the $40 million as a reason, only as a position. We are, he said, in the structure of the dollar auction that the game theorist Martin Shubik introduced: the prize goes to the highest bidder, but the second-highest bidder also has to pay and gets nothing, so the party that stops first pays for the other's win. Withdraw now and the $40 million is a certain dead loss with nothing to show for it; put in one more increment, and there is a real chance the rival folds below our bar and the deal still clears it — projections of their limit are uncertain, and that chance is what the last increment buys. That is a comparison of future outcomes from where we stand — exactly the marginal comparison the sunk-cost principle demands. Continuing isn't the fallacy, he said; it's the principle applied honestly.

3. Delia answered that he had described the trap's anatomy rather than escaped it. A sequence of locally sensible increments that lands you at an endpoint you would have rejected outright at the start is irrational even if every step passes a stepwise test; the price we would end up paying is one we would have laughed out of the room a year ago. The fallacy isn't only treating sunk costs as reasons — it is letting stepwise comparison substitute for judgment of the whole plan. That is how the Concorde got built: no single year's incremental funding looked crazy; the total was.

4. Marcus responded that if so, the standard lesson is the dangerous one. The rule that only future costs and benefits matter is precisely the rule that licenses each next bid, because at each point the next increment really does look better than stopping; taught as a slogan, the sunk-cost principle is what escalates. The safeguards that actually work in contests of this kind are different in kind from the principle itself: fix a walk-away price before the contest begins and treat it as binding, or better, refuse to enter games in which every step looks better than the exit.

5. Delia's last word was that a walk-away price fixed in advance is just a device for forcing yourself to take the global view you should be taking anyway, so the two of them agree in substance and differ only over which formulation deserves to be taught — and his prescription, she noted, concedes that the stepwise view is unreliable, which was her point from the start.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether the speaker is right, partly right, or wrong, and why — I want a verdict on each point, not just an overall winner. Then address three questions. (a) Is Delia's final claim correct — is this a merely verbal disagreement about which formulation to teach, or is there a substantive question about which decision rule is actually correct in competitive escalation? (b) Is Marcus right that the sunk-cost principle, applied incrementally, licenses escalation in structures like this one — and if he is, does that show the principle is misstated, or misapplied, or something worse? (c) What, if anything, are both of them missing about situations of this kind?
