hey! im a PhD student in formal epistemology (dissertation on scoring rules, please don't ask) and I'm putting together the spoof section for our department's newsletter. I want a piece satirizing a very specific move people make in arguments: quoting Aumann's agreement theorem at their interlocutor. you know the type — "we're both honest agents with a common prior, so we literally cannot agree to disagree, so you have to update toward me." the theorem gets used as a rhetorical crowbar, and I want to satirize that practice — not Aumann himself, and honestly not even Bayesians. the target is the move, not the math.

the form: a deadpan corporate FAQ page, roughly 550-650 words, for a fictional startup. Call it something like "Convergence™ — Disagreement Resolution as a Service." The pitch: you and your interlocutor submit your credences, their trained facilitators walk you through rounds of mutual belief revision, and they guarantee agreement. FAQ entries should cover things like:

- what exactly is guaranteed — agreement on the posterior, with fine print conceding that the theorem guarantees you'll converge but not that you'll converge to anything in particular, and definitely not to the truth
- the common prior requirement (something in the spirit of "if you and your partner did not enter the relationship with a common prior, that is a you problem," or whatever version of that joke you like better)
- honesty ("strategic updating voids the warranty")
- whether it works on value disagreements (no — credences only, take your divorce elsewhere)
- communication costs / why the basic plan is so expensive
- a testimonial, maybe from a couple who converged and now aren't sure what to talk about at dinner

the rule I care most about: every joke should exaggerate a real feature of the theorem or of how it gets quoted, not invent one. the load-bearing features are: common priors, common knowledge of the posteriors, honesty, posterior-guarantees-not-truth, and the fact that the theorem is silent on what the agreed number turns out to be. if you happen to know the follow-up literature (the back-and-forth communication results, the communication-complexity stuff), feel free to mine it too, but don't lean on anything someone who's only read the 1976 paper couldn't follow — the newsletter audience is smart but not specialist. and keep the irony legible and deadpan: no winking at the reader, the company is entirely sincere about its product.

take the role of a formal epistemologist who also happens to write good deadpan satire — you know both the theorem and the genre well enough that a specialist reads the piece and laughs rather than winces. think through where each joke is actually coming from before you write (a quick private brainstorm is fine), and cut any joke that boils down to "Bayesians say weird things" without real content behind it.

format: begin with the FAQ itself, no preface and no commentary before or after. company name and tagline first, then the Q&As.
