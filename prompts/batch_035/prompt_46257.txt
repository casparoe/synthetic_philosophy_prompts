Last spring I taught iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies as a straight lecture and it went in one ear and out the other — students could run the algorithm on toy matrices fine, but they walked away thinking dominance is something you spot by eyeballing rows, and then got completly lost the moment a mixture was doing the dominating. So this year I want them to hit the wall themselves before I name anything. The class: intermediate game theory for econ undergrads at a mid-sized state school, about 25 students, one 75-minute session, and they've already had dominance solvability over pure strategies (Nash equilibrium is next week, if that helps you stage anything).

Here's the wall I want them to run into. I need a game where elimination over pure strategies alone stalls — nothing is dominated by any single strategy, so the procedure they learned returns the whole game back unchanged — but where one strategy is in fact strictly dominated by a mixture of two others, and once you delete it and re-run the procedure on what's left, the game solves down further. Ideally the discovery comes from them: they get stuck applying what they know, someone eventually tries rolling a die between two rows, and the score sheets show it beating the dominated strategy. So design the whole thing for me — the actual payoff matrices (I want at least a 3x3 for the row player, with a strategy that no single pure strategy dominates but a 50/50 mix does, and clear roles for both players), a payoff scheme in points that I can redeem for something small at the end of term, the round structure and timing, what I should say and not say while it runs, and then a debrief that maps what happened onto the formal distinction between dominance by a pure strategy and dominance by a mixture, and onto why the iteration restarts on the reduced game after every deletion instead of just continuing. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my guess is some loud kid annouces the trick within five minutes, and that the per-round variance hides the mixture's advantage so it only shows up in expectation — and be honest about which part of the idea the activity just can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief myself.

One last thing: write it all up in American English please, my department style guide is fussy and I keep slipping into the spellings I grew up with.
