I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group that has lately drifted into arguments about AI, and last session one of the members — a committed Searlean — deployed an analogy I couldn't properly evaluate on the spot. He retold Dennett's two-bitser thought experiment, roughly as follows: imagine a simple coin mechanism that accepts US quarters and reliably rejects slugs; now ship it to Panama, where the local coin is minted to the same specification, and it thereby becomes a balboa-detector. Dennett's point, as my friend told it, was that once the machine is embedded in its new niche, its internal states are genuinely about balboas — real, if modest, intentionality — which is meant to show that Searle's line between original and derived intentionality is far shallower than Searle pretends. My friend then turned the example around: a large language model, he said, is a two-bitser through and through — its 'meanings' are as derived and contextual as a coin slot's balboa-detection, parasitic on the humans who built and trained it, so whatever is going on inside it, it is never really understanding anything, and no amount of scale will change that. It struck me only afterwards that he may have been leaning on an analogy against its own author's conclusion, which is either a clever double-cross or a cheat, and I can't tell which.

Please audit the analogy properly. First, set out the correspondence table: what plays the role of the coin mechanism's discrimination, the designers, the users who exploit its states, the Panamanian niche in which balboa-detection becomes genuinely useful, and the migration itself — and then say what fills those slots for a language model (training, the human trainers, the users, the surrounding ecosystem of use). Second, say where the mapping breaks: which disanalogies between a coin slot and a trained model actually matter for the question of intentionality, and which are just superficial differences that shouldn't affect the verdict. Third, and most importantly, adjudicate whether my friend's conclusion — merely derived intentionality, hence nothing real going on, no matter how capable the system gets — actually survives the audit, or whether Dennett's original use of the two-bitser cuts against him; you might also consider what Searle's own appeal to derived intentionality (for instance, his claim that the symbols manipulated in the Chinese Room are like words on paper, meaningful only to outside interpreters) would need to be true for the friend's argument to go through, and whether Dennett's story gives us reason to doubt it. I'd particularly like to know whether the 'parasitic' framing smuggles in anything — some idea that derived intentionality is somehow counterfeit or ungrounded — that the two-bitser itself was designed to undercut. Please answer in British English, and feel free to be blunt about where my friend is on firm ground rather than only defending the Dennettian side.
