Frank Ramsey died in 1930, a few years after his 1926 paper "Truth and Probability" gave the world what we now call the Dutch book argument: an agent whose degrees of belief violate the probability axioms is disposed to accept a set of bets on which she loses money in every state of the world. Suppose Ramsey could be shown the probabilistic debates of the following ninety years and asked for his considered view on imprecise credences. Present him with the development as follows. In 1961, Daniel Ellsberg described decision problems — the famous urns whose color compositions are partly unknown — in which sophisticated subjects systematically violate the sure-thing principle by preferring bets whose probabilities they know over bets whose probabilities they do not. The diagnosis that stuck is that such an agent does not hold a single sharp credence function over the relevant propositions but rather a set of such functions, and the imprecise-credences framework built on that idea in the later twentieth century claims to inherit the spirit of Ramsey's own consistency requirement: so long as every probability function in the agent's credal set is itself coherent, no finite set of bets she accepts is a guaranteed loss under every member of the set, so she is immune to the original Dutch book. Defenders of sharpness have pressed diachronic versions of the argument in reply: an imprecise agent who updates her credal set by conditioning can, in certain cases, be led step by step to accept a sequence of bets over time on which she loses money overall — and conditioning on new information sometimes spreads her credal intervals rather than tightening them, a phenomenon known as dilation. What makes this an interesting question about Ramsey rather than merely a question about the modern debate is a footnote to the 1926 paper itself, in which he conceded that ordinary degrees of belief are not really numerically precise and are at best partially ordered, even while building his theory as though they were sharp. So: would Ramsey regard the imprecise framework as the honest completion of that footnote — his partial orderings made systematic, and his betting-criterion operationalism relaxed only as far as necessary to accommodate Ellsberg-type agents — or as a confusion of the psychology of belief with its logic, with the betting criterion requiring any rational agent to quote exact odds, the footnoted concession concerning mere measurement noise rather than an opening for a rival theory of partial belief? In answering, please do three things. First, lay out which features of his actual philosophy — his operationalism about measurement, his verificationism, his pragmatism about truth and assertion, and his insistence that probability theory is a logic of partial belief — cut on each side of the question. Second, take a definite position on where he would come down, and defend it against the best case for the opposite verdict. Third, say whether Ellsberg's subjects come out rational or irrational by Ramsey's own lights, and whether that verdict should be read as a cost of, or a vindication of, the Dutch book methodology he founded. Please answer in American English, and write at length; I care more about the quality of the reconstruction than about brevity.
