Everyone cites the 2018 Uber self-driving fatality in Tempe as the textbook responsibility gap case, and I want to actually test whether that label fits instead of just repeating it. Quick facts in case they're fuzzy: a Volvo test vehicle in autonomous mode hit and killed Elaine Herzberg, who was pushing a bike across the road at night, away from a crosswalk. The system detected her about six seconds before impact at 43 mph but kept reclassifying what she was and never braked; the car's built-in emergency braking had been disabled; and the human safety driver, Rafaela Vasquez, was looking at her phone streaming a TV show and only grabbed the wheel less than a second before impact. Afterwards prosecutors declined to charge Uber, while Vasquez ended up charged with negligent homicide (she later pled guilty to endangerment and got probation).

So here's what I want. First, do the careful mapping: who are the candidate responsible agents in this case, and for each one, say what the case for holding them responsible would even be (the engineers who disabled the braking and built the perception system, Uber as a corporation, the safety driver, the regulators who let testing happen on public roads, even Herzberg for crossing where she did). Second, explain where the responsibility gap idea — the claim from the autonomous weapons literature that when a system acts autonomously enough, responsibility for its behavior falls out of the hands of every human involved — actually bites here. Does it? The system wasn't fully autonomous, there was a human in the seat the whole time, so maybe the "gap" story doesn't even apply and this is just a bog standard case of negligence by a distracted employee? Third, the part I really care about: the legal outcome dumped everything on the nearest warm body, the safety driver, while the company walked. Analyze that. Is it a decent-faith attempt to locate responsibility where it actually tracks fault, or is it the system manufacturing a responsible party because it can't stomach a gap, and does it matter that she was the least powerful person in the chain?

Then run it the other direction: what does this episode show about the responsibility gap thesis itself? One possibility is that the case is evidence gaps are real and our institutions just paper over them badly. Another is that so-called gaps are just distributed responsibility plus lazy prosecutors, in which case the philosophical problem dissolves into an ordinary design-and-policy problem. Say which reading you find more persuasive and why, and be honest about where the gap framework is silent or stretched (e.g., whether it matters that the fatal error was arguably a boring software defect rather than anything "autonomous" in the interesting sense).

Lastly: does your analysis change if we imagine the same crash with no safety driver at all, fully driverless? Does the gap become obvious then, or does responsibility just migrate to the manufacturer and get absorbed as a cost of doing business — and if that's the likely outcome, is "responsibility gap" the right description, or should we be talking about something more like responsibility laundering?
