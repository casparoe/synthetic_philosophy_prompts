A member of my decision-theory reading group set out to defend Hofstadter's superrationality and ended with an argument that, if it works, seems to swallow the very idea it was meant to rescue. I cannot locate the false step, and I would like a careful diagnosis rather than a verdict on superrationality itself. Please write your answer in British English.

Some background first, so that nothing depends on looking anything up. In Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma, an eccentric trillionaire gathers twenty people and promises a billion dollars to whichever of them is the only one to send him a telegram by noon the next day; if the telegrams number zero or more than one, nobody receives anything, and the players may not communicate. On the standard analysis, each player should send: sending weakly dominates not sending, since if nobody else sends it wins the billion, and in every other case it leaves the sender no worse off. So twenty conventionally rational players all send, the count comes to twenty, and everyone gets nothing. The superrationalist instead reasons that identical reasoners facing identical information reach identical conclusions, so that only common strategies are on offer; the best common strategy is for each player to send with probability 1/20, which works out to an expected payoff of roughly nineteen million dollars each. That result is what my friend set out to defend. His argument runs as follows.

(1) A rational agent performs the act whose expected utility, conditional on everything the agent knows, is highest.

(2) If I know that another agent reaches conclusions by the same reasoning I use, on the same information, then my reaching a conclusion is, for me, conclusive evidence that they reach it; and this holds not only for conclusions but for the acts those conclusions prescribe.

(3) So in Platonia the twenty identical reasoners each randomise at 1/20 and each expects about nineteen million dollars, where twenty self-interested maximisers each send for sure and collectively get nothing. Superrationality is thereby vindicated, my friend says: same reasoning, better outcome, and with no causal claim anywhere in it — nobody's telegram makes anybody else send; it is only that one logic yields one answer.

(4) But (2), he continues, is just an instance of evidential reasoning, and it cannot be stopped at perfect mirrors. If a stranger is ninety-five per cent likely to reason as I do, then my reaching a conclusion is strong evidence of his reaching it; at eighty per cent, somewhat weaker evidence; and there is no threshold at which evidential support snaps off. Every human is to some degree a mirror of every other.

(5) Applying (1) with whatever evidence (4) licenses: in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a stranger known to resemble me in judgement, my settling on cooperation is evidence that he is settling on it too; conditional on my act, cooperation then maximises expected utility; so I should cooperate — and the same goes for anyone whose reasoning correlates with mine at all, in any game of this shape.

(6) Therefore superrationality was never a distinctively game-theoretic idea: it is evidential decision theory applied to people who resemble you. And the implications run well beyond games. I should give to charity whenever my giving is evidence that similar people give; I should keep promises whenever my keeping them is evidence that similar people keep theirs; in general, my character is, evidentially speaking, everybody else's, and I am obliged to supply evidence about it in whatever way the conditional expected utilities dictate.

My friend is pleased with (6) and has become an evidentialist. I am not pleased: (6) seems to prove too much. Yet every place I might draw a line looks bad to me. If I deny (4), I owe a principled difference between the perfect mirror and the ninety-five per cent mirror, and I do not have one; a boundary drawn exactly where the superrational payoff is computed looks gerrymandered to save the phenomenon. If I accept (4) and (5), I am left cooperating in one-shot prisoner's dilemmas with strangers, and I cannot see what restrains (6) from making my character a public good that I am obliged to manage for the sake of the evidence it emits. For what it is worth, I would most like to keep (3): the Platonia calculation strikes me as genuinely right, and the fact that twenty superrationalists come away roughly nineteen million dollars better off than twenty self-interested maximisers does not seem like an accident.

So tell me: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the error lies in the passage from (3) to (5) — from identical reasoners to partial resemblance — name the difference that does the work there and explain why it is not gerrymandered. If the argument is sound as far as it goes, so that the real choice is between its premises rather than within its inference, set out the price of each way of paying, including the price my friend has accepted. And if you think the flaw is subtler than either of those — some shift between what my reasoning settles and what my act reveals — locate it precisely; I have a vague suspicion that the randomisation in (3) matters here, since in Platonia the shared reasoning prescribes a lottery rather than an act, but I cannot say why that should help, and I would rather know whether the suspicion is worth anything than be spared it.
