I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and one recurring theme in our perennial crypto arguments is the assumption that money needs either intrinsic value or state backing to work. It struck me recently that George Berkeley — the immaterialist bishop who argued that physical objects can't exist unperceived — also wrote extensively on money. In The Querist, his series of short questions about curing Ireland's poverty, he suggested that money is fundamentally a ticket or counter for commanding human labor rather than wealth itself, that gold and silver have no privileged monetary status in principle, and that Ireland needed its own paper national bank to activate idle labor. And here's the interpretive hook I find compelling, which I'd like you to take seriously: given that Berkeley thought value is mind-dependent in general, his monetary theory arguably amounts to a proto-social-constructionist view, on which a currency's value consists in nothing more than the stable structure of collective opinion sustaining it. If that's right, Berkeley looks like a forerunner of Chartalism or of modern accounts of money as a social technology — but it could also be a misreading, and part of your job is to tell me where the analogy between monetary value and, say, secondary qualities genuinely holds and where it breaks down.

So my question is: what would Berkeley make of the monetary world that emerged roughly 280 years after his death?

Please act as an expert historian of early modern philosophy with a strong side expertise in monetary economics, and reason carefully rather than reaching for the first association. Before writing your final answer, do some quiet brainstorming about which of his commitments are load-bearing: the labor-command theory of money's function, the hostility to treating hoarded specie as wealth, the protectionist wall of brass around Irish industry, the willingness to separate currency from commodity entirely, and the metaphysical thesis about perception and value. Then predict, with explicit reasoning at each step, how he would likely come down on at least these modern developments:

1. Bitcoin and fixed-supply cryptocurrency — would he see digital scarcity as a vindication of his view that a currency's worth is constituted by collective opinion, or would the deliberate design of deflationary hoarding strike him as exactly the sterilization of circulating tickets that he diagnosed as Ireland's disease?
2. Algorithmic stablecoins — tokens that attempt to engineer the "collective perception of value" without any state or commodity anchor, and that periodically collapse in spectacular fashion. Does their fragility tell against a pure social-construction reading of Berkeley, or does it actually confirm his view that confidence must be institutionally engineered?
3. Modern monetary theory and the fiscal authority of states — would Berkeley endorse the idea that a sovereign that issues its own currency can't run out of it, or would he insist that Ireland's subordinated colonial position makes his case fundamentally different from a truly sovereign issuer like the United States?
4. Quantitative easing and negative nominal interest rates — policies that deliberately degrade the store-of-value function of money in order to force circulation. Would he see this as a straightforward application of his own principle that money matters only insofar as it moves and excites industry?
5. Central bank digital currencies — and, as a fun extrapolation, whether a land-backed national digital token for a small open economy like modern Ireland would be a natural update of his national bank scheme or a betrayal of its logic.

Where the evidence about Berkeley runs out, say so plainly and distinguish reasoned extrapolation from guesswork; I care more about the quality of the inference than about a confident verdict on each item. Please also address one meta-question: to what extent is it fair to reason from the general metaphysics (esse est percipi and the mind-dependence of value) to specific monetary opinions, versus treating his economics as largely detachable from his idealism? Assume no access to sources; reason from the views themselves. Think step by step, and please write your answer in American English.
