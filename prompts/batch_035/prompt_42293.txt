I'd like you to take on the role of a careful epistemologist, think through both sides before committing to a number, and then give me a genuine credence rather than a survey of positions. Here is the case, stated plainly so we are sure we are discussing the same thing. Over many weeks, on each occasion I look at a surface, it looks red to me, and I come to believe that the surface is red — just by looking, with no instrument cross-checking me. Each time, I also note that my vision represented the surface as red. Eventually I assemble these notes and reason: on occasion one my vision said red and it was red; on occasion two, likewise; and so on for many occasions; therefore my vision is highly reliable about red. Call the conclusion R: my color vision is reliable about red surfaces in ordinary conditions. The question is whether this procedure can give me justification for R that I did not already possess before running it. In the literature this goes under the bootstrapping or "easy knowledge" label; the same pattern would let me confirm the reliability of my car's fuel gauge by repeatedly reading the gauge and, with no external check, taking the tank to contain whatever the gauge says, one reading at a time.

My own credence that the pattern is epistemically permissible — that it can generate new justification for R — is about 0.35. A colleague who works on perceptual justification puts his at 0.8; he suspects the objection just presupposes an over-strong requirement on how justification for a reliability belief must be acquired, and that nobody who took that requirement seriously could ever be justified in trusting anything. Give me your own number, but what I mainly want is the decomposition into the sub-questions the credence rides on. At minimum I would expect something like: (a) the probability that the individual step is justified — that when a surface looks red in good viewing conditions, my belief that it is red can be justified by the look alone, without any antecedent independent reason to trust my vision (the premise friends of perceptual dogmatism accept); (b) the probability that, given individually justified steps, the aggregation into R preserves justification rather than exploiting it — the worry here being that my justification for each "it was red" already tacitly depends on something in the neighborhood of R, so the reasoning pays for itself with its own currency; (c) the probability that there is no independent epistemic rule specifically forbidding this route to beliefs about one's own reliability; and (d) whatever further terms you think belong in the decomposition — for instance, whether the alternative view, which demands an external calibration standard before R can be justified, is itself coherent or instead launches a regress that no finite agent could satisfy.

Please assign each term a rough probability with a single line of justification for it, show how they combine into your final credence, and be explicit about your confidence levels: say where you are guessing versus reasoning from something you actually hold fixed, and single out the one term you are least sure of, together with the argument or variant case that would move it most.
