I'm a novelist, three books into a career spent mostly in what we call, a bit loosely, the "omniscient narrator" — a voice that can enter any character's mind, report what no character witnesses, and glance ahead at the future. But I've come to realize that this omniscience is a highly disciplined affair. Narrators withhold, misdirect, feign ignorance, and reveal on a schedule; and when a writer lets the voice drift carelessly between minds, workshop criticism says she has failed at craft — not that she has exercised more omniscience. Meanwhile my characters suffer from a different ailment entirely: they hold beliefs whose implications they never draw, and the plot often turns on exactly that blindness — the reader sees the consequence pages before the character does, which we call dramatic irony. A philosopher friend recently told me that epistemic logic has what she called the problem of logical omniscience, and I suspect it lives somewhere in this terrain of mine.

As she explained it over a too-long dinner: the formal models of belief and knowledge treat an agent as closed under logical consequence — if the agent knows p, and knows that p entails q, the model automatically credits her with knowing q, and iterating the rule makes every agent know every theorem and every consequence of everything she believes. Human agents are nothing like this, which is why the idealization is regarded as a problem to be managed rather than a fact to be recorded. I'd like you to restate this problem in the vocabulary of my craft — narrator, point of view, free indirect discourse, what a character knows as against what the reader knows, the information economy of a scene — as faithfully as you can, resisting the temptation to make the translation sound tidier than it is. In particular, I want to know whether the discipline imposed on an omniscient narrator is anything like the failure of closure in human believers. My narrator is not credited with knowing what she never bothers to report; but is that a gap in her knowledge, or a gap in her telling, and do the logicians have any way of marking that difference?

Then give me what I can only call the residue report. Which parts of the philosophers' problem translate cleanly into talk about what a narrator may report, and when; which parts you had to distort to make them fit; and whether anything in the problem — for instance, that the idealized agents are built that way precisely so their knowledge can be represented as a set with a closure rule — has no counterpart at all in how fiction manages minds and information. Finally, a verdict on my working intuition: that the omniscient narrator solves what the epistemologists cannot, by installing a valve between what is known and what is told, a distinction their formalism lacks. Is that right, or am I flattering my own craft?
