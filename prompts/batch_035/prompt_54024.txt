My study group has been going at this for two weeks and we're stuck. Quick background for context: the centipede game is where two players take turns, and on each turn you can either take the pot on the table (you get the bigger share and the game ends) or pass (the pots grow and it's the other player's turn). It's finite and everything is out in the open. Backward induction says: at the last node the mover takes, so at the second-to-last node you take, and so on all the way up, so player 1 takes immediately and the game ends at the first node with tiny payoffs — even though both players would be much richer if they passed for a while first. We ran it in class with real money and literally nobody took at the first node. The prof shrugged and said that's the backward induction paradox, go figure it out yourselves.

One of us, Dan, thinks he's dissolved it, and I think his argument proves way too much, but I can't put my finger on the bad step. It goes: (1) Suppose rationality is common knowledge — everyone's rational, everyone knows it, everyone knows everyone knows it, etc. (2) At the last node the mover takes; passing there just gives money away. (3) If the mover at node k knows the mover at k+1 would take, then passing at k ends the game right after and leaves her worse off than taking, so she takes at k. (4) So under common knowledge of rationality the game ends at node 1 and no later node is ever reached. (5) But notice what the argument needed along the way: every conclusion about nodes 2 and up is conditional on those nodes being reached, and by (4) that can't happen. Anyone who actually arrives at node 2 has just watched player 1 pass, which (4) says a rational player never does — so if node 2 is reached, the common knowledge assumption has already been refuted on the spot. (6) So the premises about later nodes all attach conditions the argument itself proves impossible, and a claim whose antecedent is impossible is vacuous — it carries no information. The induction only has real content at node 1. (7) And the step that gets us to node 1 needs a claim about node 2 (player 1 knowing that player 2 would take there), which is vacuous by (6). So the whole chain collapses and backward induction never gets off the ground. (8) Conclusion: even with common knowledge of rationality, passing at the first node isn't established to be irrational, and since people plainly profit by passing, rational players pass and the "paradox" is just an artifact of a broken proof.

Dan is extremly pleased with himself. Here's what bugs me. The principle in step (6) — claims about impossible situations are vacuous — can't be right in general, can it? If it were, everyday planning would collapse: "if the flight gets cancelled I'll take the train" would become vacuous the moment I become confident the flight won't be cancelled, and I'd be left with no plan at all for the day things go wrong. And in other games the same move seems to strike down totally uncontroversial reasoning — ruling out an incredible threat in an ultimatum game is exactly a judgment about what the threatener would do at a node that (by the theory's own lights) won't be reached. So either Dan is right and something much bigger is broken in how we handle conditionals, or there's a slip somewhere between (5) and (7) that I keep circling aroun but can't name. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it's worth, I'd rather keep (1) than accept that common knowledge of rationality is incoherent, but I also can't swallow that the entire backward induction result is an illusion. Tell me what each way out has to pay.
