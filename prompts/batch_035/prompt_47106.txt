I am a novelist, five books into a career, at work on a novel told entirely by animals: consecutive chapters narrated by a working dog, a pig in a truck bound for slaughter, a crow, an octopus, a migratory shorebird, and — most difficult of all — a beehive, which I have been trying to render as a single dispersed voice. In the practical business of pitching each voice, I have noticed a pattern I cannot name, and I would like to know whether the philosophers have.

The nearer a creature's inner life is to being imaginable by me, the more readily my readers accept its chapter as an imaginative exercise — a rendering of something that is really there. The farther it is, the more the same act of narration starts to be read as either an admitted fabrication or a philosophical thesis. No one calls the dog chapter presumptuous; the octopus chapter is treated as though I have taken a stance. And when I defend the octopus, I catch myself arguing from my own imaginative failure, and in both directions at once: with the hive I say that I cannot imagine what it is like to be one, and then slide toward saying that perhaps there is nothing it is like; with the dog, my inability to imagine being a bat-like stranger does nothing at all to dent my certainty. The same slide seems to do enormous work in ordinary talk about animals: the worm, the fish, the shrimp are excluded from the circle of experience not by any evidence but by our inability to tell a story from inside them, while the dog is admitted purely on the strength of how much he resembles us.

So: is there an established concept for this family of moves — the inference from imaginative intractability to experiential absence, and the gradient of attribution that runs, not along any line drawn by evidence about the animals themselves, but along the line of what we can narrate? I know Thomas Nagel's essay on what it is like to be a bat stands somewhere in this neighborhood, and I know the charge of anthropomorphism is routinely traded back and forth, but neither seems to name the specific thing I keep running into, which is the use of my own imaginative limits as evidence about which minds exist. If there is an established term, I want to know exactly what it covers, how it differs from its nearest neighbors, and which of those neighbors best fits what I have described; if there is none, then coin one, define it, and mark out its edges — what counts as an instance, what does not, and where the hardest boundary cases fall. I am not collecting a label. I am four hundred pages from a decision about whether the hive speaks in the first person plural, and I need to understand precisely what I am doing when I give the pig a voice and deny one to the lobster.
