I write a newsletter about how different cultures handle other people getting ahead — we've done the American Dream, Australian tall poppy syndrome, and Scandinavian Jantelagen, and my readers keep using the three labels as if they're synonyms, which drives me up the wall. They're obviously not the same thing: cutting someone down because you enviuos of them, cutting them down because you think sticking your head out is an offense against the group, and only cutting down people whose success was ill-gotten — those are three different animals, and the quiz should be able to tell them apart.

So: please write me a "which reaction to other people's success do you actually have?" quiz for the newsletter.

Here's what I need:

- Around ten questions, each a concrete case with answer options, written for smart non-philosophers. Readers are mostly US/Australia/Nordics, in case that helps you pick cases that land.
- The diagnostic rule: no question may ask someone which label they identify with. They should find out what they are from cases, not from self-description. People who say they're against tall poppy syndrome are often the best poppy-cutters in the room.
- The quiz has to be able to separate at least these: (a) cutting people down out of envy — their success hurts because you wanted that thing yourself; (b) leveling as norm enforcement — the Jante thing, where anyone who sticks their head out is harming the group or asking for it, regardless of whether you personally wanted what they got; (c) indignation — you only mind success that seems undeserved or ill-gotten; (d) genuine celebration, including the American-Dream-flavored kind where someone else's success reads as evidence that yours is possible too.
- I also want you to work in two axes I care about. One is benign vs malicious envy — the "I'll work harder" reaction vs. the "I hope they fall" reaction. The other is what I've been calling the aspiration tax: whether other people succeeding makes your own life feel worse by comparison even when nothing about your life actually changed. Maybe design at least one case where the reader objectively benefits from being near a successful person (mentor, colleague whose wins reflect well on the team) — I'm curious whether that separates sincere warmth from strategic celebration.
- Case ideas to consider, use or replace as you see fit: coworker promoted over you, friend getting rich from something dumb like crypto, a rival failing publicly (schadenfreude check), a lottery winner, someone quietly succeeding vs. loudly succeeding, and someone being knocked down publicly — where you might enjoy it differently depending on how they got up there.
- Scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions, with names. Coin names where no standard one exists (you're allowed to invent something like "the conditional celebrant" — just be clear you're coining). Flag any combination of answers that leads nowhere: is nobody in that corner because the view is incoherent, or just rare?
- Be honest about where the quiz can't separate positions. My pet example: sincere egalitarianism vs. envy dressed up as egalitarianism. If the quiz can't split those with the case format, say so in the key instead of pretending it did.

You're a philosopher of emotions with a social-psychology streak — think hard about which cases actually do the splitting work before writing, brainstorm the case list first, and cut any question that doesn't pull its weight. I don't want filler that just re-asks the same thing in a different costume. Also please no citations or source lists, my readers never click them anyway. Take your time and get the scoring key airtight — if two different answer patterns give the same position, that's fine, but the same pattern should never give two.
