I'm a science journalist working on a long-form feature about the state of fundamental physics a decade on from the Higgs discovery — the LHC found no superpartners, no dark matter candidate has materialised, and several of the theorists I interview concede that the next genuinely new data relevant to their frameworks may be decades away, if it ever comes. Into that vacuum, the defenders of string theory and the multiverse keep reaching for the same analogy, in one form or another: positing the string landscape with its enormous number of possible vacua, or the bubble universes of eternal inflation, is no different in kind from what Le Verrier did in 1846, when he inferred the existence and position of an unseen planet from irregularities in the orbit of Uranus, sent his predicted coordinates to Berlin, and Galle found Neptune on the first night of looking. The moral being drawn is that physics has always been licensed to infer unobservable entities from their effects, or from the explanatory and unifying work a hypothesis does, and that a theory with no serious rivals is being confirmed even before — perhaps even in the absence of — direct observation. A sceptical cosmologist I spoke to counters with a rival picture: this is Ptolemy's epicycles, a flexible framework endlessly adjustable to fit the appearances, whose very adjustability should count against it rather than for it.

Please audit both analogies rather than adjudicating by slogan. Lay out what maps onto what in each — what plays the role of Uranus's orbit, of the predicted coordinates, of the telescope in Berlin, and, on the other side, of the circles upon circles — and then say where each mapping breaks, distinguishing the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion actually being drawn from the ones that are incidental. The obvious trouble for the Neptune side is that Neptune was eventually seen and the multiverse, by hypothesis, never can be; I want to know whether that kills the argument or only wounds it, given that its users typically lean on inference to the best explanation and on the lack of alternatives rather than on the promise of future observation. Then take a definite position: does the Neptune comparison, once repaired, carry the conclusion that the multiverse research programme is being confirmed rather than merely sustained by sociological momentum? Or does the epicycle analogy better capture the epistemic situation of post-empirical physics? And if both pictures fail, say what a better analogy would be and what it would predict about how this chapter of physics ends. Please give me a committed verdict rather than an even-handed survey of the options — I need to know where to hang the piece — and write your answer in American English.
