I've been trying to get clear on what economists actually mean when they say people "maximize utility," and I've managed to confuse myself into a corner. As I understand it, revealed preference theory was supposed to solve the problem that utility is unobservable: since we can't look inside anyone's head and measure satisfaction, we simply define a person's preferences by their choices — whatever you choose, ipso facto, is what you preferred — and utility is just the number we assign to those choices after the fact. If that's right, then "people maximize utility" can't be an empirical claim at all. It's true by definition every time a person makes any choice, and there's nothing anyone could ever observe that would count against it.

But I've also read that the Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference is a genuine restriction that data can violate — that preference reversals and similar experimental results count as evidence against utility maximization — and that there's a result (Afriat's, I believe) showing exactly when a finite set of observations can be rationalized by some utility function at all. So apparently utility maximization is falsifiable after all.

These two pictures can't both be right. Either utility maximization is just definitional bookkeeping over whatever a person happens to choose, in which case nothing could ever refute it; or it imposes real restrictions on patterns of choice, in which case it can't be true merely in virtue of the bare fact that choices occur. Which is it — and where exactly does my first picture go wrong?

There's a follow-on worry, too. If utility in the revealed-preference sense is nothing but a summary of choice behavior, I don't see how economists can use it to make welfare claims. When a policy is said to make people "better off," isn't that at best saying they get more of what they chose, given whatever constraints they faced — including constraints that might themselves be the problem (say, choosing between two terrible options)? If someone's choices are shaped by addiction, or by poverty, or by the absence of any alternative, calling the outcome their "preference" seems to launder the circumstances into the agent. Is there a principled way for the economist to keep the choice-based notion of utility for predicting behavior while still meaning something independent by "welfare," or does the whole apparatus quietly slide between the two notions? I'd appreciate a careful walkthrough, since I suspect I'm equivocating somewhere but can't locate it.
