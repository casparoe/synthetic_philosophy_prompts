Next week's guest is a philosopher who has spent the better part of a decade arguing that the Self-Indication Assumption — roughly, the principle that one's credence should favor hypotheses on which more observers exist, since a priori one is more likely to find oneself existing under them — dissolves the doomsday argument entirely rather than merely weakening it. Please draft my question list, organized as follows.

Cover at least the following pressure points: the Presumptuous Philosopher objection, and whether his version of the Self-Indication Assumption can be diluted in a principled way to avoid it or whether any dilution is ad hoc; the worry that the assumption, applied to a setting where competing hypotheses allow infinitely many observers, pushes one's credence toward certainty that the universe is infinite, and whether imposing a cap on the number of possible observers is a principled move or an epicycle; the anthropic shadow problem — whether boosting hypotheses with more observers makes us systematically underestimate the base rates of extinction-scale catastrophes, and whether he regards that as a bug to be patched or a cost worth paying for defeating doomsday; and whether the reference class problem really vanishes under his view or merely reappears as a question about which observers count as possible in the relevant sense.

End with a short section titled 'The last word' (one or two questions), including one that asks him what empirical discovery, observation, or thought experiment would most shake his confidence in the view.
