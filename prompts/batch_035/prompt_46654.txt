Some context first: I grew up evangelical, drifted away in my twenties, and now I'm 40 and seriously dating a practicing Christian, and we've started talking about how we'd raise kids. Which means I suddenly have to work out what I actually beleive about who Jesus was, instead of just dodging the question. She lent me a popular book about the historical Jesus that talks a lot about the Jesus Seminar, and now I'm kind of obsessed.

Quick recap of what it said, in case the details matter: starting in the mid-80s, a group of Bible scholars met regularly over about two decades and voted on the sayings of Jesus using colored beads — red for "he definately said this," pink for probably, gray and black for later invention. They ended up attributing only a small fraction of the sayings to Jesus himself. And their method was the so-called criteria of authenticity: a saying counts as more likely genuine if it's embarrassing to the early church, or attested in multiple independent sources, or dissimilar to what Christians would have made up — and in the strong version, also dissimilar to what other Jews of the time would have said.

Treat the Jesus Seminar as a test case for those criteria. Map it for me carefully: for each criterion, what kind of saying does it actually authenticate, and what does it structurally never deliver? I've read that the dissimilarity criterion, taken seriously, can only certify what's out of step with both the church and Judaism, which would mean it can only ever find a dissident and systematically filters out the Jewish Jesus — is that right, and does the Seminar's result (a Jesus of a handful of wisecracks) bear it out? Then tell me honestly what the criteria can't settle even in principle: his intentions, whether he expected the world to end soon, his deeds, basically anything about him as a person rather than his one-liners? And does some other approach do better — contextual ones that put him back into first-century Judaism, I guess — or does that just trade one filter for another?

One more thing that's nagging me. Voting in a big group, with beads, and announcing results to the public: did the format itself shape what the criteria could deliver? A criterion might be decent for ranking sayings by likelihood, but then it gets forced to produce a clean yes/no vote — does that distort the output, and if so how much of the Seminar's skinny Jesus is the criteria and how much is the voting?

Bottom line for me: can criteria of authenticity ever settle who Jesus actually was, or do they only ever tell you which fragments are hard to explain away, leaving the person himself out of reach? I need a real answer, not a pox on both houses.
