I'm a retired physical chemist who let herself get roped onto the ethics panel of my old professional society, and we're stuck revising our code of conduct, which is due to go to the membership vote in June. Two camps formed and I somehow have to draft the recomendation memo. One camp wants the new code to be a tight set of principles -- honesty, confidentiality, duty to public safety -- with concrete cases derived from them like theorems, basically how I'd have written a lab protocol. The other camp, led by a former safety inspector who took up philosophy late in life, says that's a fantasy: the same fact can count for you in one case and against you in another depending on everything else going on -- disclosing a colleague's sloppiness is owed when a bridge is at stake, spiteful when it isn't -- so the code should mostly be a growing body of adjudicated past cases plus a short preamble about judgement and integrity, with at most loose rules of thumb in place of principles. Someone then floated a compromise: a handful of rebuttable presumptions that hold by default but can be defeated when the context is bad enough, and now everyone's staring at me. Draft the memo I'll circulate before the next meeting: recommend a concrete structure for the revised code, treat the strongest point on whichever side loses seriously instead of a paragraph of throat-clearing, and be honest about whether the presumptions idea actually settles the disagreement or just papers over it. Write it as plain continuous prose, no headings, bullets, or tables -- our chair reads these out loud like letters and hates formatted text.
