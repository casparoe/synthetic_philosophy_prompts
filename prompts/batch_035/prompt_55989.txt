My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about surrogate goals — the device by which an agent makes itself less attractive to threaten by pre-committing to treat any threat to its actual interests as though it also damaged some further, "surrogate" interest, so that the expected cost to the threatener of issuing a threat outweighs the gain. He concedes the device works when the threatener can observe the commitment, but he thinks it collapses precisely in the setting where surrogate goals are supposed to earn their keep: acausal threats, where threatener and target have no channel between them, cannot verify each other's commitments, and can only reason about each other's decision procedures. His claim is that an unobservable commitment deters nothing, so in that setting a surrogate goal is pure expected loss: the agent pays the escalation premium and no threatener is ever influenced.

Construct a thought experiment that adjudicates this. Requirements:

- Two agents with no causal channel between them — no communication, no inspection of each other's code or commitments — one of whom is positioned to issue a threat that pays off only if the other's compliance is rationally forced.
- The threatener's model of its target may be no better than a shrewd human's: inference about what decision procedure the target plausibly runs, not simulation or prediction.
- The surrogate goal must be generated by the case rather than stipulated: show the reasoning by which the threatener concludes the target has adopted one, and the reasoning by which the target is better off having adopted it.
- Then exhibit the split: under an updateless or functional-decision framing the surrogate goal deters the acausal threat, while under causal decision theory the same commitment is exactly the self-inflicted expected cost my friend describes. Identify which structural feature of the case does the work, and say what happens to the split if that feature is removed.
- Close with the strongest version of my friend's reply — what he would say on reading the case — and whether it survives.

The scenario itself should be one paragraph; the rest is analysis. No demons, no perfect predictors, no payoffs an ordinary person could not face.
