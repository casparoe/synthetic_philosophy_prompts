I am a novelist, and I have written myself into a philosophical corner that I now need a proper essay to get out of. My current protagonist is a woman of almost pathological deliberateness — a former chess prodigy turned archivist — who at the novel's midpoint faces two futures that her own reflections render exactly indiscernible: the directorship of a small archive in one city and an identical post in another, each supported by precisely the same weight of reasons, the same foreseen regrets, the same faint pull of a person she has left behind in each place. My problem is not plot but credibility. My editor insists that no reader will believe in a choice made with no motive tilting the scale: either something in her secretly wants one option — and I should show it — or she is not a character at all, merely a thought experiment in a skirt. My sister, a psychologist, insists on the opposite: that no human being can hold perfect equilibrium in the first place, that attention oscillates, and that hunger, noise, and fatigue always break the symmetry before it can matter. Between them they have left me unsure whether my scene depicts an impossibility, a pathology, or an ordinary fact about choosing that fiction has simply failed to notice.

The scholastics' donkey — set down, as tradition has it, exactly between two identical haystacks and left to starve; an ass attached to Buridan's name though scholars doubt he ever wrote it, and a puzzle older than him — appears to be my protagonist's ancestor. Write an essay on whether that scenario is a genuine possibility or a reductio, and, if it is a reductio, on what exactly it refutes. I take it there are several distinct questions hiding inside the one donkey, and I would like them kept apart. First, a metaphysical one: whether perfect symmetry of reasons is even available to a situated creature, or whether the principle that nothing happens without a sufficient reason forbids the setup outright — in which case the story is a proof that my scene, strictly written, cannot occur. Second, a decision-theoretic one: whether a rational agent with exactly tied options is entitled to select arbitrarily, and whether randomization — my archivist reaching for a coin — is the rational thing to do under indifference, a confession that rationality has run out, or an act of a different kind altogether, one that belongs to choice rather than to preference. Third, a phenomenological one: whether there could be such a thing as a felt state of exact indifference, or whether felt indifference is always one of the options wearing a mask. Somewhere in the neighborhood, too, lies the old thought that the power to choose without a determining reason is the mark of freedom rather than a defect: I have seen it suggested that choosing under perfect indifference is, on some views, the purest exercise of the will, and on others its lowest grade, and I would like to know what can be said for each of those claims.

I do not want citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I want the arguments themselves, reconstructed and weighed. Reach a verdict, and make it answer my practical question as well as the theoretical one: can I write a motiveless choice that a reader will experience as true, or does the paradox, properly understood, show that my editor is right, that my sister is right, or that both are wrong in instructive ways? Begin the essay with its conclusion, stated plainly, in the first sentence — no preamble, no restating of my question, no scene-setting before the thesis. Everything after should be the reasoning that earns it.
