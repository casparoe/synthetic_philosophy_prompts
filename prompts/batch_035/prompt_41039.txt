I'm a backend engineer who spends way too much of my life on observability -- logs, metrics, tracing, alerting, the whole "you can't fix what you can't see" religion. A friend of mine is doing a philosophy masters and tried to explain Fitch's paradox of knowability to me over beers, and honestly it sounded weirdly familiar. As I understand it, the verificationist (anti-realist) idea is that every truth is knowable in principle -- no truth is forever beyond reach of any possible inquiry. And Fitch showed that this innocent-sounding claim, plus some pretty minimal logic, forces the absurd conclusion that every truth is already known. The key move is considering a truth of the form "p is true but nobody knows that p": if that were knowable, whoever knew it would know p while also knowing that nobody knows p, which bascially detonates on contact. So it's unknowable, so if all truths are knowable it can't be true, so there are no unknown truths. Which is, uh, a strong claim.

My instinct is to map "known" to something like "an alert fired or a human actually looked at the dashboard", and "knowable" to "the instrumentation exists, so it could in principle surface".
