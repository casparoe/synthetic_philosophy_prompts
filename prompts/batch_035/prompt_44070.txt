I write explainers on physics for a mid-sized news outlet, the "what is actually going on when..." kind of column. Last month I was at a panel where a cosmologist waved off the whole "why do we remember the past but not the future" question with one line: "time has a direction because entropy increases." The moderator nodded along, I wrote it down, and now that I'm actually drafting the piece I keep stalling, because I honestly can't tell which of several claims that sentence is making. It could be the near-trivial one: that every irreversible process we actually observe is entropy-increasing somewhere in the total system (ice melting, eggs scrambling, and never the reverse). That's true but doesn't obviously touch the deeper question. It could be the one about us: that creatures like us register "later than" through entropy gradients, which is supposed to explain why memory and anticipation feel so different. Or it could be the genuinely wild one: that the direction of time itself -- what makes an event earlier rather than later, full stop -- just is the direction in which total entropy increases. And here's were I get stuck: my coffee cools, so entropy in the coffee is going DOWN, and I don't experience time running backwards inside the mug, which makes me suspect the slogan quietly means "total entropy of the universe" all along, which is a much stronger and weirder claim. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses packed into that one sentence as a list I can work from, and for each say whether it's a platitude, contentious but defensible, or plain false? And then tell me which one the cosmologist was probably invoking to shut down the memory question, and which readings would let me write a single correct sentence in my article -- because right now the draft asserts something I can't back up.
