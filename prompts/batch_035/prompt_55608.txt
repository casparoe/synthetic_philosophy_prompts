I run the combined philosophy and computer science club at my high school, and I would like help designing an activity for an upcoming meeting. There are sixteen members, plus me as facilitator, and we meet for one hour in a classroom with movable desks and a whiteboard. The session I have in mind concerns a thought experiment from Douglas Hofstadter's book I Am a Strange Loop (2007), specifically the chapters on the careenium. In case the details are fuzzy, the setup is roughly this: Hofstadter imagines a billiard-chamber-like enclosure, the careenium, filled with tiny magnetized balls he calls simms, which careen around and, because of their mutual attraction, clump into larger, more persistent structures called simmballs. From up close, everything that happens is just one simm bumping into another; from a distance, the simmballs move, merge, and split in ways that seem to mirror the world outside, and the walls of the careenium feed information about that world in by changing how bouncy they are. Hofstadter's question, from the chapter titled Who Shoves Whom Around Inside the Careenium, is whether the simms push the simmballs around or the simmballs push the simms around — with the careenium standing in for the cranium, the simms for neurons, and the simmballs for symbols or concepts.

What I want is a game the members actually play, not a lecture followed by a discussion. They should run into the shoves question themselves: first they should experience only the simm level, then something should happen that makes the simmball level feel real and causally powerful, and only at the end should I name Hofstadter's question. My rough sketch — which you should feel free to improve or replace entirely — is that each student is a simm obeying purely local rules, allowed to perceive and respond only to immediate neighbors and to signals from the walls of the room, while the group's collective local behavior gradually makes some higher-level pattern visible on the whiteboard, a pattern that then acquires its own name and vocabulary. The twist comes when I begin issuing instructions only in that pattern-level vocabulary, never in simm-level vocabulary, and the group has to work out whether the pattern level can move the simms around at all, or whether every pattern-level instruction must be smuggled down into local pushes before anything happens.

Please provide: (1) concrete rules for sixteen students, including exactly what a simm may perceive and do, using simple materials I either already have or can buy at a stationery store (index cards, colored tokens, painter's tape, a timer); (2) a mechanism by which the simmball level gets built up so that it feels discovered rather than assigned; (3) a round-by-round sequence with rough timings for the full hour; (4) a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto Hofstadter's distinction between the two levels of description, including how to handle the awkward fact that in the game, unlike in a brain, there is a facilitator standing outside the system who translates between the levels; (5) an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle each failure; and (6) a frank note on which feature of the careenium idea no live classroom game can reproduce — for instance, that in the real thought experiment nobody designed the simm rules or stands outside issuing simmball instructions, so the symbol level's apparent causal power in my game may be an artifact of me rather than a genuine case of symbols pushing the substrate around. I would also appreciate one or two variations in case fewer members show up or we finish early. Please answer in American English.
