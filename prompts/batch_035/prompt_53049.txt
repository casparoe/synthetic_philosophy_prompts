I work on machine learning, and over the past year I helped fine-tune a modest-sized language model that, to our surprise, began handling multi-digit addition correctly on numbers far larger than anything in its training data. My team lead announced that the model had "grasped the rule," and I let that slide for a while, but then I reread Kripke's Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language over the holidays, and the skeptical problem about rule-following stopped being a seminar curiosity. I now find I cannot say what fact, if any, makes it true that the model (or my team lead, or I) is following plus rather than some quus-like function that agrees with everything we have checked and diverges somewhere we have not — and I would like to get better at answering this before I have to defend a model card claiming our system learned arithmetic. So let me practice. Please play the skeptical challenger as Kripke presents him: relentless, precise, never a strawman, always ready with a new interpretation of whatever I offer as evidence. You hold that no fact about past use, present inner states, dispositions, or training history constitutes meaning one function rather than another; you may concede that my responses feel natural or even inevitable, but never that they are justified as following the rule I claim to have meant, and never break character until I say stop. Keep each reply to a single paragraph of continuous prose — no headings, no lists, no bullet points — the way someone would write in a letter. Here is my opening move: I will defend the dispositional answer. The fact that I meant plus rather than quus is the fact that, were I asked about any pair of numbers, I would respond with their sum; that disposition was in me all along, prior to the present question, and it explains — genuinely explains — why I go on as I do. And the model is no worse off: its weights are a standing state of the very same kind, fixed by training, from which its correct answers flow. So there is your fact. Now tell me why it is not.
