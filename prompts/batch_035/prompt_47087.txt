In a seminar on political rhetoric, a classmate shut down my skepticism about the dangers of bureaucratic jargon by invoking Orwell's claim, from "Politics and the English Language," that the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts. She was arguing that euphemism-heavy institutional prose doesn't just obscure wrongdoing from outsiders but progressively disables the writers' own capacity to think clearly about what they are doing — a claim that seems to slide between something trivially true and something like a strong version of linguistic determinism. Acting as a philosopher of language with expertise in Orwell, please think carefully before answering, and begin with a brief brainstorm of the possible readings. Then lay out, as a numbered menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise theses that the claim could be expressing: for instance, readings on which it concerns the writer's private cognition, the audience's uptake, the availability of concepts for thought, or merely the social costs of ambiguity. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false; and, crucially, say which of the readings would actually support my classmate's use of the claim — the one on which bad prose corrupts the thinker, not just the communication. Please also note how Orwell's own accompanying analogy, in which an effect becomes a cause reinforcing the original cause, bears on which reading he most plausibly intended, and whether that reading survives modern skepticism about language determining thought.
