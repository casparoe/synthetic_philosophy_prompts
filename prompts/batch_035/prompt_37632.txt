Some months ago I joined the investment committee of the small endowment that funds the nonprofit I work for. At our most recent meeting we deadlocked, five votes to four, on whether to move a substantial portion of the portfolio out of emerging markets, and the disagreement survived a genuinely thorough discussion: everyone laid out their evidence, their models, and their confidence levels, and no one was accused of hiding anything. Afterward, a colleague who studied economics circulated a note invoking Aumann's agreement theorem. As I understand his summary, the theorem shows that two honest agents who start from the same priors and whose posteriors are common knowledge between them cannot rationally maintain different credences in the same proposition. My colleague draws two practical conclusions from this. First, since we shared all of our evidence in a long open session, our credences should now count as common knowledge, so our continued five-to-four split is, by the theorem, straightforward irrationality. Second, he proposes a standing rule to cure it: whenever we remain deadlocked after one full exchange of views, we should defer to the chair's stated credence, on the theory that the chair's post-discussion position is the one a properly functioning honest Bayesian would converge to, and that deferring to it restores us to compliance with the theorem. A few of us find this rule attractive precisely because it would end our meetings on time; others suspect it is a mathematical veneer over deference to authority. Before we vote on adopting it, I want to understand the situation properly. Is deferring to the chair's credence the rational way for our committee to dissolve disagreements, given the theorem? Relatedly, if some of us do persist in our positions after our deliberations, does the theorem entail that the dissenters are being dishonest or incompetent — and if it doesn't, what exactly does it entail about committees like ours?
