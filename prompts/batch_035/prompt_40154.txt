A friend of mine claims to have proved Zorn's lemma in ZF alone, with no appeal to Choice anywhere. His argument:

(1) Let (P, ≤) be a nonempty poset in which every chain has an upper bound. Suppose, for contradiction, that P has no maximal element.
(2) Then for every x in P there is a y in P with x < y.
(3) Climb. Fix any x_0 in P. Given x_α, use (2) to pick x_{α+1} strictly above x_α. Given the whole chain {x_β : β < λ} at a limit ordinal λ, the hypothesis of the lemma hands us an upper bound of that chain, and since nothing is maximal we can pick a strict one; call it x_λ. (He adds that in the familiar applications — independent sets ordered by inclusion, ideals, filters — you don't even pick at limit stages: the union of the chain is canonical and lies in P, so nothing is being smuggled in there.)
(4) By construction, β < γ implies x_β < x_γ, so the map α ↦ x_α is an injection from the class of all ordinals into the set P.
(5) But no set admits an injection of all the ordinals, and a strictly climbing injection would give exactly that. Contradiction.
(6) So P has a maximal element, and since every move in the climb was either forced or a single selection from a nonempty set we were already promised, no appeal to Choice was made. Zorn's lemma is a theorem of ZF.
(7) As a corollary, every set can therefore be well-ordered in ZF — the reals included — so the well-ordering theorem never needed Choice either.

When I pressed him on step (3), he said that choosing one element from one nonempty set is just existential instantiation, that logicians do it in first-order proofs all the time, and that he is doing it one set at a time, never all at once.

Here is my problem. My instinct is that the trouble is at the limit stage in (3), but when I press on it I find that the hypothesis really does guarantee an upper bound there, so I cannot articulate what is missing; and the union remark he appends makes the standard applications feel more solid to me, not less.
