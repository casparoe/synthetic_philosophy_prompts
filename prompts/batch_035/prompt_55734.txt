I'm a novelist, and I'm deep in a book whose POV character is a retired wine taster who's gone off the wines she used to love — and genuinely cannot tell whether they taste different to her now or whether they taste the same and she just likes them less. The harder she probes it, the less she can say. My editor, who knows I'm stuck on this, lent me a pop-philosophy book about conciousness that has a whole chapter on Dennett's quining qualia argument, built around two coffee tasters he invented, Chase and Sanborn, who work as tasters for Maxwell House.

As the book tells it, Chase has simply gone off the coffee — he no longer likes the taste of the blend he used to love — and there is no way for him, from the inside, to tell whether the coffee now tastes different to him or whether it tastes the same and only his liking has shifted. Sanborn, meanwhile, has been getting better at discriminating between coffees, and can't tell whether the tastes he experiences have actually changed or just his ability to detect them. The book says the dilemma is undecidable full stop — not just for introspection but in principle, no method could sort out which happened to poor Chase. Dennett's point, per the book, is that if we really had direct, infallible access to our own experiences — which is what qualia are supposed to be, being intrinsic, ineffable, private and immediately known — Chase would settle this instantly. He can't, so qualia turn out to be a confused posit of philosophers with four defining features that don't fit anything real, and Dennett concludes that experiences of taste just are dispositions to react, and that subjective experience generally is a kind of illusion.

Something about the last stretch bugged me — it seemed like a lot to squeeze out of two imaginary coffee tasters — but I don't know enough to say where it goes wrong, if it does. Can you audit this for me? What would a Dennettian accept, what's a harmless simplification, what's subtly off, and does the illusion conclusion actually follow from the accurate parts? Also, if you can: is my character's predicament (can't tell whether her perception or her preference has changed) genuinely the same dilemna Chase faces, or have I botched it in a way that matters for the novel? Please answer as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, with no introduction or conclusion — one per claim if that works, otherwise split it however makes sense. And British English please, my publisher's in London and I'm trying to get into the habit before I turn the manuscript in.
