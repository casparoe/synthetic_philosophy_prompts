I write novels for a living — domestic realism, mostly, so what my characters keep to themselves is basically my raw material. At a residency last month a memoirist gave a talk about interiority in fiction, and at some point she said something like this, which I scribbled down because it seemed punchy: privacy is just secrecy with a nicer name; nobody needs to keep something private unless they'd be embarrassed or punished if it came out, which is the "nothing to hide" argument stated honestly; so in fiction a character's privacy is never really theirs — it's always about an audience they fear. And historically, she said, the whole idea is pretty new: in a village nobody had privacy, everyone lived in full view of everyone else, and nobody suffered for it, so when novelists write "private" thoughts we're writing a modern anxiety backwards onto people who would've just said what they thought.

She's a sharp writer and the room definately ate it up, but something about it nags at me. It seemed to shrink privacy down to only the shameful or risky stuff, and I'm not sure that's all there is — my mother-in-law closes the bathroom door even when she's home alone and no one is going to walk in, and I don't think she's afraid of anything. So I want an honest audit before I let this reshape how I write: which parts of what she said would a philosopher who works on privacy actually accept, where are the subtle misrepresentations (I suspect there's a conflation in there between privacy and secrecy, but I can't quite put my finger on it), is the historical bit about villages right or an overstatement, and does her conclusion — that a character's private life is really just fear of an audience — follow from whatever part of her setup survives? I'd also take any thoughts on what this means for writing characters, but mainly I want to know what to trust in her account.
