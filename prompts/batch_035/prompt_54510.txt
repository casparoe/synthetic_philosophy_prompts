I'm a second-year graduate student taking a seminar on philosophy of language, and my study group has spent three meetings now going in circles on vagueness without converging on anything. I've decided the honest way forward is to stop defending positions and instead write down my actual verdicts on a bunch of cases, so that we can see whether any single theory of vagueness captures them or whether I'm holding an unstable mix. Here is what I find myself saying. On the sorites itself: I accept that 10,000 grains stacked in the usual way is a heap, and I accept that removing a single grain from a heap leaves a heap — each such claim, considered on its own, seems completely fine to assert. Yet I flatly deny that a single grain is a heap. So I take it that at least one of those individually fine-seeming claims is false; I simply cannot, even in principle, identify which one, and I'm suspicious of anyone who claims they can. On borderline cases: my friend Bob sits right on the border of tall, and I'm inclined to say that "Bob is tall" is neither true nor false. But I'm just as strongly inclined to say that "Either Bob is tall or Bob is not tall" is true — that's just logic. Oddly, "Either Bob is tall or Bob is short" strikes me as false, since he's neither. On context: at a basketball tryout I'd hesitate to call a six-foot guard tall, while at an ordinary party I'd say it without blinking; yet for "heap" context seems to do almost nothing — two hundred grains in a neat cone is a heap wherever you ask about it. On higher-order matters: "There is a sharp cutoff between tall and not tall" is obviously false, but "There is no sharp cutoff" also somehow sounds wrong to me, too strong, though I can't pin down why; and I have no idea what to say about whether "Bob is borderline tall" is itself borderline when Bob is borderline-borderline.

One more set of verdicts, from a case my TA raised. Suppose a lease says the landlord may raise rent when there has been "significant deterioration" of the property. I think that clause determinately applies to some cases and determinately fails to apply to others, and that for the middle cases there is simply no fact of the matter about what the contract requires — not a hidden fact we can't know, but no fact at all. Yet when I imagine an arbitrator actually deciding one of the middle cases, it feels to me like she is discovering what the contract requires rather than making it up; and my TA, who I believe is an epistemicist, says her view explains exactly that feeling. I can't tell whether my legal-case intuitions are consistent with my "no fact of the matter" verdict or quietly betray it.

So here is my request. Is there one account of vagueness — supervaluationism, epistemicism, contextualism, some hybrid, or something I haven't named — that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts: the sorites denial with no identifiable false step, the "neither true nor false" verdict on borderline sentences alongside my refusal to give up excluded middle for those very sentences, the strong context-sensitivity of "tall" alongside the near context-insensitivity of "heap," the higher-order discomfort with both "there is a sharp cutoff" and its negation, and my two-faced reaction to the arbitration case? If no single account can, tell me which of my verdicts should go, and why those rather than the others. Then tell me what the best overall package predicts about a case I haven't mentioned: consider the sentence "The number of grains in the smallest heap is either even or odd." Is that true, false, or neither? And does the answer it gives match what I would say if I heard it cold, which I confess I don't myself know?
