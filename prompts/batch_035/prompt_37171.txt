I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the mid-2010s, and one thing I've noticed is that two very different appeals to Gödel circulate there with almost no cross-contamination. On one side there's the old Lucas–Penrose family of arguments — that human mathematical understanding can't be mechanized because we can "see" the truth of the Gödel sentence of any given system that we happen to be — which the community treats as a canonical example of illegitimately exporting incompleteness out of mathematics. On the other side there's agent-foundations work in the MIRI-adjacent tradition where Gödelian self-reference appears to do real work: the Löbian obstacle to an agent trusting its successor, or to cooperation between agents that verify each other's source code. Both are "Gödel outside mathematics," yet one is treated as crackpot-adjacent and the other as a genuine technical constraint, and I've never seen the boundary between them articulated carefully.

I'd like a careful explanation of the underlying issue. Specifically: what exactly do the incompleteness theorems license one to conclude when carried over from formal systems to talk about minds, proofs, or agents? Where, precisely, does the Lucas–Penrose reasoning go wrong — which premise is doing the illegitimate work? My half-formed understanding is that the failure is something like quantifier-shift: from "for each system S, I can see that if S is consistent then its Gödel sentence is true," the argument slides to "I can see that this particular machine, which is exactly me, is consistent," and the first doesn't entitle the second. Please make that diagnosis precise, or correct it if it's not quite the right diagnosis.

Conversely, I want to understand why whatever kills the Lucas–Penrose argument doesn't also kill the agent-foundations results. What is it about the Löbian constructions that keeps them inside the range where self-reference is legitimately exploitable rather than rhetorical? Relatedly, please walk through why Löb's theorem holds at a gut level. I can follow the derivability-condition bookkeeping to the conclusion that PA proving "if PA proves P then P" commits PA to P, but what I want is intuition for why this is a genuine structural feature of arithmetized provability rather than a paradox or a trick — why the theorem doesn't collapse into absurdity the way naive self-reference arguments do. (I'm aware of the chocolate-vending-machine parable that circulates as a way of conveying the flavor; I'm after the version of that intuition that could actually survive contact with the proof.)

Finally, I'd like you to use all of this to draw the line explicitly: a criterion, or at least a reliable rule of thumb, for when a Gödelian argument has stepped outside its domain and become "Gödel shows minds transcend mechanism" hand-waving, versus when it is doing honest work. If you think the standard rationalist dismissal of Lucas–Penrose is itself too quick in places, say so and explain why.
