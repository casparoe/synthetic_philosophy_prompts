I should say up front how this became personal, because it shapes what I want comments on. Six months ago a stroke left me with homonymous hemianopia: the right half of my visual field, in both eyes, simply isn't there — not dark, just absent, the way the region behind your head is absent. I read philosophy as an undergraduate and have kept it up since, and during convalescence I've been drafting an essay, for the long-form section of a general-interest philosophy magazine, on Berkeley. The essay's thesis is that the most famous objection to his most famous argument cleans up the argument a little too quickly, and that what the objection sweeps out the door comes back in through a window I can now describe from experience.

Below is the draft, lightly condensed.

The argument in question is the one the literature calls the master argument: Berkeley invites us to conceive, say, a tree existing with no perceiver of any kind; but the tree we manage to think of is, precisely, one we are thinking of — perceived in imagination, if not by the eyes. The standard reply is that this trades on conflating the act of thinking with the thing thought: that I think of the tree by thinking of it does not make the tree itself a thought-of thing. I accept that reply, and I do not defend the argument as a proof. But I want to claim it fails revealingly.

Here is the residue. Take any imagining that actually succeeds — a tree on a bare hill — and inspect it. The imagined scene has a point of view built in: you are somewhere relative to the tree, at a distance, under a light, at an angle. The scene is given from somewhere; it is never given from nowhere. And when you try to strip the viewpoint out — to imagine the tree, as it were, from no place — one of two things happens. Either the imagining fails and you are left holding something schematic, a diagram or profile, which on Berkeley's own terms (his rejection of abstract ideas, his insistence that we entertain only particulars) is not an idea of a tree at all; or the viewpoint quietly smuggles itself back in as an unremarked horizon. So the thought "tree existing unperceived" is always executed as "tree as if seen," and the as-if is doing all the work. Read this way, the master argument is not the sophism the textbooks enjoy refuting. It is an observation about the obligatory perspectival character of imagery, wearing a proof as a disguise.

Berkeley has the resources for this reading, or so I claim. He holds that we do not see distance, that the visible world is a two-dimensional field of light and color which functions as a language whose signs stand for the tangible world. What my field loss has taught me is how much of that is report rather than paradox. I would once have said, unthinkingly, that I see objects arrayed in a container. What I notice now, whenever the missing half announces itself — reaching for a cup that isn't where the remaining field told me it should be — is that I have a field, bounded by an edge that is not the edge of any thing, and that objects are posits that stitch the field together. The visible world looks less like a window onto a room and more like a surface that ends. On this reading, the master argument's real content is that experience is constitutively from somewhere; the view from nowhere is not an experience we have had and generalized, but an abstraction projected back onto experience.

I close the draft by conceding what I think must be conceded, and I cannot tell whether the concession guts the essay or completes it. From "imagining is perspectival" it does not follow that trees cannot exist unperceived; at most it follows that we cannot imagine them unperceived without smuggling in a viewpoint. A critic will say: so the master argument, even on my charitable reading, establishes a fact about imaginers, not about trees. My instinct is to accept this and narrow the claim: unperceived existence is not something we can so much as represent non-parasitically, and the interesting question is what such existence can be for us. But I genuinely cannot tell whether that is a deep narrowing or a dressed-up capitulation.

The comments I'd like are of three kinds, and I'd ask you to be blunt throughout, since the magazine's readers will not know me and owe me nothing.

First, accuracy. Does the perspectival reading attribute to Berkeley something he has the resources for — his rejection of abstract ideas, his claim that distance is not seen, the sign language of the visible — or am I retrofitting a modern point about the indexicality of experience onto a man who would have resisted it? Where does the reading distort him most? I am aware his own conclusion is much stronger than anything my residue supports, and I would rather know now than in print.

Second, the personal material. Does the hemianopia earn its place in the argument, or is it ornament — a sick person's anecdote stapled to a philosophy paper? If it is ornament, what would make it earn its place? The draft currently uses it twice, once in the middle and once at the close, and I suspect one of those uses is a mistake, though not which.

Third, the closing move. Is the narrowing in the final paragraph a deepening of the essay or a surrender? If you think it is a surrender, I would like to hear what the honest version of the essay says instead — even if that version is shorter and less impressive than the one I wanted to write.

And finally, if you had to name the single weakest section of the draft and say why, I would take that as a favor.
