I cover science and ideas for a national daily, and in that job I keep running into a situation I don't have the right vocabulary for. It goes like this: some fact is privately known to practically everyone involved — every source I call already knows the CEO is on his way out, every economist I interview already believes the downturn has arrived — and yet nothing happens. People keep behaving as though the fact were unknown. Then it gets said out loud, on the record, in front of everybody — a memo, a televised statement, a front page — and behavior changes within days, sometimes hours. The striking thing is that the announcement adds essentially no information in the ordinary sense: nobody learns the fact itself; they already believed it. What seems to change is something about who knows that whom knows. Once it's been said publicly, nobody has to worry about being the only one reacting to it, so reacting becomes safe, and a quiet consensus turns into coordinated action overnight.

Is there an established term for this configuration in game theory or philosophy — the situation where individually held beliefs only become behaviorally consequential once they are made public in a way that makes the belief itself shared ground? I've heard people use "open secret" for the before-state, but that's colloquial and doesn't name the transition. I've also come across "pluralistic ignorance," but I suspect that's different: that seems to involve people misperceiving what others believe, whereas in my cases everyone's beliefs are accurate and mutually consistent — they're just not, or not yet, publicly established. Am I right that those come apart, and if so, what exactly is the difference?

I have a second, sharper question about the edges of whatever this concept is. In practice, "public announcement" is often not truly simultaneous: I read the front page, you hear about it from a colleague, a third person sees a forwarded screenshot. Each of us ends up pretty confident that the others know, and that the others know that we know, and so on for a few levels — but not, it seems, all the way down. Does the concept I'm after require the full infinite tower of "everyone knows that everyone knows," or is some finite depth of mutual knowledge enough to trigger the behavioral flip? If finite depth can be enough, is there any principled reason why, and if it can't, does that mean there is a real and consequential gap between hearing something publicly and merely hearing it from a chain of intermediaries — a gap a careful analyst of, say, market panics or sudden political resignations should be tracking? I'd appreciate not just the label, if one exists, but a clear account of what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its boundary with mere shared awareness lies.
