I've gotten pretty deep into AI-risk arguments lately, and the one I keep snagging on is the strategy-stealing assumption. Quick recap for context (as it was explained to me): in game theory there's an old trick showing the first player can't lose Hex — if the second player had a winning strategy, the first could make an arbitrary opening move and then just play the second player's strategy, since an extra stone can't hurt you. The AI-safety version, the way I've seen it stated, goes something like: whatever strategy lets one actor gain an advantage from AI, other actors can adopt more or less the same strategy — copy the techniques, redeploy the models, imitate the plan — because what makes AI powerful is information, and information spreads. People lean on this to argue we shouldn't expect any single actor to get a decisive, lasting edge even as systems get very capable.

Now my housemate, who did maths and enjoys being annoying, says the assumption proves too much, and he's got an argument I can't quite beat. (1) Any advantage anyone gets from any technology comes via a strategy — some plan for arranging things. (2) Strategies are information, and information can in principle be copied or reinvented by anyone with the underlying resources. (3) AI is nothing special here — weights leak, methods get published, techniques get re-derived; there's no magic ingredient that makes AI strategies stealable while, say, early nukes or a search-engine moat aren't. (4) So if strategy-stealing holds for AI, it holds for everything, and no actor can gain a durable advantage from any technology whatsoever. (5) But durable advantages are ten a penny — the US held a nuclear monopoly for four years, plenty of companies have sat on dominant positions for decades off network effects and data moats. (6) So the assumption, or at least its usual justification, is dead. When I pushed him he shrugged and said either it's false, or it's been quietly restricted to some short time window, in which case it's just an empirical bet dressed up as a principle — and he says he can't tell which, which is exactly why it's a bad argument to lean on.

For what it's worth, I'd like something strategy-stealing-shaped to survive, becuase the "one actor gets a permanent decisive edge from AI" scenario genuinely scares me, and this is one of the main things people cite against it. So: which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I keep staring at the move from "the extra stone can't hurt you in Hex" to whatever the real-world analogue is supposed to be — it feels like the whole argument lives or dies in that gap, but maybe I'm wrong about that. And if my housemate is right that it's secretly an empirical bet, I'd want to know what the bet actually is, and what would show it's a bad one.
