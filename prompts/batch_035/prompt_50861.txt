I'm taking an anthropology course this semester on the origins of inequality, and last week we hit the question of whether the transition to agriculture actually made human lives worse. My two friends from that class, Dana and Marcus, have been going at it in our group chat for days and eventually asked me to referee. I tried and honestly couldn't decide who was winning, so I'm outsourcing it to you.

Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Dana opened with the classic case: hunter-gatherers worked fewer hours, ate more varied diets, and the skeletal evidence shows early farmers ended up shorter, with worse teeth and more degenerative damage from repetitive labor, plus you get crowding diseases and the first real hierarchy once there's surplus to hoard. She'd read Sahlins's original affluent society essay at some point and quoted Jared Diamond's line about farming being the worst mistake in the history of the human race. (2) Marcus said the whole picture is romanticized: the forager groups we actually studied ethnographically were observed in marginal habitats like deserts and the Arctic, after farmers had pushed them off the good land, so the baseline is skewed downward; and anyway framing it as a mistake implies somebody chose it, when it looks more like something that happened to people. (3) Dana answered that the trap framing doesn't rescue him: if anything it makes it worse, since stepping into a trap you can't step out of is still a mistake from the perspective of the person caught in it — and the marginal-habitat point cuts against him, because if foragers were doing okay even on bad land, they'd presumably have done even better on the good land farmers took. (4) Marcus came back that Sahlins's affluence is rigged: he defines it partly by people having few wants, so any society can be declared affluent by saying its members want little, which makes the thesis hard to test; and he said the "trap" language just concedes his point that nobody erred, it's path dependence, which is a description, not a verdict. (5) Dana's last word was that whatever we call it, the question that matters is whether the average person's life got worse, and the skeletons don't lie; Marcus closed by saying skeletons tell you about everyday wear and tear but not about famine frequency or how anybody actually felt about their lives, and we can't run the counterfactual where foraging scales to billions of people.

So, please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the five moves, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, and why — and then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing (I suspect there's something about comparing the average farmer to the average forager that neither of them articulated, but I'm not sure). You're basically an expert on hunter-gatherer ethnography and the archaeology of the agricultural transition, so think carefully and maybe sketch out the moving parts before you give the verdict. I don't care who wins overall — I want to know where each argument actually lands and where it goes limp. Also feel free to tell me if the question itself is confused, since I have to write a short essay on this for the course and I'd rather not build it on a broken framing. No need for a reading list, just the analysis.
