I'm a science and ideas reporter working on a column about how Godel's incompleteness theorems keep getting smuggled out of mathematics and into everything else. Over the years I've collected six claims that have actually shown up in books, talks, or op-eds I've covered:

1. Godel proved there are eternal truths the human mind can never in principle know.
2. No system can fully account for itself, so an AI can never understand its own workings, which means human consciousness is safe from machines forever.
3. Since no system can be both complete and consistent, legal systems are mathematically guaranteed to have loopholes, so constitutional lawyers should stop searching for the "correct" interpretation.
4. Godel actually found a flaw in the US Constitution that could legally allow a dictatorship — he tried to bring it up at his citizenship hearing and Einstein had to shut him up.
5. Because arithmetic can't prove its own consistency, all of math is on borrowed time; one day a contradiction could surface and the whole edifice collapses.
6. The theorems show every field, from physics to ethics, is doomed to incompleteness, so we should embrace pluralism and give up on final unified theories.

For the column, please rate each claim on a 1-5 scale for how misleading it is for a general reader, with one sentence per claim on where it goes off the rails (or whether it's roughly ok if carefully hedged). Also tell me which single one is the most tempting to keep because it sounds the deepest, and what the honest version of that point would actually be. My editor wants the piece to be punchy but I don't want to be the next guy misusing Godel.
