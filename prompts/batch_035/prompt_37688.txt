I run a little philosphy night at my local pub — mostly regulars, a couple of pints in, nobody has a philosophy degree, me included. Next month it's my turn and I've decided to do "is pain actually bad?" I got onto this because of something my cousin (she's a nurse) told me: there's this weird condition, I think it's called pain asymbolia, where patients say they can feel pain just fine — they can tell you it's sharp, where it is, how strong it is — but they simply don't seem to mind it. They'll describe the pain calmly, even smile while doing it, and they don't flinch away from the thing that's hurting them. That's been rattling around my head for months. If someone can feel pain and not care, then maybe what's bad about pain isn't the feeling itself but the caring about it — or maybe the not-caring is itself the malfunction, like someone calmly watching their own house burn down. I genuinely can't decide which way that cuts, and that's what I want the night to be about.

What I need from you is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself, since I'm the one who has to deliver it in about half an hour with a whiteboard and a room that gets rowdier as it goes. What has to come first — I'm guessing the distinction between "it hurts" and "I mind it" has to be in place before anything else makes sense, but you tell me. What single example should carry the whole evening — the asymbolia patients themselves, a jab at the dentist, stubbing your toe and laughing because you're in a good mood, something else entirely — and if it is the patients, how do I stop the drunkest guy in the room turning it into "so pain isn't real, then?". Which wrong turns should I expect the room to take — I'm already dreading "that's just being tough" and "so suffering doesn't matter and we can ignore people in agony" — and at exactly what point in the sequence I should head each one off. Also where I should draw the line and openly say "not tonight, that's a different pub" rather than hand-waving, and one question I can throw out at the end that would show me whether they actually got the distinction instead of just nodding along. Please write it all in British English — half the regulars are English teachers and I'll never hear the end of it otherwise.
