I am a novelist, and my editor and my agent have deadlocked over the moral logic of my ending; since the disagreement now threatens the rewrite, I want an outside referee. In the final chapters, the protagonist's mother, dying and in pain, refuses further antibiotics. The protagonist, her sole caregiver, does not argue. When the hospice physician asks whether her mother has been running fevers, she answers that her mother has been comfortable — true of the last day, but a concealment of the fevers of the week before. The mother dies two days later. My editor contends that the protagonist has killed her: once the intention is fixed on the death, he says, the doing/allowing distinction collapses, and the situation is on all fours with the classic pair of cases in which one heir drowns his cousin for the inheritance while another, wanting the same inheritance, merely watches him drown. My agent answers that the cases are not alike: the disease is the killer, the mother herself refused treatment, and the daughter merely declined to become an additional agent of her prolongation — the same consideration, she says, that explains why we judge someone who kills one person in order to save five more harshly than someone who declines to save five at the cost of one. The editor replies that the protagonist did not merely decline to intervene: she answered a direct question with words engineered to leave a false impression, which is an act aimed at the death and severs the case from the pure letting-die line. The agent counters that the deception changed what the doctor knew, not what killed the mother — a warned doctor could only have offered treatment the mother was entitled to refuse and would have refused. The editor's last word is that this is too fast: omissions by someone with a special duty, like a lifeguard's, are not mere bystander failures, and the protagonist was her mother's caregiver, which makes the omission close to commission. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: at each step, say who is right, and about what. Then tell me what both of them are missing — including anything about the mother's own refusal that might restructure the whole question — and whether the moral logic of my ending holds together as they have framed it.
