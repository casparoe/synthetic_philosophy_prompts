I teach a third-year seminar on the historiography of "collapse" — the Bronze Age palatial systems, the Classic Maya, Easter Island, that whole literature. 14 students, one 90-minute session, no lecture up front. Design an in-class simulation where they play six successive generations of a single island society. The key mechanism I want: each generation should experience their own situation as normal and continuous (theres always a temple, always a market day, always somebody in charge), even as the material conditions drift between generations — population shrinks, the old script falls out of use, the capital relocates, the gods get quietly renamed. Each generation should also write a short "this is what our society is" ledger, listing the features they consider essential. At the end, split the students in two: one group judges whether the society "collapsed" using generation one's ledger, the other using generation six's, and they should get to argue about the verdicts. The debrief then maps the disagreement onto the actual fault line in collapse studies: is collapse a failure of a society or just a description of change — and failure by whose standard, exactly? Also be honest about what the activity can't reproduce, namely that real historians of collapse are themselves outsiders many generations later with their own value-laden ledgers, and tell me how the facilitator should supply that in the debrief. And predict what'll go wrong the first time — my guess is half of them will treat it as a civ-building game and try to optimise their generation, tell me whether that actually ruins the point or is useable material.
