I'm a retired physicist -- thirty-some years of beam dynamics at a national lab, and since retiring I've gotten proper hooked on philosophy, mostly decision theory and game theory because those are the parts where I can still kid myself that my old training helps. I run a monthly discussion group at the library, about a dozen of us, mostly retired engineers and chemists plus one ex-auditor who keeps everyone honest. Normally we just argue about a paper, but next month they've voted for something interactive, and I want to build the whole session around an idea my nephew (he works on AI safety) told me about over Christmas: safe Pareto improvements. The gist as I understand it: when two agents are headed into a game with inefficient equilibria, like chicken where the mixed strategy leaves some chance both players drive straight into each other, they can sometimes both commit in advance to an arrangement -- often as dumb as flipping a coin to decide who swerves -- that leaves each of them at least as well off as whatever outcome the original game would have produced, for every combination of what they each would have played. Nobody gives anything up relative to their default, the bad outcomes just stop happening. The part I find beautiful is the "safe" part: the arrangement has to dominate every possible play, not just beat the equilibrium in expectation. That's a much stranger and stronger condition than it first sounds.

So design the session for me: 90 minutes, 12 people, whiteboard, dice or cards, no real money (chocolate coins are the usual currency). I want them to actually play some chicken-like game in pairs first and feel the crash risk coming out of the mixed equilibrium, and then arrive at the point where they'd want to negotiate -- and here's the part I care most about, I want the sequence arranged so they more or less invent the coin-flip-and-commit trick themselves instead of me announcing it. I'm guessing the design has to do with what happens when you let them talk between rounds versus not, but you tell me. Then a debrief that maps what they actually did onto the formal idea: what the safety condition really amounts to, why dominating every outcome pair is different from just improving on the equilibrium, and where the whole thing quietly leans on commitments actually binding -- which in my group they won't, since it'll all be handshake agreements, and honestly that might be the most interesting part of the discussion.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. My bets: some pairs will just agree to both swerve every round and think they've solved it, someone who loses the coin flip will want to renegotiate, and one of the engineers will insist that flipping a coin over who swerves isn't really playing chicken at all so the exercise is rigged. Where in the sequence do I head each of these off, and which of them should I deliberately let happen because they teach something? And be frank about which feature of the real theory the activity can't reproduce -- my nephew says the serious applications are to agents that can genuinely bind themselves, like programs inspecting each other's source code, and my retirees can't do that, so I'll need to know exactly what I have to supply myself in the debrief. One last thing: give me a check at the end that'll show whether they got the idea itself and not just the chocolate.
