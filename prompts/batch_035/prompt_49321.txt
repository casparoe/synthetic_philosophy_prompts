I am preparing an application for a six-month master's dissertation in philosophy of mind, and I need a two-page proposal built around the following half-formed idea.

Douglas Hofstadter, in I Am a Strange Loop (2006), introduces the careenium: a vast billiard table on which enormous numbers of small magnetic spheres, which he calls simms, careen about, clumping together and breaking apart. When one zooms out, one sees higher-level, relatively persistent structures — simmballs — whose behaviour both mirrors events outside the table (information reaches the simms through changes in the bounciness of the table's edges) and unfolds with a regularity of its own. Hofstadter maps this onto the cranium: the simms are the neural substrate, the simmballs are symbols or concepts, and he asks, roughly, who pushes whom around inside the system. His answer is that both levels of description are causally legitimate: the substrate level, though one hundred per cent responsible for everything that happens, is an utterly hopeless way of describing what happens, so the symbol level can equally well be read as doing the pushing. He labels this the causal potency of patterns.

My worry is that this quietly runs together two different claims: (a) that symbol-level descriptions are vastly more compressible and predictive than substrate-level ones, and (b) that symbol-level states are themselves causally relevant — that they, and not merely their physical implementers, help push the substrate around. Claim (a) seems uncontroversial, and it would hold even in a system with no symbols at all, such as a gas described thermodynamically; claim (b) is the interesting one, and I suspect it needs an account of what it is for a state to be causally relevant at a given level of description — perhaps something along the lines of the determinable/determinate distinction that some philosophers of causation have appealed to — rather than an analogy with pools of marbles. What I want funded is a dissertation that (i) states precisely what it would take for claim (b) to be true, (ii) tests whether the careenium picture, once the ambiguity between (a) and (b) is removed, still supports the conclusion Hofstadter draws from it, and (iii) says what survives for the philosophy of mind if it does not.

Please produce the proposal document with the following elements: a precise statement of the research question; an explanation of why the existing positions leave it open (epiphenomenalism, Kim's causal exclusion worry, and Hofstadter's own answer are the obvious reference points, but I do not want a literature review — I shall add citations myself); the method, including what I would actually do, month by month; a statement of what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of whether this is achievable in six months, including which parts I would cut first if it proved too large. Do not include a bibliography.
