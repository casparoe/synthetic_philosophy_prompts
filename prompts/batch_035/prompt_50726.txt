I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am preparing a long essay for the blog I keep for my book club on Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, which we read last month. While researching it I came across Daniel Dennett's idea, from Consciousness Explained, that the self is a center of narrative gravity: a useful theoretical posit rather than a thing — a point around which a person's story is organized, computed by the brain the way a center of mass is computed for a physical object, and real in roughly the way that abstraction is real. The pairing immediately clicked for me, because as an engineer I spend my days relying on entities like this. A load balancer's virtual host, a service's public interface, the "primary user account" in a system — nothing in the system is that object, but everything programs against it, and it would be silly to say it isn't real or doesn't do causal work. Stevens, Ishiguro's butler-narrator, seems to me like a character whose self is precisely such an interface: he keeps the narrative of the great butler with dignity running long after the underlying system — Lord Darlington, the era, his own heart — has changed out from under it, like a monitoring dashboard reporting a service that was decommissioned years ago.

I would like you to read the novel through Dennett's idea, taking both seriously. Some questions I want the essay to answer: Stevens narrates the entire book himself, in a diary kept during his 1956 motoring trip — so on Dennett's picture, we never meet anything except the spin; the reader is effectively the audience for whom the center of narrative gravity is being maintained. Does the novel show this process working, and where does Stevens's narration demonstrably diverge from the facts (I am thinking of his account of the evening Miss Kenton tried to pry the sentimental novel from his hands, of his conduct on the night his father died during Lord Darlington's conference, and of his recasting of the dismissal of the Jewish maids as a question of professional dignity)? Is his tragedy, in Dennettian terms, that he outsourced his narrative to an employer and a class ideal — that the fiction acquired its own inertia — or is it something Dennett's account cannot quite capture? And the ending, where Stevens, retired and alone at the seaside, resolves to practice banter with his new American employer: is that the abstraction being gracefully deprecated, or being honestly rewritten, or is it just a new interface over the same emptiness?

Two further asks. First, please be an honest critic and tell me where the novel resists the reading: my readers will notice that the grief we feel on Stevens's behalf seems to presuppose a real self being wasted, not merely an abstraction being poorly maintained, and I want to know whether that intuition refutes Dennett or is itself just the abstraction talking. Second, tell me frankly whether my engineering analogies (the interface, the stale dashboard) illuminate Dennett or merely flatter my profession: where does the analogy hold, where does it break, and what does the novel add to Dennett's account that the philosophical literature, in your understanding of it, does not. Please avoid direct quotation from either the novel or Dennett, since I will be checking my own copy when I turn your reading into prose.
