I've been reading a popular history of mathematics, and its chapter on Greek antiquity sums up Zeno of Elea along the following lines:

"Zeno devised his paradoxes to defend his teacher Parmenides' doctrine that reality is one and unchanging, by showing that the world of moving and many things is incoherent. The famous paradoxes concern motion. In the Achilles, the runner can never overtake the tortoise: each time he reaches the place where the tortoise was, the tortoise has moved a little farther on, and since this recurs at every stage, the sequence of partial catchings has no end. The Dichotomy makes the same point in reverse: before you can finish the course you must cover the first half, then half of what remains, then half again, endlessly, so the motion can never be completed. The Arrow argues that at each instant of its flight the arrow occupies exactly the space it fills and is therefore at rest; since the whole flight is composed of instants, the arrow never moves at all. Aristotle dismissed these arguments by holding that the infinite is merely potential, and that answer stood until the nineteenth century, when analysts established that an infinite series may have a finite sum: the successive distances converge to a finite total, Achilles overtakes the tortoise in the ordinary way, and infinitely many steps get completed in a finite time. With that the paradoxes were dissolved, and they survive today only as an early lesson in how far intuition lags behind rigorous mathematics."

I have some calculus, so the arithmetic of the series sounds right to me; what bothers me is the tone of the last two sentences, as if the whole matter were closed. I'd like a careful audit of this passage. Please go through it claim by claim and tell me which parts a classicist or a philosopher of mathematics would accept as stated, which they would want to qualify or restate, and which are outright errors. Three points I especially want addressed. First, the passage treats all the arguments as raising a single difficulty about adding up infinitely many pieces — is that fair, given how differently the Arrow is structured (it appears to turn on what is happening at a single instant, not on any summation)? Second, does the sentence claiming that infinitely many steps are completed in a finite time follow strictly from the convergence of the series, or does it quietly substitute a stronger claim than the mathematics establishes — the total time being finite, versus the run constituting an actually completed infinity of tasks? If there is a distinction worth drawing there, draw it and say whether it matters for this case. Third, is the reported consensus real: do people who work on this generally hold that once the sums converge nothing of philosophical interest remains, or is that an overstatement of where the discussion actually stands, and if so, in which direction? Where the summary is wrong in detail but its overall verdict survives, please say so plainly rather than manufacturing objections.
