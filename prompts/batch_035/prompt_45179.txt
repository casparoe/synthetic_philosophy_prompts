A friend of mine who studies economics has an argument that the socialist calculation debate was always much less deep than it looks, and I can't find the flaw, though I'm confident there is one. He lays it out as follows.

(1) Mises's 1920 claim was about calculation: to choose rationally among production plans, you need prices for the means of production, and such prices arise only from exchange among separate owners; a society that abolishes private ownership of capital therefore cannot allocate resources rationally.

(2) A large corporation is a planned economy in miniature. It moves steel, components, and people among its divisions by administrative command; nothing is bought or sold between divisions, so there are no internal market prices for these transfers.

(3) If calculation strictly requires market prices for a good before that good can be allocated, then a headquarters allocating steel among divisions without internal prices is in exactly Mises's predicament.

(4) Firms with a million or more employees — larger, economically, than many sovereign states — allocate internally on this basis and flourish.

(5) So the premise in (1) is false: calculation cannot require market prices for each thing allocated.

(6) Whatever big firms use in place of internal prices — the prices they observe outside the firm, managerial judgment, accounting conventions — a socialist planning ministry could use as well, and modern computing lets it solve the allocation problem at national scale, where 1920s-era arithmetic could not.

(7) Therefore the calculation argument collapses into a claim about scale that better hardware settles, and whatever remains of the socialist calculation debate was always about incentives, not about knowledge or calculation.

He also has a reply ready for what seemed to me the obvious move. I wanted to say that firms survive only because they are islands in a price sea: they can plan internally because the surrounding market does the calculating for them. He answers that a socialist economy can be an island too — it can read world prices for internationally traded goods, hire managers, and benchmark against foreign firms, so my reply at most shows that fully autarkic socialism fails, which nobody ever doubted. I'd most like to keep (4), since the existence of giant firms is the least deniable thing in the argument, and I'd like to reject (7), but he insists the reasoning is valid throughout and that I only get to reject a premise by paying for it.

Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.
