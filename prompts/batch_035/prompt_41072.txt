I write long-form pieces on science and its history for a general readership, and I am at work on an essay about what science can claim to have gained from theories that turned out to be false. The natural frame for that question is the one Karl Popper set: since even our best theories may be false, progress must consist in getting closer to the truth rather than arriving at it. Popper's attempt to make this precise — a theory is closer to the truth than a rival when the true consequences of the former include those of the latter, and its false consequences are included among the rival's — famously collapsed. In 1974 Pavel Tichý and David Miller showed, independently, that on that definition no false theory can ever come out closer to the truth than another, and Popper conceded the point almost immediately. That much I can report as settled history. My difficulty is an argument I built on top of it, which now appears to prove a great deal more than I can accept, and I would like to know which step to give up and what giving it up costs.

The argument runs as follows. (1) Being close to the truth is not a property a theory has on its own; it is a relation between the theory and the truth about its domain — there is no being nearly right without something to be nearly right about. (2) Nobody possesses the truth about any substantive domain before inquiry in that domain is complete; that is simply what it means for a domain to remain open. (3) To judge that one theory is closer to the truth than another, one must be able to compare both against the truth — and for theories of the scope of Newton's or Descartes's, that means the truth about more or less everything. (4) So no one can judge, before inquiry in a domain is complete, that one theory in that domain is closer to the truth than a rival. (5) No inquiry is ever complete: every scientific result remains open to revision, so the end of inquiry never arrives within science as actually practiced. (6) So within the actual history of science, no one has ever been in a position to judge that a new theory was closer to the truth than the one it replaced. (7) If no one could ever have judged this, then no one ever had a reason grounded in truth for preferring one theory to another; a reason that could not, even in principle, be possessed is not a reason anyone had. (8) Therefore the preferences scientists actually formed — Copernicus over Ptolemy, Newton over Descartes, Einstein over Newton — were never grounded in truth or its approximation, but at best in convenience, conformity, or taste; and since the end of inquiry is no nearer now than in 1543, no future scientist will ever have such a reason either.

The conclusion seems to me not just false but self-undermining: it would apply with equal force to an editor rejecting a manuscript, a referee weighing a data set, or a patient asking which diagnosis is nearer the mark. Yet every premise looked innocent when I wrote it down. I would most like to keep (1), since my whole essay leans on the thought that truth is the fixed target, and (5), since I have built a good deal of my journalism on the permanent-revision picture of science. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine, and what each alternative rejection costs — and if more than one step is defective, say which I should surrender first.
