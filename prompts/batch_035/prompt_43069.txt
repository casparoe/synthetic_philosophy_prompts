I've spent the past several hours arguing with a friend of mine who works on formal epistemology, and by the end of it we'd established that my position on higher-order evidence is a lot less unified than I'd thought. Before I commit it to writing for our reading group, I'd like an audit of whether my various commitments actually hang together. Here is the package: (1) Evidentialism: whether a belief is justified supervenes entirely on how things stand evidentially from the subject's perspective, and the right norms are exclusively truth-conducive ones — no distinctively epistemic duties beyond responding correctly to one's evidence. (2) A general anti-akrasia principle: it is always irrational to believe p while also believing that one's total evidence better supports not-p; no exceptions, however exotic the case. (3) Full-blown higher-order defeat: when I acquire reason to think my first-order assessment of my evidence is unreliable, this can destroy my justification for p even though my first-order evidence is entirely unchanged — and, crucially, even if the higher-order evidence is itself misleading, since a bad guide can still be my guide. (4) A case verdict we fought over: a reasoner whose first-order case for p is overwhelming then receives apparently authoritative testimony that she has been drugged into overestimating the strength of her evidence, but also has independent and decisive reason to think the testimony is a hoax. My verdict: she is justified in believing p outright — full stop, not with a hedged credence — and not merely excused. (5) Fallibilism: justified belief does not require conclusive support, and holding p together with "but for all I know, not-p" is perfectly in order and not akratic in the slightest.

My friend claims that (2) and (3) jointly with my verdict in (4) commit me to saying that misleading higher-order evidence changes what one's total evidence supports, which she thinks collapses the first-order/higher-order distinction I elsewhere insist on; she also thinks (5) is inconsistent with (2), on the ground that a fallibilist who recognizes she might be wrong already believes her evidence fails to settle the matter, which is a step toward akrasia. I half-suspect she's wrong about at least one of these, but I genuinely can't tell which, and I'd rather not find out in front of the reading group.

So please go through the five commitments pairwise where they interact, and tell me which tensions are real, which only look like tensions, and how severe the real ones are — rank them rather than treating them as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the package (for instance, whether giving up the no-exceptions clause in (2) forces me to reconsider the verdict in (4), or whether (1) is doing more work than I realize). Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Finally, since I'll be defending this under questioning: say where your own audit is most likely to go wrong — which of the dissolutions you offer is the one a smart objector could most plausibly crack, and what argument or case would change your verdict on it.
