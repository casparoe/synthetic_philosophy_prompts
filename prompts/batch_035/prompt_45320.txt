I serve on the curriculum committee of a mid-sized public school district that is rewriting its eleventh-grade American history standards, and we are stuck on a question I hadn't expected to be philosophically hard: what the organizing narrative of the course should be. One faction wants the course anchored to 1619, treating the arrival of enslaved Africans in Virginia as the nation's real founding and making slavery and its afterlife the lens for everything that follows; the other wants it anchored to 1776, presenting the Declaration's equality claim as a promise the country spent two centuries redeeming. What strikes me is that the two factions accept almost the same historical facts — they trade footnotes and emphasis rather than accusing each other of outright error — and both claim to be defending honest history, one meaning honest about the victims, the other honest about the ideals. A colleague who grew up in Seoul keeps invoking Japan's textbook controversies, where disputes over how to describe wartime coercion were fought as battles over children's loyalties, and says we are doing exactly the same thing. Is there a principled way to choose between the two framings — some criterion more substantive than which coalition currently controls the board — or is the choice political all the way down? And if some aims of history teaching are epistemic and some civic, how should I think about which of those a public school is entitled to prioritize, and at what cost to the others?
