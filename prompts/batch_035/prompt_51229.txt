In a philosophy of biology seminar I'm auditing, the instructor has been defending the selected-effects account of biological function against the causal-role alternative, and the defense runs almost entirely through an artifact analogy. To ask for the function of the heart, she says, is like asking what a hammer is for: a hammer is for driving nails because it was made in order to drive nails, so the function of a part is fixed by why it is there, not by what it happens to do. A hammer that has snapped in half is still for driving nails even while it can't drive any; so a diseased heart still has the function of pumping blood even as it fails to pump. And the causal-role theorist, she claims, is like someone who says that a hammer's function is whatever you happen to use it for — paperweight, doorstop, improvised weapon — which plainly loses the distinction between what a thing is for and what it merely does. The conclusion she draws is that function is essentially historical, that there can be malfunction without any further story, and that any account that lets current contribution to some capacity determine function has abandoned the subject.

I'd like to know how much of this the artifact analogy can actually carry. Please give me an explicit correspondence table for the mapping: what in the biological case is supposed to play the role of the designer, the intention, the manufacturing process, the blueprint, and the use an owner later puts the artifact to. Then identify where the mapping breaks. Some places to probe, though you should also strike out on your own: does natural selection really occupy the designer's slot, given that it does not design ahead of time but only filters what already exists? Can the analogy distinguish what a trait was selected for from mere effects that came along for the ride, and does the artifact side even have an equivalent of that distinction? What does the analogy predict about structures with no selection history for their current job — a duplicated gene that has newly taken over a role, or an organ co-opted for a novel function — and is that prediction the right one? And is the hammer-as-paperweight retort fair to the causal-role view, or does it equivocate on what counts as a system whose capacities matter?

Finally, separate the disanalogies that matter for her intended conclusion from the ones that don't, and say whether the historical-function conclusion survives once the breakdowns are on the table. If you think there is a better analogy available for one side or the other — for instance, something from social role or office-holding rather than from designed artifacts — sketch it and say what it changes.
