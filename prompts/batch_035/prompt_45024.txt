I have defended for some years a version of constitutive panpsychism: roughly, the dispositional properties physics catalogues are grounded in categorical phenomenal properties, and ordinary subjects like us are built bottom-up out of micro-level subjects, in something like the way a sentence's content is fixed by its words. Everyone agrees the combination problem — above all its subject-summing version, the question of how many centers of perspective could compose into one — is the standing objection. I am not looking for another recital of the objection. What I want is a map of my view's exposure. What would actually count against it, and in what order of severity — which arguments, which possible cases, which empirically flavored considerations should be ranked fatal, which merely serious, and which merely awkward? Where does the view stand or fall together with other commitments I hold, e.g. about composition and vagueness in general, or about whether phenomenal properties could be partial grounds of anything? And which of the candidate defeaters would the view's own serious defenders concede as counting against it, as opposed to absorbing with a qualification?

A few things I want treated specifically rather than in generalities. First, the vagueness of phenomenal unity: I suspect the view can slide between saying that subjects genuinely compose and saying that they merely become associated in a structured aggregate, and I do not know whether that slide is a weakness or just the correct response to the fact that 'one subject' may be vague at composite scales. Second, whether anything in the vicinity of empirical evidence bears on the combinatorial assumptions at all: cases like split-brain patients, where one subject appears to become two, or the staggered return of capacities from anesthesia, where sub-functions presumably reintegrate at different rates — do such cases constrain a bottom-up combinatorial picture of experience, or are they too coarse-grained, too much at the level of report and behavior, to touch it? Third, the thought that micro-experience might not be point-like but in some way already open to fusion; I suspect that if I helped myself to that, I would have converted my constitutive panpsychism into something much closer to cosmopsychism, with a single prior subject and ordinary subjects as its fragments, and I want to know whether that is a genuine cost or merely a re-description.

Finally, the question I care about most. Over time my view has accumulated escape routes — retreat from constitutive to emergent panpsychism, retreat to panprotopsychist placeholders where the micro-level is 'proto' rather than genuinely experiential, retreat to a principled quietism about how combination works — and each retreat is individually reasonable, but jointly they threaten to make the position impossible to lose. Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, is that a defect in a metaphysical position of this kind, whose evidence is largely a priori and whose rivals face their own degeneration problems, or is it just the ordinary condition of the genre? For my part, I would be most moved by a demonstration that constitutive panpsychism is committed to some specific, honorable consequence it cannot give up — say, that there can be no phenomenal novelty in composition, no experiential property of a composite that is not in some recognizable sense inherited from its parts — and that this consequence is one the view also cannot deliver. If that dilemma is real, I want to see it pressed; if it is not, I want to see exactly where it breaks.
