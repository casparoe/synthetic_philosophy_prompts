I'm teaching a unit on drug policy in an applied ethics course — prohibition, the Portuguese decriminalization model, and regulated cannabis legalization — and for the closing session I want to screen the Hamsterdam arc from season three of The Wire, in which Major "Bunny" Colvin quietly designates three tolerated zones for the open-air drug trade in West Baltimore and lets the rest of his district go quiet. Read that arc through the drug-policy debate rather than as a cop show.

A few things I want the reading to do. First, say what the arc actually models. My worry is that Hamsterdam is neither decriminalization nor legalization: Portugal decriminalized possession for personal use while keeping dealing criminal and building in treatment referral and administrative panels, and legalization regimes replace the illicit market with a regulated one; Hamsterdam is something else — state tolerance of an unregulated, concentrated market, with essentially no public-health apparatus attached until the harm is already visible. So is the arc a fair test of any real policy option, or does it stack the deck by giving us a caricature of what reformers want and then showing the bodies? Second, say what it dramatizes well about prohibition: the violence and recruitment economics of the illicit trade, the distortion of policing by statistics, the relocation rather than reduction of suffering. Third, be honest about where the work resists the policy reading: my own view is that the season's real thesis is institutional — the experiment is destroyed by bosses, numbers, and a mayoral campaign, not by its own pharmacological or criminological failure — so maybe Hamsterdam isn't an argument for any drug regime at all, but an argument that no reform can survive the institutions The Wire depicts. If that's right, I want to know it, because I don't want to walk my students out of the screening thinking they've seen an argument for Portugal when they've actually seen an argument about Baltimore.

Along the way, tell me which scenes you'd point the students to and what each one is evidence of; say where the show's treatment of the addicts in the zones is a moral argument in itself (harm reduction as containment versus as respect); and say what the arc contributes that the policy literature doesn't — what only fiction can show about what prohibition feels like from inside a neighborhood that has been given back its corners.

No need for a policy scorecard. But end with a single line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a short label (at most about eight words) naming which of the following the arc, read honestly, most nearly vindicates: prohibition, decriminalization with treatment, regulated legalization, harm-reduction-without-sanction, or, if you think the honest answer is that it vindicates none of them, label it "none — institutional fatalism instead."
