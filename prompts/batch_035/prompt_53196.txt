I write novels, and over the last few years I have become professionally fascinated by a certain aftertaste that some of my characters carry and others do not. I cannot find the philosophical concept for it, and I would like to know whether one exists and, more importantly, where its edges are. Let me describe it concretely rather than abstractly.

In one manuscript, a woman hiding with her family in an occupied country is forced at gunpoint to say which of her two sons the soldiers should take away. She names the younger one. The rest of the book is about the fact that she never stops feeling that she chose, even though she knows, and every reader knows, that refusing to answer would have cost both boys. In another book, a doctor during an epidemic works a triage table, and the rule he follows — a rule he endorses and would follow again — sends a man who arrived four minutes too late to his death. What interests me about both characters is what I have to write in the scenes after the decision. It is not relief, and it is not clean grief. It is a stain: a guilt that the text has to treat as fitting rather than as a mistake. If I write it as a mistake, the character rings false; if I write it as nothing, the character rings false there too. The wrong, insofar as there is one, appears to be located in the situation; the residue lands on the agent; and the residue presents itself, in my fiction, as the rational response to the situation rather than to anything the agent did.

I have tried to find out whether philosophy has a name for this configuration — right action, no error anywhere, and yet an apparently appropriate remainder of guilt on the person who acted rightly. The nearest things I have turned up do not quite fit. People say colloquially that such figures have dirty hands, but that phrase seems to belong to politics and to doing wrong in order to gain or keep power, whereas my characters gain nothing. I have also read, in passing, that some philosophers hold that a coherent morality can never really confront you with two things you must do when you can do only one — that if you examine the case carefully enough, one consideration always outweighs the other, and the felt conflict is an artifact of imperfect information. If that were right, the stain I keep writing would presumably be a stain left by an illusion, and it does not behave that way on the page.

So, my questions. Does the configuration I am describing have an established name in philosophy? If it does, I need more than the label: I need to know what the term covers and what it excludes, because I have to be able to tell, scene by scene, which of my characters it applies to. Does it extend to the doctor, whose rule did the wrong while he merely administered it, or only to the mother, who seems to have done something herself? Is it the same thing as regretting a bad outcome one caused, or is the difference between those two precisely the point? And if philosophy has no single term for it, I would be grateful if you would coin one and define it strictly enough that I can apply it — and withhold it — with confidence.
