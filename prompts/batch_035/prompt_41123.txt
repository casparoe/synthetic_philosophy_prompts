I'm writing a term paper on the value of originals, and there's a design problem I can't solve on my own. The two positions I'm working with are familiar from the literature on forgery: the aesthetic view, on which a painting's value is fixed by what can be encountered in the work itself, so that if two pictures are visually indistinguishable they are aesthetically equal, authenticity being historically interesting but not value-conferring; and the performance view, on which a painting is valuable as the record of an artistic achievement, so who did what, and under what circumstances, matters — the value rides on the work's causal history the way a document's value rides on its provenance. In the standard cases these two views line up in the expected way: the aesthetic view rates a perfect forgery equal to its original, and the performance view rates it worse. What I want is a case engineered to reverse the second verdict: the performance view's own commitments, applied honestly, should rate the forgery more valuable than the painting it copies, while the aesthetic view continues to rate them equal.

Some constraints, so the case can't cheat. It should be set in the ordinary art world — a conservation studio, a museum, an auction house — with no exotic devices: no molecular duplication, no sci-fi replication, nothing a normal person couldn't verify. The two pictures must be visually indistinguishable to an attentive viewer standing in front of them. The forger must not be more famous, more technically brilliant, or historically more important than the original artist — the forgery's greater value cannot come from the forger outshining the master. It has to fall out of the performance view itself, and I suspect the way to get this is by making the "original" a work whose causal history the performance view must, on reflection, treat as thin: something like a studio product where the named artist's own hand barely appears, so that when the theory looks to the actual performance behind the picture, there is disappointingly little of it there, while the forgery, whatever else it is, is the unaided work of one person's hand throughout. The verdicts must be generated by the theories, not stipulated: if the scenario simply declares the forgery better and the original worse, it's worthless to me.

After the scenario — one paragraph, no more — do the walkthrough. State what the aesthetic view says about the two paintings and why it must rate them equal. Then state what the performance view says, and show, using only its own resources, the reasoning that leads it to prefer the forgery to the picture it copies. Then tell me honestly which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if I remove that feature (does the case collapse back into the standard forgery dispute, with the performance view again preferring the original?), and how a defender of the performance view would most plausibly attack the case rather than swallow the verdict. And if you think the specification as I've stated it can't be met without cheating, say so directly, explain where it breaks, and tell me what the nearest satisfiable version looks like.
