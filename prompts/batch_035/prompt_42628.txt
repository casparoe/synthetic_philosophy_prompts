My roommate has very firm opinions about what counts as weakness of will and what doesn't, and I keep a list of her verdicts because I suspect she's running some rule she's never made explicit (she's read a bit of philosophy and a bit of behavioral econ, for whatever that's worth). The record so far:

1. A smoker who sincerely judges that quitting is best all things considered, resolves to quit, buys a pack anyway, and hates herself for it — akratic, obviously.
2. Someone at a dessert table with no view about which cake is better who just picks one on a whim — not akrasia, that's just caprice.
3. Someone who genuinely reassesses their life, decides money matters less than they thought, and quits banking for pottery with zero inner conflict — not akrasia, that's just changing your mind.
4. Someone who, a month out, prefers $100 later over $80 tomorrow, but when tomorrow actually comes grabs the $80 — and does this exact reversal every single time — akratic.
5. A compulsive hand washer who judges that the washing is pointless, "has to" do it anyway, gets no pleasure from it and in no sense prefers it — not akrasia, she says: that's compulsion, the urge isn't really theirs.

Now we have four new cases we argue about over dinner, and I want your read on what she'll say:

A. Our friend who procrastinates. Every morning she firmly prefers finishing the report that night over goofing off; every evening, in the moment, she sincerely prefers the goofing off; and she goes to bed scrolling having at no point beleived "the report is the better thing to do right now" while opening the streaming app. She regrets it every morning. Her preferences just flip depending on how far away the deadline is.

B. Her ex, an addict in a bad stretch. In calm moments he keeps a firm resolution never to use, fully endorses it, plans his life around it. When craving actually hits he reports no thought of the resolution at all — autopilot, hand already moving. Asked mid-episode, he'll say the resolution is "true but not live."

C. Me. I judged, all things considered, that donating my bonus was the right call, then impulsively blew it on a trip to Lisbon while fully aware I was doing what I judged worse. Zero regret, would do it again, and looking back my considered judgment has quietly updated itself to match what I did. I claim I was akratic; she's not so sure.

D. Her other friend, who judges the risky career move is best and actually takes it, but spends the whole time terrified and wishing she wanted the safe option instead.

Predict her verdicts on A through D. What rule (or the best few candidate rules) actually fits her track record — and note the record seems consistent with at least two different principles, e.g. one about acting against a concurrent all-things-considered judgment versus one about preference reversals driven by how far away the reward is, versus maybe something about regret or what you'd endorse from a calm distance. Which of those rules come apart on which of the four cases, and what's your best guess for her actual verdicts and why? And if we wanted to settle which rule she's really using with one more test case, what would you put in front of her?
