I'm the research editor at a magazine of popular history, and a large share of my job is deciding, quickly and without any special training in the ancient languages, whether we can print claims about the pre-modern past as fact. Every month brings eight or ten of these: a writer wants to say that a newly announced inscription "proves" a previously unattested governor and overturns the standard chronology; a freelancer wants to repeat a lurid casualty figure for an ancient battle that comes from a single author writing centuries after the event; someone wants to assert an ancient ruler's motives as though we had access to his inner life. I have maybe an afternoon per claim, I can consult translations and modern scholarship but not archives or untranslated material, and my editor-in-chief wants a written rationale she can point to if a historian complains. What I lack is any principled middle ground between "a classicist somewhere doubted this once" and "it's on a reputable website, run it."

Please turn what source criticism actually knows -- from the things Herodotus was already doing when he flagged that he reports what he was told without vouching for it, through the more systematic skepticism of Thucydides, down to the working principles modern historians of the distant past use, like independent attestation, distance between event and surviving source, genre conventions, and the different weights carried by narrative texts versus inscriptions, coins, and material remains -- into a procedure I can actually run. I want the questions in order, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points at which the procedure stops and says "not publishable as fact" regardless of how the rest comes out; and the points at which it honestly hands the question back to me as an editorial judgment rather than pretending to be a fact-finding step. I'd especially like it to handle the cases that bedevil us: claims resting on a single late source that could still be true, claims where archaeology and texts point in different directions, and claims that are really modern back-projections wearing ancient costume.

Then run the procedure on three claims: first, that a battle fought around the fifth century BCE had roughly a quarter of a million combatants, a figure appearing in one ancient author writing several centuries later; second, that a festival or ritual attested only in a single source whose genre is satirical was nonetheless genuinely practiced at a specific shrine; and third, a story I personally love and want to run -- that a shipwreck cargo of amphorae shows a named merchant lost a fortune on a speculative voyage -- where I suspect the procedure will tell me the claim is probably true but still not good enough to print as stated, and if it does, I want to see exactly which bar it fails and what weaker formulation would pass. Please be candid about where the procedure will misfire or run out, and keep the whole thing, worked cases included, under 1,000 words.
