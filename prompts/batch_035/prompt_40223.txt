I repair industrial machinery for a living and have never studied economics, political science, or philosophy, but for several years now I have been trying to work out whether voting makes any sense, and I have gotten far enough to be confused in an organized way. I have done the arithmetic myself: in a national election, the chance that my one ballot is decisive seems to be something like one in ten million, and in a safe state perhaps far worse, so the expected effect of my vote on the outcome is effectively zero. And yet I vote anyway, and I feel like a bad citizen when I imagine staying home. When I argue about this with friends, I notice that people who all say "of course you should vote" believe completely different things: one thinks it is a duty like paying taxes, one thinks it is about "sending a message," one thinks that if everyone reasoned the way I do democracy would collapse, and one cheerfully admits it is like going to a football game and shouting for your team even though the shouting does not affect the score. I want to sort them out.

Please take the role of an expert in decision theory and political philosophy — someone who knows the debate about the rationality of voting inside and out but writes for people like me — and build me a diagnostic quiz, "Why do you vote (or not vote)?", that sorts people by the position they actually hold rather than by the label they would choose for themselves.

Before writing anything, please brainstorm: map out the logical space of positions first, generated by the questions that I think actually split people and any further questions you believe belong there, and only then design the quiz. Then deliver eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, followed by a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions.

Some cases I want you to work in, because I think my friends genuinely come apart on them:

- The coin-flip case. Some elections resolve exact ties by drawing lots. Suppose my vote creates a tie rather than breaking one, so the outcome is decided by a coin flip. Does that change whether my vote "mattered"? I want a question here, because it seems to split people who count only actual difference in the final outcome from people who count the chance my vote creates, and I would like to know whether that split is real.
- The statistician case, which I invented and would like you to sharpen. A perfect statistician tells me that people with dispositions exactly like mine decide almost exactly alike, so my deciding to vote is strong evidence that a million people like me also voted — though none of us caused any of the others. Some of my friends say that is a reason to vote; others say it is superstition, like deciding to vote because my choice is statistically correlated with the weather. This one cleanly divides my friends who say "your vote matters," and it must be in the quiz.
- The landslide case: the election is predicted to be a blowout by everyone, and my vote is worth as little as a vote can be worth. Who still votes, and why?
- The small-association case: my homeowners' association has eleven members, and my vote is genuinely close to pivotal. Does the same principle cover both scales, and if not, what changes between them?
- The third-party case: voting for a candidate who cannot possibly win, to "send a message." 
- The secret-ballot case: nobody would ever know I abstained.

For the scoring key, include at least these positions as leaves wherever they have real names: the straight instrumentalist conclusion that voting in large elections is irrational or nearly so; the view that the tiny probability of decisiveness is outweighed by the enormous stakes once you count strangers' welfare; the view that voting is worth it for what it expresses rather than what it causes; the duty view; and the view that my vote's job is to sustain the general norm of turnout rather than to affect this particular election. For combinations of answers that no position occupies, label them "unnamed" and say one sentence about why nobody is there — whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.

Rules for the questions: no question may mention a theory, a philosopher's name, or a label; every question must be answerable by someone who has never heard of any of this; the answer options must be things a real person at my kitchen table would actually say; and if two of the positions above would answer every one of your questions identically, flag that at the end instead of inventing a question that pretends to separate them.

Finally, tell me briefly which of my six cases you found genuinely diagnostic and which you had to drop or change, and why. I would rather learn where my framing is wrong than receive a quiz that flatters it. Please think carefully about whether each question truly splits the positions it is meant to split before you commit to it.
