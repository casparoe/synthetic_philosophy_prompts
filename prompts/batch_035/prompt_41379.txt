I'm on the constitution comittee for our university's student association -- we're rewriting the whole governing document this year after the old one collapsed in a governance mess I won't get into. I'm also taking a political philosophy course this semester where we just covered Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment, so maybe I'm seeing this everywhere now, but here's the fight.

A chunk of the committee wants to entrench a clause: a permanent Sustainability and Mutual Aid Fund that gets a fixed percentage of our budget every year, and the clause itself can only be changed by a three-quarters vote of two consecutive elected assemblies, sitting at least a year apart. Their argument is basically that future students are like our future selves with a hangover: we know our best selves want this fund, so we should tie the future to the mast now, because if we leave it up to year-by-year majorities it'll be the first thing cut whenever someone runs on "giving money back to clubs."

The other faction says this is the dead hand problem in miniature: the students being bound literally weren't there when we voted, some of them weren't even enrolled yet, so it's not self-binding at all -- it's us binding strangers. One of them also pointed out that Ulysses at least told his crew to untie him once the danger had passed, and our clause has no "the sirens are gone now" condition -- it's forever, even if the campus goes fully renewable and the fund runs out of things to do.

We vote in two weeks and I'm supposed to circulate a written recommendation beforehand. Can you draft the memo for me? Take a position -- pass it as is, reject it, or propose the in-between version we can actually defend (people have floated sunset clauses, a lower threshold, a renewal referendum every five years, etc.) -- and give the losing side's strongest point a fair shake rather than a strawman, since I have to look these people in the eye at the meeting. One more wrinkle worth thinking about: the university re-recognizes our charter every ten years or so, which means it's not clear we can really promise anyone "forever" anyway, and I don't know if that helps the entrenchment side or undermines them.
