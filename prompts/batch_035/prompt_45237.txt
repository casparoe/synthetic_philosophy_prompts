I run a little monthly philosophy night at a bar — about fifteen people, smart, well-read, but zero philosophy training, a couple of them have read pop philosophy books and will definitely bring those up. Next month I'm doing hedonism, and my plan is to spend most of the time on what I find the actually interesting part: not whether pleasure is the only thing that's good for you (everyone will say no, experience machine, done), but the earlier and weirder question of whether "pleasure" even names one thing. Like, the pleasure of a warm shower after being cold, the pleasure of relief when a medical test comes back negative, the pleasure of being so absorbed in something you forget yourself, the pleasure of revenge, of listening to a Bach fugue, of scratching a mosquito bite — what (if anything) do all these share? I want to plan the session around that.

Don't write me the explanation itself, plan it. What has to be in place before the question even feels like a question — I suspect they'll initially think it's obvious that pleasure is a single familiar feeling, and the whole session depends on breaking that, but I'm not sure whether to do it with the relief case or the absorption case or something else entirely. Which example should carry the session (one that has to be simultaneously clearly a pleasure and clearly not fitting the obvious candidates: not a warm glow, not liking-something, etc.), and which candidate accounts of what pleasures have in common I should actually put on the table — the "common felt quality" view, the "pro-attitude/liking" view, maybe the "there's nothing but family resemblance, and hedonism was never a coherent theory about a single thing" view — and in what order, since I worry some of them presuppose others. And where each misconception comes in: the "it's all just dopamine in the end" deflection, the leap from "we can't find the common thread" to "so hedonism is false," and the one I'm dreading, people wanting to relitigate the experience machine when that's actually a totally different objection.

Also tell me what to openly refuse to get into at this level — I assume metaethics, and probably the whole literature on adverbial theories and feeling theories unless you think otherwise — and one question I can ask at the end to check whether they've understood the problem rather than just gotten dizzy from it. Maybe something like giving them a new case and seeing if they can say what each candidate account would say about it? Forty minutes, whiteboard, no jargon allowed past the second beer.
