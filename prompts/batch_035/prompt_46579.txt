I've spent two semesters on the free will literature, and I've reached a stable but unexamined conviction that the Frankfurt cases of the 1960s onward really do refute the principle of alternative possibilities — the principle that a person is morally responsible for an action only if they could have done otherwise. My advisor thinks the cases fail, mostly on the strength of the dilemma defense: either a Frankfurt case is set in a deterministic sequence, in which case it presupposes a compatibilism about responsibility that is at least as controversial as the conclusion, or it leaves the agent some alternative — some "flicker" — however thin, in which case the principle survives in the only form anyone ever cared about. My credence that the principle is false, properly stated, is about 0.6; hers is about 0.35.

I want your number for the proposition that Frankfurt cases show moral responsibility not to require alternative possibilities, together with the derivation, not a survey of the literature. Decompose it into at least these terms, plus any others you think belong: (a) the probability that the dilemma defense succeeds, i.e., that every Frankfurt case either smuggles in a flicker of alternatives or begs the question against incompatibilism; (b) the probability that the flickers left over in the best cases — the mere nonoccurrence of the counterfactual intervener's intervention, or the absence of the prior sign — are too insubstantial to be the alternatives PAP was meant to protect; (c) the probability that the conclusion generalizes from "no alternative to the action" to responsibility not depending on alternatives at all, rather than only to some restricted class of them; and (d) the probability that the disagreement is verbal — that "could have done otherwise" is ambiguous between a counterfactual about what the agent would have done in other circumstances and a claim about the actual sequence's dependence on the agent's own capacities, and that Frankfurt's cases threaten only the former reading. Give each term a rough probability with a one-line justification, show how they combine to your final number, and say which term you are least confident in.

Then tell me, given your decomposition, where the disagreement between the 0.6 and the 0.35 most likely lives — which single sub-question, if resolved, would collapse the gap between us. Finally, identify what would move your number: which argument or consideration, if it worked, would change your verdict, and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong. Please answer in American English.
