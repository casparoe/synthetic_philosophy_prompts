I've spent twenty years as a science journalist, and most of what passes for my epistemology is newsroom practice: the two-source rule, the fact-check desk, the embargo system, the chain of custody a claim travels from lab notebook to press release to wire copy, the standing question of whether I verify a finding myself or attribute it to a named expert and let the attribution do the justificatory work. I have lately been reading the epistemology of testimony — the dispute between reductionists, who hold that testimony transmits warrant only because hearers possess an inductive track record of testimonial reliability, and anti-reductionists, who hold that testimony is a basic source of justification, available to a hearer absent defeaters — and I keep feeling that I already know this fight from the inside, under different names. But I distrust the feeling, so I want the translation done properly.

Restate the reductionism versus anti-reductionism debate in the vocabulary I actually use. Map it carefully: the reductionist demand that testimonial warrant trace back to non-testimonial evidence onto what a fact-checker can independently confirm; the anti-reductionist's no-defeater condition onto the editorial judgment that a source may be run absent red flags; the background induction over past testimonial reliability onto the beat reporter's accumulated sense of which labs' press offices exaggerate; the dispute over whether a hearer must possess the track record or merely the world must contain one onto the difference between what I can check on deadline and what my institution's accumulated credibility does for me. Where it helps, use a concrete case: a reporter deciding whether to publish a physics result that exists as one arXiv preprint plus confirmations relayed through five independent press officers. Show me how a committed reductionist and a committed anti-reductionist would each reason about running the story, in my terms.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which parts of the philosophical disagreement map cleanly onto distinctions we draw every day; which parts I have to distort to fit into newsroom terms; and which parts have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary. I have two suspicions I want tested rather than confirmed. The first is that a newsroom is an institution built to first-hand-ify claims — to convert testimony into something checkable — and that this makes my vocabulary structurally blind to the anti-reductionist's strongest point, that almost everything anyone knows, including journalists, arrives uncheckable and stays that way; if that is the residue, it is my vocabulary that is missing something, and I would like to know what. The second is the opposite: that once both sides are forced through my practical cases, the dispute mostly dissolves into a disagreement about the word basic, with no operational difference in when either side would run the story — in which case the philosophical debate is less substantive than it looks, and the newsroom vocabulary is the clearer one. Tell me honestly which suspicion survives contact with the material, and where the translation had to cheat to make either side come out coherent.

Do not soften the philosophy to flatter my experience; if the reductionist's picture of a self-auditing knower is, in my world, a reporter who has never met a deadline, say so — but then explain what the philosophers were actually getting at that my world cannot see.
