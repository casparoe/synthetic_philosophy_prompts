so something happened and now I actually have to know what I'm talking about. quick background: I'm the person in my neighborhood association who won't shut up about policing stuff, and our city council just released a reform task force report that name-drops Camden, NJ like it's a magic word. Council members keep saying we should "study the Camden model." Next month I'm supposed to give the neighborhood take on it, and I've realized I don't actually know what Camden shows, because everyone quotes it for opposite conclusions.

In case you don't have the details memorized, here's what happened, as I understand it. Camden in 2011-2012 was broke, had one of the worst crime rates in the country, and a city police department that laid off ~170 officers when the union contract negotiations stalled. So the city dissolved its entire police department and set up a county-run force that took over in May 2013. The old officers had to reapply (long application, psych testing, interviews), got hired back at lower pay and worse benefits, the union called it straight-up union busting, and the new force actually grew to roughly double the street presence of the old one. New chief came in with a guardian-not-warrior culture thing, use-of-force rules were rewritten, cops introduced themselves door to door. Over the following years crime fell a lot, and now everyone from Obama-era reformers to, recently, people pushing much bigger changes cites Camden as their case.

Here's my confusion. The three camps in our meetings all claim Camden:

1. The reform people say it proves you can fix a department from inside the system — rewrite the rules, change the culture, and it works.
2. The "defund" people say it proves you can actually disband a police department as an institution — the thing everyone says is impossible — and the sky doesn't fall.
3. The abolitionists I know say it proves nothing of the sort, that Camden just swapped one state monopoly on violence for a bigger, cheaper one, and calling that progress is the whole problem.

What I want is a careful analysis of the Camden episode through the reform/defund/abolish debate, in both directions — what the episode shows about the positions, and where the positions distort the episode. Specifically:

- First, get the mapping straight. What actually changed in Camden institutionally? A city monopoly on violence was formally dissolved and immediately reconstituted at the county level with more officers. So is the Camden move an instance of reform, of abolition, of neither, or does the question itself expose that these three labels aren't really on one axis (spend more/spend less vs. who holds the monopoly vs. whether there's a monopoly at all)?
- Second, the part nobody at the meetings wants to touch: the mechanics. Camden's disbanding was driven partly by money — getting out from under a union contract — and it ended with MORE police on the street, paid less. What does it mean that the clearest real-world example of "you can actually abolish a police department" was a union-busting cost-saving move that expanded the force? Does that support the defund claim, embarrass it, or just show that "disband the department" and "shrink the state's capacity for violence" are totally different moves that happen to share a legal mechanism?
- Third, where each camp's Camden story breaks. The reform reading has to explain away that nothing changed until the whole department was legally dissolved — the "fix it from inside" version never happened there. The defund reading has to explain away the doubling of the force. The abolitionist reading has to explain why a city that lived under a much heavier police presence ended up with better outcomes and residents mostly preferring it — or whether that's even the right way to read the outcome.
- Fourth, where the analogy to my city legitimately breaks. Camden was a small, broke, extremely high-crime city where the police force had basically collapsed already. My city is none of those things, our force is fully staffed, and the union contract issue that made disbanding attractive in Camden doesn't exist here. Be honest about how much of the "Camden model" is even portable, and whether the people citing it here are importing a policy or just borrowing its vibes.
- And last, tell me where the whole debate goes silent on this case. What questions does nobody's framework have an answer for here — I'm guessing things like how you'd know whether it was the new rules, the extra bodies, the pay cut, or just the economy and demographics changing, and whether residents' preference for the outcome should settle it or just restates the problem. I'd rather stand up at the meeting and say "here's what Camden shows, here's what it doesn't, and here's what nobody can honestly claim" than pick a team. So give me the version that all three camps would be annoyed by but couldn't actually refute.
