I am putting the final revisions to my third novel, a long historical piece, and my editor has sent me an essay by a critic well known for demolishing precisely the kind of book I have written — not a review of my manuscript, I should say, but a sweeping attack on the conventions of the genre itself, written with unusual verve. Her advice is that I read it before my last pass through the proofs. Her reasoning is economical and, she believes, airtight: being handed information is like being handed a free option. An option you can always decline to exercise; nobody sensible would pay to be deprived of one; therefore reading the essay cannot make me worse off, since I retain at every point the freedom to disregard whatever it says and revise exactly as I would have revised anyway. Foreknowledge, on this picture, is pure upside — a small windfall with no possible downside.

A friend of mine who works in decision theory tells me that my editor's reasoning is not merely folk wisdom but has a pedigree: there is a classical result — he attributes it, I believe, to I. J. Good — showing that for an idealized agent with stable preferences who maximizes expected utility, the expected value of any costless piece of information is non-negative, precisely because the informed agent can always mimic the choice the uninformed agent would have made. So my editor appears to be applying a theorem. And yet the application strikes me as suspicious in a way I cannot quite articulate. When I say I can disregard the essay, that is true of its thesis but not of its prose; the sentences will have done their work on my ear regardless of any resolution I make about my own sentences. And what is at stake in a final revision is not merely my beliefs about the market for my book but my taste itself, which the essay would act upon and perhaps revise, so that the "I" who chooses after reading is not the same "I" who now deliberates about reading. I do not know whether these worries are the load-bearing disanalogies or just my nervousness dressed up as philosophy.

So here is what I would like, and I should say at once that I am not asking for advice about my book. Assume, for the sake of the exercise, that everything the essay says is true and that the critic's only power over me is the power of being right, elegantly. What I want is an audit of the free-option analogy as my editor is deploying it. First, the correspondence table: in her comparison, what exactly plays the role of the option, the exercise decision, the underlying asset, the option holder, and the expiry — and, on the theorem's side, what plays the role of the prior, the signal, the available acts, and the utility function? Second, an explicit statement of the assumptions under which the theorem holds, followed by the places where my situation departs from them — and here I want the departures sorted, with some argument, into those that genuinely matter for her conclusion (reading cannot hurt) and those that are incidental. Third, a verdict: does her conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table — perhaps reading is advisable for some other reason, in which case the analogy was never carrying the conclusion and something else was — or does it fall with the analogy? And finally, if the option picture fails, I would like to know what picture would fit better: what is reading that essay actually like, structurally, if not like acquiring a costless signal that a rational agent is always entitled to want?
