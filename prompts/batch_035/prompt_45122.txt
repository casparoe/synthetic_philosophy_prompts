A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about iterated amplification, and specifically about the step where a task is decomposed and the answers recombined, and we ended it disagreeing about which part of the scheme is actually doing the philosophical work. So let me put the question to you: for someone with, say, two years of conceptual work to spend on the scheme, which open questions matter most, and in what order? The ones we at least managed to name were (a) whether decomposing a task into subtasks can be value-preserving in the sense the scheme needs — whether solving aligned subpieces and aggregating them can lose, or manufacture, values that neither the decomposition nor the aggregation ever explicitly represented; (b) whether amplification actually preserves competence, i.e., whether the decomposition-and-recombination step is guaranteed to be at least as capable as the agent being amplified, or whether some tasks are only solvable by agents that were never assembled from smaller solvers; (c) whether the human whose judgments get amplified needs to be idealized in some way the scheme quietly assumes, and what happens to alignment if the idealization and the actual human come apart; and (d) whether the fixed point of the amplify-distill loop is even well-defined, or whether the process could drift or cycle. I'm less interested in a survey than in a defended ordering: for each question, say why it's upstream of the others, whether it's tractable for a philosopher rather than an ML researcher, and what would have to be true for it to drop off the list. If there's a question neither of us thought of that belongs above all four, I want to know that too, and why.
