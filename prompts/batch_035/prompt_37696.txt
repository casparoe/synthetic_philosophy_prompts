I produce a long-form interview program, and our next guest is a cognitive scientist who has spent her career defending global workspace theory, the idea, associated with Bernard Baars and his successors, that a mental content becomes conscious when it is broadcast widely across the brain rather than processed in a single module. I have no academic training, but I have been reading about this on my own, and what I keep snagging on is the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness: the workspace story seems to explain why a content is available for report, memory, and reasoning, but not why there is anything it feels like to host it, and the guest is known for waving off that gap as a question for another day. Draft exactly five numbered interview questions, each one or two sentences long, that make the wave-off progressively harder to sustain — for instance by pressing on whether the neural ignition that makes a stimulus global adds anything phenomenally, or on what she would say about reports of vivid experience in patients whose damage supposedly removed the broadcasting hub, or on whether any experimental result could make her give up the theory rather than merely redescribe it. No introduction or conclusion; just the five questions.
