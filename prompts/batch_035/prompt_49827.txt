here's the question, written by the instructor:

"6. (20 points) The Overhead Case. Suppose an artificial system — of a kind for which the question of moral status is at least open — is audited, and the auditors find that a large fraction of its internal activity is inert: perturb it, silence it, or route around it, and nothing changes anywhere downstream — not the outputs, not their timing, not the subsequent evolution of the rest of the internal state. Call the remaining activity load-bearing. A regulator proposes that if a system of this kind ever merits protections, they should attach to the load-bearing activity only. (a) State the principle about the relationship between computation and moral status that the regulator needs for this to be anything more than an accounting convenience, and explain what it commits her to. (b) Present the strongest objection to that principle, and assess it fairly. (c) Say which you accept and why."

and here's the student's answer, copied word for word:

"The regulator's principle has to be something like: moral status attaches to the computations that make a difference, not to computations that merely occur. Otherwise the proposal is just bookkeeping — convenient for the auditors, but with no moral content. I take 'load-bearing' in the counterfactual sense, like a load-bearing wall: take it out and something changes; take out the inert activity and nothing changes. So the principle is basically 'no difference made, no status conferred.' The inert activity is like a guy standing in the back of the factory not touching anything: the product doesn't depend on him, we wouldn't pay him, and no moral status flows to the overhead either.

The strongest objection is the one from behavior never settling anything. A giant lookup table that produced the same responses as a caring person wouldn't be caring — we covered this in lecture — so outputs can't be where status lives. Someone could say the same about the inert activity: maybe it matters in a way that never shows up downstream, and the auditors just can't see it. But that objection can't be checked, and Turing already showed that behavior is all we ever have to go on with other minds, so an objection that can't be checked loses to a proposal that at least tracks something we can measure. And anyway, if the inert activity were doing something, changing it would change something, and by hypothesis it doesn't.

So for (c) I accept the proposal, with one amendment: keep re-running the audit, because activity that's inert today could become load-bearing later, and the protections should follow the load."
