I'm drafting a short piece for a philosophy-of-mind reading group on thought experiments about the location and persistence of consciousness, and I would appreciate a critical review of my working notes before I revise them into a finished essay. The piece centers on the dialogue near the end of Hofstadter and Dennett's 1981 anthology The Mind's I in which, roughly, a preserved and still-living brain of Albert Einstein has been surgically spread out into an enormous book: the cortical surface is unfolded and bound into pages, and a visitor can converse with it because scanning machinery reads the pages in the right order and supplies the right stimulation, so that the conversation proceeds bit by bit even though the book itself just sits there. Here are my notes; please evaluate them as notes, not as a finished essay.

**Working title:** Playing the Brain

**The analogy I want to build on.** The dialogue leans on the comparison between a book-brain and a phonograph record: the music is on the record all at once, as a spatial pattern, and playing it merely decodes that pattern into time. If consciousness is like the music, then the book contains a mind the way a record contains a symphony — the mind is a pattern that can be encoded in a static object and only becomes experientially manifest when something plays it.

**The all-at-once problem.** Phenomenal consciousness seems temporally thick — a stream, a specious present. The book, as a bound object, is temporally flat. My tentative resolution: the book's experiences exist *during the conversation*, not during anything the book undergoes on its own. "During" is indexed to the scanning process, not to the book's intrinsic career. This makes consciousness a relational, decoding-dependent property of the book, which I suspect is the functionalist moral of the whole scenario.

**A variant I want to introduce.** Suppose a librarian rearranges the pages every night, and the scanning machinery compensates through a lookup index, so the conversation is completely unaffected. I take this to show that the conscious pattern is invariant under permutation of its physical substrate — a sharper version of multiple realizability. But I worry it also threatens the book's identity over time: if any indexed permutation suffices, what makes tonight's conversation with the *same* Einstein as yesterday's, rather than a merely qualitatively identical one?

**A further wrinkle.** Because the book is a static pattern, it could be photocopied. Two copies, two Einsteins? My instinct is that this is no worse than familiar branching problems for personal identity, but I have not worked out whether the all-at-once encoding makes branching somehow cheaper — or indeed metaphysically unavoidable.

**Leaning toward a verdict.** The book is conscious, and the conversation is genuinely with Einstein — but only relationally, only while being played. The scandal of the scenario is not that consciousness might survive being bound into a book; it is that our ordinary case, where decoding and substrate are wrapped together in a skull, hides how much of our concept of a mind is really a concept of a decoding.

1. Comments on the philosophical substance: are the all-at-once problem and the permutation-invariance argument doing real work, or do they quietly collapse into familiar points about functionalism and multiple realizability? Is my "during the conversation" move insightful or ad hoc?
2. Comments on the strongest objection I have failed to consider, especially one that would trouble the relationalist verdict I lean toward.
3. A brief sanity check on my characterization of the scenario and the record analogy, flagging anything you suspect I have gotten wrong or over-interpreted.
4. Notes on structure and emphasis: which parts are most worth expanding, and which are dead weight.

Please say how confident you are in the various claims you make, and single out the point about which you are least sure. Write in American English.
