I'm TA-ing a section for an intermediate phil course on paradoxes and rationality, and next week is my session on the surprise exam paradox. It's about 20 students, 75 minutes, in a room with movable desks, and they've already seen backward induction once this semester (centipede game, briefly). What I want is an activity where they run into the paradox themselves before I name it — the idea being: the announcement says there will be a quiz next week and you won't know which day, and then the students' own reasoning in the game ends up doing the thing the paradoxical argument does (walking backwards from Friday and eliminating every day), while the actual outcome keeps surprising them anyway. I'd rather they build the elimination argument with their own hands than watch me do it on the board.

Here's the rough shape I was imagining, but feel free to overhaul it: the "week" is compressed into 5 or 6 rounds, maybe 5 minutes each, and in each round every student privately commits to a guess about whether the quiz happens in that round (or guesses the exact round up front — you tell me which works better, and whether they should be allowed to revise between rounds). I'm the teacher and I've sealed my quiz round in an envelope, or committed to it via some verifiable method, I don't care about the details as long as they can see I'm not cheating. Some kind of token or point system for correct predictions vs. being quizzed unawares, no real money obviously, and ideally the payoffs are set up so that the "logic" says one thing (never guess, or always guess round 1?) but actually guessing feels insane round by round. If there's a way to have a second phase where a subgroup plays perfectly-rational-robot style according to the elimination argument and gets destroyed by it, I'd love that, but don't force it if it's clunky.

For the debrief, I want them to locate exactly where their own elimination reasoning broke down in the game — was it the knowledge assumption, the vagueness of "you won't know," something else — and I want an honest note on which part of the real paradox a 75-minute classroom game just cannot reproduce (e.g., whatever it is about genuinely idealized reasoners that makes the argument feel airtight on paper). Also please tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm guessing some students will try to game the prediction mechanic itself, or the envelope thing will make them distrust me — and how to handle each. Please structure your response as: (1) Setup and rules, about half a page; (2) Round-by-round flow with the payoff scheme, half a page to a page; (3) Debrief plan, half a page; (4) What will go wrong and fixes, a short paragraph or list; (5) What the game can't reproduce, a short paragraph. No lecture notes needed, they get the reading after.
