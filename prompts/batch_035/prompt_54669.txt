I'm TA-ing a first-year ethics course and my section has 16 students, 75 minutes. This week the reading is on moral testimony -- the idea that taking someone's word for moral stuff (vs. just asking them to give you reasons) is somehow defective, in a way it isn't for weather forecasts or medical diagnoses. My professor says the reading always slides right off them, and honestly when I was an undergrad it slid off me too. So: design me an activity where they run into the problem before I name it, no lecture up front.

Here's the shape I was imagining, but feel free to improve it: students get paired, and one student in each pair recieves a sealed "verdict card" from a made-up ethics consultation bureau about a concrete case (e.g. whether to tell a friend her fiance is cheating, or whether to renege on a club commitment to take a better one). The card gives only the verdict, no reasons -- the bureau is "paid by the verdict" or whatever fiction makes the setup natural. The other student gets the full case description and isn't allowed to say what's in it. The verdict-holder has to decide whether to act on the verdict alone, then they compare notes and find out how far apart they were. Then maybe a second round where the "bureau" offers reasons on request, but only at a cost, so they feel the pull of outsourcing vs. demanding reasons.

What I want at the end: a debrief that maps the moments in the game onto the actual issue -- the asymmetry between deferring on facts and deferring on morals, and the difference between using someone as a proxy and using them as a pointer to reasons. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (my gut says some students will just treat the verdict as a coin flip and not feel any discomfort at all), and be honest about which part of the moral testimony debate an activity like this just can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief. Keep the whole thing runnable by one stressed TA with a printed stack of cards and no budget.
