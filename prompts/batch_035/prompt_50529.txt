I'm on the volunteer parks committee in my small town (pop ~40k), and we have a fight going about the summer mosquito program. If we pay for aerial spraying, basically everyone who uses the parks gets relief from bites — call it 30,000 people who'd each be spared some itchy evenings, nothing worse than that. But we know of maybe 20-25 people in town with serious chemical sensitivities, including one kid who's ended up in the ER before after exposures, for whom the spray would cause real weeks-long health problems, and there are also some beekeepers worried about their hives. If we don't spray, the 20-25 are fine and everyone else grumbles about mosquitoes. We vote on this next week.

My instinct says don't spray, but a friend on the committee thinks that's nuts. His argument: 30,000 vs 20, and the bites are real even if each one is small — "you're just letting a bigger number lose to a smaller number because the smaller number's problem sounds more dramatic." He says if you add up all the itchy evenings and compare it to a few bad weeks, spraying wins easily, and refusing to do the addition is just squeamishness. Something about the way he just totals it up bothers me, but I can't put my finger on what. So my question is: when a choice gives a small benefit to a huge number of people but does bigger harm to a few, is there a principled way to think about it, or is it really just whoever's side has the bigger total? And if it's not just the totals, whose situation should we actually be weighing — the worst-off person's, the average person's, something else? I'd appreciate a way of deciding this that doesn't come down to whoever talks loudest at the meeting.
