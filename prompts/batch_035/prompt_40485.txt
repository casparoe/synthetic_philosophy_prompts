I'm a PhD student in social psychology, and my dissertation argues from longitudinal well-being data to policy prescriptions: which working conditions employers ought to provide, which childcare arrangements parents ought to prefer. At an interdisciplinary workshop last month, a philosopher objected that my whole inference pattern had been refuted long ago, and on reading up on it I've come to think he may be right. As I now understand the dialectic, Hume demonstrated that no set of purely descriptive premises entails any normative conclusion, and Moore's open question argument proved the same thing by a different route: since it always remains an open question whether the natural property you've picked out is good, any attempt to define goodness in natural terms commits the naturalistic fallacy. Taken together, these look like two proofs of a single result — that no amount of empirical information about how people fare, what they prefer, or what their brains are doing can so much as support a normative conclusion, let alone entail one. If that's right, my project isn't merely incomplete but in principle misconceived, since no descriptive finding could do any normative work without committing the very fallacy Moore identified.

So, three questions. First, is this characterization of Hume and Moore accurate, or do the is-ought gap and the naturalistic fallacy target different moves — one logical, one perhaps semantic — such that my inference might traverse the terrain without violating either? Second, if the gap is real, what exactly does it forbid: does it show that facts are evidentially irrelevant to what ought to be done, or only that some normative premise must be admitted before the facts can get a grip? Third, if the objection succeeds against directly identifying well-being with goodness, is some form of non-naturalism about value the only consistent fallback, leaving an empirical moral psychology either eliminativist or bankrupt? Please distinguish what is settled among metaethicists from what is genuinely contested — for instance, whether the open question argument really survives the reply that water turned out to be identical to H2O without being analytically definable as such — and flag any point where you'd be reporting a minority position rather than a consensus.
