After eight years at a design consultancy, I've just been promoted into the role that decides how our annual bonus pool is divided among the eleven people on my team. This is the first time I've had to allocate rewards rather than receive them, and I'm finding it much harder than the work itself. Two of my designers stand out this year, but for different reasons. The first — call her M. — produced by far the most impressive client work. She also, however, walked in with advantages: educated at a famous school entirely on her parents' money, and, more to the point, assigned to our most prestigious account purely by scheduling accident, the year that account happened to blow up in a lucrative way. The second, T., produced good but unremarkable work, but he came to design late, taught himself the tools while working warehouse shifts, and I know exactly how much more effort each polished deliverable costs him — the late nights spent catching up on fundamentals that M. absorbed as a teenager at the kitchen table. My director says the answer is obvious: we're a meritocracy, we reward performance, full stop. But this seems to me to just push the question back a step, since M.'s performance is partly the luck of her assignment, and her polished "merit" is partly the luck of who got trained where.

So I have two questions I'd like a serious answer to, not a management-seminar answer. First: is there a principled way to think about what exactly a meritocracy is supposed to be rewarding — the outcomes people produce, the effort they expend, the abilities they've developed, or the use they make of whatever raw materials they were handed? In practice these come apart constantly, and I'd like to know what the main positions are, where the strongest arguments for each lie, and which of them could survive being defended to M. and T. in the same meeting. Second, and this is the part I can't articulate well: I keep feeling a difference between what our announced compensation system owes people and what people actually deserve in some deeper sense. M. is plainly entitled to a large bonus under any system we publicly committed to, but I'm much less sure she deserves it, if that distinction is even coherent rather than sentimental. Is it? And if desert and entitlement really can come apart like this, which one should an organization like mine be in the business of tracking — and what does it owe to the person on the losing end of whichever answer I pick?
