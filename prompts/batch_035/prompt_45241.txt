Next month my film class is doing Do the Right Thing, and I want to teach it through the old Du Bois–Locke fight about what Black art is actually for. Quick sketch of the fight as I understand it: in his 1926 Crisis essay, Criteria of Negro Art, Du Bois basically said all art is propaganda and always has been — the only real question is which side it's propaganda for — and he was openly suspicious of Black writing that exists mainly to entertain white readers. Locke, pushing back around the same time, wanted the new Black art treated as art, judged by aesthetic standards, not loaded down with the job of representing the whole race. Now the film. Lee made it in the shadow of real racial incidents in New York in the 80s, and when it came out some mainstream white critics fretted it would literally cause riots on opening weekend — which is the propaganda fear, aimed straight at him. So the assignment: read the film as a test case, and say whether it vindicates Du Bois or Locke (or neither).

A few things I want the reading to actually take a stand on, not hand-wave. The direct-address montage where characters of each ethnicity reel off slurs straight into the camera — is that propaganda, art, or both at once, and can the Du Bois/Locke vocabulary even seperate those? Then Mookie throwing the trash can through Sal's window at the climax: the film famously refuses to say whether he did the right thing, which looks like the opposite of propaganda in Du Bois's sense — but is withholding the verdict itself a move in the propaganda game, since the audience you trust to fill in the blank is exactly the audience Du Bois said the art was for? And the two title cards at the end, one from Malcolm and one from King, both about violence — Lee puts them next to each other and refuses to rank them, same trick again.

Also be honest where the film resists being read by either man, don't force it into an illustration. But the part I'm most curious about is this: what does the film show about the 1926 debate that the essays couldn't see? My hunch is it's the double-audience problem — a movie is one artifact shown simultaneously to two audiences with opposite interests, so the same scene lands as a threat for one and a vindication for the other, and the filmmaker knows it. Is that idea already latent in Du Bois (he did go on about writing for white eyes), or is it something only a mass medium like film could discover? If the reading ends by saying what the film adds to that debate that argument alone can't, you've earned your keep. Keep it aimed at a bright undergrad audience — they know the film beat by beat but nothing about the Harlem Renaissance fight, so give them just enough of Du Bois and Locke to work with.
