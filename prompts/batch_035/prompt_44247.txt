For roughly two decades, epistemology has hosted a standoff over bootstrapping and easy knowledge, and as far as I can tell the principals have stopped moving. The shape of the dispute, briefly: one camp — reliabilists, and dogmatists about perceptual justification in the vein of Pryor — holds that you can acquire justified beliefs from a source without any antecedent justified belief in that source's reliability; otherwise skepticism wins, since vindicating perception would itself have to run through perception. The other camp, heirs to Fumerton's demand that justification not be circular, replies with a track-record argument: given the first camp's liberal picture, a thinker can start from particular perceptual beliefs and, one at a time, reason her way to the conclusion that her vision is reliable — a bit of inference that appears to secure a foundational epistemological result on the cheap, by consulting a source almost exclusively about itself. The replies on offer include: declaring the track-record reasoning rational after all and treating the intuition against it as noise; imposing a rule that a source's verdicts about its own reliability require independent support; restricting the ban to premises the reasoner is actively relying on in the very inference; and diagnosing the anti-bootstrapping intuition as an assimilation to cases where the source is genuinely questionable. I do not want another entry in this exchange. I want a diagnosis of it. My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the disagreement is not verbal — both sides use 'justified' for the same normative status — but it may turn on two different questions travelling under a single heading: a third-person question about which belief-forming methods are good, and a first-person question about what a thinker is entitled to conclude while she is in the middle of using a method. Test that hypothesis against the positions as I have described them; consider the alternatives (a hidden disagreement about the epistemic force of first-person evidence, or simply bedrock intuitions about cases that no argument reaches); and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a new case that pries the two questions apart, a formal model of entitlement, a change of subject — or whether the impasse should be expected to persist, and why. Please adjudicate the structure of the disagreement, not its substance: I am not asking who is right about perception.
