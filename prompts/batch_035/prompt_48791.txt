I am writing my Extended Project Qualification on Moore's paradox — the puzzle that a first-person, present-tense sentence such as "It is raining, but I do not believe that it is" sounds absurd to utter, even though it might perfectly well be true. I have learned that philosophers distinguish an omissive version ("p, but I do not believe that p") from a commissive version ("p, but I believe that not-p"), and that the absurdity vanishes if you shift to the past tense or to the second or third person.

My supervisor has suggested that the best test of whether I really understand the standard explanations would be to commission a counter-case, and I would like your help constructing it. The assignment is this: build a scenario in which a speaker sincerely, appropriately, and without any hint of absurdity utters the omissive Moorean sentence about themselves, in the first person and the present tense throughout. The scenario must obey the following constraints, which are meant to block the known escape routes. First, the utterance must not occur in fiction, on stage, in quotation marks, or as a report of what someone else has said. Second, the speaker must not be lying, acting, self-deceived, or otherwise mistaken about their own beliefs. Third, you may not rescue the sentence by appending something like "I know it is", so that "believe" gets heard as "merely believe". Fourth, the setting should be an ordinary workplace, with no clever technology, and the whole scenario should be describable in one paragraph.

After the scenario, please demonstrate that it meets the specifications. Walk through what each of three explanations of the paradox would predict about the case, and why: (1) the view that asserting that p somehow implies or expresses believing that p, so that the second conjunct clashes with what the first conjunct has already conveyed; (2) Hintikka's view that the trouble is doxastic inconsistency — a rational speaker who believes that p is in a position to believe that they believe that p, so believing p whilst believing that one does not believe p is jointly untenable; and (3) the broadly Wittgensteinian thought that "I believe that it is raining" is not really a report on one's mind at all but rather a way of asserting that it is raining, so the sentence pulls in two directions at the level of what is said. For each explanation, state whether it still predicts absurdity for your case, and identify exactly where its prediction goes wrong or holds.

Then finish with three things: which single feature of the case is doing the work; what happens to the utterance if that feature is removed — does the case collapse back into the ordinary paradox; and how a defender of whichever explanation comes out worst would attack the case. To be clear about what the case is for, and hence what counts as cheating: the point is to probe whether the absurdity of the Moorean sentence is a constitutive norm of assertion or merely a defeasible conversational expectation, so a case that simply stipulates that the sentence sounds fine, rather than generating that result from its details, would be worthless. My supervisor warns that the best-designed cases of this kind are exactly the ones defenders of the standard explanations will want to explain away, so please be explicit about the point at which your construction is most vulnerable.
