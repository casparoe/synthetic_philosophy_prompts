My oldest friend and I have spent thirty years arguing about how positions, prizes, and duties ought to be assigned, and over that time she has issued a running series of verdicts, usually with great confidence and never with an appeal outstanding. Before I put a new question to her, I would like your help in predicting the answer, and I should warn you that her track record is easier to admire than to summarise. On Parliament, she has said more than once that the Commons would improve if its members were drawn by lot as juries are, elections being, in her phrase, a way of choosing people who are good at being chosen. On juries themselves she will not hear a word against trial by lot; it is the one part of the legal system she defends without qualification. When our city orchestra had a vacancy in the cello section, she badgered them into holding the audition behind a screen, with no names on the music, and regarded the result as vindication. On ordinary hiring she is scathing: an unstructured interview, she says, measures the applicant's polish and the interviewer's mood, and a coin tossed between the shortlisted candidates would be both more honest and about as accurate. Yet when someone proposed that the referee for the cup final be drawn by lot, she dismissed the idea within a sentence: one wants the best official available, and ranking officials is something we actually know how to do. When our allotment society had more applicants than plots, she insisted on a public draw and was content with it even though her own name did not come out. And when our residents' committee was filled simply from those who volunteered, she objected that a dozen retired professionals had appointed themselves to speak for the street, and that a draw from all the households would have given us the street rather than the golf club.

The new case is this. The district council is convening a citizens' panel of roughly a hundred residents to hear evidence on housing and offer advice. The organisers do not propose interviews, nor yet a simple draw: they propose a stratified one, in which names are drawn at random but the draw is engineered so that the panel's mix of ages, sexes, tenures, and neighbourhoods mirrors the district — which means that, to meet the quotas, some residents, young renters most likely, must each stand a rather better chance of being drawn than a randomly chosen retired homeowner would. I want to ring her about it beforehand, and I should like to know whether to expect delight or a tirade.

So please do three things for me. Set out the candidate rules that could be governing her verdicts, saying what each one predicts for the stratified panel; give your best guess at her reaction, with your reasons for preferring that guess; and tell me what further case, if I were to put it to her, would most cleanly separate the rules that currently fit her record equally well. I am less interested in the final answer than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her verdicts just as well, and which case would best tell them apart. Please write in British English, in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the sort of thing one might send as a letter.
