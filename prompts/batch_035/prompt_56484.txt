For background reading before a tutorial on Lewis's account of convention, our course pack included a chapter from a recent popular book on game theory in social life, and I want to check whether it can be trusted. The chapter summarizes Lewis as follows: Lewis analysed conventions as solutions to coordination problems — a convention is simply whatever regularity of behaviour lets a community solve one, so wherever there is no coordination problem there can be no conventions. Because conventions are held in place by nothing more than first-order expectations (each person conforms only because she expects the others to conform), no convention ever needs enforcement, and indeed no convention could intelligibly be sanctioned, since punishment would be pointless where deviation is already penalised by failed coordination. Lewis also argued that conventions are arbitrary in the strong sense that no convention is better than any of its rivals: since the community would have coordinated just as well on an alternative regularity, no one can ever have reason to criticise a convention from outside it. And because language is for Lewis the paradigm convention, he concluded that linguistic meaning is nothing over and above regularity in the production of sounds and marks.

My tutor mentioned in passing that Lewis builds an infinite hierarchy of mutual expectations into the definition, and that he explicitly discusses conventions backed by sanctions, so she suspects the chapter is seriously misleading — but the book is written by someone with a professional background in the field, and I can't tell which of her worries are supposed to attach to which sentence, or whether some of the claims are defensible simplifications of the kind popular books are licensed to make.

Could you audit the summary claim by claim? For each element, tell me which parts a careful defender of Lewis would accept, which are simplifications a popularizer might reasonably make, and which contradict things Lewis explicitly says or builds into his definition. Please be specific about what the definition actually requires — for instance, what role the preference structure and the availability of an alternative regularity play, and how common knowledge is generated — rather than just saying the summary is unfair. Then there is a second question: the chapter closes by claiming that since conventions are arbitrary in the strong sense and sanction-free, the sense in which one ought to keep to a convention cannot be explained by the convention itself, so the 'ought' must be either prudential or imposed from outside — and that our practice of criticising people for breaching conventions is therefore confused. Does that conclusion follow from the accurate parts of the summary, or does it trade on one of the inaccuracies? Please answer in British English.
