I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and this spring I'm also guest-teaching a seminar at a journalism school on how to cover the deep past — basically a course about writing on events that unfold over centuries, where the temptation to reach for dramatic words is huge. We're currently on a unit about "collapse," because I've become convinced the word does a lot of unexamined work in popular coverage. For their take-home essay I gave the class this question:

"'Collapse' shows up everywhere in writing about the past: the Late Bronze Age collapse, the Classic Maya collapse, the supposed collapse of Easter Island. Choose at least two of these cases and answer the following. First, state concretely what was lost in each case and what carried on. Second, say whether 'collapse' names one kind of event — a society failing — or whether the cases differ so much that a single word papers over the differences. Third, say whether any of your cases is better described as transformation than as failure. Defend your verdicts with specifics, not vibes."

One of the students turned in the answer pasted below, and I want to grade it properly, point by point, instead of just going on general impression — I definately don't want to reward length or confidence. Please give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. The first item should state a letter grade together with a one-sentence case for it. Each of the remaining four should take one specific claim or move in the student's answer — name it clearly — and say exactly what's right or wrong about it. If the answer mixes real strengths with real problems, I'd want the four items to reflect that mix.

The student wrote: "These cases are usually lumped together, but they aren't the same kind of event, and once you look closely, 'collapse' turns out to be partly a judgment call rather than a neutral description. The Late Bronze Age is the clearest case of a system failing rather than a people. Around 1200 BCE the palace economies of the eastern Mediterranean — the Mycenaeans, the Hittites, Ugarit — went down within a few decades of each other, taking the international tin and copper trade with them. What was lost was the administrative apparatus: Linear B writing disappears, and the Hittite capital was destroyed. But the Greek speakers were still there; the language carried on and the villages were reoccupied, so this is the failure of a system, not of a society. The Maya look similar but are really the opposite. The Classic collapse after 900 CE finished Maya civilization as a going concern: the great southern cities like Tikal stopped putting up monuments and emptied out, and the collapse then rolled north, so Chichén Itzá and the other northern cities were abandoned in the same wave. What survived, the calendar reckoning and the oral tradition, survived in the villages, and the loss of writing and of the royal cult makes this closer to a genuine civilizational failure than to mere change. Easter Island is the cleanest case and shows the pattern can be entirely self-inflicted. The Rapa Nui cleared the native palm forest for farmland and to help transport the moai, and the population crashed from perhaps twenty thousand to a few thousand before a single European arrived in 1722, so the society had already undone itself with no outside help. Across all three cases, then, the driving cause was internal, and what differs is only the speed and how much carried on. On failure versus change: the Bronze Age is transformation, the Maya and Easter Island are failure."
