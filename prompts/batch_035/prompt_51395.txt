You are a philosopher of biology with a deep working knowledge of the levels-of-selection literature, from the early group-selection controversy through the Price-equation treatments of the past few decades. Please think carefully before answering: start by brainstorming the possible diagnoses of the disagreement I describe, and only then commit to the one you think best supported. Do not produce a survey of positions for its own sake; every part of your answer should serve the diagnosis. Bear in mind I have no way of checking citations, so please do not lean on bibliographic detail — I mention a few works only to make the shape of the debate concrete.

Here is the situation. The units-of-selection debate has, on my telling, been running since roughly 1970 in recognisably the same form: one side holds that the gene is the only unit of selection in any sense that matters, because genes are the replicators and everything else — organisms, groups, populations — is a vehicle that they build and discard; the other side holds that selection can act at several levels, and that between-group selection, when it opposes within-group selection, is a real causal process that our theories ought to represent explicitly rather than fold away. What strikes me is that the debate has survived what looked like a decisive intervention: a family of results showing that the two camps' formal frameworks are inter-translatable. Given the Price equation, or a partition of total selection into within- and between-group components, a gene-centric accounting and a group-selection accounting of the same population change are just different bookkeepings of one quantity. Various authors have drawn the moral that the disagreement is merely verbal — that the sensible move is pluralism about accounting schemes, choosing whichever partition makes the biology easiest to see in a given case. And yet the fighting has continued, sometimes with real heat, over whether inclusive-fitness methods should be preferred wholesale, whether group selection is a rare curiosity or a major force, and what a proper theory of social evolution must look like. So the formal equivalence, which should have ended the matter, has not; if anything the debate has shifted onto the question of which framework deserves to be the discipline's default, and there it stalls, with both sides talking past each other about rigour, generality, and tractability.

My working hypothesis is this: the disagreement stopped being about what happens in populations — the empirical and formal questions are largely settled or settling — and became a disagreement about what a scientific theory is for, namely whether a theory of evolution should track the causal path of the heritable material, in which case the gene-level description is privileged in principle, or should identify the level at which the selection process makes a difference to the outcome, in which case the causal story is genuinely multi-level and the gene-level description is one projection among several. On that reading, the two camps are answering different questions under one heading, and the heat comes from neither side admitting it. But I am not confident, and I can see at least two rival diagnoses worth taking seriously: first, that there is a hidden methodological disagreement about what should count as an explanation in evolutionary biology, such that one side requires a mechanistic, individual-level account and the other accepts population-level, statistical accounts, and the equivalence results cannot touch this because they say nothing about explanation, only about prediction; and second, that the debate is sustained less by philosophy than by sociology — by entrenched textbook traditions, training, and the reputational stakes of older public quarrels, so that there is no residual substantive question at all and the field is waiting for a generational change rather than an argument.

Please do three things. Second, assess my hypothesis and the two rivals against each other; tell me which best fits the fact that the equivalence results produced pluralism in principle but not in practice, and say frankly if none of them does. Third, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a class of empirical cases, a change in how the frameworks are taught — and whether I should expect any such progress, or whether the honest answer is that this disagreement will persist because the parties' standards for what would settle it are themselves part of what is disputed. Please be specific about the mechanisms of the impasse rather than lamenting it.
