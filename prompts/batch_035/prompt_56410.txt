I help run a monthly rationality meetup — about 18 regulars, most of whom have been reading LessWrong and the Scott Alexander blogosphere for years. They can already recite Schelling on focal points and give the standard summary of Lewis's Convention, so I don't want yet another activity that just re-illustrates that conventions solve coordination problems. What I want is an activity that makes them feel the two harder facts: that a convention is held together by nothing but mutual expectations (I keep conforming because I expect you to conform, and you conform because you expect me to), and that an entrenched convention can keep winning even after it becomes strictly worse for everyone than an available alternative.

We have one 90-minute session, movable furniture, whiteboards, and no appetite for real-money stakes. Design a game with roughly this arc, filling in the details: repeated play with rotating partners out of which some convention emerges and entrenches itself without anyone announcing it; then a payoff shift that makes the entrenched convention dominated; then a displacement phase, in which a few participants are quietly assigned norm-entrepreneur roles with limited and costly moves for seeding the alternative, and a wave of newcomers joins who never saw the original coordination problem and only ever see the inherited convention. Give me the rules, materials, a point- or token-based payoff scheme, and a minute-by-minute timeline. Then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the formal machinery — the recurring coordination problem, salience, the conformity condition, and the asymmetry between how little it takes to create a convention and how much coordinated expectation-shifting it takes to replace one. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of Lewis's account no one-shot classroom game can reproduce and I'll have to supply by hand.
