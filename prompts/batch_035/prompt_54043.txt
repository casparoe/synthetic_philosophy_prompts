i'm a hospitalist, and there's a recurring thing that happens on rounds: families ask whether to make a call now or wait. start the iv antibiotics tonight or hold for culture results and the id consult on monday. sign the dnr now or wait until after the chaplain visit. transfer to hospice this week or let the trial of therapy play out first. i once went to a talk where the speaker mentioned a principle from philosophy called Reflection, roughly: if you're certain today that on monday you'll believe X to degree p, then today you should already believe X to degree p. on that view, deferring to your future self should never change what you think, only when you act on it — which makes "let's wait and see" either rational (new information is coming) or a disguised way of doing nothing.

the catch is the speaker also said Reflection has known counterexamples, cases where you expect your future self to be compromised — drunk, sleep-deprived, in love, forgetting things. that maps onto medicine uncomfortably well: the family meeting goes sideways, the night on call scrambles everyone, or the patient who survives to day 3 changes what we're willing to say out loud. so deferring isn't automatically honest.

so here's the ask: turn this into a procedure i can actually run at the bedside in about five minutes with a family in the room. i'm a resident, so anything big needs the attending to co-sign, and the stakes are usually irreversible either way (starting treatment you can't walk back, or delaying past the point where it helps). give me the questions in order, what counts as enough of an answer to each, the points where the procedure outputs "decide now," the points where it outputs "defer," and — this is the part i care about most — where it has to stop and admit the question is one of values (how much suffering to trade for how many days) rather than facts, and hand it back to the patient instead of pretending the philosophy settles it.

then run it on three cases: (1) start broad-spectrum antibiotics now versus wait 48 hours for cultures in a clinically stable patient, (2) defer the dnr conversation until the estranged son flies in for the weekend, and (3) one case where the procedure gives the answer i wont like — namely that my "let's wait" was just me avoiding a hard conversation, and Reflection calls it. and be honest about which steps of the procedure are judgment calls wearing a lab coat.
