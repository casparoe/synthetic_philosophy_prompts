I'm a novelist, about three quarters through a book where my narrator's mother is a physical chemist who is dying, and who tells her daughter that being alive was never anything more than being a fire — a slow, complicated flame — so there's nothing death takes from her that the flame going out doesn't take from a candle. The daughter half beleives this, and the last chapter hinges on whether she should. My beta readers are split on whether the mother is supposed to be wise or just evading, and honestly I wrote her position by gut, so now I want to actually know what the flame analogy can carry before I lock the ending.

Here's the correspondence as I understand it: feeding is fuel, metabolism is combustion, the body is a flame front that holds its shape while matter flows through it, death is the flame going out. Even the whole thing where the matter isn't the same over time and only the process is — the river you can't step into twice — seems to map fine. What I can't figure out is where it actually breaks. A candle lighting another candle looks like reproduction, and yet everyone says fire isn't alive, and I can't tell whether that's a real disanalogy or biologists just being territorial. Same with the borderline cases I keep circling in the book — a dry seed seems more fire-adjacent (dormant, just a stored pattern waiting for fuel) than an animal does, and viruses seem like they only get to be alive by borrowing machinery, the way a flame borrows whatever it's burning. If the mother's claim is supposed to survive contact with viruses and seeds, I need to know whether it does.

So, what I'm asking for: lay out the full mapping first, then the places it breaks, then tell me which of the breaks actually matter for the mother's claim — that there's no principled line between a living thing and a fire, so death isn't a special kind of loss, just an extinguishing. I know there are real definitions of life people use (the NASA-flavored Darwinian evolution one, self-organization ones, metabolism-first ones) and I'd like to know what each does to the flame picture — which definition the mother could use and which ones quietly defeat her. Also: is there a better analogy I could put in her mouth, one that's more honest about heredity and information but still gives her the deflation she wants — or does every honest analogy take the deflation away from her? And does the candle-lighting-another-candle case show that reproduction, as a criterion, isn't doing real work, or the opposite?

Please end with a single line of the form: Verdict: [one sentence saying whether the flame analogy can carry the mother's conclusion, and if not, what kind of analogy could].
