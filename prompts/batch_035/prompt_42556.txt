My partner and I have been circling a dispute for weeks, and rather than keep trading intuitions, I want to see whether a careful decomposition can locate where the disagreement actually lives. Here is the proposition: a nation, considered as a nation rather than as a collection of individuals, can bear guilt for atrocities committed by earlier generations of its citizens, in circumstances where no perpetrator is still alive, no current citizen causally contributed to the wrong, and the state's present institutions are at least formally continuous with those under which the wrong occurred. My credence in that proposition is around 0.35; hers is around 0.7.

Please give me your own number, but the number is the least of what I want. What I want is the decomposition. Some terms I can already see, though you should add, subtract, or restructure as you see fit: (1) the probability that collectives can be genuine subjects of guilt at all, rather than guilt being attributable only to individuals with metaphorical or shorthand talk about nations; (2) given that collectives can be such subjects, the probability that a nation persists as the same morally accountable agent across generational turnover, major constitutional ruptures, and border changes; (3) the probability that guilt, as opposed to responsibility, obligation, shame, or the duty of remembrance, is the right category for anything a present population could stand in — that is, whether guilt constitutively requires some form of voluntary involvement in the wrong; (4) the probability that a distinctively non-individual category of guilt, akin to what Jaspers called metaphysical guilt, is coherent rather than an honorific that trades on guilt's rhetorical force while abandoning its conceptual core; and (5) the probability that reparations and acknowledgment obligations are grounded in inherited collective guilt specifically, rather than in some other relation, in which case the practical stakes of the question might come apart from its conceptual stakes.

Assign a rough probability to each term with one line of justification, then combine them into your final number, being explicit about how the combination works and about any interdependencies you are smuggling in between terms. Finally, two diagnostic questions: which single term is your final number most sensitive to, and — the part I actually care about most — given the two of us at 0.35 and 0.7, which of those sub-questions is the likeliest site of our disagreement, and what argument or scenario would you put to each of us to find out?
