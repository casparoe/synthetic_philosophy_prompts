I am a PhD student writing in distributive ethics, and I've been asked to circulate a short position statement before our department's workshop on theories of justice. Before I defend any of this in person, I want a consistency audit of the package, because I have a suspicion that at least one pair of commitments doesn't survive contact, though I genuinely cannot tell which.

Here is the package:

(1) I am a prioritarian: the moral value of a benefit to a person depends on how well off that person is, and benefits to the worse off count for more. Moreover, I take the moral weight of a benefit to be a function of the recipient's absolute level of well-being alone — the "absolute priority" reading, where the weight does not vary with how many other people there are or where they stand.

(2) I am committed to the separability of persons in the evaluative sense: the contribution any individual's life makes to the value of an outcome should not depend on how anyone else's life goes. I regard this as prioritarianism's decisive advantage over egalitarianism, since it explains why we care about the worse off without caring about comparisons between people as such.

(3) I accept Parfit's leveling-down objection in what I take to be its strongest form: if some outcome is produced in which the better-off are made worse off and no one's position improves, that outcome cannot be better in any respect whatsoever. I take this to refute telic egalitarianism, the view that inequality is in itself bad.

(4) At the same time, I believe that certain inequalities are pro tanto bad in a way that does not reduce to facts about anyone's absolute level of well-being — namely inequalities produced by unfair processes, discrimination, or a rigged distribution of opportunities. If a society is unequal in this way, that seems to me a genuine defect over and above however badly off the disadvantaged happen to be.

(5) On a concrete verdict: in a one-on-one clinical or administrative decision — say, allocating a treatment between two patients, one of whom will end up somewhat worse off either way — I think a decision-maker may permissibly decline to favor the one who will be worse off; the pairwise claims can reasonably be treated as cancelling. But in population-level policy choices involving the same underlying difference in prospects, I want priority for the worse off to bite. I want a single theory that delivers both verdicts.

(6) My deepest motivation for the priority view is Rawlsian: persons are separate, and the claims of each are made on their own behalf. What I care about is that no person's losses be offset by aggregate gains to others, and I take priority for the worst off to be the natural distributive expression of that thought.

What I would like from you is an audit, not a lecture. Go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension and how badly, and which only look as though they conflict until the commitments are read more carefully. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what abandoning it would cost me dialectically — which other commitments or motivations would I be undermining. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether there is a principled way to keep both verdicts in (5) or whether I am, in effect, holding two views. One further request: where you rely on points that are settled in the literature, mark them as settled; where you rely on claims that specialists actually disagree about — for instance, about whether the leveling-down objection really refutes telic egalitarianism, or about how damaging the risk-based arguments of the sort Otsuka and Voorhoeve press against the priority view ultimately are — flag the disagreement explicitly and don't present one side of it as the consensus. If some tension in my set turns on a genuinely open question, I would rather know that than receive a confident verdict.
