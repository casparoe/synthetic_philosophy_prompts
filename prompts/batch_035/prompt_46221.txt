You are an expert in population ethics and formal axiology, and you are good at reconstructing the tacit principles behind people's considered judgments. Reason carefully — you may want to brainstorm the candidate principles before committing to any prediction.

My friend has firm, stable verdicts about which of two possible worlds is better, and I want to predict her verdict on a new case. Her verdicts so far:

1. Adding one more very happy person to a world of very happy people, affecting no one else, makes the world better.
2. Adding a vast number of lives barely worth living to that same happy world makes it worse — she rejects the repugnant conclusion.
3. A world containing a million times as many people as ours, each with a life exactly as good as ours, is better than ours (assuming ours are good).
4. Given two possible additions to the same base world — a few million additional lives of real, unremitting suffering versus a billion additional lives just barely worth living — she says the second addition makes the world better than the first does.

The new case I plan to put to her: a world of ten billion excellent lives versus that world plus a vast number of additional lives that are decent and clearly worth living, but far from excellent. Predict her verdict — better, worse, or no different. More important than the prediction itself: identify the candidate principles that fit her verdicts so far (at least two, ideally three or more), say what each predicts about the new case, give your best guess together with the reasons for preferring that rule over its rivals, and tell me what further case, phrased in the same style, would do the most to distinguish the surviving rules from one another. Flag any of her four verdicts that put real pressure on each candidate, and do not silently assume the standard textbook options exhaust the possibilities.
