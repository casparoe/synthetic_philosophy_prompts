A friend in my effective-altruism student group, otherwise sympathetic to longtermism, has produced an argument that the simulation argument doesn't just qualify fanaticism about the far future — it dissolves it. The steps each look plausible, the conclusion is one he can't accept, and I can't find the seam. Please tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.

(1) The simulation argument poses a trilemma: either (a) almost no civilizations at our technological stage survive to technological maturity, or (b) almost no mature civilizations run large numbers of ancestor simulations, or (c) almost everyone having experiences like ours is simulated.
(2) Longtermist projects — existential-risk reduction, steering toward a flourishing mature civilization — presuppose that we have a non-negligible chance of being among the founders of such a civilization, so the longtermist must assign a low probability to (a).
(3) The optimistic picture of maturity that motivates longtermism — the feasibility of planetary-scale computation, the scientific curiosity of our descendants, our historical eagerness to run whatever simulations we can — makes (b) hard to believe: mature civilizations would run vast numbers of ancestor simulations.
(4) So the longtermist's own commitments push toward (c): conditional on taking the far future seriously, we should believe we are almost certainly simulated.
(5) Our simulation, as an artifact, runs only as long as its owners find it worth the compute, and nothing gives us reason to expect them to carry it through a posthuman future of astronomical duration; our apparent trajectory inside the simulation constrains the simulators not at all.
(6) If the far future we might influence exists only inside an artifact whose owners have no reason to sustain it across the timescales that generate astronomical stakes, then the probability that our far future is real and reachable is negligible.
(7) The expected value of longtermist work is roughly the probability that the far future is real and reachable times the value of influencing it; with the first factor negligible, fanaticism about the far future should be replaced by ordinary short-horizon priorities — or at least a steep simulation discount.

Here is what bothers me. Substitute retirement savings, or climate policy, or a five-year research program for longtermist work, and steps (5) through (7) seem to go through just as well: if we are probably inside an artifact with inscrutable termination conditions, everything with a payoff beyond next week collapses together. But nobody in my group actually stops saving for retirement on simulation grounds, and I don't think that is mere irrationality. My friend answers that the inference is blocked by an asymmetry in step (6): ordinary projects target horizons short enough that the simulation's expected runtime doesn't matter, while astronomical-scale projects target horizons so long that it matters decisively. I cannot tell whether that is a real asymmetry or a sleight of hand between having no reason to expect the simulators to run it long and having reason to expect them not to.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it's worth, I would like to keep (4) — the anthropic reasoning seems sound to me — and I would prefer to break things between (5) and (6), since it seems open to say that a simulated far future is a perfectly real far future, full of people whose flourishing counts. But I suspect that isn't free: if simulated flourishing counts, doesn't the longtermist project survive inside the simulation, with its stakes bounded only by however long the simulators do run it? And can the probability in (6) be made well-defined at all, given that we are guessing at simulator purposes from no evidence — or does retreating to an undefined expectation merely prove too much in the other direction?
