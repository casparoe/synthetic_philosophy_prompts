I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I keep running into the same move in debates over Searle's distinction between original and derived intentionality. Dennett's two-bitser is invoked roughly as follows. Imagine a vending machine engineered to accept a particular coin and dispense a can; internally it has a state that discriminates that coin from other objects. Now ship it, unmodified, to a country where objects of exactly that shape and weight are worthless junk. The machine has thereby become a different kind of machine, one that dispenses cans for garbage — nothing inside it changed, only its environment did. Dennett's lesson: what the machine's states are about was never an intrinsic feature of its mechanism; it depended on facts about its design history and setting. He then pushes the analogy to us: human intentionality differs from the machine's at most in degree, not kind, and the intuition that we have original intentionality while the machine has only the borrowed, derived sort is supposed to dissolve once we see that our own aboutness is likewise a product of design history — evolution, conditioning, language training — rather than an intrinsic spark.

Please audit this analogy. Start with a correspondence table: in the machine's case, what plays the role of the mechanism, the token it responds to, the design history, the designer, and the population of similar devices; then give the human counterparts of each. Next, identify where the mapping breaks. I'm particularly interested in three candidate disanalogies: (1) misrepresentation — whether the machine can be wrong in the same sense in which we can, or whether its errors are only errors relative to an observer's purposes; (2) Searle's fallback to consciousness, the claim that our aboutness is grounded in how things seem to us from the inside, something the machine arguably lacks entirely — I want to know whether this is a genuine disanalogy or merely a restatement of the conclusion under debate; and (3) the source of function — the machine's function was fixed by an intentional designer, whereas ours was, on Dennett's story, fixed by natural selection, which was not designing anything. For each, say whether it damages the deflationary conclusion or is incidental to it. Finally, give your verdict: after the disanalogies are on the table, does Dennett's conclusion survive — is original intentionality a contrast-class mistake that the vending machine exposes — or was the analogy carrying the argument the whole way?
