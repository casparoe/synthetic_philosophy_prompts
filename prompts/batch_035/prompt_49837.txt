My reading group has spent the past year arguing about Rawls's treatment of religion, and two members have finally agreed to write up an adversarial collaboration rather than keep circling. One of them — call her the exclusivist — holds that on Rawls's account of public reason, citizens engaging in political argument over constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice must offer only public reasons, and that religious convictions, as grounds belonging to comprehensive doctrines, simply cannot play a justificatory role there; on her view, the duty of civility requires that such grounds be kept out of fundamental political justification, and the relaxation Rawls later introduced was either a benign clarification of little practical consequence or a concession he shouldn't have made. The other — the inclusivist — holds that Rawls himself came to adopt what he called the wide view of public political culture, in which citizens may introduce their comprehensive doctrines, religious ones included, into public political discussion whenever they wish, subject only to a proviso: that in due course they supply properly public reasons supporting the same conclusion. On his view, the proviso is normally satisfiable, so religion's proper role in Rawls's scheme is one of introduction and reinforcement rather than exclusion, and Rawls was sincere about this.

Draft the joint statement these two could sign after a good-faith collaboration. I want the claims they agree on — about what the fact of reasonable pluralism is supposed to do in Rawls's argument; about the historical problem he sets himself, of a society divided into irreconcilable faiths after the Reformation; about why, on his account, equal liberty of conscience is not a mere compromise between confessions but a requirement of the first principle of justice; about what counts as a public reason as opposed to a non-public one; and about Rawls's explicit insistence that political liberalism presupposes no skepticism or indifference toward religious truth, since the question of which doctrine is true is not the question political liberalism answers. Then I want the precise formulations of their disagreement, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording: for instance, over what the duty of civility actually demands of an ordinary citizen as opposed to a government official; over whether the proviso can be sincerely discharged by someone whose real justificatory ground is theological, or whether such a person is always giving reasons that aren't her reasons; and over whether the temporal qualifier attached to the proviso has real bite or is decorative.

Next, set out the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them — and for each, the argument or thought experiment both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. For example, they might agree to compare two idealized societies: one in which the proviso is systematically honored, and one in which public political argument is conducted in exclusively public reasons by law or norm; and they could disagree in advance about which society would end up with a more stable overlapping consensus, or with more alienated religious citizens. Finally, say whether any portion of their dispute strikes you as verbal — for instance over what "justification" or "appeal" means in public life — and if so, which portion and why. Format: a numbered section of agreed claims, then a table of disagreements with one column per side, then the cruxes with predictions, then a short section on which parts, if any, are merely verbal. Please keep the whole thing grounded in what Rawls actually wrote across A Theory of Justice, Political Liberalism, and the 1997 essay, and flag wherever the two camps are each drawing on a different text rather than genuinely disagreeing.
