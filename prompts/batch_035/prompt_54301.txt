for a course I'm T.A.-ing next term I have to write up where I actually stand on the whole innateness / poverty-of-stimulus mess so the students can poke holes in it. here's my honest list, and my officemate says at least two of these flatly contradict eachother but she wouldn't say which. so audit it for me, like you would a proof:

(1) The classic POS argument is basically sound: kids figure out that questions are formed by moving the auxiliary of the main clause rather than the linearly closest one, and they don't overgeneralize the way pure induction over the input would predict, and nobody ever corrects them on it — so a general-purpose learner with nothing language-specific built in couldn't get there from child-directed speech alone.

(2) Modern LLMs, trained on nothing but next-word prediction over unannotated text, end up with really solid grammatical knowledge, and I take that seriously as evidence about what general-purpose learning can extract from positive evidence only. when people wave it off with "but they see so much more data than a child," I think that concedes the interesting point — it quietly turns POS from a claim about possibility into a claim about efficiency.

(3) The real surviving POS question is efficiency: kids get grammars on a tiny fraction of the data an LLM needs, and my honest bet is that whatever biases explain that gap are mostly not language-specific — parsing things into hierarchical part-whole structure is something we already do with tools, action, social scenes.

(4) Newport's less-is-more point is underrated: the kid's small working memory isn't a limitation on acquisition, it's a filter that makes the induction problem easier — developmental constraints doing the constraining that innate linguistic content was supposed to do.

(5) Nicaraguan Sign Language is the best argument for rich innate structure: the early cohorts had something pidgin-like, the later kids systematized it into real morphology (verb agreement in space and so on) that was never in anyones input — that structure had to come from the learners themselves, so I read NSL as straightforward support for (1)-style innateness.
