I direct a mid-sized public library, and on Saturday I'm giving the farewell speech for our head of reference services, who is retiring after thirty-eight years. She is famous in our system for treating patron privacy as something close to sacred: she spent years fighting our software vendor until our circulation records were purged the moment a book came back, and she trained generations of desk staff to treat the question "what has so-and-so been taking out?" as one you simply do not ask or answer — not from staff, not from spouses, not, without proper legal process, from the police. She had a phrase for the service she ran, half-joking but only half: a no-questions room. Her underlying view, worked out over decades of arguments with me at closing time, was that the value of privacy has nothing to do with hiding shameful things. It is the precondition for free inquiry: you can only follow a question honestly if you are not yet accountable for it, and a reader who knows she is observed starts reading for the observer — reaching for the defensible book instead of the honest one. Her brother thinks her vigilance was paranoid, especially since Amazon logs everything anyway; her reply was that this is exactly the point — an institution that forgets is a civic good precisely when everything else remembers.

Please write the speech, around five minutes when spoken. It must land with a mixed audience: the staff who shared her convictions, the Friends of the Library, her family (including the skeptical brother, whom I'd rather win over than lecture), and — awkwardly — a city council member who years ago proposed mining borrowing records to "know our community better," which led to a mild public quarrel that I do not want reopened or turned into a verdict against him. So the constraints: it should honor her philosophy so that it actually does philosophical work rather than decorating the speech with her name, it should not concede anything she would consider false (for instance that privacy is mainly a refuge for people with secrets, or that observation is harmless if the observers are benevolent), and it should not lecture — the argument should arrive through her, through stories of the no-questions room, rather than through me. A little humor would help, especially anything that the brother can laugh at without feeling mocked.
