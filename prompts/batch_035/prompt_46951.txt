I have recently accepted a three-year post in a country whose funeral and dietary customs my family finds genuinely disturbing, and my teenage daughter has started using the word "barbaric" with unsettling confidence. A colleague recommended Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals," and I have now read it twice. I want a clear-eyed assessment of it, not admiration.

Rank the following argumentative moves from the essay by how much independent weight each can bear against ethnocentric judgment, using a tier list (strong / moderate / weak), with one sentence of justification for each placement:

1. His opening observation that each of us tends to call barbarism whatever is merely unfamiliar to our own practice, so the label tracks custom rather than rational assessment.
2. The claim that the Tupinambá live closer to nature and therefore closer to reason, in the spirit of the ancient poets' portraits of a golden age.
3. His use of the cannibals' courage and composure in warfare, and especially in the face of death and suffering, as evidence of genuine virtue.
4. His contrast between ritual eating of already-dead enemies and the tortures and massacres of Europe's wars of religion, with the implication that the label of barbarism attaches to the wrong continent.
5. His report of what the Tupinambá visitors to Rouen found baffling in French society, especially grown men obeying a child king and the poor begging outside doors while others live in abundance.

After the tier list, answer two further questions. First: do any of these arguments actually support relativism — the view that the practices in question are not objectively better or worse — or do they only support humility about the reliability of our judgments? Distinguish the two carefully. Second: which single argument would you press into service in my situation, that is, in persuading my daughter that calling something barbaric is not yet saying anything?
