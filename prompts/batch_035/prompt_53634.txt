For the final session of my decision theory seminar I close with Newcomb-adjacent cases, ending on Death in Damascus, told like this: a traveller in Damascus sees Death across the market; Death looks faintly surprised, and the traveller takes that to mean Death expected to find him the next day in Aleppo. When the traveller asks, Death confirms it: if he stays, Death will come for him in Damascus; if he flees to Aleppo, Death will come for him in Aleppo.

I would like a sestina about the traveller's night of deliberation before dawn, with the form carrying the argument. The six end-words should be the same six words throughout, as the sestina requires, and that sameness should do the philosophical work: however the traveller rearranges his reasons from stanza to stanza, the same six words return at the line-ends, in an order he did not choose — Death's arrangement rather than his. The envoi, with its two end-words per line, should be the moment of departure at first light; by then the reader should feel that the decision was fixed before it was made, and the end-words landing mid-line, in the middle of things, should suggest that Death is already there. If you want to let the deliberation regress — each fresh resolution turning out to be something Death predicted in advance — the sestina's cycling through the same permutation is the natural vehicle for that; but keep it a poem: someone who knows the case should find the reasoning right, and someone who does not should find a good poem about a night spent choosing between two doors.

The traveller must actually resolve one way on the page — stay or flee — rather than hovering between them; commit to a single decision-theoretic reading (causal, evidential, or the traveller's own fatalism) and show it through the reasoning rather than by naming it. Then append a short prose note stating which reading you committed to and why, and flag any liberties you took with the standard analysis of the case.
