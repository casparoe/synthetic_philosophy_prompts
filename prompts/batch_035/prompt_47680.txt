My study group has been going in circles on hedonism all term, and I wrote down what I think is the strongest anti-hedonist argument anyone in the room produced, but now it's starting to look like it dissolves everything, not just pleasure, and I can't find the seam. Help me locate it.

The argument:

(1) If "pleasure" names a real mental kind, its instances must share some property that unifies them, and that property has to be either a felt quality -- something you could in principle catch by introspection -- or a causal/functional role, what the state tends to do in you.

(2) There's no common felt quality. The pleasure of finally getting a joke, the pleasure of a hot shower after hours in the cold, the pleasure of your team winning, and the slow pleasure of listening to a sad song on purpose have nothing introspectively in common. When you subtract what's specific to each, there's no residue left over.

(3) There's no common functional role either. Some pleasures quiet desire (satiation), others require desire to stay alive (anticipation, the build-up of a chorus), some coexist with wanting the whole thing to stop (the runner who's enjoying the race and wants it over), and some make you want more while others make any more impossible to imagine. Causal profile: all over the map.

(4) So no property unifies pleasures.

(5) A term that picks out no unified kind doesn't refer at all -- it's not that it refers to a loose cluster, it's that it's an empty term, the mental-health version of "the present king of France."

(6) So there is no such thing as pleasure, and hedonism (well-being = net pleasure) is built on an empty term.

Here's the problem. Last week we ran the exact same schema on belief and got that there are no beliefs: no common felt quality (most beliefs feel like nothing at all), no common functional role (beliefs from perception, from testimony, from wishful inference, from math all behave totally differently). You can run it on desire, on emotion, and -- the one that really bugged me -- on "experience" itself. An argument-schema that eats the entire mental vocabulary isn't a refutation of hedonism, it's an acid. But when we checked each premise individually they all looked fine, which is exactly why I can't put my finger on where it slides.

Two side things I want you to actually engage with, not wave off:

First, one of us (the one with the neuroscience minor) proposed that the unifier could be subpersonal -- something like a common valenced signal under everything, one introspection can't reach, the way you can't taste the water in the coffee. If that's the escape route, tell me which premise it's rejecting and whether hedonism even survives the rescue, since it starts to look like a claim about brains rather than about felt lives, which seems like it changes what the theory was supposed to be.

Second, I did experience sampling for a week -- random alarms on my phone, and whenever one went off I wrote down whatever was pleasant and what it felt like. About 40 samples. I got no common feel out of it. So I'd like to keep (2) if possible. But I'm open to being told my method couldn't have found the common property even if it's there, in which case tell me why the method is doomed rather than just that it's informal.

So: which premise (or inference) goes, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? If some step only works under a specific reading -- like a slide between "no one candidate property unifies them" and "no property unifies them," or between pairwise differences and the absence of a shared feature -- point at the exact reading the argument needs. And if you think the honest answer is that (5) is the weak one and pleasure really is just a ragbag we've mistaken for a kind, say so and explain what that does to hedonism's claim to be a theory of well-being at all.
