I've been in a philosophy of mind reading group for about a year now, and we've gone round and round on what emotions are -- the judgment camp (emotions are evaluative judgments, like judging that I've been insulted), the perception camp (emotions are perceptions of value or bodily change, like seeing a snake rather than believing there's a snake), and the feelings camp (emotions are just felt sensations, and everything cognitive is upstream or downstream). What's struck me is how static it all is. The judgment people have had the same counterexample on the table forever -- you can be terrified standing on the glass walkway while flatly judging you're safe, so fear can't just be a judgment -- and the reply is always some variant of "the judgment is dispositional" or "there are two systems," which somehow never gets tested. The perception people get pressed on how an emotion can represent anything without conceptual content, and the answer is always "well, perceptual content can be nonconceptual," and nobody budges. The feelings people are told they can't distinguish anger from fear without importing cognition, and they say the felt profile differs, and around we go again.

My working theory, offered as just a hunch, is that this isn't really one debate at all -- that the judgment theorists are mainly answering a question about what individuates emotions (why is this anger and not indignation), the perception theorists a question about how emotions can justify beliefs or actions, and the feelings people a question about phenomenal character, and each side's theory looks bad to the others because it wasn't built for their question. But I might be wrong; maybe there's a genuine substantive fight about the ontology here that just happens to be hard, or maybe one camp has quietly conceded the important ground and I'm missing it.
