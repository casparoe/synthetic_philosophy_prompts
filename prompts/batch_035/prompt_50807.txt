I'm a grad student working on bioethics, and I keep getting stuck on advance directives and how much authority the "earlier self" should have once someone loses capacity to dementia. i want to stop arguing about particular cases and figure out what a good theory of this actually has to deliver before I read any more of the literature. Can you act as a professional philosopher specializing in medical ethics and personal identity, think carefully, and write me a proper desiderata list?

The theory I need a spec for: one that tells us whose interests or voice should govern treatment and care decisions for someone who authored an advance directive while competent but now, with advanced dementia or something similar, is in some sense a different person, with present wishes, present moods, present experiences.

Requirements I'd want you to consider (add your own, push back on mine where there confused):

- handle the full range of antecedent expressions: a detailed directive written right after a diagnosis, a decades-old casual remark ("I'd never want to live like that"), and no directive at all but known values
- do justice to both selves — the earlier self's precedent autonomy, and the later self's current experiential interests. It can't just say "the directive wins" or "the patient who's happy now wins." The Dworkin-style split between critical and experiential interests is somewhere in here, e.g. the case where the demented patient is genuinely content day to day while the directive refuses exactly that kind of life
- say something principled about what to do when following the directive makes the later self worse off in ways the later self can't even understand
- handle surrogates: what happens when family or a proxy disagrees with the written directive, or wants to reinterpret vague wording
- handle revocation and drift: the earlier self said X, then years later, still competent but already slipping, starts acting and speaking inconsistently with X. Which utterances count?
- be consistent with a defensible view of personal identity — survival vs. non-survival in advanced dementia, Parfit-style reductionism, the works. The theory can't just refuse to take a position on whether the later self is the same person
- handle the hard case where the person said clearly "don't keep me alive past stage X" and the present person is, plainly, not ready to die, can voice that, and is distressed by the withdrawals of care the directive seems to demand
- be applicable: clinicians, families and courts have to actually use it. A theory a night-shift nurse can't act on is a theory of nothing

For each desideratum: say what it's for, what goes wrong for theories that lack it.
