I sit on the conduct-reform working group of a boarding school (roughly 850 students, grades 9–12). Ten years ago, after a wave of exam cheating, we replaced our old disciplinary system with a centralized one: an anonymous online reporting form, investigations by a professional integrity office, penalty bands fixed in advance, and appeals limited to procedural error. It works in one sense — cheating is down and students trust the process to be impartial — but the head of school has become convinced that students now treat integrity as a compliance regime rather than a matter of character, and has asked our group to review the entire model.

Three factions have formed on the working group, and I am charged with drafting the memo that will frame our final recommendation to the board.

The first faction wants a traditional honor code: a student-run committee elected by peers, a signed pledge, and the expectation that a student who witnesses a violation confronts the offender personally and gives them a day to self-report before taking it to the committee — with lying to the committee treated as worse than the underlying offense, expulsion being the standard penalty for it. Their argument is that this is the only arrangement that makes integrity internal: norms are enforced by the community you face every day rather than by an office, and the virtue actually being trained is courage, both to refuse the shortcut and to confront a friend.

The second faction wants to keep the current system, reformed at the edges. To them the honor-code proposal is nostalgia for a gentleman's club: personal confrontation is easy for the confident and socially secure and devastating for the anxious, the unpopular, and students who are already outsiders; peer juries reproduce the school's existing hierarchies; and the pledge-and-ceremony apparatus is moral theater. The anonymous form, they point out, is what made reporting possible for students who could never confront anyone, and the fixed penalty bands are what ended the era when consequences tracked how much teachers liked you.

The third faction thinks both are wrong and wants most misconduct handled informally: a first instance of copied homework should be a conversation with a teacher, not a file, with the formal apparatus reserved for the small residue of serious, repeated, or predatory conduct. Their worry about the current system is not that it is unjust but that it is totalizing — it converts every lapse into a case and teaches students that the correct response to being wronged is to route it upward to authorities rather than to tolerate, negotiate, or forgive.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the working group ahead of our final meeting. It should recommend one course concretely — which model, or a hybrid with the hybrid specified (which classes of case go to which track) — with reasoning the board could act on. It should give the strongest argument on each losing side genuine weight rather than a perfunctory rebuttal paragraph, and in particular it should not dodge the question of who bears the costs of the recommended arrangement: which students it protects and which it exposes. Finally, include a closing section on what would make us regret the recommendation — what evidence within the first five years (reporting rates, the composition of cases, exit interviews, anything measurable), and what argument or consideration, if it turned out to be right, should lead the school to reverse course.
