I've been trying to settle what emotions actually are by cataloguing my verdicts on borderline cases, and I'd like a theory that captures them — or an honest accounting of which verdicts have to go. My tentative line is that emotions are perceptions of evaluative properties (fear as the perception of danger, grief as the perception of loss) rather than judgments or mere feelings, but I suspect it can't handle everything below. The verdicts: (1) my friend's flash of terror at a stick she took for a snake was a genuine emotion, though it lasted two seconds and was gone the moment she saw what it was; (2) the jolt I get when a car backfires is not an emotion, even though it is more intensely felt than the snake case; (3) the chills and tears I get listening to the end of a piano concerto are a full-blown emotion, even though I make no judgment about danger or loss and would deny that I perceive anything bad in the music; (4) on a glass skywalk I felt real fear while flatly judging myself perfectly safe, and I want to count both the fear and the judgment as genuine; (5) while calmly sorting my late father's study, I was still grieving that whole afternoon, though there was no occurrent pang and no fresh evaluative thought; and (6) a colleague who sincerely judges spiders dangerous but feels absolutely nothing around them is not, in my view, afraid of them. Is there a theory of emotion — judgment-theoretic, perceptualist, feeling-based, or a hybrid — that delivers all six verdicts? If not, say which verdict is cheapest to overturn and why. Please structure your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: item 1 should state your favored account as precisely as you can, items 2 through 4 should test it against the cases you find hardest, and item 5 should give your verdict on the case I keep going back and forth on — stage fright that vanishes the instant the performance begins, leaving only a racing heart.
