After a career in experimental physics, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy seriously, and next month I have been invited to present at a university philosophy department's work-in-progress seminar — I suspect partly out of curiosity about what a scientist will say. My thesis is that philosophy does make progress, but that this progress is not convergence on truth; it is the narrowing of live option space by argumentative no-go results, much as experiments in my own field often failed to crown a theory yet reliably killed some. My sketch is as follows: first, we should stop measuring philosophy against the (largely mythical) picture of physics as converging on a unique true theory; second, on a constraint model philosophy's record looks respectable — the Gettier cases durably ruled out the simplest analysis of knowledge, and the classic confrontations in philosophy of mind culled behaviorism and type-identity theory while leaving more defensible positions standing; third, what look like interminable debates are often episodes in which the surviving option space is genuinely re-opened by new considerations rather than by mere failure of memory. The room will contain a formal epistemologist who models inquiry as Bayesian convergence, a Kant scholar with little patience for the suggestion that philosophy resembles an empirical science, a pragmatist, a Wittgensteinian quietist who holds that philosophical problems are confusions to be dissolved rather than questions to be settled, and a historian of early modern philosophy. Please prepare my question-period sheet as a numbered list of exactly five items: the five objections I am most likely to face from this particular room, ordered by how much damage they would do, each stated in the voice of the colleague most likely to raise it, followed within the same item — in a sentence or two at most — by my best reply and a note on where that reply is soft. One of the five items should be the question I am quietly hoping nobody asks. Please include no introduction or conclusion.
