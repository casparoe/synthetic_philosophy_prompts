In Gödel, Escher, Bach, Hofstadter argues in the chapter on the location of meaning that every message carries meaning in three layers: a frame message, which signals that the bearer is a message at all and triggers the receiver to try to decode it; an outer message, which is the implicit information needed to identify the decoding scheme; and an inner message, which is the content the sender meant to convey. His illustrations include a phonograph record, which needs a record player of the right speed and standard to yield music, and DNA, which needs a cellular machinery that "knows" the genetic code before the strand can say anything. On his telling, the outer message is necessarily understood rather than explicitly decoded — some meaning must already be shared for any decoding to begin.

Write an essay on whether this three-layer decomposition is a principled one, or whether one of its boundaries is unstable and should collapse. Focus your discussion on the following two pressures on the scheme, and feel free to add further ones of your own devising:

1. The regress pressure. If the outer message must itself be grasped before decoding can start, it looks like a message too — one whose own outer layer must be grasapsed before it can be grasped. Hofstadter answers that the buck stops with pre-existing, shared isomorphisms between sender and receiver. Assess whether this answer dissolves the regress or merely relabels it: is "recognizing an isomorphism" the same kind of act as understanding a message, or a different, non-semantic capacity that can bear the whole burden of meaning-fixing?

2. The instruction pressure. Consider messages whose inner content is itself a decoding instruction — a Rosetta-stone-like inscription whose point, once read, is to tell you how to read further material, or a signal from an unknown source that, once laboriously deciphered, turns out only to explain how to decipher the rest of the transmission. For such messages the inner content and the outer function coincide. Construct at least one concrete example of this kind (invented rather than historical), and use it to argue either that the outer/inner boundary survives as a matter of the receiver's route to the content, or that it collapses into a single notion of content differently accessed.

Reach a clear verdict: either the trichotomy stands with at most cosmetic repairs, or a specific boundary (say which) should be abandoned, with a resulting two-level or continuous picture of meaning-location. Justify the verdict rather than merely announcing it.

Throughout the essay, state how confident you are in each major claim you make, and at the end single out the single point you are least sure of, explaining what would make you revise it.
