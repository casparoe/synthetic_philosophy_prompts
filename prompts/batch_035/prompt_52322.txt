I'm a PhD student in economics working on inequality measurement; my committee includes two philosophers, and I'm about to circulate a chapter that tries to say what exactly just distributions should equalize. Before they see it, I want a consistency check on the package of views I've drifted into over the past two years. The commitments, roughly as I would defend them:

(1) Distributive justice should be responsibility-sensitive: disadvantages tracing to brute luck call for compensation; disadvantages tracing to informed choices do not. I take this to be the core insight of luck egalitarianism, and I am not prepared to give it up.

(2) The currency of justice is resources, and by resources I mean external, transferable things — income, wealth, access to services. I have never been comfortable with the move, common among resourcists, of extending the term to internal traits like talents or health; that seems like a word game concealing a welfare metric.

(3) Someone who develops expensive tastes — the person who acquires a taste that only very costly things satisfy — is owed nothing on account of them. On this I side with Dworkin against Cohen.

(4) Two people with identical external resources, identical lifetime opportunities, and no health conditions: one is temperamentally cheerful, the other chronically discontented though fully functional. The discontented one is owed nothing further. My resource metric delivers this verdict, and I endorse it.

(5) A person with a severe but untreatable condition — constant chronic pain, say, where no intervention exists that would help and no expenditure of resources would relieve anything — is, I think, still owed something by justice. I cannot make this come out of (2) at all, and I notice that the only thing I seem able to point to is what the condition does to the person's life from the inside, which is exactly the sort of fact I refused to count in (4).

(6) Following Sen, I hold that identical resource bundles can leave people with very different real freedoms, because people differ in what they can convert resources into: the pregnant woman needs more nutrients than others to reach the same nutritional state, the ill need more of everything, and so on. Justice has to register these conversion differences, not just bundle sizes.

(7) From my economics training: interpersonal comparisons of welfare have no foundations I could defend, and this was a large part of what pushed me toward (2) in the first place. I do not see how I could coherently be a welfarist about distribution while denying comparability.

What I want from you: which of these actually conflict, which only seem to, and what is the least costly way to restore consistency? A few worries of my own, which you should correct if I have misdescribed the situation. First, I cannot tell whether (4) and (5) are flatly inconsistent or whether the line between them is a matter of degree that my framework could absorb — temperament versus illness, gloom versus pain — and I don't know what would make such a line principled rather than drawn wherever sympathy runs out. Second, I suspect (6) and (2) are in tension, but I have a nagging sense there is a resourcist reply available, something about needs differing across persons so that equal resources never meant identical bundles; I can't tell whether that is a real reply or a concession in disguise. Third, it has occurred to me that (1) requires judgments about responsibility, and about the partition between choice and circumstance, that are of exactly the kind (7)'s scruples lead me to distrust elsewhere — though I am unsure whether that parallel is genuine or merely rhetorical.

Please order the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally damaging. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what that concession would cost me elsewhere in the set. For each apparent tension, show me why it dissolves and where the dissolution is fragile. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I would rather find out from you than in front of my committee.
