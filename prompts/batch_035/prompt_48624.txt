I run a weekly seminar in philosophy of mathematics for fourteen second- and third-year undergraduates, ninety minutes per session, in a room with movable desks. The students have all done a first logic course and can follow a proof by contradiction, but they have met the constructivism debate only in passing, and mostly as a historical curiosity — they have never had occasion to feel why anyone might be dissatisfied with a proof that establishes existence while yielding nothing usable. I want to fix that with a structured activity rather than a lecture, and I have decided the vehicle will be the strategy-stealing argument: the proof, familiar from Nash's work on Hex and from arguments about other placement games, that in a symmetric game which cannot end in a draw, the second player cannot have a winning strategy, because she could steal the first player's — take an extra move, then imitate, gaining rather than losing by the superfluous mark. So the first player must have a winning strategy. What I find delicious, and what I want the students to feel, is that this is a genuine proof of the existence of something that, for boards of any real size, no human being can exhibit or execute: we know the strategy is there, and it is no help whatsoever to anyone who has to actually play.

Please design the activity in full: the setup, materials (I assume printed grids and pens will do — tell me if not), the sequence of rounds with rough timings, and how the matches should be arranged so that the students discover the asymmetry themselves rather than being told. My sketch is something like this, which you should improve on rather than obey blindly: pairs play Hex on boards large enough that no student can find a concrete winning strategy but small enough that games finish inside ten minutes; then a round in which one player in each pair is told only that she holds a winning strategy and must win with it, so the class experiences the gap between knowing that a winning strategy exists and being able to use one; then the stealing argument is run as a group exercise on the board, with a designated sceptic whose job is to attack it (I want the classic misfires to surface: the claim that the second player could steal too, the complaint that the extra mark might be a liability, and the worry that the imitation breaks when the opponent plays in the stolen square). The students should not hear the phrase 'strategy-stealing' until the debrief, and the debrief is where the philosophy arrives: map the rounds onto the distinction between classical and constructive existence proofs, and get them to articulate, in their own words, what an intuitionist is objecting to when she refuses a proof like this — that the activity should have made the objection feel like a complaint about real games played by real people, not a verbal scruple about the word 'exists'.

As usual, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure: someone finds a brute-force strategy on the small board and concludes the point is trivial (I take it I should use bigger boards, but say how big); pairs run long; a student simply refuses to accept the argument and derails the debrief; or the class concludes that the whole episode shows classical mathematics is fine and the intuitionist is silly, which is the opposite of what I'm after. And be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: I suspect there is no way, inside a ninety-minute session, to make the students feel the full strength of the existence claim, since the real proof quantifies over the entire game tree and the rounds only ever show them their own incompetence — so tell me which part of the idea the debrief has to supply, and where I should concede that a game played by finite humans is only an analogy for the mathematics.
