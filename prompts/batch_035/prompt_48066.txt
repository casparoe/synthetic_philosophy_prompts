You are to act as a poet who also holds a doctorate in philosophy of mind and has taught the epiphenomenalist literature for years. I would like a villanelle on the paradox of phenomenal judgment.

The situation the poem should inhabit: if the epiphenomenalist is right, my qualia are causally idle — the redness of red, the ache of a headache, happen alongside the neural processes but push nothing, including this very utterance. Every report I give about my experience is scripted entirely by physical causes that would fire identically in a duplicate of me with no inner life at all, and that duplicate says everything I say, writes everything I write, including the poem you are about to compose. The paradox is that my judgments about consciousness seem to be *about* consciousness and to *justify* my belief in it, while the causal story of how those judgments get made never routes through the consciousness itself.

Constraints on the poem:

- The two refrains should be a line the speaker utters about her own experience and the line her exact physical duplicate would utter about *its* experience. The reader should gradually realize the two lines are, from the outside, indistinguishable — that neither refrain can certify the inner life it reports, because the causes of the saying arrive without the felt quality, and the felt quality arrives without power. Let the villanelle's alternation do this work: the refrains keep returning as if insisting on their difference while never displaying any.
- Hold to the form properly — five tercets and a closing quatrain, the refrains taking turns and finally standing side by side in the last two lines. That final adjacency, not any word within the lines, is where the paradox should live: two utterances that only the speaker, from the inside, could tell apart.
- Keep the vocabulary of the speaker's life — a kitchen, a headache, the color of something ordinary — rather than the seminar room. Do not use the words "qualia," "epiphenomenal," "zombie," or "consciousness." The philosophy should arrive only through the form.
- Do not resolve the paradox. The poem should end with the speaker (and the reader) unable to point, from outside the act of saying, to anything that distinguishes the felt report from the scripted one.

Before writing, spend a few lines working out which formulation of the paradox you are enacting and which formal choices will carry it, so that the craft decisions are deliberate rather than decorative. After the poem, append a brief author's note that says plainly where the piece takes liberties or leans on a contested reading — for instance, whether it presupposes that an absent-qualia duplicate is genuinely possible, or whether it quietly stacks the deck against the epiphenomenalist who insists a judgment can be *about* experience without being *caused by* it — and identify the single line or structural choice most likely to fail on inspection: the place where a careful critic would say the conceit has stopped tracking the argument and started merely illustrating it. If that weakness could be repaired without breaking the form, say how; if not, say why the form itself resists the repair.
