I'm teaching a second-year rational choice course this term and next week I have a 90 minute session with about 25 students on decision making under uncertainty. I want to do the Ellsberg-style urn comparison, but I don't want it to just be me polling the class and announcing they're irrational. My plan is they should run into the problem themselves. The setup I had in mind: two big jars of balls (or envelopes with colored cards, honestly cards are easier to carry), Jar A with a known 50/50 split of red and black, Jar B with 100 red and black balls in a completely unknown ratio. They each have to pick a jar and a color, and they win a raffle ticket for a small prize if their color comes up. Then, without a break, a second round with the same jars but a payoff change or a forced color swap, so that when we count hands at the end we can see whether the same students who avoided the unknown jar in round 1 also avoided it in round 2 — which is the part that can't be explained by any single belief about what's in Jar B. I want that reveal to land in the debrief, not before.

Two extra things I'd like you to work in, because the plain version of this demo feels too well-worn. First, a trading round: after the initial choices, each student holds a ticket for a specific bet, and we run a few minutes of open trading where they can sell their ticket or buy someone else's, and we track prices. My hunch is that ambiguity aversion that shows up in a one-shot forced choice partly evaporates when you actually have to post a price for the thing, and I'd rather the activity let us see whether that's true than have me assert it. Second, I want to give one or two students a designated "bookie" role: their job is to try to profit off classmates whose willingness-to-buy and willingness-to-sell prices for bets on Jar B are all below fair value. I have this idea that you can't price every color in the unknown jar below 1/2 (or below its counterpart in the known jar) without being exploitable, but I'm not 100% sure how to set the bookie's constraints up concretely so it works in 90 minutes without money changing hands.

So: full rules, materials, a timeline, the payoff scheme in raffle tickets or extra credit or whatever works, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the sure-thing principle and onto the question of whether dodging the unknown jar is a bias or a sensible response to a world where whoever filled the jar might be out to get you. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I'm guessing noisy prices with only 25 traders, and students who trade just for fun), and whether the trading round can actually show what I want or whether it overloads the session. Also, should I name maxmin expected utility in the debrief, or save it for the following lecture and let this session end on the puzzle?
