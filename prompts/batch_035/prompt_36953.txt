I'm grading midterm exams in my upper-level philosophy of biology course, and I'd like a second reader's assessment of one answer. The exam question was:

"(a) Explain the difference between etiological (selected-effect) and causal-role (system-capacity) accounts of biological function, using the heart and the circulation of blood as your example. (b) An isolated population of lizards passed through a severe bottleneck five generations ago. During the bottleneck, a mutation that flattens the tail went to fixation purely by drift — carriers and non-carriers survived at equal rates — and the allele is now fixed, leaving no variation for selection to act on. The flattened tail turns out to let these lizards glide short distances, and this contributes substantially to their capacity to escape ground predators. Does the flattened tail have the function of gliding on each of the two accounts? Explain. (c) Suppose one of these lizards has a deformed tail that cannot support gliding at all. What should each account say about that tail — in particular, does the tail still have a function?"

The student's answer reads:

"Biological function talk has two readings. On the etiological account, the function of a trait is what it was selected for: the heart has the function of pumping blood because ancestors whose hearts pumped blood were favored by natural selection, and that is why hearts exist in the form they do. On the causal-role account, the function of a part is its contribution to some capacity of the containing system: the heart's function is pumping blood because pumping is what the heart does that explains the circulatory system's capacity to deliver oxygen. The difference is history — the etiological account looks back at why the trait is there, while the causal-role account looks at what the trait does now. For the lizards, since the flattened tail now improves survival by enabling gliding, it has been favored by natural selection, so on the etiological account the tail already has the function of gliding; and on the causal-role account it plainly has that function as well, since gliding is the tail's contribution to escaping predators. So the two accounts agree in this case. As for the deformed tail: on the etiological account the tail has lost its function of gliding — it can no longer do what it was selected for, so there is no function left to ascribe. Here the causal-role account does better: since function on that account is fixed by the trait's history, the deformed tail still has the function of gliding and simply fails to perform it, which is what we ordinarily mean by a part's malfunctioning."
