I help run a lay reading group at my parish, and this season we've been slowly working through Augustine's two short treatises on lying, the one usually called On Lying and the later, longer one addressed to Consentius, Against Lying. For our final session I've talked our (long-suffering) retired classics teacher into playing Augustine in a mock interview, in period costume and everything, and I've been asked to prepare the questions. I want questions that take his position seriously rather than sneering at it, but that also don't let him off easy, and since he obviously knows his own texts better than I ever will, each question needs a planned follow-up for whatever answer he's most likely to reach for.

A few things I've noticed in my own reading that I'd like the questions to get at. First, he allows that when someone with no right to the truth asks where the fugitive is hiding, you may refuse to answer, conceal, or say something ambiguous -- he just forbids the outright falsehood -- and I'd like to press on whether that escape hatch really differs from lying or only dresses it up. Second, even though he treats every lie as a sin, he sorts lies into kinds and grades them, some as worse than others, and I want a question on whether admitting degrees of guilt quietly concedes what the total ban is supposed to deny. Third, he defines lying as speaking against one's own mind rather than saying something false, and I've wondered whether that definition catches jokes, teasing, and fiction -- I gather he discusses stage performances somewhere -- and whether his own way out of that problem doesn't end up making the intent to deceive, rather than the lie itself, the real sin, which would seem to leave a merciful lie room to come back in. Beyond those three, I'd like at least one question on why the prohibition seems to bind absolutely while other commandments get exceptions (a soldier kills, a spouse is stolen from in war, and yet no lie is ever licensed), and at least one on whether the whole edifice stands on God's inability to deceive, and therefore collapses for anyone who doesn't share the premise.

Please write eight to ten questions in total, each with its anticipated reply and a follow-up keyed to that reply, ordered so the interview builds rather than darting around. Begin with the questions themselves, with no preface or commentary; if you want to add notes, put them after the last question. Keep the questions phrased in my voice, as things a curious layman could plausibly ask a fourth-century bishop across a table, though the substance underneath should be as sharp as you can make it.
