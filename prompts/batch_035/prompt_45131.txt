My philosopher friend and I have spent several evenings arguing about surrogate goals as a defense against acausal threats, and I would like to locate our disagreement rather than continue talking past each other. Since we can't settle it, I'm asking several people (including you) for numbers with their reasoning attached. First, the background as we've been framing it, so we're on the same page:

By an acausal threat I mean a threat that operates through prediction and logical correlation rather than causal power. The paradigm: some powerful agent or process cannot causally reach you to enforce anything, but it can predict your policy, and it selects its own behavior partly on the basis of what it predicts. If it predicts you'll capitulate to threats, it pays a small cost to set up a threat (say, diverting an asteroid, or committing resources to punish whatever of its predictions says you folded); if it predicts you'll ignore threats, setting one up is pure loss for it, so it never does. Someone following causal decision theory treats the threatener's setup as already in place and capitulates; someone following an updateless or functional decision theory treats their own choice as evidence about the threatener's behavior and refuses. The trouble is that refusing ex post can be horrifically costly, and a policy of refusing makes you a magnet for exactly the test cases that establish your reputation.

The proposed escape — surrogate goals, as the idea is sometimes called: commit in advance to treat a threat against some nearly worthless surrogate target exactly as you would treat a threat against what you actually care about. You publicly bind yourself to respond to anyone who threatens to destroy a particular shed of scrap on your property as if they had threatened everything you value. The thought is that this raises the price of threatening you at almost no cost to you: the threatener can no longer find a cheap test of your resolve, and threatening the surrogate is as expensive for them as threatening the real thing.

For definiteness: my credence that adopting surrogate goals is the right policy for an agent already committed to an updateless-style decision theory is about 0.55; my friend's is about 0.15. Please give me your own number, but what I really want is the decomposition into sub-questions, each with a rough probability and one line of justification. At minimum I want your credence on: (a) whether acausal threats are a genuine practical problem for agents like us at all, as opposed to an artifact of decision theories that misapply evidential reasoning; (b) whether surrogate commitments actually deter threateners rather than merely relocating the threshold — in particular whether a threatener who can threaten the surrogate and the real interest simultaneously, or who can cheaply probe whether your surrogate commitment is genuine, leaves the proposal with any protective value; (c) whether the commitment is diachronically stable — whether an agent who has bound itself will, when a threat against the shed actually materializes and the cost of honoring the commitment becomes vivid, stick to the policy or regret and discard it, and whether an agent's knowledge that it might discard it retroactively undermines the deterrence; and (d) whether adopting surrogate goals creates exploitable behavior of some other kind — for example, whether there are threat-like agents whose behavior doesn't track your policies and to whom you are now exposed twice over. Add any further terms you think the total credence should factor through. Then combine them into your overall number, tell me which term the result is most sensitive to, say which of the two of us you most likely disagree with and on which specific sub-question, and name the single consideration or argument that would move your number the most if it went the other way. I am not looking for a survey of positions; I want your actual credence with its load-bearing assumptions marked.
