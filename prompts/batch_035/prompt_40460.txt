So a bit of context: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background at all, but I got pulled into this because of work. We do incident retrospectives at my company, and at some point I read enough pop-science stuff about Judea Pearl's do-calculus that I basically adopted the interventionist picture of causation as my working worldview. X causes Y if intervening on X (do(X), holding other stuff fixed) changes Y. This maps SO well onto debugging: when the pager goes off at 3am you're not asking some metaphysical "what if the server had never existed" question, you're asking "if I flip this config flag, does the error rate move". So I've come to think of the old counterfactual analysis -- X caused Y iff had X not happened, Y wouldn't have either -- as a kind of sloppy folk approximation of interventions, fine for everyday talk but strictly worse once things get complicated.

It even caused a fight at work, sort of. I argued in a retro that "root cause" should mean something like "the node in the causal graph where an intervention would have prevented the outage", which sounds clean until my coworker pointed out her favorite root causes are things like "we underprovisioned capacity in Q1" or a hiring decision from two years ago -- stuff you can never actually run a do() on. And the counterfactual story handles those weirdly better? You can at least ask what would've happened if we'd staffed differently, even though no experiment is possible. That plus a couple of weird cases have me less smug than I was: outages caused by *nothing happening* (the backup node was already down, so the primary's failure "causes" the outage partly through an omission), and cases where two config changes each would've been harmless but together killed the service, so it's murky which one "caused" anything under either definition.

Anyway, that's my position: interventionism, with counterfactuals demoted to a useful shorthand. Play it straight and tell me what would count against it. Which cases, which arguments, which weird structural facts about the world should actually move me? I've also heard there's a whole literature that bolts an "actual causation" apparatus onto interventions -- structural equations, default/normality weights, that kind of thing -- and honestly it smells like epicycles to me, but maybe that smell is just me not understanding the problem it's solving. So I'd want: defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, a sense of which ones a committed interventionist would concede vs. wave off as a misuse of the framework (is my root-cause application just a category error?), and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so whether that's a problem for a framework that's supposed to tell you what to do at 3am. Bonus points if you can tell me whether the underprovisioned-capacity case is a real crack in the foundation or just me using a hammer on a screw.
