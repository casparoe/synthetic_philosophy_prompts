I co-teach an intro course on political ethics with a colleague from the anthropology department, and we keep clashing over the unit on corruption. The standard NGO definition — abuse of entrusted power for private gain — she thinks it smuggles in a whole theory, and half our students (several from places where slipping an envelope to your surgeon after an operation, or giving your kid's teacher a gift at the holidays, is just ordinary decency) hear it as a Western verdict on how their societies are run. So instead of the usual lecture where we each push our line, we want to hand out a taxonomy of the actual positions on what counts as corruption, built as a tree where each branch is labeled by the claim that splits it.

The branch nodes need to be things a student can answer without knowing which position it lands them in. So the first split should probably be whether the public/private distinction itself travels, or whether it's partly a local institutional achievement that some societies never built the same way. Further down: whether the core wrong is a betrayal of role and trust, or instead an efficiency loss / market distortion — and whether those come apart. Whether a gift with reciprocity expected down the line is meaningfully different from a bribe. Whether scale changes the category, i.e. one envelope to one official versus a whole clientelist machine where a party trades jobs and favors for votes as its normal mode of operation. Whether the right standard is what local law says, or something that local law can fail to track. We'd like each leaf to carry the name of the position — some version of the Transparency-International-style universalist, the modernization/patrimonialist story where corruption is incomplete transition to rational-legal bureaucracy, a strong cultural particularist, the "grease the wheels" economist, a legal positivist, and whatever else genuinely lives in the space — plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment.

Two more things. Please flag any pair of leaves the tree can't really separate rather than pretending a question splits them (I suspect the universalist and the legal positivist collapse together in any country whose law happens to codify the NGO definition, but you tell us). And for any combination of branch answers that no one actually occupies, say so and take a stab at whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Keep it to a one-page handout — the students will get it the session before we visit a local councillor who's been accused of favoritism, and we want them arguing about the tree, not reading it. Don't worry about citing literature; we want the classification to stand on its own. Also if you could mark with an asterisk the nodes where we suspect an anthropology student and an economics student will reliably part ways, that'd be a nice touch for planning the discussion.
