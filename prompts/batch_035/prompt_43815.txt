My dad died three weeks ago and I'm giving the eulogy next saturday. Pretty much his whole adult life he ran a virtue chart straight out of Franklin's Autobiography -- thirteen virtues, a little grid he drew himself, a black mark for every slip, one virtue at a time, rotating through the list. He knew the Autobiography basically by heart, especially the parts people gloss over: that Franklin admitted he never got Order under control even after years of trying, that humility only made it onto the list late because a Quaker friend told him he was proud, and the speckled axe story at the end, where the guy having his axe polished finally says he thinks he'll keep it speckled. Dad loved that bit. His take was always that the chart was never about coming out clean, and the trying was the actual product. He'd get annoyed in two directions: at people who treated the whole thing as a self-help gimmick from the 1700s, and at people who tried to talk him up as some serene wise saint. He'd have called that lying -- and sincerity was number seven on his list, which he would definately have pointed out.

The room will be maybe half people from his little reading club, the ones who did the Junto thing with him and know the Autobiography cold, and half family who thought the chart was a charming quirk and never quite got why he kept it up after it was obviously not "working." I need the eulogy to do three things at once: not canonize him (a claim that he achieved the virtues would be, by his own lights and by Franklin's, exactly the pride the humility entry was added to catch), not apologize for the project either (making the chart out to be a quirk would undo the thing he most believed about himself), and land for both halves of the room. Somewhere in the middle I want his real failure in there -- thirty-plus years and he never once beat Order, his desk was a disaster the day he died -- but held up as part of what made the whole thing work rather than as a caveat tacked on at the end.

Lead with the eulogy itself, first word to last, no preamble and no restating any of this back at me. If you want to say anything about how you handled the constraints, put it after the eulogy and keep it short.
