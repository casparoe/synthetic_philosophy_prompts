I'm a long-form journalist who covers science and ideas, currently reporting a feature on how different societies draw the line between a bribe and a social obligation. For the piece, I have persuaded two scholars to attempt something like an adversarial collaboration: one is a committed universalist in the mold of the transparency movement, who holds that corruption is a single wrong kind of act — the diversion of a public role toward private advantage — wherever and whenever it occurs; the other is a political anthropologist of patron-client relations, who holds that much of what the universalist condemns is a functioning moral economy of obligation among kin, patrons, and clients that predates the modern state and partially substitutes for it. They have agreed, in principle, to co-sign a single statement.

Draft that joint statement for them. It should contain the following sections, in this order.

1. "Where they agree" — about 250 words, as a numbered list. I want substantive claims both could sign: things about what officials owe, about the difference between a gift and a payment, about when reciprocity becomes extraction, about the historical and institutional conditions under which each side's preferred regime works.

2. "The disagreements, in their own words" — a table of three to five rows. Each row states one disagreement twice: once in wording the universalist would sign, once in wording the anthropologist would sign. Do not let either column strawman the other. If a disagreement turns out to be partly verbal — each side using "corruption" for different things — say so in the table itself.

3. "Cruxes and the tests both accept" — about 400 words. I suspect one crux is whether impartiality in public roles is only instrumentally valuable (a design feature that happens to make modern states work) or non-instrumentally valuable (part of what the role is for, so that favoritism wrongs citizens even in a system that delivers). Another may be whether thick trust inside dense networks can genuinely substitute for thin trust in anonymous institutions. Identify these and any others you find, and for each, name a test, case, or kind of evidence both parties would agree bears on it — a natural experiment, a comparative institutional outcome, a controlled hypothetical — with each side's prediction written down in advance.

4. "Classifier cases: committed verdicts" — about 300 words. I will supply four cases; the statement must classify each. (a) A mayor awards a road contract to her cousin, whose bid is five percent higher than the lowest but who has the best local track record and will hire from the municipality; (b) a contractor, after winning a clean and genuinely competitive tender, sends the procurement officer an expensive gift six months later, the two having had no prior relationship; (c) a border officer in a system where a small routine gratuity is customary on both sides of the transaction, where refusal causes delay and offense, and where the officer has no discretion over the outcome; (d) a family that secures a hospital admission for a relative through personal ties to an administrator when the formal queue is effectively frozen and non-ties get worse care. For each case, take a definite position on what the jointly signable verdict is — corrupt, not corrupt, or corrupt under a precise institutional description you spell out — and give the reasoning both could sign. If you judge that no joint verdict is possible for a case, you may mark it unresolved, but only after saying exactly which crux from section 3 blocks agreement and what each side would predict would happen if the case were repeated at scale.

5. "Is any of it merely verbal?" — about 150 words.

One demand about the spirit of the document: this is not a both-sides feature. Wherever the collaboration could converge on a definite claim or classification, state it plainly and defend it with reasons both parties would endorse; reserve "unresolved" for genuine cruxes, and then make the residual disagreement exact rather than vague.
