I've never studied philosophy or sociology — I work in payroll — but for a few years now I've been keeping a mental list of my own reactions to generational labels, and I'd like someone to tell me whether there's a principle behind them, because my current best attempt keeps failing on my own examples.

Here are my verdicts. Someone born on 31 December 1964 is a Baby Boomer and someone born the next day is Generation X, and I think that difference is basically nothing: no real boundary there. Yet someone born in 1946 and someone born in 1964 are both Boomers, and to me they seem like genuinely different kinds of people, so the label is doing something wrong at the inside, not just at the edges. A woman born in 1997, who by every scheme I've seen is Gen Z, but who grew up as the youngest of four with hand-me-down music and humour from the 1990s, feels to me more like a Millennial than like her classmates. A man who moved to Britain at twenty-five in 2012 gets called a Millennial on every form he fills in, and I think that's flatly wrong — the label has no grip on him at all, even though it's correct by date. The cohort that lived through the Second World War feels to me like the most real generation of all, more real than any of the marketing ones — but the war cohort included five-year-olds and fifty-year-olds, so if it's a generation, then a generation isn't a band of birth years at all, and I can't make that fit with the rest. And I've noticed that we apply American labels worldwide: someone the American schemes would call Gen X who instead came of age during a civil war elsewhere — I don't think the American label describes them, but I also don't think their war cohort and our Gen X are the same kind of group, and I'm not sure why.

My own rough principle has been: a generation is real when its members shared the events and technologies of their formative years, say roughly ages ten to twenty-five. That handles the immigrant and maybe the hand-me-down sister, but it makes the 1946/1964 pair even worse (their formative years barely overlap, so by my own principle they shouldn't share a generation, yet the label says they do, and the label is the only reason anyone groups them), and it doesn't tell me whether the wartime cohort counts, since their formative events were shared by people of every age.

So my request is this. Test my principle against my cases, repair it or replace it, and give me whatever principle or small set of principles best captures my verdicts — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you haven't seen. Tell me what it predicts about two cases I haven't mentioned: a person born right on a boundary who is deeply typical of the earlier cohort in every visible way, and a whole country whose history diverged so sharply at one moment that everyone over thirty has one formative world and everyone under thirty another. And be honest with me about which of my verdicts no sensible principle can save, because I suspect the wartime one and the marketing-label ones can't all survive together, and I'd rather know which to give up than be handed something that keeps everything by becoming vacuous.

Please write your answer in British English.
