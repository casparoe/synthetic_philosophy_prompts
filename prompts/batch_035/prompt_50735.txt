Next month I present to my department's moral philosophy seminar a paper arguing that Essay I of Nietzsche's *On the Genealogy of Morality* is not, contrary to what nearly everyone in that room assumes, a debunking argument against Christian morality. The standard reading there takes Nietzsche to be claiming that tracing 'good' to the slave revolt in morality, and to ressentiment, contaminates the value and undercuts its authority. My thesis is that Essay I's genealogical findings leave the evaluative question open, and that the essay's real target is a structural self-misunderstanding internal to Christian moral consciousness: a misidentification of who does the valuing, which I locate in the lambs-and-birds-of-prey passage in section 13. The audience is mostly analytic moral philosophers trained on the contemporary genealogy-as-debunking literature, who hear 'genealogy' and immediately reach for the question of whether origins transmit epistemic taint. There are also two Nietzsche specialists: one a naturalist reader who holds that Nietzsche is committed all the way down to the unreality of moral values, and one who regards the whole debunking/vindication framework as an anachronistic Anglophone retrofit of a book that is not doing anything recognizable as argument. Finally there is one extremely careful Kantian who has just enough Nietzsche to be dangerous. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I will actually face, roughly in the order they will land, phrased in the vocabulary each questioner would actually use; for each objection, a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft; what I should concede up front and what I must hold; and the question I am privately hoping no one asks.
