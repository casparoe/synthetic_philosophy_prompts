My best friend and I spent most of last Friday evening arguing about Dennett's account of real patterns and the three stances, and I haven't been able to shake the argument since. The specific point of contention was whether the intentional stance is ever genuinely more than a convenience. I took the line that for any system we casually treat as a believer — a chess engine, a navigation app, a modern language model — we could in principle substitute the design stance, since we can always redescribe the system's competence in terms of what it was built or optimized to do, and the residual talk of beliefs and desires is just a handy shorthand for that. My friend countered with the real patterns idea: the physical-level description of such a system would be astronomically unmanageable, belief attribution makes real predictions that nothing at the lower level conveniently delivers, and a pattern can be real in the way a center of gravity is real even while being reducible in principle — so, he said, I was testing the intentional stance by a standard (strict indispensability) that Dennett never accepted in the first place. Things got really tangled when we turned to a chess engine that sacrifices material because it "expects" a kingside attack to fail: my friend insisted the expectation is a real pattern in exactly his sense, while I protested that once you know the system was optimized for winning, the attribution of expectation adds nothing that a design-level story about its evaluation function can't give you, except rhetorical color. He then accused me of assuming the stances are competitors rather than descriptions at different grains, which I'm honestly not sure I wasn't doing.

Please write an essay that takes up our dispute: is the intentional stance an irreducible mode of description in its own right, or is it a user-friendly compression of information that the design stance already contains? Weigh the case on each side — the real-patterns defense of intentional-state attribution, the worry that design-level knowledge "undercuts" belief talk, the question of whether the practical unmanageability of physical-level description can carry philosophical weight — and don't end on a non-committal note; defend a verdict, even if it's a qualified one (e.g., that the intentional stance earns its keep only for systems whose design we don't know, or only for systems that are inexplicably complex in a specific sense — but say which, and why). If you think the whole framing of our argument is off — that my indispensability test is confused, that "belief" is being used ambiguously across our two positions, or that the chess engine example can't settle anything — please say so plainly at the outset rather than dutifully answering the question as posed, and then explain what the better question would be. I'd rather be corrected than flattered, and so, frankly, would my friend.
