I'm a PhD student in political theory and I keep running into a move I don't have a name for. Someone condemns a practice they're obviously implicated in — a tenured professor denouncing the adjunct system, someone flying to a conference to lament aviation — and the expected "look who's talking" objection never lands, because they've already made it themselves. They open with something like "as someone who's complicit in this, I know I'm part of the problem" and then proceed to blame exactly as before. The self-indictment seems to work purely as a license: it costs nothing, changes nothing about their conduct, and yet it shields the blame from the charge of hypocrisy. Is there an established concept for this — blame that's insulated against the hypocrisy objection by a pre-emptive confession? The nearest things I know are tu quoque and the circumstantial ad hominem, but those are the objections, not the move that blocks them, and neither captures the oddity that the confession seems to be doing no work except disarming. If there's nothing established, can you name it, define it, and tell me where it's edges are — e.g. does it still count when the confession is plainly insincere, or when the person quietly stops the implicated behavior afterward and never mentions the earlier blame? I don't just want a label; I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is and isn't. Please answer in continuous prose, no headings, bullets, bold, or tables — the way you'd write an email.
