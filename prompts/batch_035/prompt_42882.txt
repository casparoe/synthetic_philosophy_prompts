I am a novelist at work on a chapter in which two sisters, dissolving a bakery they co-owned for twenty years, divide its assets under the guidance of a retired mathematician acting as an unpaid mediator. I should like the chapter to turn on a genuine, checkable piece of bargaining theory rather than on sentiment: I understand that the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution and the Nash bargaining solution can recommend different agreements, and I want my mediator to arrive at the ending I have planned — the sister with less money of her own keeping the shopfront — because the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution favours her, not because he is fond of her. Please construct the case to the following specifications. First, one paragraph of realistic, quotidian scene-setting: no game-show payoffs and no exotic preferences. Second, the assets must include at least two indivisible or entangled items, so the problem cannot be reduced to splitting a sum of cash; the disagreement point should be the ordinary ruin of dissolving the partnership through solicitors; and the numbers should be anchored to things a reader could verify, such as appraised values, rents, and the sisters' outside options. Third, the setup must be asymmetric, but the asymmetry must arise naturally from differences in wealth and attachment rather than from a rigged utility function — I do not want a case that stipulates the verdict instead of generating it. Then: state the disagreement point and each sister's ideal payoff, compute both the Nash solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, and show that they diverge in the direction the chapter requires. Finally, identify which structural feature of the feasible set is doing the work; explain what happens to the divergence if the sisters' valuations are made more alike or the frontier more nearly linear; and give me the objection a defender of the Nash solution would raise, ideally in a form one of the sisters could deliver to the mediator at the door on her way out.
