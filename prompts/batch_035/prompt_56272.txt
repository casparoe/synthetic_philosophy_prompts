A guy in my study group claims Bertrand's chord paradox doesn't just embarrass the principle of indifference, it destroys it entirely, and honestly his argument looks closer to right than I want it to. Walking through it:

(1) Principle of indifference: when you have no reason to favor one possibility over another, assign them equal probabilities.

(2) In Bertrand's setup — pick a chord of a circle "at random" and ask how likely it is to be longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle — there are several equally natural ways to be even-handed toward chords. Pick two points uniformly on the circumference and join them. Or pick a radius and a point uniformly along it, and take the chord through that point perpendicular to the radius. Or pick a point uniformly in the disk's interior and take the chord that has it as its midpoint. These give 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 respectively.

(3) In each of these, before you've settled on a method, you have no reason to favor any one chord over any other — that's the whole meaning of "at random" here. So by (1) each method's uniform distribution is licensed.

(4) Yet they hand out different probabilities to the very same event.

(5) If a rule lets the same question get answers 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 depending on how we happened to set the problem up, then the "probability" is tracking our representation, not the chords.

(6) So the principle of indifference licenses conflicting assignments in this case — it's inconsistent, not merely uninformative.

(7) An inconsistent rule can't justify anything. So the principle never licenses any assignment, anywhere.

(8) Which includes the fair die. Nobody thinks Bertrand's paradox shows you can't justify assigning 1/6 to each face of a die you know nothing about except its shape, but that's exactly where (7) lands us.

Something feels off between (6) and (7) — the leap from "conflicting answers in one case" to "no answers in any case" — but I can't articulate what actually separates the chord situation from the die situation. If I have to keep anything I'm keeping (1) and the die. Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost elsewhere? Also curious whether the same move that saves the die tells us anything about which chord answer, if any, is the right one.
