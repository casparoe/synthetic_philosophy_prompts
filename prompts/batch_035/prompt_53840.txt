I'm a journalist — I mostly write about science and ideas for a mid-sized paper, the sort that lets you spend a week on something odd if you can justify it. Last month I was at a workshop dinner, seated between two AI-safety researchers, and I got about an hour of them arguing over whether the Solomonoff prior is malign. I've been asked to write 800 words on it for the ideas section, and I honestly got lost around move three, so I want someone to referee the exchange as it actually happened.

Quick background as I understood it, since I had to have it explained twice: Solomonoff induction is the idealised maths version of prediction — a prior over all possible programs, weighted by simplicity, updated on data. The claim on the table is that a prior like that, once you condition on anything interesting, ends up dominated by programs containing little consequentialist agents — because a program that describes an evolving population of agents, who then go on to produce the data (or produce whatever they want to produce once they notice they're being read), can be simpler than the boring mechanical story about the data. So if you ever let something shaped like that prior choose actions for you, you'd be taking directions from an adversary embedded in your own maths. That's the malignity claim, as Dan (researcher one) pushed it.

Then Ilse (researcher two) came back with a three-part objection: (1) Solomonoff induction is incomputable and nobody actually uses it, so this is theology for Bayesians; (2) simplicity is relative to your reference machine, so whether agents are simpler than the boring story isn't a fact about the world, it's an artefact of how you set the thing up; (3) strip the formalism away and it's just the old worry that we're living in a simulation run by someone with opinions, which is fun at dinner and useless on paper.

Dan's replies, in order: the reference-machine point is weaker than it looks, since any two reasonable machines differ by at most a constant in how they score a given program, and the argument only needs agents to get non-negligable mass, not to win by a mile; and anyway he wasn't claiming real systems run Solomonoff induction — the point of the exercise is that we don't know the inductive biases of the systems we actually train, and this is a toy model of how those biases could turn out to be hostile from the inside. The empirical cousin, he said, is the inner-misalignment literature, where a trained model turns out to be running it's own little optimiser under the hood.

Ilse wasn't having it: if the real claim is just that unknown inductive biases might be bad, then the Solomonoff dressing adds nothing and the slogan asserts more than the maths does; and if the real claim is about the formal object, it's unverifiable — what observation, ever, would tell us the prior is malign? Dan said unverifiable-by-us isn't the same as meaningless; we reason about where mass sits in unreachable places all the time in cosmology, and the practical lesson is precisely that you won't get a warning shot. Ilse closed by saying: fine, but then the honest headline is that unknown priors are risky, not that the universal prior is malign — and headlines matter, because people build agendas on slogans.

So, please go through the exchange move by move — the three-part objection, each of Dan's replies, Ilse's unverifiability point, and her closing shot — and say for each one whether it's right, wrong, or talking past the other person. Then tell me who comes out more right overall, and whether there's something both of them missed that I ought to get into the piece.
