As part of my funding I serve as a teaching assistant for a large undergraduate lecture course, Philosophy of Art, even though my own work is at the graduate level — I am currently taking a seminar on art and ethics that covers much of the same territory in far more depth. For the course's midterm, students answered the question below, worth forty percent of the exam. As a calibration exercise, each TA grades a set of anonymized answers from a section other than their own before we compare results. I would like a second opinion on one of the answers I have been assigned: please assign it a letter grade (with plus/minus) and give me point-by-point comments justifying the grade — what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong, how serious each error is relative to what the question demands, and what a full-credit answer would have needed to do.

The midterm question read:

A work of art can be morally flawed in two distinct ways: through the moral character of its maker (Caravaggio killed a man in 1606; Leni Riefenstahl made films for the Nazi regime) or through the work's own moral perspective, that is, the responses to its subjects that it invites or endorses (Riefenstahl's Triumph of the Will glorifies what it depicts). Can a morally flawed work nonetheless be a great work of art? In your answer, define autonomism (the view that the moral and aesthetic evaluation of a work are independent), ethicism (the view that a work's moral defects always count as aesthetic defects, and its moral merits as aesthetic merits), and immoralism (the view that a work's moral defects can sometimes be aesthetic merits). Argue that one of these positions is the most plausible, and respond to what you take to be the strongest objection to it.

The answer I must grade:

The question whether immoral art can be good art is, at bottom, the question whether the work can be separated from the artist. If the artist's vices do not seep into the canvas, the morality of the maker is one thing and the value of the made thing another; if they do seep in, no such separation is possible and the moralist wins.

Autonomism holds that aesthetic and moral evaluation are independent. Ethicism holds that a moral defect is an aesthetic defect — always. Immoralism holds the opposite: the immorality of a work is typically the source of its aesthetic power, since great art by its nature transgresses.

Autonomism has a distinguished pedigree. Kant argued that the judgment of taste is disinterested: we take pleasure in the beautiful not because it gratifies desire or serves any purpose, but in the mere representation of it. If aesthetic pleasure is detached from all practical and moral interest in this way, then moral facts about the work or its maker cannot figure in an aesthetic verdict at all. Autonomism follows directly.

That said, the moralist is not without resources. Our critical vocabulary is saturated with moral terms: we call a film sentimental, a novel cruel, a poem humane, and these seem to be aesthetic judgments, not moral reports smuggled in from outside. The autonomist owes us an account of why this vocabulary is so at home in criticism.

It is also true that a moral defect can damage a work when it frustrates the response the work solicits. Triumph of the Will wants us to exult in the Nuremberg rally, and a viewer who knows what the rally led to resists; the intended effect fails, and it fails because of the film's moral character. That is just what the ethicist predicts: the moral defect shows up as an aesthetic one.

But ethicism collapses under its own extremity once we turn from the work to the artist. Caravaggio killed a man and went on painting; his David with the Head of Goliath, with his own face on the severed head, is a late work. If every moral defect of the artist were an aesthetic defect of the painting, that picture would be worse art for the homicide, which no one can seriously believe. Moralism and autonomism are the only live options, and moralism is refuted; so autonomism stands, and the answer to the question is yes. Where the flaw is in the maker, it is simply irrelevant to the work's value; where the flaw is in the work itself, it counts against the work only when it blocks the response the work is aiming at.
