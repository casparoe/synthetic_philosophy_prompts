I'm a PhD student in evolutionary biology — I mostly do empirical work on social behaviour in insects, so my philosophy is honestly pretty rusty. I'm taking a philosophy of biology seminar and got roped into presenting next week on the units of selection debate. There's one move in the gene's-eye-view literature that I keep failing to articulate properly, and I think it's the crux of the whole thing.

As I understand it, the argument goes something like: whatever selection does to organisms or groups, the only things that reliably persist across generations, and hence the only things whose "interests" selection could be said to track, are genes. So even if it's convenient to talk about organism-level or group-level adaptation, gene-level bookkeeping is what selection "really" consists in. My seminar leader keeps saying this kind of argument conflates two different roles a unit can play in evolution — roughly, being the thing whose frequencies you record and compare across generations (a bookkeeping or replicator role), versus being the thing that adaptations exist for the sake of, the beneficiary of the process (a causal role). She gestured at the old distinction between selection of a thing and selection for a property: selection of genes happens whenever gene frequencies change, but selection for a property is a claim about what caused those genes to do better. I sort of see it with the standard example — heterozygote advantage at a locus, where the allele frequencies are doing all the accounting but the causal story is about organism phenotypes in an environment — but I can't quite spell out how the bookkeeping argument is supposed to slide between the two roles, or why the slide is supposed to be illegitimate rather than just a change of subject.

So could you explain this for me at a gut level? In particular: does the gene's-eye view, once the slide is exposed, reduce to an accounting convention (in which case the genic partisans and the group selectionists are just describing the same process in different ledgers, and the fight is about emphasis), or does it still make some substantive claim its rivals deny? And if it does reduce to bookkeeping, what would even count as evidence for one ledger over another — is there any empirical case, not just a preferred description, where the choice of unit does real work? Please answer in British English, and don't feel any need to name-drop a bibliography at me — I want the reasoning laid out plainly enough that I can reconstruct it at the seminar without notes. Sorry if this is a well-worn puzzle btw, it's just that every explanation I've found either assumes I already get the distinction or hand-waves at "genes are immortal", which is exactly the bit I don't get.
