me and my friend Priya argued until like 2am about advance directives and I ended up with a list of cases where my gut is really firm but I can't find the thread connecting them. here they are with my verdicts:

my grandmother signed a directive years ago saying no feeding tube once she couldn't recognize family. now she has dementia, doesn't know any of us, but she's honestly kind of content — giggles at her soaps, likes her lunch. my gut says honor it anyway, tube stays out, even though it means the content person dies.

a climbing buddy used to say constantly that if he was ever paralyzed he'd want to be let go. then he had his accident, and eighteen months later he's racing wheelchairs and planning trips and clearly glad to be alive. I'd never in a million years hold him to what he used to say.

a jehovah's witness refuses blood while fully competent, then starts bleeding out and semi-conscious gasps "save me." I don't transfuse. past refusal wins.

a guy with schizoaffective disorder, while clear-headed, signs an involuntary committment contract that kicks in whenever he stops his meds, explicitly waiving his future refusals. later he's floridly psychotic and saying no hospital. I'd commit him per his own contract.

my friend's mom never signed anything but told everyone for twenty years that she'd never want to be kept alive in a vegetative-ish state. when it happened, the family followed the dinner-table statements, and I think that was right, though it felt weaker than a signed form.

and the one that keeps bugging me: a woman at 22, terrified right after watching her mom die of cancer, writes a directive saying if she ever needs a colostomy, let her go. at 60 she has dementia and needs one, and from everything about the 38 years in between — she loved her life, joked about living with the bag — the 22-year-old's horror clearly wasn't her settled values. my gut says ignore it.

priya's take was that I only honor the past self when the present self has lost competence, which makes the whole thing about the present person's welfare with the directive as a convenient tool. mine was something vaguer about a person being a whole life and the directive speaking for the parts that are gone. neither of us could get the grandmother and the colostomy woman apart — both directives signed when competent, both present selves demented.

so: is there one principle that gets all six verdicts, or close? state it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it can't save, and tell me what it predicts for a case I didn't list — like whether a still-competent person can revoke the committment contract they wrote for their own psychotic episodes, or whether the dinner-table statements would survive being one remark instead of twenty years of them.

lead with the principle itself in your first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my cases back at me.
