At my university's political philosophy reading group we have just finished a session on Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia, and afterwards a friend gave me her summary of the famous utility monster passage as a way of explaining why she thinks the whole utilitarian research programme is a dead end. Her version went something like this: 'Nozick proved that utilitarianism is self-refuting. Imagine a creature that gets enormously more utility from any resource than anyone else does — so much more that giving it everything, even feeding the rest of humanity to it, would maximize total welfare. Utilitarianism is committed to saying we ought to do exactly that, and since that conclusion is monstrous, the theory is refuted outright. The deeper lesson is that utilitarianism treats society's welfare as one big pool to be maximized, which ignores the separateness of persons — and that is precisely the argument Rawls deployed against utilitarianism, which is why Rawls insists on the difference principle. Nozick's own response, his theory of rights as side constraints, follows directly: rights can never be overridden, no matter how much aggregate utility is at stake.'

Parts of this struck me as right and parts made me uneasy, but I don't know the literature well enough to say where it goes wrong, and she is the most well-read person in the group, so I didn't push back. Could you audit it for me? Please go claim by claim: which parts would a careful utilitarian concede as an accurate statement of the challenge, which are overstatements or conflations (I suspect the use of "proved" and "refuted" is doing some work, and I have a vague sense that Rawls's separateness-of-persons objection, whatever exactly it amounts to, is not simply the same argument as the monster), and whether the monster really threatens every form of utilitarianism equally — does it bite differently for total versus average views, or for hedonistic versus preference versions? I'd also like to know how utilitarians have actually replied to the monster, and whether any of those replies blunt my friend's conclusion or merely bite the bullet. Finally, is her characterisation of Nozick's side constraints as rights that "can never be overridden" a fair gloss, and does it follow from the monster argument, as she claims, or is it a separate commitment in the book? I care about the distinctions here, not just a verdict on who won the argument.
