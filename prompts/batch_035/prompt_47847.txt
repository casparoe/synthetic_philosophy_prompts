I'm a high school senior taking an elective called Philosophy and Public Life, and we've spent the past few weeks on John Dewey. I understood the part where he treats democracy as a way of life rather than merely a form of government, but our teacher contrasted that with what she called the census picture of democracy: voters walk into the booth with preferences already fixed by their private interests, the votes are counted, the majority wins, and nobody's beliefs or desires have changed by the end of the day. On that picture, she said, democracy is basically an adding machine. Dewey, apparently, wants to claim something much stranger — that democracy is a form of inquiry, something like scientific experimentation carried on by other means, in which preferences are supposed to be transformed rather than merely counted, both through deliberation with people unlike yourself and through experiencing the consequences of the policies you live under. The adding machine I can picture. The laboratory I cannot.

Could you explain, as concretely as possible, how the analogy between democratic politics and scientific experimentation is actually supposed to work? In particular: in Dewey's picture of a public deliberating about some problem — say, a town deciding what to do about a polluted river — what plays the role of the hypothesis, what plays the role of the observation or data, and what would count as the experiment succeeding or failing? I have a second, related confusion, which is that the analogy seems to break down precisely where experiments matter most: you cannot rerun the river policy under controlled conditions, with a control group and a randomized treatment, and you only find out whether it worked years later, on everyone at once. It feels like the thing that makes experiments informative is exactly what politics can't have. Does Dewey have an answer to that — is the idea that history as a whole is one long experiment, or that a society can run many small local experiments in parallel (different towns, states, agencies trying different approaches and comparing notes), or something else entirely?

What I'd most like is a gut-level picture I could actually use in my term paper — something that would let me explain the view to a classmate who hasn't done the reading, and then say honestly where the analogy is strongest and where it stretches. Please don't give me a survey of what various people have written; I have the readings for that. What I'm missing is a way to see how the pieces are supposed to fit together, and where the fit is loose.
