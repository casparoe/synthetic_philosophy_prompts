I'm teaching an undergraduate seminar on determinism and predictability — Laplace's demon, sensitive dependence on initial conditions, the difference between epistemic unpredictability and ontological indeterminacy — and I want to pair the readings with a play: Tom Stoppard's Arcadia. On its face the play is almost too obliging: Valentine Coverly spends the second half discovering that the old game books he's feeding into his computer aren't noise at all but an iterated algorithm for modeling the grouse population, while a century and a half earlier the teenage Thomasina is already groping toward iteration and toward the idea that Newton's equations, run backwards and forwards without an attractor, will someday produce a universe of disorder rather than a celestial clock. And threaded through both eras is the second law: the universe cooling, information leaking, Thomasina's drawings of heat as "theattraction that Newton left out" of his equations. But I'm worried the play, and the critical literature on it, blurs together several things my students should be keeping apart — chaos in the technical sense (deterministic dynamics with exponential amplification of initial uncertainty), randomness proper, and thermodynamic irreversibility — and I'd like a reading sharp enough to make those distinctions pedagogically useful rather than papering over them.

So: read Arcadia as a play about predictability, and tell me what it actually dramatizes about the philosophy of chaos and determinism. Where does Stoppard's use of the mathematics hold up — is Valentine's point about the grouse data genuinely a point about sensitive dependence, or about something else (model identification, the recoverability of structure from noisy data)? Is Thomasina best read as an intuitive chaos theorist, or is her real interest entropy and the arrow of time, and does the play know it's changed subjects? What do we make of the determinism question given that the play's structure seems to side with the epistemologists: the past half of the play is reconstructed by scholars from fragments — letters, drawings, a game book, a tortoise — who keep getting things wrong (the Byron theories, the Chater identities), which looks like an argument that even a fully deterministic past is epistemically unrecoverable, which is a different claim from the future being unpredictable. And does the ending, with the two eras waltzing simultaneously to the same music, resolve the heat-death pessimism or just counterpoint it aesthetically?

I want the reading to be faithful in both directions: don't reduce the play to an illustrated lecture on nonlinear dynamics, but don't let the theatrical texture excuse mathematical confusions either — identify the confusions precisely, then ask whether they're Stoppard's or the characters', since Valentine, at least, seems to know what he's talking about. Finally, tell me what the play gives my students that the standard philosophy-of-science arguments don't: does dramatizing iteration, loss, and irreversibility on stage — where the audience is literally watching the two timelines decohere from separate scenes into a shared waltz — add anything to the debate about whether determinism and predictability come apart, or is it merely a decorative analogy? If there are places where the honest answer is that the metaphor breaks, say so plainly; my students will be tested on the mathematics, and I don't want the play teaching them folklore.
