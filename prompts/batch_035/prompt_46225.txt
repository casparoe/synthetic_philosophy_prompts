In my decision theory seminar, one member keeps deflating the Ellsberg paradox by analogy. An urn with unknown composition, she says, is just like an urn known to contain 50% red and 50% black: if your evidence is symmetric between red and black, indifference licenses assigning each color probability 1/2, so an ambiguous bet simply *is* a risky bet, and preferring the known urn is like preferring one fair coin flip to an identical fair coin flip — a flat-out error rather than a distinctive attitude toward ambiguity.

You are an expert in decision theory and the philosophy of economics. Please think carefully about how far this analogy holds. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the objective chance, the symmetry of evidence, the probability of each draw, and the reduction of the compound lottery to a simple one — and then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, consider whether the observed Ellsberg preferences (in both the two-urn case and the three-color case) can be reproduced by *any* single assignment of probabilities to the events, uniform or otherwise, and whether the coherence arguments that make ordinary probabilities compulsory carry over to a situation where the composition is unknown and unknowable at the time of choice. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for her conclusion from those that don't, and say whether the verdict — that ambiguity-averse choice is irrational — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If it doesn't, suggest what a better analogy for the ambiguous urn would be.
