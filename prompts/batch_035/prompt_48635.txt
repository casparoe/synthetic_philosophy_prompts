A popular book on philosophy of science I've been reading has a chapter on modality that summarizes David Lewis's modal realism, and I'd like a careful audit of it, because parts struck me as off but I can't pin down where. The chapter says, roughly: Lewis argued that there are infinitely many possible worlds, all of them exactly as concrete and physical as our own, spread out with ours across a single vast multiverse. Everything that could have happened does happen in one of these worlds — somewhere there's a world where the dinosaurs never died out, and a world just like ours except that you wore a different shirt this morning. Lewis's proof was essentially an inference to the best explanation: positing all these worlds is the simplest, most economical theory of modality on offer, since it lets us translate every modal claim — about what is possible or necessary — into non-modal terms, as a claim about what goes on somewhere or other in the multiverse. So when I say I could have gone to medical school, that just means that somewhere out there is another person who is literally me — the very same individual — wearing a stethoscope. The book then says that although most philosophers found the view too extravagant, Lewis had a ready answer to the stock objection (the incredulous stare): his theory costs nothing ontologically beyond what physics has already discovered, since Everett's many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics tells us that parallel universes exist whether we like it or not, and modal realism simply takes the physics at face value. The chapter closes by remarking that Lewis's view therefore stands or falls with the multiverse physics — if the Everett interpretation wins, modal realism is vindicated, and if it loses, modal realism goes down with it.

A few things about this leave me uneasy. I recall reading that Lewis was famous for caring about parsimony, so calling his ontology the economical one seems backwards. I also have a vague memory that modal realism and the quantum many-worlds are supposed to be different things, though I couldn't say why. And something about the stethoscope example nagged at me, though I can't articulate it.

Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me: which parts would Lewis himself (or a contemporary defender of his view) accept as stated; which parts are subtle distortions — conflations of things the view carefully keeps apart, or accurate ingredients put together into a conclusion they don't support; and which, if any, are simply wrong. In particular, I'd like you to say precisely what the relationship between Lewisian possible worlds and the Everettian multiverse is supposed to be — why a philosopher could accept one and not the other, and whether either could vindicate or undermine the other. Finally, does the book's closing remark follow from anything actually true in its summary: could a discovery in physics, one way or the other, settle whether modal realism is right?
