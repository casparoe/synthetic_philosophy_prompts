I've been reading about the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics in my spare time — just popular articles and some older physics books I had lying around — and I've worked myself into a puzzle about probability that I can't resolve on my own. I have no physics training, so I may be making an elementary mistake, but I'd genuinely like to know where.

As I understand it, many-worlds says that when a quantum measurement happens, the universe splits: every possible outcome actually occurs, each in its own branch, and each branch contains a full copy of me who observes that outcome. Here is what troubles me. Suppose I set up a measurement where the textbook calculation gives a 99% chance of outcome A and a 1% chance of outcome B. Under many-worlds, both outcomes happen: one future copy of me sees A, and another sees B. So it is absolutely certain that a version of me will see A, and equally certain that a version of me will see B. That makes the 99%-versus-1% talk look empty to me. Either an outcome happens in some branch or it doesn't, and every one of them does — so shouldn't each outcome be assigned probability 1? And if probabilities have to be split among the outcomes instead, the natural thing would seem to be 50-50, since there are two branches and both equally exist.

I've also read that deriving the Born rule — the rule that probabilities go with the amplitudes squared rather than with anything else — is regarded as the central unsolved problem for this interpretation. But on my picture it looks straightforward to fix. If a 99%-to-1% event is really a 99-to-1 split of the world, then there are ninety-nine copies of me in the A-branch for every one in the B-branch, and the probability of seeing A is just ninety-nine out of a hundred: the fraction of the copies. Count the branches, add up the percentages. Why is this treated as a deep difficulty when the answer appears to be simple counting?

So my questions are these. First, given that every outcome occurs somewhere, in what sense is anything uncertain at all in many-worlds — what exactly is the thing that has a 99% chance of happening? Second, is my counting proposal a legitimate solution to the probability problem, or does it fail somewhere? If I've gone wrong, I'd like you to identify the precise step where the reasoning breaks rather than just telling me the consensus view. Finally, please tell me what would change your mind: which specific assumption, if it turned out to hold, would make branch-counting the right approach after all, or would rescue the thought that each outcome is simply guaranteed because it occurs in some branch. I'd also like you to flag where your own answer is most likely to go astray, since I suspect this topic is one where confident-sounding explanations can paper over real gaps.
