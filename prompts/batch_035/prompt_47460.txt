I cover environmental policy for a regional newspaper, and I'm now several weeks into a story that has quietly turned into the hardest piece I've had to structure. The background: after our county's third severe water shortage in a decade, the commission of 2019 adopted a long-range conservation framework — staged restrictions triggered by reservoir levels, mandatory efficiency standards for new construction, and so on. What makes it newsworthy today is a clause in that framework, which its drafters openly defended at the time: any suspension or material revision of the restrictions requires a supermajority of the commission plus a public referendum, and even then only in a declared emergency. The framers said, in almost these words, that they were wiring their successors' hands to the table so that the first wet winter wouldn't undo fifteen years of planning. Last month, a majority elected on an explicit promise to loosen the standards took office and discovered they couldn't, at least not by ordinary legislation.

The letters and town-hall comments fall into two camps, each with its favorite comparison. One side says this is a person with a drinking problem asking friends to hide the car keys: the community made a considered promise to its future self, and honoring it is fidelity, not coercion. The other side counters that the alcoholic's keys were his to hand over, whereas the reservoir and the commission's authority were never the 2019 board's property to lock away — they belong to whoever the town elects now, so the arrangement is a past majority quietly outvoting the present one. A retired judge wrote to me insisting that we'd never accept a statute that could never be repealed, and that the whole apparatus of supermajorities and referenda is just an entrenched statute wearing a costume. The framers' allies reply, with some heat, that plenty of things we regard as legitimate government are hard to undo on purpose.

Two further complications keep me from writing a simple two-sides piece. First, most of the development the restrictions now bear down on happened after 2019, so the people most burdened by the framework are largely people who never voted for or against its drafters — they moved into a commitment, which the keys-comparison doesn't seem to capture at all. Second, I genuinely don't know how to characterize what happened at the last election: if the new majority ran on loosening the standards, can we really say the town consented to keep them? My question, then, is this: under what conditions, if any, is it legitimate for a political community to bind its future selves in this way — where exactly is the line between a promise worth keeping and a dead hand on the living — and do the two complications I've described (newcomers who never consented, and a subsequent election that arguably expressed the opposite will) fall on one side of that line or the other? I'd be grateful for a principled way of drawing it, not just a verdict on our local mess, since I suspect our county won't be the last to try this.
