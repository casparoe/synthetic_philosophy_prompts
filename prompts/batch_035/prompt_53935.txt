A popular book on great intellectual puzzles devotes a page to Gödel's ontological proof, summarizing it as follows:

"Gödel, the greatest logician of the twentieth century, took up Leibniz's version of the ontological argument and recast it in modal logic, the branch of logic that handles possibility and necessity. He began with a definition: a property is positive if it is good, in the moral sense, for a thing to have it. He then assumed that any property implied by a positive property is itself positive, and that the negation of a positive property is never positive. From these modest assumptions it follows that there exists a being possessing every positive property, and that this being exists in every possible world — which is just what theologians mean by God. The striking feature of the proof is that its only substantive premise is that God's existence is possible, and from that weak starting point the modal machinery delivers necessary existence. And since the whole derivation has since been checked by computer theorem provers, God's existence is now a theorem in the same sense as the infinitude of the primes — something one cannot reject without rejecting logic itself."

The middle of this reads like ordinary mathematical exposition, so I'm inclined to trust it, but the final sentence feels too strong. Please audit the passage claim by claim: which claims would a philosopher of religion or modal logician accept as stated, which are simplifications a specialist would let pass in a popular book, and which are outright misstatements? I'm particularly unsure how the proof gets from the axioms to the existence of the being, and whether describing that step as resting on the weak premise that God's existence is possible is accurate. Finally, setting aside anything you find wrong: does the book's conclusion — that God's existence is now a theorem on a par with the infinitude of the primes, undeniable without denying logic — follow from the parts of the summary that are correct?
