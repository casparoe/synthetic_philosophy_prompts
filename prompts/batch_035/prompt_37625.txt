The exam question was this:

Each year for the next 40 years, an investor may expose a fraction f (where 0 ≤ f ≤ 1) of her current wealth to the following gamble: with probability 1/2 the exposed wealth increases by 80%, with probability 1/2 it decreases by 50%. The unexposed wealth is untouched, and nothing else in her life changes her wealth. (a) [5 marks] An advisor recommends f = 1, pointing out that expected wealth then grows by 15% per year. Explain why, nevertheless, the investor who follows this advice ends the 40 years with less than her starting wealth with probability close to 1. (b) [5 marks] Find the f that maximizes her long-run growth rate of wealth. (c) [6 marks] A classmate comments: "This is just Bernoulli's 1738 solution to the St. Petersburg problem in modern dress — if you have logarithmic utility of wealth, expected-utility maximization gives you the answer to (b) without any story about time averages or dynamics." Assess this remark: say what is right in it, and say precisely under what condition maximizing expected log wealth coincides with maximizing the time-average growth rate — and what happens to that coincidence when gambles are additive (fixed sums, not proportional to wealth) rather than multiplicative. (d) [4 marks] Now suppose instead that she is offered, once, a single coin flip: heads she gains £6,000, tails she loses £5,000; the stake cannot be scaled. Her total wealth is £10,000, and she will face no other gamble in her life. What do the expected-value view, the log-utility view, and the growth-rate view each say about accepting, and which of them, if any, is in a position to give advice in this case at all?

And here is the student's answer, verbatim:

(a) With f = 1, expected wealth after N years is W₀(1.15)^N, so the advisor is correct about expectation. Actual wealth after N years is W₀ · 1.8^k · 0.5^(N−k), where k is the number of up years. Log wealth is log W₀ + k log 1.8 + (N−k) log 0.5, and since k/N → 1/2 almost surely, realized log wealth tracks log W₀ + (N/2) log 0.9, which tends to −∞ because log 0.9 < 0. So her wealth converges to zero almost surely even though its expectation explodes. The per-year geometric mean factor is √(1.8 × 0.5) = √0.9 ≈ 0.97, a decline of about 3% a year, and the median outcome after 40 years is about 0.9^20, so she keeps roughly a tenth of her starting wealth. The advisor is right about the expectation and wrong about everything that matters.

(b) The growth rate is g(f) = ½log(1 + 0.8f) + ½log(1 − 0.5f). Setting the derivative, 0.4/(1+0.8f) − 0.25/(1−0.5f), equal to zero gives 0.4 − 0.2f = 0.25 + 0.2f, hence f = 0.375: she should expose 37.5% of her wealth each year. At that fraction g ≈ 0.027, so in the typical run she compounds at roughly 2.7% a year.

(c) The classmate is right, and in fact understates her own point: Bernoulli in 1738 didn't merely anticipate the growth-rate answer — his derivation of the logarithm essentially is the time-average argument, since he arrived at it by considering how a venture should be judged by its effect on the compounding of an agent's fortune across repeated opportunities, not by any psychology of diminishing marginal utility. So there is no daylight between the two routes: maximizing expected log wealth and maximizing the time-average growth rate are one and the same recommendation. The condition under which they coincide is simply that the agent's utility function is logarithmic, and whether the gambles are multiplicative or additive makes no difference to the advice, since the logarithm's diminishing sensitivity operates whatever the dynamics.

(d) Expected value says accept: the expected gain is £500, and since the bet is one-off there are no compounding effects to worry about. Log utility says reject: ½log 16,000 + ½log 5,000 ≈ 9.10 < log 10,000 ≈ 9.21, so accepting would reduce expected utility — at her wealth level, the downside of dropping to £5,000 outweighs the upside of reaching £16,000. The growth-rate view also says reject: maximizing the time average just is maximizing expected log wealth, so it gives the same verdict as log utility in every choice situation, one-off or not. And that seems right — a single gamble is still part of her life trajectory, so the quantity that matters for her long-run prospects is the same whether the bet repeats or not. All three views are in a position to advise; the expected-value view is simply wrong here.
