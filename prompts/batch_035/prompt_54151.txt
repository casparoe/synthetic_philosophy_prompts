I'm the managing editor of a mid-sized academic history journal, and "presentism" has become our reviewers' catch-all complaint: some use it for any moral judgment of past actors, others for any use of modern vocabulary at all, and our authors justly complain that no one can tell what the charge amounts to. Please write a decision procedure our reviewers can run in under half an hour per submission: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a verdict of "not presentism, just contested history"; and where it must hand the call back to human judgment rather than pretend to decide it. In grounding the procedure, keep at least the following distinctions alive, since collapsing them is exactly what makes the charge useless: judging past actors by present moral standards, describing past events in present-day vocabulary, and cases where the concept itself was coined after the things it now picks out — plus the difference between concepts that claim to track a kind we should expect to find in any era and concepts whose content is fixed by a particular social setting. Then run it on two hypothetical submissions: one describing a medieval abbot's lifelong despair as clinical depression, and one arguing that a seventeenth-century hereditary labor caste was racist even though its architects had no concept of biological race. For the record, our board is sympathetic both to present-minded scholarship and to extending the term "racism" backward, so the test of the procedure's honesty is whether it returns, and defends, the verdict we least want to hear on at least one of the two cases — and it should say plainly which calls no procedure of this kind can settle.
