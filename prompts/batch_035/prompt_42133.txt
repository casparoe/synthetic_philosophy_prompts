Write a dialogue between two freinds stuck on a long train ride -- one a hard-line consequentialist who thinks the idea of supererogation is basically a fudge: if an act makes the world go better than any alternative you could've done, how could you possibly not be required to do it? The other one thinks supererogation is close to the whole point of everyday morality -- the saint and the hero are praiseworthy precisely because they went beyond duty, and a morality with no beyond is a morality of drones. They should actually engage with each other's points, not just trade speeches. Get them into the weeds a bit: e.g. whether supererogation requires a real cost to the agent, and whether that sacrifice condition can survive the "cheap hero" problem -- suppose an app on your phone automatically rounds up every purchase and donates the difference, so at some point giving costs you literally nothing and you barely notice it. Does the act then slide from supererogatory to obligatory once the cost vanishes? And is that the right verdict, or does it show that tracking cost was confused all along? Have the consequentialist press hard on the idea that "beyond duty" talk is just a license to ignore the harm you could've prevented, and have the defender come back with something better than vague appeals to morality needing "breathing room." Somewhere in there they should also butt heads over whether praise itself does any real work: if our praise tracks sacrifice rather than outcome, is it tracking something morally relevant, or just our squeamishness about demandingness? They can end without a winner but they should at least manage to locate the actual crux of their disagreement. Keep the register casual, like two smart people arguing to pass the time.
