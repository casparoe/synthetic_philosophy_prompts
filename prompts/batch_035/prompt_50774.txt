So here's my situation. I'm a third-year PhD student — my main thing is social epistemology, with a side interest in virtue ethics that I mostly keep quiet about — and I've been slotted into our department's work-in-progress seminar next month. My thesis, in one breath: the standard puzzle about moral testimony (why does deferring to other people about what to do feel so much fishier than deferring to them about matters of fact?) isn't fundamentally a puzzle about testimony. It's about the kind of knowledge involved. A big chunk of moral knowledge is practical knowledge in the Rylean sense — knowing-how, the sort of thing you have by being able to tell, in context, what matters and what it's like to be acting well — and testimony is a great channel for transmitting propositions but a terrible channel for transmitting skill. So the queasiness about moral deference is basically the queasiness you'd feel about someone claiming they learned to swim from a lecture. On my view, that's the whole phenomenon; you don't need to appeal to autonomy or self-expression or moral understanding as some special epistemic good — those are downstream symptoms of the skill/transmission mismatch.

The audience is the hard part. Our department has four ethicists, two of whom work directly on moral testimony and know that literature inside out — they'll arrive with the objections pre-packaged, probably the autonomy one and the "you've just redescribed the phenomenon, not explained it" one. Then there's a virtue theorist who I suspect will like my thesis more than I do (he'll want it to be about practical wisdom and character, and I don't want to concede that much). One old-school epistemologist who thinks everything worth saying can be said in terms of justification, and one guy who regards Ryle as a marginal figure and thinks anti-intellectualism about knowledge is basically a disease — he's the one I'm least sure how to handle, because my whole setup leans on the knowing-how/know-that distinction and he'll attack the foundation rather than the application.

Format is 20 minutes of talk plus 15 of Q&A, and the seminar convener has told me flat out that if I can't survive the Q&A the paper won't go to the department workshop series, so this prep matters more than the talk itself. Give me the objections in roughly the order this specific crowd will produce them, ordered by how much damage each would actually do, phrased the way they'd phrase them (the ethicists talk differently than the epistemologists — I want to recognize the objections when they come). For each: what to concede, what to hold, a reply I can actually deliver in about two minutes, and where the reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — I think it's something like "if moral knowledge is know-how, why does moral testimony work fine for mundane cases, telling kids not to hit each other and so on," but if there's a worse one lurking, I want that too. And be honest about the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Also flag if any part of my framing is just wrong before I build a defense on it — better I hear it from you than from the seminar.
