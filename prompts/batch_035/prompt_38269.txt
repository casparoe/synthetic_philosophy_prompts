my prof hit us with this argument about pain asymbolia and i can't decide if it's a refutation of hedonism or just a sleight of hand. (1) If pain is intrinsically bad — bad just in virtue of how it feels — then it's bad for whoever feels it, however they feel about feeling it; that's just what "intrinsic" means. (2) Pain asymbolics feel pain: they report it, localize it, rate its intensity, distinguish it from tickles and pressure. (3) But they're not remotely bothered by it — no withdrawal, no grimace, they joke about it and will accept more of it for nothing. (4) And something just isn't bad for you if you're genuinely, informedly indifferent to it. (5) So pain is not intrinsically bad — whatever badness it has comes from the aversion, distress, threat-appraisal stuff normally bundled with it. (6) By parity, whatever makes pleasure good can't be the felt quality either; it's the liking and the wanting around it. (7) So hedonism about wellbeing is dead, since its whole selling point was that pleasure and pain matter in themselves and not because we care about them. Steps all sound fine to me individually, but the thing proves way too much: substitute "the clinically depressed" or "the resigned" into (3)-(4) and you get that nothing is bad for them either, which I'm not willing to swallow. Which step do I give up? I'd most like to keep (1) and drop (4), but then I owe an explanation of how pain can be bad for the asymbolic even though nothing anywhere in her mental life registers it as bad — and I don't have one. If instead the trick is hidden in the word "bad" being used two different ways, tell me which steps use which reading.
