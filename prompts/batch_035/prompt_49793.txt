I'm a survey statistician by training; my daily work is about sampling frames, duplicate records, post-stratification weights, and selection effects — the kind of person who reaches for the inspection paradox before breakfast. A philosopher friend at lunch described the Sleeping Beauty problem to me, and I've been bothered since: Beauty agrees to an experiment in which a fair coin is flipped after she's put to sleep on Sunday. If it lands heads, she's woken once, on Monday. If tails, she's woken on both Monday and Tuesday, with a memory-wiping drug in between, so each awakening feels to her exactly like the only one. Before each awakening she's sedated, on being woken she's asked her credence that the coin landed heads, and nothing she observes distinguishes the awakenings. Halfers (David Lewis's position, roughly) say her answer should be 1/2; thirders (Adam Elga's) say 1/3; and a whole literature has grown up without settling it, including a faction that keeps 1/2 in heads but refuses to say Beauty learns nothing between Sunday and Monday — the "double-halfer" move, which I confess I can't yet paraphrase.

Translate this debate into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can and without smuggling in philosophical assumptions under the guise of translation. The obvious hooks for me: whether each awakening should be treated as a record in a dataset (so tails contributes two rows and heads one, and any unweighted average over awakenings is size-biased toward tails), versus treated as a single realization of a chance process that Beauty merely observes under imperfect recall; the difference between the distribution of the coin and the distribution of the interview; duplicate rows and whether you deduplicate or weight; conditioning on "I am awake now" and why both camps apparently agree that naive conditioning on that event is too simple, even though to me it looks like exactly the event a properly-specified model should condition on; and the betting arguments, which sound like they should be trivial for a statistician and apparently aren't — Beauty is offered a bet on each awakening, and the fair price of that bet seems to depend on how you count the awakenings, which feels like a weighting convention dressed up as a disagreement. Tell me where the halfer sits relative to the deduplication question and where the thirder sits, and whether the dispute is recognizably a dispute a survey methodologist could adjudicate.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: which parts of the philosophical disagreement map cleanly onto disputes I'd recognize from practice (and could in principle settle), which translate only with distortion — say what the distortion is — and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all, particularly the material about self-locating belief, "centered worlds," and treating "today is Monday" as a proposition in its own right. I want your considered view on the remainder: is it a genuine blind spot of statistical practice (something my framework cannot express but should), or a sign that the philosophical framing smuggles in a confusion that mostly dissolves under honest translation? Don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I don't need citations, just the translation done straight. Please finish with a single final line of the form "Residue verdict: <one sentence>" stating, in plain terms, whether the untranslated remainder is real or an artifact of the framing.
