My friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether her enthusiasm for a film could count as a reason for me to see it. She had been moved by it; I hadn't seen it; I asked whether her raving about it gave me the same kind of evidence a trusted friend's report gives me about, say, a restaurant or a statistic, and if not, why not. She eventually shut the whole line of questioning down with the proverb that there is no accounting for taste — she said it in Latin, de gustibus non est disputandum, which apparently felt more conclusive. The proverb was doing two jobs at once for her: it was supposed to show that her reaction can't be evidence for me, since taste is somehow private to the taster, and also that there is no point in pressing the question, since nothing disputable is on the table. I left unsatisfied, because I suspect the one sentence compresses several entirely different claims: something psychological about how people rarely change their minds, something semantic about whether ascriptions of beauty are even in the business of being true, something epistemic about whether testimony can transmit evaluative knowledge or only belief, and something normative about the etiquette of arguing over these things at all. It also occurred to me later that the Latin is about disputing while the common English rendering is about accounting, i.e., explaining, and I have no idea whether that shift matters or whether both versions were always ambiguous in the same ways.
