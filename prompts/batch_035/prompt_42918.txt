I'm a backend engineer and I somehow got put on the steering committee for our internal tools portal — think of it like a private app store where teams publish their build scripts, dashboards, data pipeline utilities, etc. Every tool's page shows an adoption count (how many teams have installed it), and everyone quietly admits those numbers drive most decisions. Nobody reads the docs, they just check the count and install whatever's winning.

One of our data scientists thinks this is a textbook information cascade problem. She gave a talk about it last sprint: the idea is that it can be perfectly rational to copy whoever moved before you even if your own information pointed the other way, because you figure they knew something you didn't — but once two or three early adopters tip the same direction, everyone after them just falls in line, and after that the count reflects the snowball more than the tool's actual quality. Her evidence is an experiment she described where an artificial music market with visible download counts produced way more random, winner-take-all outcomes than the same market with the counts hidden — when everyone can see the counter, luck compounds. So her proposal is some version of hiding adoption numbers: either fully hidden, hidden until your own team has rated the tool, or hidden until a tool clears some threshold of independent adoptions.

But there's serious pushback, mostly from a senior infra engineer, and honestly his point is good: our early adopters aren't random. The first teams to pick up a tool are usually the platform org and the grizzled people who actually benchmark things, so their adoptions carry real signal. On his telling, copying the crowd here isn't a cascade at all, it's delegation — junior teams rationally offloading evaluation work onto the people best positioned to do it, and hiding the counts would force everyone to redo that work badly. There's also a smaller fight brewing about fairness to new tools: she wants small tools from solo authors to get a fair shot, but he points out that if a tool is genuinely broken, making the first few teams stumble onto it blind is a real cost too, just one that lands on the adopters instead of the author.

We vote on this at next month's meeting and I'm supposed to circulate a position paper beforehand, which is very much not my strength. Can you draft the memo for me? It needs a concrete recommendation out of the four options on the table (hide entirely / hide until you rate / threshold-gated / keep as is), or a fifth design if you think there's a middle path that keeps the good signal without the snowball — like maybe doing something different for the first N adoptions than for later ones, or distinguishing who the early adopters are. Whatever you recommend, the memo has to engage the delegation argument head on rather than just steamrolling it, since the committee is split and people will notice if it's one-sided. Write it for engineers, not for academics — no literature dumps, just the actual reasoning about which way we should vote.
