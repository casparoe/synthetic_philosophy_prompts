For about a year now I've been reading about the French Revolution as a hobby — no background in history or political theory, just working through it on my own — and I've reached a conclusion I can't see a way out of. As far as I can tell, there are two rival explanations for why it happened. The first is the class story: a rising middle class swept away a feudal order that was already dead on its feet, and would have done so regardless of who happened to be king. The second is the ideas story: Enlightenment principles got into people's heads, and the ideas did the work. Now, if the class story is true, then the whole thing was locked in decades before 1789, and all the things historians dwell on — Louis XVI's indecisiveness, the flight to Varennes, which way individual deputies voted at crucial moments — was just decoration on an outcome that social forces had already settled. And the same seems to go for what came after: it's often said that revolutions devour their children, and if that is a genuine law of revolutions, then Danton and Robespierre were dead men the moment the Bastille fell, and their choices, their rivalries, and their specific mistakes counted for nothing. So here is what I don't understand: given that one of these stories must be true, why do professional historians still write biographies of revolutionary figures, or argue about turning points and near misses, as though any of it mattered? If the outcome was decided either by class forces or by ideas sweeping everyone along, the individuals were just passengers. Am I missing something about how these explanations are supposed to work, or is narrative history mostly theater?
