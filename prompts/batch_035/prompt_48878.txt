In trying to settle what I think about standing to blame, I have been collecting my verdicts on cases of hypocritical and compromised blame. A colleague who plagiarised a chapter years ago and never acknowledged it plainly lacks standing to blame a student for plagiarism; yet I think he does have standing to blame that same student for cruelty to a laboratory animal, since his plagiarism seems irrelevant to the target of the blame. A friend who shoplifted habitually in her twenties but has since repented, openly and without prompting, seems to me to retain standing to blame a shoplifter, though perhaps in a weakened form. I also think a colleague who has never plagiarised anything but who quietly excuses plagiarism by her friends lacks standing to blame a stranger for it. Where I keep getting stuck is this pair: I feel that someone who has never plagiarised but would certainly have done so had a tempting chapter crossed her desk retains full standing, even though her clean record is largely luck; yet I also feel that a blamer who commits the same offence while sincerely, self-servingly convinced that his own case was somehow different lacks standing — even though the difference between him and the lucky blamer seems, on reflection, to be psychological rather than a difference in culpability. Is there a principle here — about fairness, consistency, or the manipulation of shared moral standards — that captures all of these verdicts? If not, which should I give up, and what does the best surviving principle predict about a parent who blames her adult child for an offence she herself committed at the same age and has never disclosed?
