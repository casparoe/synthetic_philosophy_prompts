So I teach intro phil of language, and this semester I'm pairing the Grice unit (implicature, speaker meaning, the cooperative principle and maxims) with Hemingway's "Hills Like White Elephants," the 1927 story that's almost entirely dialogue between a man and a woman waiting for a train in Spain. If you don't have it fresh: the story is famous for the thing the narration never names. The couple talks around an "operation" the man wants the girl (Jig) to have, which every reader works out is an abortion, but neither of them ever says so -- he calls it "awfully simple" and says it's "just to let the air in," and the whole drama happens in what's implied. It's basically a Grice case study, except I'm not sure Grice's tools, built for cooperative conversation, survive contact with it, and I want to figure out where the reading honestly breaks.

Here's my worry, spelled out. On the Gricean picture, the man's euphemisms generate conversational implicatures: he says "simple," "not really an operation at all," and we the readers (and Jig) recover the unsaid content via the maxims -- flouting quantity and manner, presumably. But the man isn't really being cooperative in the spirit of the thing; he's using implicature as cover, precisely so the proposition is never on record as his. He gets to urge the abortion while maintaining deniability ("I wouldn't have you do it if you didn't want to"), and the vagueness does real work for him. So question one: does speaker meaning still flow from him to her in Grice's sense when the mechanism is being deliberately exploited to avoid commitment, and is that something the standard apparatus (conventional vs. conversational implicature, the cancellability tests) can register, or does the story show a gap between implicature-as-communication and implicature-as-evasion that the framework papers over? Question two: Jig's low point, where she says can't we maybe stop talking -- is she openly opting out of the cooperative principle, in Grice's own terms, and can the story actually end there, in a refusal to trade in implicature at all? I'd also take a view on whether the famous ambiguity of the ending (her final "I feel fine," which nobody quite agrees is sincere) is Hemingway leaving an implicature unresolved on purpose.

I want a reading that takes both sides seriously: not "the story illustrates Grice," which would be too easy, and not "Grice fails, QED." Where the framework illuminates the story, say so; where the story dramatizes something the papers don't -- the emotional stakes of decoding someone who's using maxims strategically against you -- say that too, and tell me what exactly the story adds to the debate that argument alone doesn't. If it helps, assume I know the canonical Grice material (the logic and conversation paper, the speaker meaning definition, the cancellability/non-detachability tests) -- don't re-explain it to me, just put it to work. One more thing: don't lean on any scholarly literature about the story; base the reading on the story itself, which you can assume I know cold. Length-wise, something essay-like but focused, and please actually commit on the ending rather than listing possibilities.
