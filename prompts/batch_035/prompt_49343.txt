Two of my grad students got into it after our replication seminar and asked me to referee, and honestly I keep going back and forth myself, so let me hand it to you.

Context: we'd been discussing optional stopping — the practice of peeking at your data as it comes in and stopping once you have a "significant" result. Marco (the Bayesian) and Priya (the frequentist, roughly speaking) went at it for an hour.

(1) Marco opened by saying optional stopping is a non-issue for Bayesians: the Bayesian update doesn't care about the experimenter's stopping intention, since the likelihood of the observed data is the same whether you planned n=100 or stopped at the first hint of an effect. So if Bayesian methods are immune, the whole pre-registration apparatus is a frequentist_solution to a frequentist problem.

(2) Priya countered that this "immunity" is precisely the disease: if you can keep sampling until you cross a threshold, you can guarantee eventually getting strong-looking evidence even when there's nothing there, and that's exactly the behaviour that fuelled the replication crisis. Error control exists because scientists' intentions are unreliable, and a method that ignores intentions ignores the problem.

(3) Marco said she was conflating the maths with the practice: yes, an unscrupulous Bayesian with a silly threshold can chase significance, but a properly calibrated Bayesian who stops at a fixed Bayes factor threshold has bounded error rates too, so nothing about Bayesianism licenses bad behaviour. Pre-registration, he said, is just a clumsy public proxy for "state your prior and analysis before you look", which Bayesians should do anyway.

(4) Priya replied that his bounded-error claim quietly imports frequentist reasoning — he's defending Bayes by pointing to its long-run properties, and if long-run error rates are what reassure you, why not use the framework built around them? And she added that "just pick the right prior" is doing enormous work: in the messy settings where the replication crisis actually lives (flexible outcome measures, garden-of-forking-paths stuff), the prior is one more researcher degree of freedom, not a cure for them.

Neither would concede anything. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it's basically right, basically wrong, or right-but-missing-something, and briefly justify. Then say who comes out ahead overall, and what (if anything) both of them are missing about the relationship between the likelihood principle and the replication crisis.
