My brother-in-law thinks he has found a fatal flaw in the doctrine of double effect, and while I'm sure he's wrong somewhere, I can't locate where. The doctrine, as I understand it: it is wrong to bring about someone's death as a means to a good outcome, though it can be permissible to bring about a foreseen death as a mere side effect, when there is weighty enough reason for the act. I've used it to explain why it seems right for a trolley driver to divert a runaway trolley from five workers onto a side track where it will kill one, but wrong to shove a heavy man in front of the trolley to stop it: the one worker's death is a side effect of diverting, whereas the shoved man's death is what stops the trolley — a means.

His argument runs as follows. (1) The doctrine as just stated. (2) A death is a means, not a side effect, exactly when the plan depends on it: if the person were somehow to survive, the plan would fail. (3) Now alter the divert case in a single way: the side track loops around and rejoins the main track just short of the five. If the lone worker on the loop is hit, his body stops the trolley and the five are saved; but were he to jump clear, the trolley would continue round the loop and kill the five. (4) So in this loop case the plan depends on the worker's death; by (2) his death is a means; by (1) the driver does wrong to divert. He adds that this verdict is absurd: the driver does exactly the same thing, with the same hopes and the same foresight, as in the ordinary case, and a bend in the track that the trolley never reaches when things go as planned cannot turn a right act into a wrong one.

I can't accept the conclusion either — if I were the driver, I'd divert without a second thought, and I can't believe that would be wrongdoing. Yet I can't see which premise to give up. Dropping (1) abandons double effect entirely, which I'd hate to do, since it's the only thing I know of that explains the fat-man case. Dropping (2) seems to let the fat man back in: if being a means isn't a matter of the plan depending on the death, what stops the shoved man's death from being just another side effect of stopping the trolley? So tell me: which step should I reject, why did it look so innocent, and what do I pay for rejecting it? Take a definite stand — don't just set out the options and leave the choice to me. Please answer in American English.
