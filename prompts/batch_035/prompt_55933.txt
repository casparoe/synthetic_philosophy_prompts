Our philosophy reading group did the Smart and Williams for-and-against book on utilitarianism last month, and afterwards two of the members kept the argument going in the pub, about the case Williams uses where a man can rescue his wife or two strangers from drowning but not both. I tried to follow along and mostly did, but I couldn't tell who was actually winning, so I want an outside referee. You're a professional moral philosopher, so please think carefully and don't just give me vibes -- maybe do some brainstorming about each move before you rate them, and be honest where someone is only half right.

The exchange went roughly like this, five moves:

(1) Maya said the utilitarian answer is obvious: two lives outweigh one, and from the impartial standpoint there's no morally relevant difference between the man's wife and the two strangers, so he ought to save the two. Any other answer is just letting affection do arithmetic's job.

(2) Dan said that's exactly what Williams was on about: a man who has to work out that saving his wife is justified, or who does the sums and is relieved that the sums happened to favor her, has in some sense one thought too many. His devotion shouldn't have to win a deliberative contest. And anyway the wife is part of the man's ground projects and attachments, the stuff that makes his life his -- a theory that keeps being ready to tell him to set her aside treats him as just a container that utility passes through, which is what Williams meant by the integrity objection.

(3) Maya answered that Dan is conflating the criterion of rightness with a decision procedure. A utilitarian, even a smart two-level one, can perfectly well say the husband should NOT stand there calculating in the moment -- the right act is still saving the two strangers, and the husband who spontaneously dives for his wife is admirable but morally lucky. She also said the integrity complaint proves way too much: the selfish careerist also has ground projects and attachments he refuses to set aside, and utilitarianism won't be impressed by his integrity either, so the objection can't be that the theory overrides projects, otherwise it just licenses anyone with a project.

(4) Dan came back: if your theory only gets the husband to the right verdict by telling him not to think about it, its practical content is parasitic on the very motives it discredits, which should make you wonder what work the theory is doing. And re the careerist, that's a bad analogy -- the point was never that having projects excuses anything, it's that loving particular people, being a husband and not just an altruist with a spouse, is partly constitutive of a life worth living, and a theory that can't explain why that's valuable except as a useful input to welfare is missing something about value, not just about psychology.

(5) Maya's last word: she suspects Dan's position isn't actually a rival moral theory at all, just an account of good character, and that the two of them aren't disagreeing about what the man ought to do -- they're answering different questions, one about right action and one about what it's admirable or decent to feel. She also accused Dan of sliding between the wife case, where the impartial verdict just feels harsh, and the life-project version of the integrity objection, which is a much bigger claim, and of not noticing he'd changed the subject somewhere between them.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the five, say whether the person is clearly right, clearly wrong, or half right, and justify it. Then tell me who's more right overall, and what (if anything) both of them are missing. Its fine to bring in things neither of them said, as long as you're explicit that you're doing it.
