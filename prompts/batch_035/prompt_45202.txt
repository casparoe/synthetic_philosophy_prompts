The exam question read as follows:

"Carol Gilligan famously objected that Lawrence Kohlberg's stage theory of moral development, which culminates in reasoning from universal principles of justice, was gender-biased. (a) Explain the substance of Gilligan's objection and why it motivated the development of care ethics as an alternative approach to moral theory. (b) Explain the objection that care ethics, because it treats particular relationships and partial loyalties as morally basic, cannot adequately account for our obligations to strangers or for the demands of justice more generally. (c) Present what you take to be the strongest care-ethical reply to that objection, and assess whether it succeeds."

The student's answer, reproduced in full:

"Kohlberg held that moral development proceeds through six stages organized into three levels: a preconventional level, where rightness is a matter of punishment and reward; a conventional level, where rightness is a matter of living up to expectations and maintaining social order; and a postconventional level, where rightness is grounded in universal principles, as in Kant or Rawls. Gilligan's objection was that this hierarchy treats what is really just one voice in moral reasoning — the justice orientation — as the highest form of reasoning, and that Kohlberg's original longitudinal research studied only boys, so concerns characteristic of women's moral experience registered as deficiencies. Women, when scored on Kohlberg's scale, tended to cluster at the conventional level, and Kohlberg's framework initially treated this as slower development. Gilligan argued instead that these women were reasoning from an ethic of care: an orientation centered on responsibility for particular others, on preserving relationships, and on responding to need, rather than on applying abstract principles impartially. Her interviews with women considering abortion showed that the care orientation is not a lower rung on the same ladder but a different ladder altogether. Care ethics grew out of this: it holds that moral life begins not from the standpoint of an abstract, mutually disinterested chooser but from actual relations of care and dependence.

Nel Noddings developed this insight into a systematic position. For Noddings, the basic moral phenomenon is the relation between the one-caring and the cared-for, and she grounds the obligation to care ethically in a universal principle of benevolence: the recognition that every person has equal moral worth. This gives the view a foundation that does not depend on sentiment alone.

The objection from justice holds that, because care ethics rejects impartiality, it cannot condemn nepotism or favoritism, and it collapses into an insider morality that has nothing to say to strangers. Some defenders, notably Joan Tronto, try to answer this by expanding the analysis of care to the political level. Tronto identifies four phases of care: caring for, taking care about, care-giving, and care-taking. Once care is understood not as a private sentiment but as a social practice that structures institutions, obligations extend beyond intimates: we can be held responsible for how our institutions allocate caring labor and for the needs of distant others affected by it. On this basis the partiality problem dissolves entirely, since caring attention is no longer confined to those within our immediate relationships. The remaining worry is practical rather than principled: caring attention is scarce, and a politics of care still has to decide whom to prioritize when needs outrun resources. As Virginia Held argues, the most promising development of care ethics is as a form of virtue ethics, since the central question becomes what traits a good carer possesses."
