I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and I've mostly internalized the picture on which beliefs are instruments of prediction — they pay rent in anticipated experience, and the ideal agent is the one whose credences are well calibrated. A few weeks ago I set out to argue that this picture, combined with what I know of how cognitive science actually explains behavior, adds up to eliminative materialism about belief. The argument came out stronger than I intended, strong enough that I suspect it proves too much, but I cannot locate the broken step.

(1) In the everyday sense, a belief is a discrete, sentence-like commitment: either you believe the train leaves at 6:40 or you do not, and what you believe is something that can be true or false and can serve as a premise in further reasoning.

(2) Anything deserving the name "belief" must be whatever state actually does the explanatory work that belief is supposed to do — guiding action, supporting prediction, combining with wants to yield choices. A state that does none of this work is not a belief, whatever else it may be.

(3) The states our best cognitive science invokes to explain behavior are not discrete and not sentence-like: they are graded activations and continuously updated weights — prediction errors, cached values, learned policies — and each can shift by a tiny amount with nothing that looks like a change of view.

(4) Where the underlying states are graded, there is no cutoff: between an agent whose weights put her at 55% on p and one at 58% there is no difference in belief, and by chaining such steps we can pass from clear belief to clear disbelief without any point at which belief was lost; so there is no fact of the matter about which discrete sentences an agent believes.

(5) A state that cannot be had or lacked, and that does not carry sentence-like content, cannot be true or false and cannot serve as a premise.

(6) By (3)–(5), whatever does the explanatory work cannot be a belief in the everyday sense; by (2), whatever fails to do that work is not a belief either; so nothing is a belief in the everyday sense, and the everyday concept fails to refer.

I am half convinced and half suspicious. I can poke at (4) — it smells like the sorites, and "bald" lacks a cutoff without failing to refer — but I notice the argument may not need the cutoff, since graded states arguably fail to be premises regardless of whether some threshold story could be bolted on. And (2) looks less secure than I first thought: it is a functionalist thought, and functionalism is usually belief's friend rather than its undertaker, which makes me wonder whether the argument is quietly trading on two different senses of "sentence-like" or of "belief" between the early steps and the late ones. Tell me which step to reject and why, why that step looked fine when I laid the argument out, and what rejecting it costs — including what I should say about my own forecasting practice, where I keep a calibration score over a few thousand resolved questions and have been treating it as measuring something real. Lead with your verdict in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, no restatement of my setup, and no settling for a hedge between two steps.
