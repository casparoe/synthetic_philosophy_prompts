I've been reading about the classical Chinese mourning system, the one organized around five grades of mourning garments, and I can't figure out what it's actually committed to. The setup, roughly: how long you mourn, how coarse the cloth you wear, how much you abstain from work and festivities -- all of it is fixed by where the dead person sits on your kinship chart. A parent gets the full three-year mourning (about 27 months in practice), grandparents and uncles and cousins get progressively lighter duties the further out they are, and a stranger gets essentially nothing. And the duties track relationship category, nothing else. You owe the heaviest grade to a father you hate and haven't spoken to in twenty years, and next to nothing to the best friend who saved your life. Worse: you can be flat broke and owe an elaborate funeral to a distant uncle you never met, while mourning is the one domain where need seems to count for nothing at all.

Here's what's bugging me. The same tradition claims our concern for strangers is where morality comes from -- Mencius has the famous bit about how anyone who saw a child about to fall into a well would feel alarm and distress, without calculating any benefit to themselves. So strangers do move us, yet the ritual assigns them no duties whatsoever. And Confucius, when a disciple proposed shortening the three-year mourning, defended it by pointing out that you spend your first three years in your parents' arms before you can leave them -- so the heaviest duty is supposed to be rooted in natural affection and dependence. But that can't be why a hated father still gets the full grade.

So what I want to know: what does this whole apparatus presuppose about what a duty is? Does it presuppose that the roles themselves are morally basic, that being someone's child just is the source of the obligation? Or is it compatible with the view that roles are just an instrument -- kin are the people you can actually count on, so grading by kinship is a delivery mechanism for something else, like effective help? Can the kinship chart and the child-in-the-well story be held together coherently, or is there a genuine inconsistency there, maybe about the difference between feeling compassion and owing ritual? And is the practice even committed to any single theory, or is it underdetermined such that a role ethicist and a disguised consequentialist could both sign off on it? Finally, if you rebuilt the system starting from Mencian compassion instead of the chart -- flat mourning obligations for everyone -- what would you lose? I have a suspicion the graded structure is doing something the compassion story can't replace, maybe something like training, but I can't quite say what.
