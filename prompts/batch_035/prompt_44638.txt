In my graduate seminar on population ethics we have spent the last month on the nonidentity problem, and the discussion has stalled in a way I want help understanding rather than resolving. The setup, in brief: Parfit observed that many of our choices affect not just the welfare of future people but which people exist at all. A woman who takes a certain drug before conceiving will have a child with a serious disability; had she waited or not taken the drug, she would have had a different, healthy child instead. If the child she actually has, disability included, has a life well worth living and gladly lived, it is hard to say she was made worse off by the choice — there is no alternative in which that same child exists and fares better. Yet nearly everyone's intuition is that something was done wrong. Half of our seminar are impersonalists: they accept that morality can count making lives worth living, even when no one is made better off, and they take the case at face value. The other half are person-affecting theorists: they hold that morality is ultimately about people and their interests, that an act cannot be wrong unless it is bad for or unfair to someone, and they either deny that the case is really wrong or try to reinterpret what the harm consists in. Neither side has budged, and by now everyone can recite the other's moves in their sleep.

My working hypothesis is that at least part of the standoff is verbal, and I would like it scrutinized. It seems to me that the two camps may be using words like "harmed," "worse off," and "for the sake of" in systematically different ways: the impersonalists treat welfare as something that can be added up across possible worlds regardless of who bears it, while the person-affecting theorists treat "harm" as irreducibly comparative and relational, so that a claim of harm requires a single person who does worse in one outcome than in another. If that is right, the dispute over the drug case would be a quarrel about the logical form of moral claims, not a substantive disagreement about the case. But I can also see it another way: maybe the real divergence is about which data matter — whether the firm intuition that the drug case is wrong should constrain theory, or be explained away as an overgeneralization from cases where the same person does exist across alternatives. And a third possibility, which some in the seminar flirt with, is that the two sides are answering different questions entirely: one about what we owe to particular people, the other about what kinds of worlds it is good to bring about, and the appearance of conflict comes only from running both under the single heading of "what is wrong."

Please give me a diagnosis rather than a survey of the literature. Say which of my three guesses, or what alternative, best accounts for how the two sides actually argue, drawing on the characteristic moves each makes — the person-affecting side's appeal to the claim that an act must be bad for someone, the impersonal side's insistence that this is exactly what the cases put in doubt, and the way both camps scramble when the stakes are raised, as with Parfit's repugnant conclusion, where the same tension reappears inside each camp's own view. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a sharper definition of harm, a set of cases that forces one side to pay a price they cannot accept, a principled way of dividing the labor between person-affecting duties and impersonal value, or a demonstration that the question, as posed, is ill-posed. If you think no progress should be expected here, say so and explain why. I would rather learn that the seminar is stuck on something genuinely hard than keep assuming the other half of the room just hasn't understood the point.
