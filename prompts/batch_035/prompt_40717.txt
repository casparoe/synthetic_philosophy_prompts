I sit on the governing board of an independent radio astronomy institute, and next month we vote on whether to authorize the first deliberate interstellar broadcast in the institute's history. The proposal comes from our transmitter group: our new 2 MW X-band array, built primarily for radar calibration and asteroid characterization, would be used for an eighteen-month targeted transmission toward a Sun-like star roughly forty light-years away that hosts at least one confirmed planet in the habitable zone. The payload is deliberately not the usual neutral mathematics-and-geometry greeting. The designers argue that any civilization capable of threatening us could model us anyway, so the message should carry an honest compressed archive — a frank account of human history including our wars and ecological damage, alongside our music and literature — on the theory that candor is both more informative and more confidence-inspiring than a sanitized showcase.

The board is split three ways. One faction, invoking the dark forest hypothesis from Liu Cixin's trilogy, holds that deliberate broadcasting is close to the worst strategic move a civilization can make: given a chain of suspicion that no amount of communication can dissolve across interstellar distances, and given the possibility of a technological explosion on the target's side during the centuries a message is in flight, any civilization that reveals its location becomes a rational target, and the only winning move is silence. A second faction replies that Earth is already conspicuous — military radar leakage, atmospheric oxygen, and industrial signatures have been advertising us for a century — so a narrow targeted signal changes nothing about whether we are detectable, only about how cheap it is for a distant observer to notice us; and, further, that the dark forest logic collapses on itself, since a civilization capable of striking across forty light-years could have found us already, and a first strike against a species that might technologically explode mid-flight is not obviously the dominant strategy the fiction makes it out to be. A third member insists the real issue is decision-theoretic rather than astronomical: silence remains available at any moment, while a broadcast is irreversible; the risks fall on people in 2105 who were never consulted and who cannot recall the message; and we are being asked to weigh a small probability of unbounded harm against speculative, unquantifiable benefits of contact — a trade we have no accepted rule for making.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend approve, reject, or approve with specified conditions, and it should engage the dark forest position at its strongest — as a piece of game theory about rational actors under mutual observation, not as science fiction — and the "already visible" rejoinder at its strongest, stating plainly which of the two the decision actually turns on and why. It should also make explicit the decision rule the board is applying: whether the reversibility asymmetry alone settles the matter, how we weigh small probabilities of catastrophic and unbounded harm against diffuse possible benefits, and what the eighty-year round-trip latency does to both the threat and the value of contact. Finally, give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on whichever side loses, and make the recommendation concrete enough that the transmitter group knows exactly what it may and may not do by the end of the year.
