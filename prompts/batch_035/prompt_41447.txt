I'm a retired geophysicist — thirty-odd years of isotope dating on Precambrian rock, so I've spent my entire working life dealing with deep time — and since retiring I've fallen hard for philosophy of time. Mostly this has meant arguing with my sister-in-law Carol, who has never read a philosophy book in her life but issues verdicts about time at every family dinner, and I've got into the habit of writing them down. My current list: dinosaurs existed but don't exist now, and definately aren't "still around" anywhere; the past is fixed, what's done is done and nothing happening now can change it; there's no fact of the matter yet about wether her granddaughter takes the vet job in Glasgow, because the future hasn't been written; and time is nothing like space — she practically spat at my spacetime diagram at Christmas. Also, after her hip replacement, she was adamant that the relief she felt was down to the whole business being over and done with, not merely to it having happened earlier than the relief.

Here's my problem. From my reading I can fit at least two of the standard views onto her record: one where only the present exists, and one where the past accumulates behind us, the present being just the growing edge of it. Both can agree the dinosaurs aren't around now, both can say the past is fixed and the future unwritten, and both can be rude about the block universe. But in May we're visiting, and I've been saving up the question that I think actually splits the two: does her late husband still exist, the way the island of Staffa exists even though she's never set foot on it — just somewhere she can't get to, in time rather than in space? On one view the answer is no, he's utterly gone; on the other, yes, he's part of the accumulated past. So predict what Carol will say. Honestly I'm less interested in the answer than in the rule (or rules) she seems to be running, the rival rules that fit her record just as well, and which other question I should ask her to tell those rules apart — I have candidates like whether her present memories are a genuine connection to the wedding she remembers, and whether Caesar crossing the Rubicon is true because of things that exist now.

Please write in British English, be honest about how underdetermined her record is, and end with a single machine-readable line of the form Verdict: followed by a short phrase giving your prediction for the late husband question.
