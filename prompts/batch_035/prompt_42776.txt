My older sister issues unusually crisp verdicts about whether people know things — asked directly, she never hedges — and because the rest of the family finds this amusing, we've been keeping a list. Here is her track record so far, all from the last few months:

1. Having idly looked up the pharmacy's hours the night before, with nothing whatsoever depending on it, she says she knows it closes at nine.
2. Facing a check that will bounce Monday unless she deposits it Saturday, and having checked the bank's website an hour earlier, she says she does *not* know the branch keeps Saturday hours, and calls to confirm.
3. Running late for an international flight, genuinely worried about her passport, she says she *does* know the passport is in the drawer — because she is holding it.
4. Her neighbor, rushing to the airport on the strength of a traffic report from an hour ago, says he knows the highway is clear; she says he does not.
5. Watching her nephew, who has no plans at all, check the same pharmacy's posted hours on the same kind of aimless walk, she says he knows it closes at nine.
6. Asked, with nothing riding on it and the draw already held but unannounced, whether she knows her lottery ticket lost, she says she doesn't — no amount of odds, in her view, turns that into knowledge.

Note that the evidence in 2 seems no better than in 1 — if anything it's fresher — so the difference between them isn't just that she was better informed the first time.

Here is the case I want to put to her this weekend. She needs a specific part before Monday; the only store that might carry it closes at ten if it is open at all, and a wasted hour's drive each way carries real costs for her, so she is visibly deliberating. Her brother, entirely relaxed at home and with nothing at stake for himself, has just checked the store's website and reports it to her. I intend to ask her, mid-deliberation: does your brother know the store is open till ten?

Please predict her answer. More importantly, lay out at least two rules that fit her entire track record equally well but give different verdicts on this case — for instance, a rule on which the standards for her attribution rise with *her* situation, versus one on which they rise with the *subject's* situation, versus one on which they rise with whoever would actually act on the belief. Your rules also need to say how she gets "knows" in the passport case despite the high stakes, and what extra condition her lottery verdict forces any stakes-based rule to carry. Then give me your best guess at her answer, with your reasons, and finally propose the one further case — something I could actually put to her over coffee — that would best separate the remaining candidate rules.
