A friend and I argued late into the night about the surprise-exam paradox, and what unsettles me is not the paradox itself but a generalization he built on top of it, which appears to prove far too much. He began with the familiar case: a teacher announces an exam for one of days 1–5, adding that on the morning of the exam the students will not know which day it is; the usual backward induction then eliminates day 5, and with it day 4, and so on down, so that the announcement cannot be fulfilled. Granting all of that, he continued:

(1) The elimination drew on nothing about the students except that they heard the announcement, that they remember it, that they can reason about their own future knowledge, and that the list of possible days is finite.
(2) Nothing in it concerns exams, teachers, or schools; the same reasoning applies to any announcement of the form "E will occur at one of these finitely many times, and you will not know in advance at which."
(3) Many ordinary announcements have that form: a fire drill sometime this term; a committee publishing its decision on one of the first five days of a conference; a dentist telling a patient the pain will lift quite suddenly one of these days.
(4) So the audiences of all such announcements can prove, in advance, that the announcements cannot be fulfilled.
(5) Hence no sincere announcement of that form can ever come true, and whenever one does come true, its audience has simply failed to draw a conclusion it was in a position to draw.
(6) In particular, nobody told there would be a fire drill this term can be genuinely surprised when the alarm sounds, and my dentist could have been refuted from the chair.

He closed on (6), and that is where our argument began in earnest, because it seems to me plainly false: the pain did lift, suddenly, on a morning I had no opinion about, and I do not think I was being irrational in failing to foresee it. Yet when I go back through the steps, each of (1)–(6) still looks defensible on its own. My unease gathers around (2) — the word "same" there appears to be quietly doing work — but I cannot say what. Settle it for me: which step is the false one, what made it look acceptable, and what do I lose by denying it?
