I'm a sixth-form student writing an extended project on what 'disease' actually means, and I've got stuck on how psychiatry goes about deciding what counts as one.

The practice I want to understand is the maintenance of the big diagnostic manuals, above all the DSM. As far as I can tell, it works like this: a committee of psychiatrists periodically adds, removes and redraws disorders, and its decisions determine enormous practical things — whether insurers pay, whether research funding flows, whether someone can be signed off work, and in some contexts whether they're held responsible for their own condition. Some of the decisions look strange from the outside. Homosexuality was listed as a disorder until 1973, when it was removed after years of protest and, eventually, a vote — not after any new laboratory finding. Nicotine dependence sits in the manual as a genuine disorder, while health systems that treat it as an illness also charge smokers higher premiums, on the grounds that smoking is a choice. And ordinary grief after a bereavement isn't a disorder until it persists past some cut-off, at which point the same sadness, unchanged in character, becomes a named condition requiring treatment.

My question is what view of disease this practice presupposes. If disease were a value-free matter of biological malfunction — the body failing to do what it was designed or selected to do — then protest campaigns and membership votes shouldn't have anything to do with it, and the 1973 decision would have to be described either as the correction of a straightforward factual error or as mere politics, and I can't work out which description is worse for the manual's authority. If, on the other hand, disease is partly a normative matter — a condition has to harm you, or clash with what we expect of a person, before it counts as an illness — then the practice starts to make sense, but then I can't see what stops the line from being pure convention: whatever a committee and its era find distressing counts as a disorder. Perhaps the practice needs both ideas at once and they pull in different directions; the smoking example seems to require 'choice' and 'illness' to be true of the same person simultaneously.

So, please: reconstruct the implicit theory of disease that the diagnostic-manual practice rests on; tell me which rival theories it quietly rules out; and say whether the parts of the practice I've described presuppose anything mutually consistent. Where is the practice underdetermined, such that several theories of disease would license it equally well? And if we replaced the presupposed view with a strictly naturalist one — or, alternatively, a frankly normative one — what would have to change in how the manual is written, voted on and used? I'd be grateful if you would answer in British English, and please don't simplify on my account; I would rather be stretched than patronised.
