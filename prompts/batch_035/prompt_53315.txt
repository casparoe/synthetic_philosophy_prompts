My older sister is a CS grad student, and over winter break she showed me an argument from her fairness reading group that she now uses to claim the whole field of algorithmic fairness is confused. I follow each step but the conclusion seems way too strong, and I can't find where it breaks.

Her example is a tool that scores defendants on their risk of reoffending. (1) Such a tool is unfair to a group if its errors land on that group: if people of one race who would not have reoffended are flagged high-risk more often than similar people of the other race (and likewise for those who would have reoffended being missed), the tool is treating the groups unequally where they are equal. (2) It is also unfair if the same score means different things for different groups: if a "high risk" label picks out genuinely higher-risk people in one group but a much broader slice of the other, the score is misleading for one group in a way it isn't for the other. (3) There is now a mathematical proof that, unless the two groups actually reoffend at the same rate, or the predictions are perfect, no tool whatsoever can satisfy both (1) and (2) at once. (4) The groups do reoffend at different rates, so that escape route is closed. (5) Therefore every possible tool — every algorithm, every human judge, every procedure you could design — is unfair by at least one of these two standards. (6) Therefore there is no coherent thing we are asking for when we demand fairness: the demands contradict each other. (7) Therefore regulators should stop demanding fairness metrics and just let developers maximize accuracy.

Step (7) seems to prove too much. By the same reasoning, since no medical test can be simultaneously perfectly sensitive, perfectly specific, and perfectly cheap, there would be no coherent thing we're asking for when we demand a good diagnostic test, and we should stop regulating test quality. But tradeoffs between criteria feel normal in engineering, not like proof of incoherence — so I suspect my sister has smuggled something in, most likely between (5) and (6), but I can't say exactly what. Which step should I refuse to grant, why did it look fine, and what does refusing it cost her argument — or is the argument actually sound and my medical-test comparison the thing that fails?
