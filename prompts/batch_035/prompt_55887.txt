I teach an introductory course on the philosophy of mathematics, and next term I want to pair Cantor and the debates over actual infinities with Borges's short story "The Book of Sand" — the one in which a Bible seller comes to the narrator's flat in Buenos Aires and sells him a book that has no first page and no last page, from which one can never find the same page twice, and which eventually drives the narrator to hide it in the basement of a library so that he is never tempted to look at it again. Please read the story through the lens of the philosophy of the infinite.

Here are the questions I would like the reading to address. First, where does the story dramatise the distinction between potential and actual infinity — the Aristotelian thought that infinities exist only as endless processes versus the Cantorian thought that an infinite totality can be a completed object sitting on your desk? The narrator's book is actual infinity in the most literal sense: not an endless series of events but a bounded, holdable thing with infinitely many pages. Second, does the story track any genuine mathematical facts about actual infinities? I have in mind two places where it might: the seller's demonstration that whatever page one opens, the book always has a page before and a page after (which recalls Hilbert's hotel, where adding or removing finitely many guests leaves the occupancy exactly as it was), and the narrator's later horror at the thought of burning the book, since he fears the smoke would be without end (which recalls the standard point that removing or destroying finitely many pages of an infinite book leaves an infinite remainder — though notice the story never says the book is countably infinite, and I'd be interested in whether the narrator's failure ever to relocate a page he has seen suggests something more like an uncountable continuum of pages, where a random re-encounter has measure zero). Third, there is a finitist reading available: the narrator's obsession, insomnia, and eventual flight from the book could be read as vindicating the finitist suspicion that a completed infinite is not merely false but monstrous — that accepting it is psychologically corrosive. Does the story support that reading, or does it undercut it, given that the narrator's practical response is not to refute the book but merely to hide it?

I would like a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not turn the story into a decorated textbook chapter, and it should not bend the mathematics to fit the mood of the story. Where the story trades on confusions — for instance, sliding between epistemic unfindability and metaphysical structure — please say so plainly, since I do not want to mislead my students. And end by telling me what you think the story adds to the debate between Cantorians and finitists that the formal arguments alone do not.
