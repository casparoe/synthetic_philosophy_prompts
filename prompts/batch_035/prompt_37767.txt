Next month im giving a talk to my university's effective altruism student group defending a strongish version of the cluelessness worry, and I need to get ready for the Q&A. The audience will be maybe 25 people: mostly students who are confident expected-value maximizers, a few longtermists who think AI safety is obviously the highest-priority cause area, and one grad student who does population ethics and thinks Parfit hung the moon. Nobody there is a decision theorist or a formal epistemologist.

My thesis, roughly: for any action with long causal tentacles — and basically every action has them once you count far-future effects — the expected value comparison is not just unknown but indeterminate. The knock-on effects come in offsetting chains we can't model, and for any argument that the tentacles net out in favor of act A over act B there's an equally plausible mirror argument running the other way. So instead of pretending to compute, we should fall back on a short-horizon rule set: keep your promises, don't kill, direct benefits toward whoever's worst off in the near term, that kind of thing. My argument leans on a symmetry principle — that absent specific information, chaotic causal chains should be treated as washing out rather than as noise we can average over — and that's honestly the part I feel shakiest about.

Prepare me a Q&A prep sheet for this specific crowd. For each objection: how much damage it really does to my case, a reply I can actually deliver in two minutes, and a note on where that reply is soft. Make sure you include the symmetry objection (why think the tentacles cancel symmetrically instead of just being uncomputed?), the "so I guess you don't swerve to avoid the pedestrian, since you can't trace those tentacles either" objection, and whether my fallback rule set is just me smuggling deontology in through the back door while calling it decision theory.
