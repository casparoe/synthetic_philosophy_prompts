I'm drafting a short paper for a graduate reading group in social epistemology, provisionally titled "The Lemons Problem of Testimony," and I'd like comments on my notes before I commit to writing the full thing. Please respond with exactly five numbered comments, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: the first on whether I'm representing the two main ideas accurately, the second raising the strongest objection to my central claim, the third identifying a place where the market analogy quietly breaks down, the fourth on something important the notes leave out, and the fifth a free comment on whether the paper is worth writing in this form. Here are the notes:

Thesis: testimonial injustice, in Fricker's sense, is better modeled as adverse selection than as a bias residing in individual hearers. In Akerlof's market for lemons, buyers who cannot distinguish lemons from good cars price all cars as if average quality were low; good cars then exit; the market unravels. I claim prejudiced credibility deficits follow the same logic: a hearer who cannot directly observe a speaker's reliability uses social identity as a proxy, and because the proxy is cheaper than investigating each speaker, the practice can be locally rational at every step while being globally unjust. This explains two things prejudice alone cannot: why the injustice is largely invisible to well-meaning participants, and why Fricker's remedy — the virtue of testimonial justice, a hearer's disposition to correct for identity-based credibility deficits — seems to misfire, since a hearer's fairness is itself private information and cannot be audited by the speaker.

Second claim: the analogy breaks at a decisive point, and the break matters. In a lemons market, sellers can eventually sort themselves through warranties and reputations — institutions that make quality public. A warranty system for testimony would require the disbelieved group to post bond for its credibility, but the resource spent would be attention, patience, and self-monitoring supplied by the group itself, so the "remedy" reproduces the injustice as a tax on the very people the remedy was for. The paper therefore ends pessimistically: the adverse-selection model explains the stability of testimonial injustice better than the prejudice model does, but it also shows that individual virtue is not the failing link — what is missing is an epistemic analogue of mandatory disclosure, and I confess I do not know what could play that role.
