I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and for a long-form piece on formal epistemology I recently had dinner with two sources: a philosopher trained in decision theory who works on epistemic utility theory, and a philosopher of science who studies how evidence is actually used in research practice. They had read each other's work, got along well, and then spent two hours politely and completely failing to agree. I took notes, because the exchange was the most illuminating part of my reporting, and I would like an outside referee to go through it move by move.

The disagreement began when the formal epistemologist summarized what he takes to be the great achievement of the accuracy-first program: if you measure how good a credence is by how accurate it is, using a proper scoring rule, then the classical norms for credences fall out of a single demand — don't hold credences that some alternative would beat regardless of how the world turns out. On that basis he claimed that accuracy is the only intrinsic epistemic value, and that anything else we call an epistemic good is either a means to accuracy or a disguised pragmatic value. The philosopher of science objected that this is conditional, not a discovery: the dominance arguments are valid only relative to a scoring rule you have already stipulated to be the whole of epistemic value, so the program derives its norms from an assumption it never defends. He replied that the assumption is not arbitrary, because propriety — the requirement that a scoring rule reward reporting your actual credence — is itself forced by the idea that only accuracy matters, so the choice of measure isn't an extra premise smuggled in but the content of the view, the way a representation theorem in decision theory tells you what you were implicitly valuing all along. She answered that representation theorems show how to interpret a pattern of choices given some values, not which values to have, and that his inference runs the wrong direction.

The exchange then turned to cases. She offered two. First, an agent whose credences are perfectly accurate but acquired by pure luck — say, confident beliefs produced by a random process that happened to land on the truth — appears epistemically beyond reproach on the accuracy-only view, yet most of us think there is an epistemic difference between getting it right and knowing, and the program seems unable to recover that difference without adding reliability or grounding as an epistemic value, which he has just forbidden himself. Second, if accuracy is the only epistemic good, then gathering evidence has no intrinsic epistemic worth: an agent who never gathers a shred of evidence but starts life with miraculously accurate credences is in the best possible epistemic state, which she took to be a reductio. He responded that the value of evidence is instrumental — it is how accuracy is normally achieved — and that luck and importance are pragmatic distinctions, not epistemic ones: whether it matters to you how your credence was formed is a practical question about error costs and inquiry management, not a further fact about epistemic value. She countered with idealization in science: researchers deliberately adopt assumptions they know to be false, and this is not merely tolerated but praised as good epistemic practice, which is hard to square with a view on which credence accuracy is the whole of epistemic virtue; he answered that idealizations are tools assessed pragmatically, and that the scientist's credences, when she actually has them, should still be as accurate as her evidence allows.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess each contribution on its merits — who is right at each step, where each overreaches, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Where the dispute turns on a technical point (for instance, what proper scoring rules actually establish, or what follows from the instrumental status of a value), please resolve the technical point rather than treating it as a matter of taste. I don't have a stake in the outcome; I have a stake in reporting it correctly.
