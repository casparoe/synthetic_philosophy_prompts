My philosophy department runs a semester-long adversarial collaboration seminar, and this term it's Frege's puzzle about identity statements -- the fact that "Hesperus is Hesperus" is trivial while "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is informative, even though they're about the same planet. Write the joint statement that a committed direct-reference theorist (who thinks names just contribute their bearers, so those two sentences say the same thing) and a committed Fregean (who thinks names also contribute a sense or mode of presentation, so they say different things) could both actually sign after a year of arguing. I want the claims both can agree on, including about the puzzle itself and about what makes it a puzzle rather than a confusion; the exact residual disagreements, each phrased so its own side accepts the wording (this is where most drafts fail -- watch for slanted terms like "merely pragmatic" or "genuine content" that bake in one side's view); and the cruxes -- I suspect one is whether an adequate semantics for belief reports requires more than reference, and another is what to say about empty names like "Vulcan" or fictional names, where there's no bearer to contribute. For each crux, say what argument or kind of case both sides would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance (e.g. what each expects from intersubstitution failures in belief ascriptions, or from how speakers react to co-referential names they don't know are co-referential). Also flag any part of the dispute the two of them conclude is merely verbal, and say which part. Format: a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements section. And please be self-critical about the drafting itself -- say where you suspect the agreed claims are quietly vacuous, where the wording of some disagreement secretly favors one camp, and which crux you're least confident you've characterized fairly, since that's where the redrafting would start.
