I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate lecture course on ancient moral and political philosophy, and I have just finished reading the midterm essays on a unit covering Aristotle's treatment of justice, exchange, and the household. The unit was based on readings from Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics and Book I of the Politics, and the essay question I distributed was as follows:

"Aristotle discusses justice in exchange in the Nicomachean Ethics. Present his account of how the exchange of dissimilar goods — for instance, a house produced by a builder and shoes produced by a shoemaker — can be just. What makes such goods commensurable, and what role does money play in the account, including Aristotle's reasons for treating money as a matter of convention? In your answer, also explain the distinction drawn in the Politics between the acquisition of wealth that is natural to the household and the art of making money (chrematistics), and state why the latter falls under criticism."

"Aristotle's account of just exchange appears in Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics, within his treatment of particular justice. His starting point is that exchange requires equality: the parties must give and receive what is equal, since otherwise one is benefited at the other's expense. Here Aristotle takes over the Pythagorean definition of justice as reciprocity, and he accepts it for exchange: an exchange is just when the two sides are equal in the arithmetical sense, that is, when the total value given on each side is simply the same. The difficulty is that the goods exchanged — in his central example, a house produced by a builder and shoes produced by a shoemaker — differ entirely in kind and so cannot be compared directly. They must first be made commensurable, and this is the role of money. Money, Aristotle says, came into existence by convention, by agreement, so that it could serve in exchange as the common measure of all goods; it measures them the way a ruler measures lengths. But money is only the outward sign of a deeper commensurability. What ultimately fixes the ratio at which a house and shoes exchange is the relative quantity of labor the builder and the shoemaker have expended: when the exchange is just, equal amounts of labor change hands on the two sides, so that if the builder has worked twice as long as the shoemaker, his house trades for correspondingly more shoes. Money merely represents this underlying labor equality by convention; the labor spent is what really makes incommensurable goods commensurable. In the Politics, Aristotle distinguishes between two kinds of acquisition. The natural kind, practiced by the household through activities such as farming and herding, is limited by the household's real needs: it has a natural terminus, because there is a limit to how much wealth is useful for living well and for the household. The other kind, the art of making money (chrematistics), aims at the unlimited accumulation of coin, especially through retail trade and the taking of interest, and because the end of mere accumulation has no natural limit, this pursuit is against nature. Accordingly, Aristotle condemns money-making through exchange, while the natural acquisition of wealth through the household's own work remains a proper part of household management."
