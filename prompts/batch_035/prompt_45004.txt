Everyone knows the split-brain basics: sever the corpus callosum, and under lab conditions you get two streams that can't compare notes. The chimeric-face experiments are the showstopper -- you flash a composite face so that each hemisphere sees a different half, and the patient, who speaks with the left hemisphere, calmly names the person shown to the right visual field while his left hand, controlled by the other hemisphere, points at the face the other half of the brain saw. When asked why he's pointing there, he confabulates. Textbook stuff.

And the philosophical reactions are textbook too, if I remember them right. Nagel, in his 1971 paper on brain bisection, ended up saying that the case fits neither one subject nor two: our concept of a single person starts to break down here, but it doesn't get replaced by the straightforward idea that there are two, because the two hemispheres aren't individually enough like whole persons. So we just can't say how many minds are on the table. A few years later Puccetti (I think his name was Roland Puccetti -- please check me on this) bit the bullet all the way down: the surgery doesn't create two persons, it reveals them. Everyone has always been two persons, one mute; the callosum just let them cooperate so smoothly that we fooled ourselves into thinking we were each one. And Parfit took cases like this as grist for his mill that identity is not what matters.

My problem with all of these reactions is the same, and its the point of my sketch: they all treat "how many subjects are there in this head?" as an anatomical question. Count the commissures, count the streams. And I think that's wrong, because of a fact everyone in this literature mentions and then ignores: outside the lab, these patients are basically fine. They drive, work, socialize, and mostly report feeling like exactly one person. The drama only ever appears when an experimenter deliberately walls the hemispheres off from each other with a tachistoscope and segregated stimuli. The lab is the only place anyone ever divided them.

Also, and this is the part I find most interesting: the effects fade over the months after surgery. Patients develop workarounds -- little cross-cuing tricks, scanning movements, taps of one hand that cue the other -- plus whatever integration survives through subcortical routes. But notice something about those workarounds: they mostly route through the world. And here's my claim, which is what I want the group to chew on: that was always true of everyone. In a healthy brain the callosum is an accelerant, not the load-bearing wall. The two hemispheres of an intact person are kept in step primarily by things outside the skull: two eyes pointed at the same scene, one shared body that both halves issue commands to and both halves get proprioception from, one shared environment with labels on the doors. Strip the world away -- the way the lab does -- and the unity of an ordinary person would come apart too, no knife required.

So my line is: the number of subjects in a head isn't an intrinsic fact about the organism, fixed by wiring. It's relative to an informational setup, and most of the relevant wiring was never in the brain to begin with. Split-brain patients leave the operating table as two subjects only in the artificial setup, and they stop being two the moment they walk out the door, because walking out the door is the operation in reverse.
