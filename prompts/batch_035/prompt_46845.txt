I've been collecting my gut verdicts on inside-view versus outside-view reasoning, and I want to know whether they add up to a principle or whether I'm just rationing epistemic modesty to whoever happens to be in front of me. The verdicts: my friend's startup will fail even though his plan is detailed and coherent, because the base rate for startups is brutal, and I'd say the same to him. My own career pivot, though, I'm confident about despite an equally grim base rate, because I know things about my situation the reference class can't see. A doctor who tells me only the survival statistics for my condition, waving off the specifics of my case as noise, seems like she's reasoning well; an engineer who tells me the launch will succeed because every launch in the reference class succeeded, ignoring the specific component he knows is marginal, seems like he's reasoning badly. Predictions of imminent civilizational doom I discount heavily on the outside view, since such predictions have a perfect record of failure; but when my community's own favored catastrophe scenario is challenged on exactly those grounds, I find myself saying the reference class is a trap, because the class of failed doom predictions was assembled by people who never considered the mechanism. And in forecasting tournaments I've noticed I trust a forecaster more when she beats the crowd by a small, boring margin than when she claims a large edge from special insight, which feels like modesty -- yet the forecasts that actually changed my life came from people with extreme, conviction-driven inside views.

Is there a single principle -- something about when reference classes are informative versus malformed, or about how much private information it takes to override a base rate -- that captures all of these? Or do I have to give some up? I can see at least two tempting but suspicious principles I'd want you to test rather than endorse: that the inside view is legitimate exactly when you have genuine private information (which my startup-owning friend also plausibly has), and that the inside view is legitimate when the reference class is heterogeneous or gerrymandered (which is exactly what someone with a vested interest in the inside view always says about their reference class). Tell me which principle, if any, survives my cases; where it forces me to overturn a verdict, say which one and why; and what it predicts for a case I haven't listed -- say, a small organization deciding whether to attempt a project that has failed everywhere it has been tried, but under materially different conditions each time. Please answer in American English.
