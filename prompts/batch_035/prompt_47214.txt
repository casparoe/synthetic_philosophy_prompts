Every year around Advent my church book club rereads one of the Narnia books, and this time it's The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe. I want to shake things up a bit instead of the usual atonement theology chat, because I stumbled on this short paper by David Lewis (the Princeton metaphysician, not the Narnia one — I find the coincidence hilarious and want to use it) called "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" from 1997. His starting observation, roughly, is that we're all of two minds: if a friend can't pay a big fine, most of us think it's perfectly fine for someone else to pay it for him, but if the sentence is imprisonment or death, we think letting anyone else serve it for him would be absurd and wrong — authorities would refuse the offer as senseless. He notes the oddity that Christians who accept penal substitution as a theory of the atonement never extend the principle to ordinary criminal justice, and he presses on whether there's any principled difference between substituting for a fine and substituting for a death.

So here's my question for the club: read Aslan's death on the Stone Table through this paper. Aslan — innocent, willing — dies in Edmund's stead to satisfy the Deep Magic that the White Witch invokes for treachery, which seems to be a literal case of exactly the substitution Lewis says nobody accepts in mundane justice (it's the death penalty, and the substitute is a volunteer). But then the Deeper Magic, from before the dawn of time, revives him precisely because he was a willing victim who had done no treachery, which arguably means it wasn't really a penalty at all — he paid a fine he could afford, in Lewis's terms, or maybe just performed a ritual with known exit. Does the resurrection gut the substitutionary reading, or is a temporary death still a death, still humiliation and suffering, so it counts? And the Witch accepts the deal, which cuts against Lewis's point that authorities would decline such offers as senseless — does that make Narnia's legal order more substitution-friendly than ours, and is that a feature of the fantasy or a dodge?

One more wrinkle I'd like addressed: C.S. Lewis in Mere Christianity famously treated theories of the atonement as secondary, more or less saying the fact that Christ's death reconciles us matters and the various theories about how are just attempts at explanation, none of them the thing itself. Yet his most famous scene in all his fiction looks a lot like penal substitution — or is it really closer to a ransom or a victory-over-death story dressed in substitutionary clothing, as some theologians would insist? Give me a reading that takes both the story and David Lewis's question seriously, says where the book resists the penal substitution framing, and ends by telling me whether the novel quietly teaches child readers to believe in the very substitution Lewis says nobody really believes in. And maybe flag one or two scenes I should have the club linger on — the shaving and muzzling scene, the private negotiation with the Witch, whatever you think carries the most philosophical weight.
