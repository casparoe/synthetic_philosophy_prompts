My friend and I got into a really long argument over dinner last night about whether virtue ethics can survive the situationist critique, and I ended up defending a position I'm not actually sure I believe, so now I want to stress-test it.

Here's the position I took: the situationists (the Milgram obedience stuff, the "Good Samaritan" study where seminarians late for a talk stepped over a guy slumped in a doorway, the finding that finding a dime in a phone booth made people way more likely to help someone who dropped papers) supposedly show that what looks like character is really just situational pressure. And the standard virtue-ethics reply is either "the studies don't show what you say" or "the traits just need to be weaker than we thought." Both of those always struck me as kind of desperate. So the position I defended instead was this: a character trait isn't a tendency to behave the same way across wildly different situations, and the situationists never should have defined it that way. A trait is a competence at *handling* situations -- the compassionate person isn't the one who helps regardless of circumstances, she's the one who notices she's hurried and compensates, or who has arranged her life so she isn't hurried in the first place. On this view, the phone booth dime stuff isn't a counterexample to character, it's just a description of what un-virtue looks like: being at the mercy of whatever tiny feature of the environment drifts past you. Virtue is the opposite of that, and the studies actually illustrate the difference rather than undermining it.

My friend thought this was sleight of hand. She said if the trait only ever shows up in behavior, and the behavior is being driven by dimes and hurriness and whether there's a pleasant smell in the room, then the "competence" I'm talking about is unobservable by construction -- nothing could ever count as evidence against it, and a view that can't lose isn't really a view about people anymore, it's a relabeling exercise. I didn't have a great answer for her at the time, and it's been bugging me.

So: what would actually count against this position? I want a serious map of its exposure, not a defense of it. Specifically -- what observable patterns in how people actually behave would make this reconstruction of character untenable (not just awkward)? If someone really did have the competence I'm describing, what would we expect to see that we wouldn't see if my friend is right and it's all situation all the way down? And is there a way the competence could be measured or detected at all without circularity -- without first deciding who's virtuous and then reading the trait off their behavior? Also be honest about the unfalsifiability worry: has this move insulated the view from any possible empirical result, and if so, is that fatal for a position that claims to be partly empirical, or is it fine because it was always a conceptual claim wearing an empirical hat? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely embarrassing, and say which ones a committed defender of this view would concede versus explain away.
