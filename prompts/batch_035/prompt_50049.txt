I'm a game designer and we're prototyping a mechanic where the player can freeze the world and get a subjective year to act, while for everyone else in the game no time passes at all. Pitching it got me rereading Borges's "The Secret Miracle" -- the one where the Nazis are about to shoot Jaromir Hladík and God grants him a frozen year, in which only his mind can move, so he can finish his unfinished play mentally before the bullet lands. The thing I keep stumbeling on is that Borges himself argued, in "A New Refutation of Time," that time might be unreal -- he runs the Berkeley/Hume idealist line that each moment is self-contained, so a succession of moments can't add up to anything, and then he sort of takes it back at the end anyway.

So read the story through that essay's view of time. Does the frozen year only make sense if duration is mind-dependent -- real because Hladík lives through it, with nothing more needing to be true of the world? Or does the story quietly need time to be objective, container-like, precisely in order to pause it, with Hladík's year as a private side-channel running inside a stopped public clock? And where the story and the essay pull against each other, tell me what our game mechanic is actually committed to: when we freeze the clock but let the player think and work, are we staging the idealist's dream of time as nothing over and above experience, or just assuming a world-time we've arbitrarily suspended?

Don't just tell me it's ambiguous and leave it there -- commit to the strongest reading, flag where you're extrapolating past what the story and essay actually settle, and tell me how confident you are in it overall and which specific claim you're least sure of.
