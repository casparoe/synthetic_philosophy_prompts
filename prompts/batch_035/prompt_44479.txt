I'm a practicing intensivist. My working vocabulary is clinical: withdrawing versus withholding life-sustaining therapy, comfort-focused care orders, titrating opioids and midazolam at the end of life under the doctrine of double effect as we actually apply it at the bedside, declaring death by neurologic criteria, extubation for palliative purposes, deciding what counts as the moment a treatment becomes a mere prolongation of dying. I have read essentially none of the philosophical literature and want it brought to me, not the other way around.

Restate the philosophical debate over doing versus allowing — and its special case, killing versus letting die — in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Cover, at minimum: Rachels's argument that killing and letting die differ in no morally important way, Foot's case that they do, Quinn's distinction between directly and indirectly harmful agency, and the doctrine of double effect as philosophers understand it, contrasted with the rougher version we use to justify opioid titration. Where a philosophical case study (baths, trolleys, rescue parties) has a natural clinical analogue, use it; where it does not, say so rather than forcing one. My unit also has practices that may not have any tidy analogue in the literature: staged de-escalation, where vasopressors and ventilator support are tapered over hours rather than stopped at once, so the transition from treating to not-treating has no sharp temporal edge; and cases where the same intervention counts as withdrawal of therapy or as escalation of comfort care depending on which order is written and how the chart is coded. Place these in the translation too, rather than setting them aside as administrative noise.

Then give me the residue report: which philosophical claims translate cleanly into my vocabulary, which survive only with distortion (and what the distortion consists in), and which cannot be expressed in it at all. Finally, tell me whether the residue points to something the clinical vocabulary is missing — a distinction we make charting decisions without the concept to back it — or to something in the philosophical debate that is less substantive than it looks, an artifact of armchair cases with no edges.

Throughout, indicate how confident you are in each of your renderings of the philosophers' positions, and single out the one point in your translation you are least sure of.
