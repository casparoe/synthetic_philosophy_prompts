I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind, drafting a chapter on Dennett, and I would like an essay on a question I keep going back and forth on: does Dennett's indistinguishability argument actually support the multiple drafts model, or does it only undermine a narrower target than he claims?

The argument I have in mind is the one in Consciousness Explained built around the Orwellian and Stalinesque explanations of perceptual illusions such as color phi. On the Orwellian story, the subject consciously experiences the sequence unadorned and the record is subsequently rewritten; on the Stalinesque story, the record is doctored before it is ever presented to consciousness. Dennett's claim is that the two stories make all the same predictions about every observation, introspective reports included, and that therefore there is no fact of the matter about when, or whether, content gets presented to consciousness at all. From this he draws the multiple drafts model: since there is no moment of presentation, there is no inner audience for a Cartesian theater to seat.

Here is what I want the essay to weigh. First, a scope worry: even granting that the Orwellian and Stalinesque versions of a theater theory are predictively tied, this seems to show at most that one internal contrast within the theater framework is empirically idle, not that the framework's central commitment — a place and moment at which content arrives for consciousness — is false. Comparisons to underdetermination in general philosophy of science seem apt here: we rarely take the empirical tie between rival formulations of a theory to license the conclusion that there is no fact of the matter at issue. Why is this case different? Second, Ned Block's distinction between access and phenomenal consciousness: the tie between the Orwellian and Stalinesque models is arguably a tie only at the level of access and report, and a defender of the theater can maintain that the phenomenal facts differ even when every observable does not. Dennett's response, in effect, is that positing such differences just presupposes the theater; but a critic will say that this makes the argument circular, resting on the very conclusion it is supposed to establish. Third, and more speculatively, I have encountered the suggestion that careful psychophysical timing work could in principle discriminate between pre-experiential and post-experiential editing, which, if right, would dissolve the empirical tie the whole argument depends on.

The essay should weigh these lines of objection against the strongest versions of Dennett's position and reach a definite verdict: does the indistinguishability argument genuinely warrant abandoning the theater and accepting the multiple drafts model, does it warrant something weaker, or does it fail outright? Please do not end on a conciliatory note that retreats to the observation that the issues are contested. Also, and this matters to me, close the essay with an explicit statement of what would change your verdict — which single argument or consideration, if it failed or went through, would flip your conclusion — and say where you think your own reasoning is most vulnerable. I would rather have a committed answer with visible failure points than a defensible one that hedges.
