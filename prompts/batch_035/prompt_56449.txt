My roommate is a cog sci grad student who keeps ruining dinner with pain stuff, and last week she walked me into an argument about pain asymbolia that I can't get out of. Quick background in case you don't know the condition: pain asymbolics (usually after damage to areas like the insula or anterior cingulate) can still detect noxious stimuli fine -- they localize them, rate their intensity, tell sharp from dull, report them as painful -- but they just don't mind. They sometimes smirk while their hand is held over a flame or they get an injection. The standard moral is that pain and suffering come apart: the sensory-discriminative component is intact while the affective component is gone. Fine so far. But her argument goes further, and I'll lay it out in numbered steps like she did. (1) If the badness of pain were a property of the sensation itself, that badness would show up wherever the sensation occurs. (2) In asymbolia the sensation occurs -- these patients identify, rate, and discriminate the noxious stimulus, and call it painful. (3) But asymbolic pain is not bad: nothing about it gives the sufferer a reason to avoid it, it doesn't distress them, and there would be no pro tanto reason not to inflict it. (4) So the badness of ordinary pain lies not in the sensation but in the affect, the thing asymbolia strips away. (5) The affect is nothing over and above a cluster of dispositions: avoiding, flinching, distress behavior, reporting unpleasantness, wanting it to stop. (6) Dispositions are multiply realizable, so any system with the same dispositions has pains just as bad as ours, and a system with the sensations but none of the dispositions has nothing bad in it. (7) Therefore the felt quality of agony, considered in itself, contributes literally nothing to badness -- two worlds identical in their dispositions but different in felt quality are exactly equally good. (8) So inducing full-blown agony in someone chemically stripped of all aversion would do nothing pro tanto wrong, and if we raised children to be asymbolic we'd lose nothing of value.

I think (8) is false, and not just false but obviously false -- something is lost in a world of agony-no-one-minds, even if I can't say what. But here's my problem: I actually want to keep (5) and (6)! I'm broadly functionalist, they sound right to me, and I don't want to smuggle in spooky qualia-value. My roommate says that just means I should accept (8) and get comfortable. The only escape hatch I've thought of is denying (2) -- maybe asymbolic "pain" isn't really pain at all, just a sensation of tissue damage reported through the word "pain" -- but that seems desperate: these patients localize and rate the thing better than I do my own headaches, and it's not like they're confabulating the label. There might also be a slide somewhere between "bad for the person" and "bad, period," though my roommate swears she stayed inside bad-for the whole way through. So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd especially like to hear what the least painful way is to keep (5) or something near it, or failing that, the honest price of keeping everything and biting down on (8). And if the flaw is an equivocation or a scope shift, please point at the exact premise where it happens rather than gesturing at the whole argument.
