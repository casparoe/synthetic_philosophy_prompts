I have spent the better part of my career in physical chemistry, running a laser spectroscopy group at a national laboratory, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of physics with the enthusiasm — and, I am beginning to suspect, the naivety — of a convert. I have worked my way into the modern debate over whether the quantum state is something in the world or something in our heads: what the literature calls psi-ontic versus psi-epistemic views, where an ontic view treats the wavefunction as a real physical state of an individual system and an epistemic view treats it as encoding an agent's knowledge or information, perhaps about some further underlying state of affairs. I think I understand the distinction in the abstract, but I want to stress-test it against a concrete historical case rather than the stylized textbook presentations, because I have found that I only understand a philosophical lens when I have tried to point it at something.

The case I propose is the 1935 exchange in Physical Review: the paper by Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen arguing that the quantum-mechanical description of reality given by the wave function is incomplete, and Niels Bohr's reply, published a few months later under the same title. In case the details matter to the analysis: EPR considered two systems that have interacted and then separated, described by an entangled wavefunction such that measuring a quantity on one system allows one to predict with certainty the value of a corresponding quantity on the other. Their sufficiency criterion for reality read: "If, without in any way disturbing a system, we can predict with certainty (i.e., with probability equal to unity) the value of a physical quantity, then there exists an element of physical reality corresponding to this physical quantity." From premises of this kind, together with a no-action-at-a-distance assumption, they concluded that the wavefunction description cannot be complete — that quantum mechanics fails to capture some elements of an individual system's reality. Bohr answered that the criterion of reality is ambiguous when applied to quantum phenomena, since the expression "without in any way disturbing a system" cannot be given its classical meaning; on his account, the measuring instrument and the system must be treated as an inseparable whole, and the quantum-mechanical description is complete within its proper scope. The exchange is often described as Einstein attacking quantum mechanics and Bohr defending it, but I take it the real question was about what kind of thing the wavefunction is, which is exactly why I want the modern lens applied to it.

What I would like, concretely:

1. A careful classification, with the mapping made explicit. Where does Einstein-in-1935 sit on the ontic/epistemic axis, and where does Bohr-in-1935? I say "in-1935" deliberately: Einstein later maintained that the wavefunction describes ensembles of similarly prepared systems rather than individual ones, and he was reportedly unhappy with the way the argument had been presented in the published paper (Podolsky did the drafting). Does the psi-epistemic label actually fit him, or does his realism about a more complete underlying description put him somewhere the dichotomy has no leaf for? And is Bohr's position properly epistemic in the modern sense, or does complementarity evade the classification altogether — perhaps because he refuses the very separation between what the system is and what can be known about it in an experimental context that the ontic/epistemic distinction presupposes?

2. An account of how the EPR argument itself looks under each reading of the quantum state. If the wavefunction is ontic, what exactly is the incompleteness charge? If it is epistemic, do the EPR correlations carry the same implication about action at a distance, or does the tension simply relocate? I want the analysis to be disciplined about what is doing the work at each step: the locality assumption, the completeness assumption, the criterion of reality, the projection postulate, whatever else belongs in the mapping.

3. The analysis run in the other direction: what does the episode show about the modern distinction? Two things I specifically want addressed. First, Bell's later theorem and the experiments it inspired: given what we now know about the violation of Bell-type inequalities, does the ontic/epistemic axis track what was actually at stake in 1935, or does it retroject a distinction that carves the debate along the wrong joint — one that was structured more by completeness and separability than by the status of the state vector? Second, there is a result from the 2010s, due to Pusey, Barrett, and Rudolph, which is often summarized as showing that any purely psi-epistemic model — one in which distinct quantum states can be consistent with the same underlying physical state — is incompatible with standard quantum predictions, provided one assumes that separately prepared systems have independent underlying states. Taking that gloss as given, does Einstein's ensemble leanings survive it, and would the 1935 argument have to be rebuilt differently on each side of the axis?

4. Where the lens is silent. I want an honest accounting of what the ontic/epistemic classification cannot see in this exchange, rather than a forced verdict. If, for example, Bohr's reply is best read as neither ontic nor epistemic but as rejecting the question's presuppositions, say so plainly. And where the historical record is genuinely contested — how to read Bohr's actual commitments is notoriously disputed — flag the contest rather than settling it by classification.

A caution: please do not write me a bibliography or name-drop interpretations as a substitute for the mapping. I am not asking whether Bohr was right or whether Einstein has been vindicated (I have read enough popular treatments of both claims to last a lifetime). I am asking what the classification does and does not illuminate about this one exchange, and what the exchange does and does not test about the classification. Where the two-directional analysis yields an uncomfortable result for the ontic/epistemic distinction, I would like that result stated without hedging.
