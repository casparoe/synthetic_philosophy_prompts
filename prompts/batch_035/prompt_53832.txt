I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy TAing my first intro epistemology course; my discussion section runs 75 minutes and has about twenty students. They've just finished the unit on the classical analysis of knowledge as justified true belief, but they haven't yet been assigned the Gettier paper or anything post-Gettier, so the name and the standard zoo of cases are still ahead of them. Design an in-class activity in which students don't evaluate described cases but instead form beliefs live in the room and then discover, on a reveal, which of their own beliefs were true and justified only by luck.

Here's the shape I have in mind, which you should refine, extend, or push back on: a "belief stations" round in which small groups rotate through stations where a belief is induced in them directly (a photo the facilitator vouches for, a clock face projected on the screen, testimony from a student planted in advance), and each group stakes tokens from a fixed budget on which of their resulting beliefs count as knowledge; then a reveal round in which the hidden facts come out (some beliefs were formed through a false premise that happened to land on the truth, at least one through a merely reliable-appearing environment in the spirit of the fake-barn structure); then a repair round in which each group must write a payout rule -- a criterion for which stakes get honored -- that survives everything the reveal round disclosed, only for the facilitator to introduce further hidden complications that break each proposed rule in turn. Please invent fresh station cases rather than reusing the textbook ones verbatim (a disguised stopped-clock is acceptable; a tenth restatement of the sheep-that-is-a-dog is not), and calibrate them so the luck is invisible ex ante but undeniable ex post.

Deliverables I need: full rules, materials list, the token economy (no real money, and nothing that touches their grades), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and a facilitator script for the reveal. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto parts of the issue -- where justification was present and truth was present and yet the students themselves want to say they didn't know, where their payout rules replicated each familiar repair and why each one leaked -- and it should end by naming Gettier only after the students have articulated the problem in their own words. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect at least one student who has read ahead and wants to shout the answer; also students collapsing justification into high probability) and how to handle each. Include an honest note on which feature of the issue the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself -- for instance, the classroom setup may make luck detectable in a way it rarely is in the wild, and the planted-testimony stations make the deception salient in a way that could be doing work the Gettier structure itself isn't doing.

In the facilitator notes, please clearly separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and do not present any one repair proposal as if it were the consensus response; treat the status of the various repairs, and the question of whether the anti-Gettier intuition is as widely shared across different populations as philosophers once assumed, as open empirical and philosophical territory rather than as background fact. Please write everything in American English, and where the design forces you to choose between competing readings of what "knowledge" tracking in the payoff scheme should mean, say so explicitly rather than smuggling one reading into the rules.
