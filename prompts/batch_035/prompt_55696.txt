I'm a first-year grad student in origins-of-life chemistry, and I've somehow gotten roped into running a two-hour session for an undergrad seminar on astrobiology and philosopy of science (they keep changing the title, I've stopped asking). My plan is to hand out a quiz at the very start — which definition of life do you actually hold? — let everyone answer before they've seen any of the literature, then reveal the scoring key and use the disagreements as the agenda for the rest of the session. Can you write me this quiz? Nine or ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options — I want "life", "not life", and "genuinely unsure / it depends" as the allowed answers, no forced choices — plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions. The leaves I need: the Darwinian-lineage view (roughly the NASA-style working definition, a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution), the autopoiesis/metabolic view where being alive is about self-maintenance and maintaining a boundary, the thermodynamic view where life is a kind of far-from-equilibrium dissipative structure, and the deflationary view that life is a cluster concept and looking for an essence is a mistake. If you can see a fifth position that the cases force apart from these, add it.

The questions have to do real diagnostic work, so nothing like "do you think metabolism is necessary for life" — the point is to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they like. Cases I'd like included, though you can reword or swap some out if you have better ones: a virus; a prion; a vial at liquid nitrogen temperature containing the last living cells of an extinct species, with all metabolism stopped (and consider splitting this one — same vial before and after it's thawed, if you think that matters); a self-replicating RNA strand in a test tube with fresh nucleotides flowing in; a sterile animal like a mule or a worker bee, alive but unable to found a lineage; fire, the classic contrast case, worth one slot just to see if anyone bites; and a digital organism in something like Avida — code that replicates, mutates, and evolves on a server. If you have room, maybe a synthetic cell whose genome was fully designed on a computer, or an autocatalytic reaction network that gets more complex over time without anything you'd call replication — I have a feeling that last one splits the Darwinian people from the autopoiesis people in an interesting way, but you'd know better than me.

Two more asks. First, treat the "genuinely unsure" answers as informative rather than missing data — half the seminar will probably give a lot of them, and I want the key to say something about what that pattern means (cluster-concept sympathy? plain caution? something else?). Second, flag honestly any pair of positions your cases can't actually separate — I'd rather the key admit it's messy than paper over it. And don't tell me what the right answer is on any case, the whole point is that they fight about it for two hours.
