I'm a teaching assistant for an intermediate philosophy of biology course, and I'm grading our midterm, which included a question on the units of selection debate. Since I want the grading to be fair and consistent across the class, I'd appreciate a careful assessment of one student's answer rather than a quick impression.

The exam question was: "Explain what is at stake in the debate over the 'units' or 'levels' of selection. Present the case for taking the gene as the central unit of selection, and the case for allowing selection at the level of groups. Then discuss whether the remaining disagreement between gene-centered and group-centered approaches is substantive or merely a matter of how one keeps the books. Illustrate with at least one example."

The student wrote the following:

"Natural selection can in principle sort entities at multiple levels: genes, organisms, and groups. The gene-centered view, developed by G. C. Williams and popularized by Dawkins, holds that genes are the only replicators: they are copied with high fidelity across generations, whereas bodies and groups are broken up by meiosis and by membership turnover. Because selection ultimately adjusts the frequencies of genes, Dawkins concludes that the gene is the unit of selection, and that organisms are mere survival machines — vehicles that contribute nothing causally, since all the real work of adaptation happens at the genetic level.

Group selection, proposed in the 1960s by Wynne-Edwards and later revived by Sober and Wilson, holds that selection can act on whole populations, favoring traits that benefit the group even at a cost to individual carriers. The classic objection was that self-interested types would invade and undermine any altruistic group. Hamilton originally introduced kin selection as a version of group selection: altruism spreads because related individuals share genes, so the family functions as the unit being selected.

Today the dispute is essentially settled. The Price equation shows that the same change in gene frequencies can be decomposed either into selection within groups plus selection between groups, or into a single gene-level selection term. The two frameworks therefore make identical predictions, so the disagreement is purely verbal: gene-centered and group-centered theorists are describing the same process with different bookkeeping, and there is no empirical content left to the debate."
