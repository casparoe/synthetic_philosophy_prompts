I'm in a graduate seminar on scientific change this semester, and a long-running argument with a friend has crystallized around what I take to be the surviving core of Kuhn's incommensurability thesis. As we read him, the radical early version — the suggestion that scientists on opposite sides of a paradigm shift live, as it were, in different worlds and cannot even fully talk to one another — collapsed under objections. What survived, and what Kuhn spent his later career retreating to, is something much narrower: that when a paradigm changes, a small, interdefined cluster of kind terms shifts its meaning wholesale, so that sentences built from those terms cannot be translated without loss across the divide, while the bulk of the language is untouched. The textbook illustrations are the mass of Newtonian versus relativistic mechanics, phlogiston versus oxygen as accounts of combustion, and — more accessibly — the 2006 redefinition of planet, after which the sentence that Pluto is a planet seems somehow to have changed its truth value without any new observation.

My friend and I disagree about whether this deflated thesis is both true and load-bearing, and I'd like an outside estimate to locate our disagreement. Please state a credence for this two-part proposition: first, that local meaning change of this taxonomic sort genuinely occurs in the historical record (not merely that scientists acquire new beliefs about the same old kinds); and second, that where it occurs, it blocks any neutral, algorithmic comparison of the empirical merits of the pre- and post-paradigm theories — leaving comparison possible, but only from within one paradigm or another. My friend puts the conjunction at 0.7; I put it at 0.3, mostly because the Newton-versus-Einstein comparison strikes me as something we manage without difficulty, which makes me suspect the meaning change is real but inert.

Give me your own number, but I care far more about the decomposition than the headline figure. Break the question into the sub-claims it rests on — for instance: the probability that kind terms shift reference in paradigm change rather than just accruing new theory; the probability that such shifts actually impair cross-paradigm comparability rather than merely making translation slightly awkward; the probability that the Pluto case and its kin belong to the same phenomenon as the mass case rather than being mere bookkeeping conventions; and the probability that the phrase same term, same meaning picks out something determinate enough for the original question to have an answer. Assign a rough probability to each with a one-line justification, show how you combine them, and then tell me which term the final credence is most sensitive to and what specific argument or historical case would move it the most. I'd also like your read on whether my friend's 0.7 and my 0.3 most plausibly diverge on the truth of the meaning-shift claim or on its downstream consequence for comparability. Please reason from what you already know; I have no interest in a reading list or citations, only the reasoning.
