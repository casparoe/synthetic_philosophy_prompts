Write a parody agony-aunt column, roughly 600 words, three or four letters with replies, for a lifestyle magazine aimed at rationalist-adjacent tech workers. The columnist is a serene, utterly sincere dispenser of epistemic advice — think "Dear Abby, but her one tool is Bayesian decision theory" — who answers ordinary domestic and workplace disputes by applying Aumann's agreement theorem with total commitment. The letters should be mundane (where to spend the holidays, whether it's time to rewrite the legacy codebase, a husband who keeps a spreadsheet of the couple's past arguments to establish a track record), and every reply should assume, deadpan, that both parties are uncomputational Bayesians with a common prior, common knowledge of each other's honesty, and costless communication. The humor must come from getting the theorem right rather than from sneering at it: among other moves, the columnist should decline all bets offered by readers on the ground that rational agents with common priors cannot trade on disagreement; explain that simply telling your spouse what you believe is not sufficient because the theorem requires the posteriors to be common knowledge, not merely announced once; note that repeated exchange of bare posterior probabilities can by itself drive the pair to agreement; and remain scrupulously aware that the theorem only fixes where the pair ends up, never who should move or by how much — so she keeps proposing "fair" splits of the posterior that do not actually follow from the result. She must never break character or notice that spouses do not share priors, that communication is not costless, or that people value things as well as believe things; the target of the satire is the habit of applying idealized decision theory to messy human disagreement, and the reader should feel that gap without the columnist ever stating it. I'm a software engineer with no background in philosophy or economics, so don't lean on specific papers, authors, or dates — the machinery described above is all the source material you need. After the column, add a short note outside the fiction (a few sentences) on where the parody takes liberties: which of the columnist's moves would misrepresent the actual results, where a careful reader of the agreement literature would say the satire is unfair to the theorem rather than to its popularizers, and which step in the piece's implicit argument — that the theorem's failure to describe real couples shows something important rather than just idealization — is the most contestable.
