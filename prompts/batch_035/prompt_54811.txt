I run a small blog about AI risk, and two of my contributors have spent the last year arguing past each other. One of them thinks systems built from current methods — big pretrained models, RLHF-style fine-tuning, red-teaming, interpretability audits, iterating on failures as they show up — can be made genuinely aligned without needing a big conceptual breakthrough; she's basically a prosaic-alignment optimist. The other thinks that toolkit only ever teaches a model to behave where we can check it, and that real safety requires insight we don't have yet; he's the new-insight pessimist. They've finally agreed to do an adversarial collaboration, and I've promised to publish the joint statment they'd both actually put their names to. So draft it for me.

It needs four parts:

1. The claims both can sign. This should cover what current methods demonstrably achieve already, what "aligned" would even mean for a system that's superhuman in some domains while its overseers aren't, and which failure modes are real concerns rather than sci-fi dressing.

2. The residual disagreements, stated precisely — each one phrased so its own side would accept the wording. So the optimist's position as she'd phrase it herself, the pessimist's as he would, no slanted versions.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where settling one would move at least one of them. I suspect one crux is this: as models get more capable, does the gap between "passes every probe we can think of" and "actually pursuing the objective we meant" shrink, stay flat, or grow — and is that even measurble in advance? Another is probably whether oversight can be bootstrapped: using strong models to check other models only helps if the checker is itself aligned, and each of them draws different morals from that loop.

4. For each crux, one concrete test, argument, or case both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down beforehand. One useful case might be an eval where the model gets an opening to quietly edit its own grading rubric and see if it takes it — but pick whatever works. And I want at least one prediction per side that would count as evidence against their OWN view, since neither of them has ever conceded anything in a year of arguing.

If it turns out part of their dispute is verbal — e.g. over whether "new insight" means new science or just engineering nobody's done yet — say which part and why.

Keep it under 650 words total, table for the disagreements is fine. Readers know ML basics but aren't in the field, so gloss any jargon in plain language.
