ok so I just came back from a six hour argument-slash-dinner with my friend Priya and I need to get this down while it's fresh. Background: Priya is a decision theory person and I'm on the pediatric ethics committee at our children's hospital, and we got going on whether parents can ever make a rational choice for a kid when the choice itself changes what the kid will want. Now I actually have a live case to bring to Thursday's meeting and I want to circulate a written opinion beforehand instead of winging it in the room like last time.

The case: deaf couple, both from Deaf families, their 9 month old is a strong implant candidate by the audiologists' lights (good residual hearing in one ear, early enough that the window is closing over the next year or so). The parents have declined the implant. Standard stuff so far, we respect parental refusal, except two complications: the hearing grandmother has been calling the hospital and a lawyer, and one of the parents told our social worker that if the child ever asks about it when he's older she honestly doesn't know what she'll say. The team is split between "accept the refusal, document it, move on" and "we owe these parents a real informed-refusal process where they actually hear from implanted adults and Deaf adults both, not just a form they sign."

here's where the Priya argument comes in and why I want it in the memo. Her whole thing was that this is a decision made from a position of total ignorance in a specific sense: the parents, the audiologists, us — none of us knows what hearing-with-an-implant is like for a kid who's never heard, and the kid obviously can't weigh in, and whichever way it goes the kid grows up into someone whose preferences were fixed by the choice. Priya thinks this means the committee shouldn't pretend there's a rational answer at all and we should just defer to the parents since it's their culture and language on the line. I pushed back that "no rational answer" doesn't automatically mean "any answer is as good as any other," and also that deferring to the parents is itself a transformative decision made on the kid's behalf, it's not neutral. She had a comeback that I still can't fully answer: that the real difference between the options isn't better/worse for the same child but kind of choosing which adult comes to exist, and that our whole informed-consent framework kind of breaks there. I want the memo to take her point seriously, not steamroll it.
