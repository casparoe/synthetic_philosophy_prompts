I'm on the jury of a mid-sized national fiction prize (winner gets a decent chunk of money and a televised ceremony, it's not the Booker but people care). Two years ago we gave the main award to a novel by an author who, last month, was credibly accused by multiple former students of sustained harassment. The foundation that funds the prize has asked our jury for a formal recommendation on what to do about that award, and the options currently on the table are: (a) revoke it retroactively and remove the book from the prize's promotional materials, (b) let it stand with a public statement distancing the prize from the author, or (c) let it stand silently and quietly stop inviting him to future events.

Complication, and it's the reason I'm asking you rather than just drafting something myself: it's not only about the artist. The novel itself is narrated by an unrepentant abuser, and two of our jurors have gone back and reread it since the news broke. One of them now says the book is basically in love with its own narrator -- that it aestheticizes the harm rather than examining it -- and that our rereading is evidence that aesthetic judgment never was the separate thing we pretended it was. Her argument, as best I can reconstruct it, is that if the book could win us over two years ago and repulse us now, then whatever we were calling "purely literary merit" was partly a moral response we simply failed to have, and the whole fiction of evaluating the work on its own terms was doing the work of shielding us from noticing. Another juror says this proves way too much: on that logic any later shift in the jury's sensibility gets to rewrite the historical record, and the prize becomes whatever the current mood says it was.

I need to circulate a short memo before our meeting next week recommending one of the three options -- or a variant, if you think there's a genuinely better one (a fourth juror keeps floating the idea of formally redesignating past awards as given to "the work" rather than the author, and donating the money portion to a support organization, but I honestly can't tell if that's a real position or a way of not choosing). The foundation people will read the memo, so it needs an actionable bottom line, not a philosophy seminar. But I want it to actually engage the strongest version of the rereading juror's argument -- not just gesture at "separating the art from the artist" like everyone does -- because if her view is right, our options (b) and (c) are both quietly incoherent and we should know that going in. And if your recommendation depends on her being wrong, say so plainly. I dont want a memo that buries the hardest part to keep the peace.
