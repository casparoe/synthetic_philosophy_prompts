I am a novelist some way into a book set in Princeton in the early 1950s, in which a central character — I shall call him the physicist — is modelled closely, and avowedly, on the historical Einstein, down to his recorded opinions; I want no scene in which he says something the man himself could not plausibly have said. For the scene I am now drafting I have reconstructed the physicist's track record from Einstein's well-known positions: he rejected a personal God who intervenes, answers petitions, or dispenses rewards and punishments after death; he aligned himself instead with Spinoza, understanding God as the lawful harmony of nature rather than a being concerned with human fate; he affirmed a cosmic religious feeling, the scientist's awe at the intelligibility of the universe, and argued in his popular essays that science, rightly understood, leads towards that feeling rather than away from religion; he dismissed church dogma and creed, and held that a morality resting on hope of reward and fear of punishment was degrading; he denied the immortality of the individual; and he denied free will outright, being a strict determinist from his student days, fond of invoking Schopenhauer's dictum that one can do what one wills but cannot will what one wills. In the scene itself, a young colleague comes to him late at night: her mother is dying, her family expects her to lead the prayers at the bedside, and she finds that she wants to, even though she no longer believes anyone is listening; she also confesses that she cannot stop feeling guilty over a betrayal she committed years ago, and asks whether his determinism means the guilt is as misplaced as a belief in petition. The physicist must answer her before the scene ends.

Answer in exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: first, your predicted verdicts — what he would say about the bedside prayer and about the guilt; second, one rule that fits his track record and predicts exactly that; third, a second, different rule that fits the record just as well but predicts something else, together with what it predicts; fourth, the further case, within my novel or beyond it, that would most cleanly decide between the two rules; and fifth, a single sentence on which rule a novelist could safely have the character act upon without falsifying the record.
