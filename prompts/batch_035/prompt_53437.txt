I'm in a little aesthetics reading group that's been meeting for a couple of years now, and the "paradox of tragedy" conversation has gone completely nowhere and I want to know why. Not why we enjoy sad art — why the *debate about* it is stuck.

Here's the state of play as I see it, so you don't have to guess. The puzzle goes back to Hume's short essay on tragedy: spectators sit through a play designed to produce grief and terror, weep openly, and then call it a wonderful evening, when the same emotions in real life are just misery. Everyone in the group accepts the phenomenon. The theories on the table are the usual suspects: the Aristotle-flavored catharsis line (some kind of purgation or clarification happens), Walton's make-believe response (the fear we feel for a fictional character is a quasi-emotion, not real fear, so strictly speaking there's nothing to explain), Feagin's meta-response idea (we're not enjoying the sorrow, we're enjoying noticing that we respond with proper compassion), the control/dominance family — Smuts and others saying negative emotions are fine when we're holding the remote, so to speak — and Hume's own conversion account where the eloquence of the artwork transforms the pain into pleasure, which nobody in the group actually likes. And after two years: zero movement. People nod, restate their favorite, and we go home.

You're a professional philosopher of art, so think carefully about this rather than giving me the survey I could have written myself. My working hypothesis: the "paradox of tragedy" is really three or four questions wearing one hat. Sad music, horror movies, tearjerkers, and actual Greek tragedy might have entirely different explanations, each of the theories on our list is secretly a decent theory of exactly one of those cases, and so the debate can't move because the participants aren't disagreeing — they're answering different questions under one label. Is that right? Or is there one live underlying dispute instead — maybe about what "enjoy" even means here (liking the whole evening vs enjoying the sadness itself vs enjoying it only in retrospect), or about whether this is an empirical question for psychologists or a conceptual one for philosophers?

Please brainstorm a bit before you commit, but then actually commit: pick a diagnosis, defend it against the strongest runner-up, and tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a distinction we should draw, a study someone should run, a way to split the question — or, if you really think nothing would move it, say so and explain why. And don't hedge into "there's something to be said on all sides." I want one answer with reasons.
