I've been reading about wild animal suffering on and off, and I've noticed my gut verdicts on specific cases don't obviously add up to anything, so I want help figuring out what principle, if any, is behind them. Here's what I actually think when I picture each case:

- Filling in a suburban pond thats choked with tadpoles, where something like 1 in 100 ever makes it to adulthood: fine, maybe mildly good.
- Bulldozing old-growth forest for a parking lot, home to relatively few long-lived animals: bad. Really bad actually.
- The same acreage but early-successional scrub, full of mice and small birds, way more total animals and way more total dying, most of them young: somehow feels like it barely matters, or at least matters less than the old growth. This is the one I'm least proud of.
- Reforesting marginal farmland: good. But I also half-believe most wild lives are net-negative, which should make creating habitat bad. I just can't make myself feel bad about planting trees.
- Eradicating invasive rats on a seabird island: good, unambiguously.
- Culling deer to prevent a winter die-off: feels wrong, even though the culled deer would otherwise starve slowly.
- Removing wolves so the deer population booms: bad, trophic cascade vibes, even though if deer lives are net-positive and each wolf kills alot of deer over its lifetime, the arithmetic seems to point the other way.

My tentative principle was something like: add up total welfare across lives; assume lives are net-negative for small, short-lived, r-selected animals and plausibly net-positive for big long-lived ones; so habitat destruction is good insofar as it removes the former and bad insofar as it removes the latter. But I can already see this doesn't handle the reforestation case (it creates exactly the net-negative lives) or the deer cull (replacing slow deaths with fast ones should count as good on my own numbers, and I just don't feel that). And with the rats — rats are small and short-lived, so net-negative lives on my framework, but my verdict there can't only be about the rats. I genuinely can't tell whether it's really about the seabirds, or about some "the rats don't belong there" naturalness thing I haven't admitted to having.

So what I want from you: is there one principle that captures all or most of these verdicts? Does the r/K life-quality assumption have to go, or the total-welfare framing, or am I smuggling in naturalness or species-typical-function intuitions I haven't owned up to? Please state whatever principle you land on precisely enough that it gives verdicts on a couple cases I haven't listed — say, converting grassland pasture into a hog barn, or a beaver dam that drowns a meadow full of voles and replaces it with a pond full of tadpoles — and then tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which one you'd tell me to overturn.
