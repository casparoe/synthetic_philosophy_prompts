I'm a retired geochemist who fell into philosophy a few years back (long story involving my daughter's bookshelf and a long winter), and lately I've been chewing on the nonidentity problem, the one Parfit made famous with the depletion case and the fourteen-year-old mother. My coffee-argument partner Ruth, a retired judge, has strong verdicts on all these cases and I've been keeping notes because I'm pretty sure she's running some consistent theory without realizing it. Her track record so far:

1. Depletion: society uses up resources so that future people -- all of them different people than would have existed otherwise -- have lives that are worse but clearly worth living. Verdict: the policy is wrong, full stop, even though it wrongs no one.
2. The fourteen-year-old who conceives now rather than waiting, knowing the child will have a rough start in life (and waiting means a different, better-off child). Verdict: she does wrong, but doesn't wrong her child.
3. A woman who conceives while on a drug known to cause birth defects when she could've waited a month for a different, healthy child. Verdict: wrong action, no one wronged.
4. The risky nuclear waste policy, where there's a small chance of catastrophe centuries out, and the people exposed would only exist because of the policy. Verdict: wrong, and she doesn't budge even when I point out the nonidentity wrinkle.
5. The deaf couple who deliberately pick a deaf donor so their child will be deaf like them, when a hearing child was possible. Verdict: not wrong at all, "their kid, their call," and she gets visibly annoyed when I press.
6. A couple with a genetic condition such that any child of theirs would face constant pain and a life that's only barely worth living, and they conceive anyway. Verdict: wrong, and -- unusually for her -- she says it wrongs the child.

Next week's case is one I made up to test her: a government choosing between two energy policies. Policy A leads to future people (say ten billion, all different depending on the choice) living decent lives at, call it, level 7 out of 10. Policy B leads to slightly different future people, most living at level 8, but a few million, because of side effects, living at level 4. Total and average welfare are both higher under B.

Predict what she'll say. More importantly, lay out the candidate rules that fit her track record -- some kind of impersonal view, a wide person-affecting view, a sufficiency-threshold view, whatever else you can construct -- and show me where they agree and where they'd split on my energy case. Then give me your best guess with reasons, and tell me what further case I should save for the week after, the one that would actually tell the surviving rules apart. I care less about her answer than about what theory she's implicitly running.
