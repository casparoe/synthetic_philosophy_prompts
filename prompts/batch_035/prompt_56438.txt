I'm a software engineer at a company that builds LLM-based agents — the kind that read tickets, modify cloud infrastructure, and draft replies to customers. I have no formal philosophy background; everything I know about the frame problem comes from secondhand reading, and I may be using the term loosely. Our agents maintain a workspace of derived state: cached facts, working assumptions ("the staging cluster is still on the old schema version," "this customer is on the Business tier"), and this state goes stale when the world changes out from under it. Right now every agent developer handles invalidation ad hoc: some re-read everything from source on every step, which is expensive and slow; some trust the cache until something breaks; and the worst failures are the silent ones, where the agent acts confidently on a fact that stopped being true an hour ago.

My rough understanding is that philosophers and classical AI researchers spent decades on exactly this cluster of issues — how a system reasoning about the effects of actions figures out what stays true without exhaustively rechecking everything, what counts as "relevant" to a change, and all the ways the obvious formal solutions failed. I want that translated into something my team can actually run.

Please write a decision procedure an engineer can apply whenever our agent is about to act on a cached or assumed fact: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure says "stop, re-verify from source" versus "proceed on the cached value"; where it hands the decision to a human because the question is about how much risk the business is willing to accept rather than anything an engineer can settle; and where it should say outright that the procedure cannot help. Keep whatever distinctions from the philosophy are load-bearing — I gather there's a difference between the frame problem proper and something called the qualification problem, and a genuine question about whether relevance to a change can ever be captured by a finite list of rules — but drop the formalism; nobody on my team will read logic notation, and they shouldn't have to.

Then run the procedure on three cases from our backlog: (1) an agent that cached a customer's plan tier at the start of a long support session and is about to offer a refund policy that depends on that tier; (2) an agent maintaining a model of a Kubernetes cluster that must decide whether to re-query the cluster's live state before resizing a deployment; (3) an agent drafting a reply that relies on a fact from a document fetched earlier in the same conversation, in a session where a human is editing that document concurrently. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — I'd rather learn that now than in a postmortem.

Two honesty requests. First, mark plainly wherever the procedure is a judgment call dressed up as a checklist; this is going into an engineering runbook, and false precision is worse than none. Second, if my framing is confused — if the frame problem as philosophers actually understand it doesn't apply to caching and assumption-invalidation in LLM agents, if I'm conflating it with something else, or if the honest answer is that this is ordinary cache-invalidation engineering and the philosophy contributes nothing a runbook can use — tell me that directly instead of dressing up the connection to make me feel erudite. But if there is a real connection, be specific about which parts of it earn their place.
