My roommate at summer math camp left me with an argument I can't refute but can't accept, and I need to know where it goes wrong. (1) A rational person updates by Bayes' rule: what you should believe after seeing some evidence is fully determined by what you believed before, together with that evidence. (2) So two people who start with the same prior beliefs and end up with the same evidence must end up believing the same thing. (3) Honest people who argue long enough can make their evidence the same, since they can just tell each other everything they know and each can tell that the other isn't lying. (4) Therefore two honest, intelligent people who have fully talked through a question -- free will, say -- cannot rationally remain in disagreement; if they still disagree, one of them is failing at (1) or lying at (3). (5) But my two philosophy teachers, both honest and sharp, have disagreed about free will through twenty years of lunchtime arguments, and neither of them is stupid or deceptive. (6) So either the argument proves that nearly all persistent disagreement among decent thinkers is irrationality, or something in it is broken. He added that there's a famous theorem that rational agents who know each other to be rational cannot agree to disagree, which makes me suspect the flaw is somewhere subtle rather than obvious. Which step should I give up, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me? For what it's worth, the steps I'd most like to keep are (3) and (5), but I'm not even sure the flaw isn't hiding in (1) itself.
