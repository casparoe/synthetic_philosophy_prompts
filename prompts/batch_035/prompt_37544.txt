I'm TAing an upper-level seminar on free will and moral responsibility this semester, and I have to grade a batch of midterm answers this weekend. The course spent two weeks on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument (the version in his 1994 paper in Philosophical Studies), and the midterm question was:

"In Strawson's Basic Argument, what would a person need in order to be truly or ultimately morally responsible, and why does Strawson think this is impossible? Be sure to state clearly whether the argument depends on the truth of determinism. Then present one objection to the argument and assess whether it succeeds."

Here's one student's answer, which I'd like you to grade. Please give it a letter grade and then go through it point by point -- what it gets right, what it gets wrong, and how much each issue should matter for the final grade. I want comments specific enough that the student can see exactly which sentences are earning or losing points, not a general impression.

"Strawson's Basic Argument is his argument that no one is ever morally responsible for anything they do. The core idea is that to be truly responsible you would have to be responsible for how you are -- specifically for your character and mental states -- in such a way that you somehow brought yourself into existence, or at least caused your own nature. This is what he calls being causa sui, a cause of yourself. Strawson uses the Consequence Argument to show this: everything we do flows from our character, and our character results from heredity and environment, things fixed before we were born and outside our control. So like all incompatibilist arguments, the Basic Argument relies on causal determinism being true. If determinism turned out to be false, and some of our choices were genuinely undetermined, the argument would lose its grip, and something like agent-causal libertarianism could restore responsibility, since then we would be originating causes of our actions. The reason self-creation is impossible is that it would require an infinite regress: to create yourself you'd have to already exist, and to be responsible for the self that did the creating you'd need another act of self-creation, and so on forever, and obviously nobody has time to complete an infinite series of self-creations. Strawson distinguishes this ultimate responsibility, which is impossible, from ordinary moral responsibility, which he also denies -- he says no one ever deserves praise or blame in any sense, since the ordinary kind collapses into the ultimate kind once you press on it. As an objection, consider the compatibilist response that we can still be responsible in the everyday way if we act from our own desires without coercion. Strawson's answer is that this just isn't the kind of responsibility worth wanting, so the objection doesn't touch the argument, and I agree -- the argument seems sound to me."

For what it's worth, the class did cover Peter Strawson's 'Freedom and Resentment' and the Consequence Argument separately earlier in the term, so I do expect students to keep the pieces straight. Give me the grade and the specific comments that justify it.
