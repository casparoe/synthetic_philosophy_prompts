For about a year and a half my blame reading group has been going in circles on the hypocrisy thing and I'd like an actual diagnosis of why it's stuck, not another round of the arguments themselves.

The setup everyone accepts: there's something off when someone blames another person for a fault they themselves have. Classic case, the serial adulterer who gets self-righteous about his wife flirting with a coworker. Her flirting isn't excused, and yet his blaming her somehow misfires. Fine. The problem is explaining the misfire, and we've been looping between the same three stories. One: he's inconsistent — by blaming her he commits himself to a standard he flouts, so he's violated some norm of standing that blame requires. Two: inconsistency isn't it, since we're fine with lots of inconsistent blamers (nobody has clean hands); the real problem is meddling — he's butting into a moral relationship that isn't his business, like a third party poking around someone else's marriage. Three: the unfair-advantage story — he gets to look morally superior on the cheap, hoarding the benefits of the vice while charging her for hers.

Each story gets worked over with the same counterexamples every month. The reformed hypocrite — he did the same thing for years, stopped, genuinely changed, and it still feels weird when he blames people for it. The meddler who isn't a hypocrite at all. The jerk who never did what he's blaming you for but somehow has less standing than someone who did. And then the collapse worry: if standing is what the non-hypocrite has, does every decent person have standing to blame everyone for everything? That can't be right either.

Here's my working theory, offered as just that: standing looks less like a fact about whether his blame is warranted and more like recusal in law. A judge with a conflict of interest gets pulled off the case even when his ruling would've been correct. We don't agonize over whether the conflicted judge's verdict is "unfitting" — the disqualification is role-based, practical, and totally compatible with him being right. Maybe blame's standing is the same kind of thing, and the debate stalls because half of us are asking a question about the normative status of the blame itself while the other half are asking a question about who's allowed to perform the act of blaming in public. If that's right, the disagreement is partly verbal, "standing" being one word for two jobs.

But maybe I'm wrong and it's a real substantive fight, or maybe the whole concept of standing is a borrowing from the courtroom that never earned its keep in ethics — a couple of people in the literature have basically said we should retire it.

So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? And given your diagnosis, what would even count as progress here — a unifying account, a formal distinction, just dropping the concept, or nothing? Be honest if the answer is nothing.
