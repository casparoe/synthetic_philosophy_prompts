I'm part of a small online reading group on decision theory, and our most recent meeting dissolved into a genuinely drawn-out argument about two cases that look nearly identical on the surface but seem to pull in opposite directions: counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail. In the version of the mugging we discussed, a known-reliable predictor tells you that before you woke up this morning it flipped a fair coin: had the coin landed heads, it would have deposited $1,000,000 into your account if and only if it predicted you would hand over $100 upon being asked; the coin in fact landed tails, and it is now asking for the $100. In the blackmail case, an equally reliable party demands $1,000 to prevent an asteroid from striking your city, but with a twist in the fine print: it sent you the letter if and only if exactly one of the following holds — its simulation of you concluded you would pay, or the asteroid is genuinely on its way, but never both. Since you received the letter, paying is apparently evidence that the asteroid will not hit, though nothing you do causally affects its trajectory. Half our group insisted that whatever verdict you give in one case, you should give the mirrored verdict in the other; the other half thought the two could come apart.

I'd like a careful, well-organized comparison of the two cases. Spell out precisely what the structural difference is: in the mugging, refusing to condition on the observed coin toss is what makes paying attractive, whereas in the blackmail, conditioning on your own response is what makes paying look attractive even though it is causally inert. Explain which decision rules come apart in which case — updateful versus updateless reasoning, causal versus evidential styles of conditioning — and whether the scenarios are genuinely symmetric inversions of one another or whether some asymmetry permits a consistent agent to pay in one and refuse in the other. Please also be explicit about whether you think a single, stable decision procedure handles both cases without changing its story midway.

Finally, please evaluate a hybrid one of our members proposed, half as a joke: a predictor flips a coin hidden from you. If it lands heads, it sets aside $5,000 for you, payable at day's end if and only if it predicted you would pay $100 on tails. If it lands tails, it threatens to leak your embarrassing diary to your employer unless you pay $100 — but it only makes the threat at all if it predicted you would cave. The coin has in fact landed tails, the threat has arrived, and you know all of the above. Should someone who refused the original blackmail also refuse here? Should someone who paid in the mugging also pay here? I'd like an actual verdict on the hybrid, with the reasoning behind it laid out step by step rather than left as an exercise.
