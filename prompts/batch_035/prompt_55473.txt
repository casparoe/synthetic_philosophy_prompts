I'm a novelist who teaches a semester-long craft seminar in an MFA program — fourteen students, mostly mid-draft on their first novels — and I've become obsessed with a problem that I suspect is philosophical rather than technical, though my philosophical education ended with an undergraduate survey twenty years ago. When I write a reveal (the masked stranger has been Elena's brother all along; the narrator is the child from chapter two), the identity sentence feels strange in a way my students can sense but not name: it reads as shocking, yet once it lands, everyone agrees it was true all along and could not have been otherwise without breaking the book, and yet nobody inside the story — and arguably nobody writing it, before the reveal — could have known it by sitting down and thinking. A friend with a philosophy PhD tells me I have stumbled into Kripke's territory: rigid designation, and identity statements that are necessary but only knowable a posteriori. She also tells me the same machinery bears on why names in my notebooks don't behave like disguised descriptions — "the detective" can be true of many characters, but the name I gave her at age eleven locks onto her whether or not she still qualifies as a detective by the final draft. I want my students to feel this rather than be told about it, because craft lectures about naming go in one ear and out the other.

The parameters: one three-hour workshop block, fourteen students, zero philosophy background, no lecture up front, and the philosophical vocabulary withheld until the debrief at the very end. Design me an activity — a game, a collaborative serial-fiction exercise, a staged case with real constraints, whatever serves it — in which the students fix reference by naming characters of their own invention, accumulate descriptive dossiers about those characters, and then discover identities between them, so that they run into the two halves of the phenomenon on their own: that a reveal was "true all along" and that retconning it feels less like learning something new and more like changing the world, and simultaneously that nothing in the dossier ever determined who was identical to whom. I'd like concrete rules, materials (I can print cards, I have a whiteboard, no laptops in the room), a minute-by-minute timeline, and some mechanism that makes the "discovery" feel like genuine investigation rather than the author simply decreeing an answer.

For the debrief, I want the mapping made explicit afterward: which moment in the activity corresponds to fixing a rigid designator, which to the discovery of an identity that was necessary but unknowable in advance, and how the pull they felt against retconning relates to the intuition philosophers lean on here. The classical example — the evening star turning out to be the morning star — should come out only in the debrief, not before. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I suspect some students will insist their character was "really" the other one all along because the dossier pointed there, and I suspect others will happily rewrite their fictions mid-game), and how I should handle each. Most importantly, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: I take it that whatever necessity holds within a fiction holds by authorial fiat, which is not the metaphysical necessity at issue, and a novelist's godlike relation to her characters is not an epistemology anyone has toward the actual world. Say plainly which parts of the real doctrine the students will have genuinely felt, and which parts I will have to assert from the front of the room because no in-fiction exercise can produce them.
