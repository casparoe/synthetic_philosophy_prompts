My friend Maya (second-year PhD in philosophy of science, very opinionated, refuses to state her principles on the grounds that stating them is how you get in trouble) and I have a running thing about the raven paradox — the one where "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens," so a white golf ball, being a non-black non-raven, seems like it should confirm the raven hypothesis just like a black raven does, which everyone agrees feels insane. Maya has very specific rulings about which observations count as confirming and which don't, and I've been keeping a list so I can finally pin down the rule she's running. She just says it's "obvious" every time I ask, which means it's definately a rule.

Her track record so far: (1) seeing a black raven while out birdwatching at the local park — confirms, obviously. (2) me marching to my closet, grabbing the white shoe I already knew was in there, and triumphantly presenting it as evidence — no confirmation at all, and she was genuinely annoyed, said that's the "lazy version" of the paradox. (3) my housemate filled a huge barrel with a few thousand random objects of unknown mixed contents, we drew blind and got a white golf ball — she said yes, that one technically confirms, by a smidge. (4) a second barrel that we both watched being filled with nothing but golf balls of assorted colors, blind draw, got a white one — no, confirms nothing about ravens whatsoever.

Now I've got two new cases lined up and I want to predict her before she rules, since she's the type to notice if I switch questions on her. Case A: her labmate built a joke machine that picks a random object from among the non-black things in a warehouse and dispenses it, and it dispensed a white golf ball. Case B: a raven netted at a bird sanctuary that, as a matter of stated policy, only admits birds already known to be black. The obvious reading of her four verdicts is something like "it only confirms if the procedure could have handed you a white raven," but I can see how that's not the only rule that fits — for one thing she never once bothered to ask how the birdwatching was done. So: what does each plausible rule predict for A and for B (note the two cases might not pull the same direction!), which rule would you bet she's actually running and why, and what further case would cleanly split the candidates apart? I'm less interested in the verdicts than in the rules, the disagreement, and the experiment.
