I spent last autumn on exchange in Tokyo and a summer working in Amsterdam, and the contrast between the two cities stuck with me: how differently each seemed to tolerate jaywalking, noise, dress, odd working hours, public drunkenness. When I mentioned this at a dinner, a friend who had just finished a pop-science book on cultural psychology announced that there was a whole research program explaining exactly this, and gave me her summary of it over coffee. It went, more or less, like this: "A famous cross-cultural psychologist proved the world splits into tight cultures, like Pakistan, Singapore, and India, and loose ones, like the Netherlands, Brazil, and the United States. Tight cultures got that way because they faced constant threats — famines, natural disasters, invasions — and threat causes tightness; the causation runs strictly one way, so once the threats recede, a culture inevitably loosens. It's basically collectivism relabeled: tight just means the group swallows the individual. Tight societies buy order at the price of authoritarianism and intolerance, while loose ones trade a little order for creativity, tolerance, and better mental health — and it all hangs together because people in tight cultures are simply born more rule-bound and conscientious by temperament. So the takeaway is that tight societies would be better off loosening up, and the rest of the world should encourage them to."

I have no way of judging whether this is a fair rendering. My instincts pull in different directions: the threat story fits what I noticed in Japan better than the alternative explanations people offered me, but a few phrases sounded off even to me — "collectivism relabeled" and "born more rule-bound" in particular — and the breezy "just loosen up" conclusion bothered me, since the Japanese students I met didn't strike me as repressed or as waiting to be freed from their own culture. I also can't tell whether the book's author actually makes the normative claim my friend ended with, or whether that was my friend's own editorializing layered on top of a descriptive finding.

Could you go through her summary claim by claim and tell me what to trust? Which parts would the researchers behind this framework accept as a fair statement of their findings, which are conflations of distinct things the framework deliberately keeps apart (is tightness really the same axis as individualism versus collectivism, for instance, or is that precisely the kind of confusion the researchers complain about?), which are overstatements of modest or contested results (how one-directional is the threat-causes-tightness story, and what does the framework say about trade-offs — do loose cultures really come out ahead on balance?), and which claims would the authors actively deny? Finally, please tell me whether her policy conclusion — that tight societies should loosen up and outsiders should encourage them to — follows from whatever portion of the summary survives the audit, or whether it smuggles in a value judgment about how much rule-following a society ought to demand that the research itself never takes a stand on. I'd like enough of the correction spelled out that I can go back to my friend with something more precise than "that's not quite it."
