I'm a software engineer with about fifteen years in distributed systems — consensus protocols, replication, exactly-once delivery, the usual. I have no formal philosophy background. I keep bumping into discussions of "acausal trade": cooperation between agents who cannot exchange messages, each paying costs to benefit the other because each can predict the other, in roughly the way two instances of the same deterministic program produce matching outputs without a network round-trip. The examples I've seen run from two civilizations at opposite ends of the universe, outside each other's light cones, each deciding to leave a gift for a counterparty it will never meet, to agents trading across time with a partner already dead. And the surrounding claims get stranger: that this trade can be real in the sense that a rational agent ought to pay costs for it now, that simulation substitutes for communication, that obligations to agents you will never interact with can nonetheless bind you. When I try to map this onto anything I know, it slides around. It isn't the two-generals problem, since there is no channel at all, unreliable or otherwise; it isn't shared configuration, since the parties never shared anything at runtime; it looks something like coordination by common provenance. But I can't tell how much of the philosophical claim survives that mapping and how much I'm flattening.

Please restate the debate over acausal trade in the vocabulary I actually use. On the pro side: the arguments that prediction can do the work of communication, that agents reasoning through similar procedures become logically correlated in a way that makes cooperation rational, that a simulated counterparty's interests count for something. On the con side: the arguments that you cannot really owe anything to an agent with which you share no causal history, that prediction without contact does not license paying costs, that the whole thing is a category error dressed up as a protocol. Use my terms as faithfully as you can: protocols versus messages, deterministic execution and replay, forks and common ancestry, optimistic execution ahead of a confirmation that will never arrive, distributed transactions with no commit point, out-of-band assumptions like both binaries being built from the same source tree. And be honest about the mapping — don't import my vocabulary's assumptions and present them as translation. In my world, coordination assumptions are stated up front, and a system that relies on an unstated one is a bug; I suspect that habit of mind is exactly what your residue report should be examining.

Then give me the residue. Which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto things I already know how to reason about? Which translate only with distortion — where does simulation-substitutes-for-communication get squeezed into replay, and what gets lost in the squeeze? Which parts have no expression in my vocabulary at all — is there anything the philosophers are arguing about with no counterpart in distributed systems, and if so, is that because my vocabulary is missing something or because their disagreement is less substantive than it looks? I want a verdict, in particular on whether acausal trade as presented smuggles in a commitment mechanism that no protocol I have ever shipped actually has, and on whether the residue points to something real my framework cannot say or to a place where the philosophical claim was never as precise as its proponents take it to be.
