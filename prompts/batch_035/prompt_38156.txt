Analyse the wave of Confederate monument removals in the United States — set off by the June 2015 murder of nine Black worshippers at a church in Charleston by a white supremacist who had photographed himself with the Confederate battle flag, and running through the 2017 removals in New Orleans and the violence in Charlottesville over a statue of Robert E. Lee — as a test case for the German concept of Vergangenheitsbewältigung.

Begin by laying out what the concept actually involves, treated not as a vague synonym for remembering history but as a specific institutional programme: a successor polity's working-through of a criminal past, comprising public admission of responsibility, memorialisation of the victims, criminalisation of the offending ideology and its symbols, and a generational confrontation in which the children of the perpetrators indict their parents — the Eichmann trial and the West German student movement, the Warsaw genuflection of 1970, the historians' dispute of the mid-1980s. Then apply this framework to the American South with a disciplined mapping: who plays the role of the successor state, what corresponds to denazification, what corresponds to the memorials to the victims, and where the analogy has no counterpart at all.

I want you to be honest about the points at which the mapping breaks, because I suspect they are the interesting part. In Germany, the working-through was imposed by unconditional defeat and occupation, and the offending state ceased to exist; in the South, the defeated side won the peace, wrote the historiography as the Lost Cause, and erected its monuments decades after the war, during the establishment and consolidation of Jim Crow, as assertions of continuing dominance rather than as acts of remembrance. The victims in the German case were largely abroad and the harm was concluded; in the American case they are internal to the polity and the relevant harms are ongoing. There was no caesura in Southern political identity comparable to 1945, and no external enforcer of confrontation — which is one reason the monument disputes had to be won argument by argument, city council by city council, over more than a century.

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction and tell me what the episode shows about the concept itself. Is Vergangenheitsbewältigung a general model of how a polity can come to terms with a criminal past, or is it an artefact of a configuration that almost never recurs — military defeat, total collapse of the regime's legitimacy, occupation, and a victim population with an eventual state of its own? Where the concept is silent or distorting when applied to the American South, say so rather than forcing it to speak, and consider whether importing the German vocabulary into these debates illuminates them or merely lends a moral authority that the local conditions never earned. If you think a different lens — for instance the Turkish or Japanese cases, where the criminal past is an empire or a colonial war rather than a slave system, and where the state never acknowledged defeat — carves the terrain better, sketch that alternative and say what it captures that the German framework misses.
