hey, so I need a decision memo drafted and I'm hoping you can help. Bit of background: I sit on the risk-assessment subcommittee of a national research council that advises on funding for AI safety programs. We have a fixed pot, roughly 500 million over five years, and we have to split it between two portfolios the secretariat is calling "burst mitigation" and "gradual governance." Burst mitigation assumes a fast takeoff: once AI systems can meaningfully accelerate AI research itself, progress stops responding to normal regulatory timescales, so the money goes to things like emergency-deployable alignment teams, pre-built automated oversight infrastructure, and fail-safe mechanisms designed to work without human deliberation. Gradual governance assumes the opposite: progress is dragged out over years or decades by bottlenecks outside raw cognition — compute supply chains, the need for real-world experiments, human approval loops, economic integration being slower than lab progress — so the money goes to monitoring capabilities, incremental auditing requirements, and building institutions that get better as deployment expands. The split has to be concrete (an actual percentage), and we also have to say whether there's a trigger condition that would justify reallocation mid-cycle.

The problem is the committee is deadlocked, and the deadlock isn't about facts anyone can look up — it's about the underlying conceptual picture. The fast-takeoff faction basically holds that intelligence is the kind of thing that compounds: better AI research produces better AI researchers, and the loop, once closed, runs away. The slow-takeoff faction thinks this picture smuggles in a big assumption, namely that "intelligence" is a single dial you can keep turning up, rather than a bundle of different capacities with separate bottlenecks, some of which (physical experimentation, tacit human knowledge, organizational slack) don't yield to more cognition no matter how much of it you have. And they point out that even if the loop closes, returns might diminish rather than accelerate — each round of self-improvement harder than the last. The fast faction replies that the loop doesn't need to be frictionless, just faster than our ability to adapt institutionally, which is a low bar.

So here's the ask. You're a professional philosopher of science and forecasting expert who's spent years on exactly this cluster of questions — recursive self-improvement, takeoff dynamics, the nature of intelligence as a variable. Think carefully and maybe do a little brainstorming on paper first before commiting to a recommendation. Then draft the memo: recommend a specific split, justify it, and — this is important for the vote to go anywhere — engage the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously, not as a token paragraph. The chair will smell a whitewash a mile away.

One more thing, and please take it seriously: a lot of the argument here runs through claims that are genuinely contested among people who know this stuff well — whether intelligence is one scalable thing or many, whether self-improvement returns accelerate or diminish, whether institutional lag is the real danger or a red herring. Some things are more settled (e.g., that AI is already being used to accelerate parts of AI R&D itself, and that compute is a real constraint with real lead times). I want the memo to clearly separate the settled stuff from the contested stuff, and not present some contested view as if it were the consensus of the field — the committee has people on it who will notice if it does, and honestly that's the fastest way to get the memo ignored. If the honest answer is that the split should be driven by uncertainty itself rather than by betting on one side, say that.
