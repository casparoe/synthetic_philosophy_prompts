The exam question (25 points):

"Explain the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and say precisely what assumption, or combination of assumptions, each is supposed to put pressure on. Then evaluate the response that abandons flat-out belief in favor of credences: does that response dissolve both paradoxes, or does one of them survive in some form? Defend your answer."

The student's answer, reproduced with light typo fixes:

"Both paradoxes attack the Lockean thesis, which says that belief just is sufficiently high probability. In Kyburg's preface paradox, an author of a nonfiction book believes each individual claim in it — she checked each one carefully, and each is independently supported — and each belief is rational. But in the preface she sincerely writes that she is confident the book contains at least one error, since any book of that length by a fallible author will contain one. So she believes each claim, believes that at least one of the collection is false, and by closure under conjunction believes the conjunction of all the claims — which she also disbelieves. Contradiction. Makinson's lottery paradox works the same way in reverse: with a million tickets and one winner, each ticket has probability 0.999999 of losing, so by the Lockean thesis you believe of each ticket that it will lose, and by closure you believe no ticket wins, contradicting what you know, that one will win.

So both paradoxes show that probabilism itself is incoherent: you cannot simultaneously hold that rational belief should track probability and keep your beliefs closed under conjunction. The upshot is that the Bayesian program of replacing belief with credences fails, and the paradoxes prove this. On the credence view, the lottery case is fine — there is no flat belief in 'ticket 7 loses' to be inconsistent, just a credence, and the agent's credence that some ticket wins is 1. But the preface case still refutes it: the author has credence about 0.99 in each of a thousand claims, and credence nearly 1 that at least one is false, and those credences are themselves probabilistically inconsistent with each other, since probabilities that high across that many claims cannot coexist. So the credence-only view handles the lottery but is caught by the preface, and the paradoxes together show that no purely quantitative account of belief can work. The real lesson is that rationality has to be qualitative at bottom: belief is not a degree but a stance, and the preface author's stance toward each claim, unlike her stance toward the lottery, is based on evidence about that very claim, which is why the paradoxes cannot be treated as the same case and why the author is rational to believe each claim and also that not all of them are true."
