**Exam question (10 points):** State Aumann's agreement theorem as precisely as you can, including its assumptions, and briefly discuss what it does and does not imply about the rationality of real-world disagreement.

**Student's answer:** Aumann's 1976 paper "Agreeing to Disagree" proves that two Bayesian agents cannot rationally disagree about the probability of an event if their posteriors are common knowledge between them. The theorem rests on two key assumptions: first, that the agents share a common prior over the states of the world; second, that they have the same information, so that their posteriors are based on identical evidence. Given these assumptions, if each agent's posterior probability for some event becomes common knowledge, the posteriors must be equal — any difference would mean at least one agent is violating Bayesian updating. The theorem describes a dynamic process: the agents exchange their posterior estimates back and forth, and each announcement functions like new evidence that forces the other to update, so that the posteriors converge step by step until agreement is reached. In terms of real-world implications, Aumann himself drew the conclusion that everyday disagreement is straightforwardly evidence of irrationality — since people who genuinely shared a common prior and common knowledge could not disagree, the fact that we do disagree shows that we are not the Bayesians we like to think we are.
