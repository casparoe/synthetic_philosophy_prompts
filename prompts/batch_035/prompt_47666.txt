The exam question was:

"Q4. Putnam asks us to imagine Twin Earth: a planet just like ours, except that the liquid in its lakes, rivers and oceans is not H2O but a different substance, XYZ, indistinguishable from water in everyday contexts (clear, potable, boils, freezes, etc.). An Earthling, Oscar, has a twin on Twin Earth who is molecule-for-molecule identical to him, and it is 1750 — nobody on either planet knows anything about the chemical composition of either liquid. (a) Explain what Putnam thinks this shows about the meaning of natural-kind terms like 'water'. (b) Explain what work the 1750 setting does in the argument. (c) Briefly present one objection to the argument."

And the student's answer, as written:

"Putnam's point is that the meaning of a term like 'water' is not fully determined by what is in the speaker's head. Oscar and his twin are internally identical — same brain, same dispositions, and in 1750 the same total ignorance of chemistry — yet 'water' as used on Earth refers to H2O, while 'water' as used by the twin refers to XYZ. If meanings supervened on individual psychological states, those two words would have to mean the same thing, but they don't, since one picks out H2O and the other picks out XYZ. So for natural kind terms at least, meaning depends partly on the environment. This is essentially the point Burge later made with his Twin Earth arthritis thought experiment: what's in your head doesn't by itself fix what your words mean. It also connects to Putnam's division of linguistic labor — ordinary speakers can use 'water' correctly while deferring to chemists for the real reference.

The 1750 setting: Putnam includes it to show that the Earth/Twin Earth difference is not a difference in knowledge. In 1750 'water' meant the same thing on both planets — just watery stuff — since nobody could tell H2O from XYZ. It was only once chemists worked out the compositions that the meanings came apart: 'water' came to mean H2O on Earth and XYZ on Twin Earth. So the reference of a natural kind term gets fixed by the state of science, and ordinary usage before the science had a different, more everyday meaning.

Objection: Fodor's narrow content. Fodor argued that Oscar and his twin still share the same narrow content — there is a level of content that stays entirely in the head — so in one sense they do mean the same thing by 'water'. Putnam's reply is basically to grant this but insist that narrow contents aren't meanings, they're at best ingredients for a theory of meaning."
