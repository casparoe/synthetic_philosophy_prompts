I am setting the formal epistemology section of our PhD comprehensive examination. Please write one essay question on the common prior assumption, answerable in ninety minutes, that cannot be passed by reciting the statement of Aumann's agreement theorem. The question should turn on a line a colleague of mine presses in teaching: that a prior is less like a belief than like a coordinate system, so two agents with different priors are not really disagreeing about anything but are merely describing the same state space in different terms; on this reading, the agreement theorem shows not that persistent disagreement is irrational but that it is impossible in any strict sense, and the common prior assumption is then a convention about which coordinate system to share, justified the way conventions about units of measurement are. Candidates must decide whether this reading can sustain the normative use of the common prior assumption in arguments against the reasonableness of disagreement, engage the familiar Harsanyi-style suggestion that differences in priors are really hidden differences in information and the worry that this makes the assumption trivial or unfalsifiable, and say something about what happens to learning and communication when priors do differ, including whether communication must fail to produce agreement. Please supply the question itself, a model answer of roughly six hundred words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth four marks each out of twenty, each with a one-sentence note on how candidates characteristically miss it. The model answer must defend a definite verdict on the coordinate-system reading rather than surveying the options and leaving the choice to the reader, and please deliver everything as continuous prose only: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
