I've become a fairly committed entitlement theorist and I'd like to have the position stress-tested rather than just defended. Some background: I took a political philosophy seminar last spring where we worked through Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia alongside Rawls, and I came away persuaded by the historical approach to justice in holdings — a distribution is just if it arose from a just earlier distribution through voluntary transfers, tracing back to acquisitions that satisfied something like a Lockean proviso, with rectification cleaning up past violations. The Wilt Chamberlain-style argument still seems to me to show that any patterned or end-state principle requires continuous interference with voluntary choices, and Nozick's likening of taxing earnings to forced labor strikes me as roughly right. My seminar friends keep pushing back with intuitions about the undeserving rich and the moral arbitrariness of natural talents, but those have never moved me much, and I've started to suspect I'm just not the kind of person those arguments reach.

So instead of another round of that, I want a map of the position's exposure. What would actually have to be true for the entitlement theory to be wrong — which arguments, which hypothetical cases, which commitments it has in other areas (say, about self-ownership, or about what makes a choice voluntary enough to generate title)? I want the genuine defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an account for each of whether a serious entitlement theorist should concede that it counts against the theory or explain it away, and on what grounds. A few things I want treated as live vulnerabilities rather than footnotes: the theory's own admissions about rectification, since I understand Nozick conceded the theory is deeply incomplete without an account of how to repair historical injustice, and rectification threatens to drag patterned considerations back in through the side door; the baseline problem for the proviso, where it matters a lot whether the relevant comparison is being no worse off than before the appropriation or something more egalitarian; and cases that probe the voluntariness of transfer — I have to admit that even I waver when I imagine someone selling a kidney to pay off a crushing debt, and I can't tell whether that wavering is a defeater or a bias.

Beyond the ranking, two further things. First, an honest assessment of whether the theory, once qualified — by proviso baselines, stringency conditions on transfer, the rectification caveat — has been insulated into something that no possible case could count against, and whether that would even be a defect in a theory of this kind, which is meant to be a prioristic rather than empirical. Second, hold your own answer to the standard you're applying to mine: close by telling me which single consideration would most likely move your ranking of the defeaters, and where your reasoning here is most likely to fail — if I later found that you'd gotten something wrong about, say, the proviso's baseline structure or about what the theory's defenders actually concede, which of your verdicts should I treat as unstable?
