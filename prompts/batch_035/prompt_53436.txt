I'm the registrar of a mid-sized regional maritime museum, and I need a decision memo for our board of trustees before their November meeting. The memo concerns the *Margaret Alden*, a 1902 Gloucester fishing schooner we've displayed afloat since 1968. Here is the situation I need the memo to address.

Over fifty-plus years of maintenance, we have, as all such museums do, replaced timbers as they rotted. Our best guess from the shipwright's logs is that roughly 65% of the vessel's current material postdates 1950. We kept every significant replaced plank, frame section, and fitting in climate-controlled storage, tagged with the date and location of removal — a practice begun by a former registrar on her own initiative, which is now about to become our problem. A local foundation has offered to fund a full restoration, and their proposed method is unusual: rather than only replacing the failing members, they want to also reassemble the stored original material into a second, freestanding hull. When the project finishes, our dock would hold two vessels, each with a strong claim to being *the* *Margaret Alden* — the continuously maintained one, and the one made of the original wood.

Complicating this: our founding donor's deed bequeathed her endowment "for the perpetual preservation of the schooner *Margaret Alden*," and the trustees must tell the probate court which vessel the endowment should now fund. We also have a pending loan request from a museum abroad that has asked to borrow "the original keel" for a shipbuilding exhibition, and our insurance broker wants to know, in writing, which of the two vessels is insured as the 1902 artifact and which as a 2027 reconstruction.

You are a professional philosopher specializing in material objects, identity over time, and cultural heritage. Before drafting, brainstorm the main lines of analysis — mereological essentialism, the best-candidate or sortal-continuity approach, the thought that ships are historical artifacts rather than mere collections of matter, and the pragmatic view that "the *Margaret Alden*" is just a name whose reference the museum can legitimately fix by decision rather than discovery. Consider which of these actually changes what the board should do, as opposed to merely re-describing it, and consider whether the deed, the loan, and the insurance question might pull in different directions.

Then draft the memo the trustees could act on: a concrete recommendation on (1) whether to accept the restoration as proposed, modify it, or decline it, (2) which vessel the endowment should fund and how to justify that to the probate court, and (3) how to word the loan and insurance designations. Aim for roughly 900–1200 words in plain institutional prose that a trustee who is a retired dentist can follow. Engage the strongest consideration on the losing side of each recommendation seriously, not as a strawman — particularly the view that we have already destroyed the *Margaret Alden* decades ago and are now merely choosing which successor to deceive the public about. Do not pad the memo with the history of the Ship of Theseus puzzle or name-drop philosophers for display; the trustees should see the reasoning, not the bibliography. Close with a short section flagging what the board must decide on grounds philosophy cannot settle.
