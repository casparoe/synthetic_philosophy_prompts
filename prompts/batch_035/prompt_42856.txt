For about a year now I've been chewing on the old heap puzzle and the bald man puzzle in my spare time, mostly through arguments on a philosophy forum where two camps have formed. One camp says there really is a sharp boundary — some exact number of hairs where baldness begins — we just can't ever know which number it is. That always struck me as absurd, so I was drawn to the other camp, whose position I gathered is the more popular one among philosophers who work on this: vague words like "bald" simply aren't fully precise, and a statement using them is true just in case it comes out true no matter how you make the word precise, so long as you keep the clear cases clear — the man with no hair stays bald and the man with a full head of hair stays not-bald no matter what. This seemed to respect my gut feeling that there's no magic hair count, while still letting me say useful things like "a man with no hair is bald" and even "either he's bald or he isn't."

But last week I noticed an argument that seems to wreck the view I liked, and I've been unable to unsee it. (1) A statement involving a vague word is true exactly when it comes out true on every admissible way of making that word precise. (2) A way of making a word precise, by its very nature, settles every case: it puts the man with 200 hairs either in the bald group or in the not-bald group, and it draws its edge between two neighbouring hair counts. (3) So consider the statement that there is some number of hairs n such that a man with n hairs is bald and a man with n+1 is not. (4) That statement comes out true on every admissible way of making "bald" precise, since every one of them draws its edge between two neighbouring counts. (5) By (1), the statement is therefore true, full stop. (6) But that statement is precisely the claim that there is a sharp boundary — the claim only the rival camp believes, the one the whole view was invented to avoid. (7) So the view I liked ends up asserting exactly what it was meant to deny, and I can no longer see any daylight between it and the camp I rejected.

I'd most like to keep the thought that there is no sharp line, since that's the entire reason I found the view attractive, and I'd also like to keep (1), because it's what lets the view explain why "either he's bald or he isn't" still comes out true while individual borderline statements don't. But every way I try to break the argument seems to cost me one of those two things. Tell me which step I should reject and what rejecting it costs, including whether the cost is worth paying or whether I should just accept that the two camps were saying the same thing all along.
