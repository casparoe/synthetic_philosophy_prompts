I'm a phd student in political theory, and in two weeks im presenting chapter 3 of my dissertation at a departmental workshop. The chapter is on the Condorcet jury theorem and what happens when you stop assuming independent votes. My line, roughly: the standard repair job -- letting votes be correlated, via common information sources, media effects, herding, whatever -- technically saves the theorem but at a price nobody talks about. Once you have correlation, the probability of a correct majority no longer just depends on average competence, it depends on the correlation structure, which voters can't observe and institutions can't audit. So the theorem stops being an argument for majority rule as such. But I don't want to just concede to the skeptics: I argue that correlation isn't fatal, it's a design variable. If you treat correlated error like correlated evidence -- something you discount rather than throw out -- you get something like a supermajority quota rule: the more correlated the votes, the bigger the margin you should demand before you call the outcome epistemically justified. Democracy survives, but with the decision rule indexed to the error structure, not fixed at 50%+1.

The audience matters here. It's a mid-sized political theory department: mostly people who know the CJT at the textbook level (competence, independence, sincerity, big n, hooray for majorities), plus one formal theorist who genuinely knows the correlated-jurors and network stuff, plus one senior person who thinks the entire epistemic-democracy framing is a mistake and the proceduralists settled this decades ago. There will also be a methods guy who will absolutely ask me what actual numbers I'd plug in for voter competence and correlation.
