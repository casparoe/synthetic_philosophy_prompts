I'm putting together a unit on whether thought experiments can yield genuine knowledge for a module on scientific reasoning. The classic cases — Galileo's falling bodies, Maxwell's demon, Schrödinger's cat — are so familiar that students recite the verdicts instead of reasoning about them, so I want a fresh case built to order. Construct a thought experiment meeting these specifications: it must be set in an ordinary domain with no idealisations of the frictionless-plane sort; it must be performable in principle as a real experiment by an ordinarily funded laboratory; and it must be engineered so that the intuitive verdict most people give is wrong, with the error traceable to the audience's background experience — the intuition must track what is typical in their lives rather than what the case actually stipulates. After the scenario, please: (a) explain what, if anything, the thought experiment teaches before anyone runs it, and address the obvious objection that, being performable, its epistemic work is simply deferred to the real experiment; (b) identify exactly which background belief is doing the work, and show how the verdict changes when that belief is varied; and (c) assess whether the case fits the view that a thought experiment is merely an argument from premises we already accept, or whether it pressures that view. Finish with the strongest reply available to someone who denies that thought experiments can yield knowledge at all. Please write in British English.
