I've been testing my intuitions about when something is genuinely implicated rather than merely said, and my verdicts don't fit together as neatly as I'd hoped. Here they are. A reference letter that praises only the candidate's English and punctuality implicates that he's no good at philosophy, and I'd hold the writer responsible for that even though every sentence is true. If a colleague tells me some of the students passed, I take that to mean not all did, and this inference feels nearly automatic — it survives even when the colleague is a stranger I know nothing about. Yet when I read the same sentence in a terse automated email the department sends out, I still draw the not-all inference, even though no person wrote it. And when a thirteenth-century chronicle says some of the besieged escaped, I read it as implying that not all did, though the chronicler's intentions are inaccessible in every way that matters to me. My tentative principle is Grice's own: an utterance implicates p when the speaker intends the hearer to work out p by recognizing that very intention, against the background of the Cooperative Principle and the maxims. That principle handles the reference letter and the colleague, but it seems to leave the automated email and the chronicle without implicatures, which contradicts my verdicts. Two further cases muddy things. If I deliberately play a voice memo aloud in a café so a journalist at the next table overhears my boss confess, without intending her to recognize that I intend her to hear, I don't think I've communicated anything to her — I've merely influenced her. But if I leave my partner's hospital bracelet on the counter so my flatmate infers she's been admitted, that feels closer to telling him something, though no one has uttered a word and the intention is equally hidden. What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures all of these: state it precisely, say which clause each case motivates, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save. In particular, should I abandon the idea that all implicature involves a speaker's recognizable intention, or should I instead deny that anything is implicated where there's no speaker — and if the latter, what becomes of my reading of the chronicle? Please also tell me what your preferred account predicts for a case I haven't listed: a parrot that has been trained to say lovely weather whenever it rains, and an auction catalog's stock phrase that a painting's condition is consistent with age.
