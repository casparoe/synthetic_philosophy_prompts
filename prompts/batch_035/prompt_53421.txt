I've been reading up on the doomsday argument (the Carter/Leslie style one, where your birth rank among all humans ever gets used as evidence about how many humans there will ever be) and something keeps bugging me. People keep saying the math is basically just Bayes, so once you accept the setup you can't really argue with it. Fine. But my issue is with the setup: doesn't treating yourself as a random sample of humanity presuppose that humans are spread out roughly evenly across our whole run as a species? Like if the future holds 500 trillion people living in space colonies, or conversely if population collapses right after 2100, then a "random human" isn't evenly placed in calendar time at all, and it seems like the sampling assumption should break, no?

And here's the thing, we actually have data to fix this. There are UN-style projections showing population peaking somewhere around 10 billion near the end of the century and then declining for a long while after. That curve is super lopsided either way you slice it. So my question: has anyone redone the doomsday math plugging in the real projected population curve instead of the uniform assumption, and does doom still come out as likely once you use the actual numbers? Feels like if the argument survives contact with real population curves someone should have shown that by now, and if it doesnt, then the people still repeating the argument should probably stop.

(also my friend has a side fix he's fond of: he says the reference class should only include people who could actually understand and run the argument, since a subsistence farmer in 3000 BC could never have been sampled into it anyway, and once the class is that arbitrary you might as well ignore the whole thing. Something about that feels too easy to me but I cant put my finger on why)
