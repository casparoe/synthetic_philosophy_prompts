A classmate and I have been arguing about epistemic akrasia — believing P while also believing that your total evidence doesn't support P — and whether it is ever rational. We ended up constructing an argument that seems to prove far more than either of us wanted, and neither of us can locate the fault.

(1) Higher-order evidence — evidence about the reliability of your own reasoning, rather than about the world — can itself be misleading. The neurologist who tells you the drug you were given selectively impairs logical reasoning might be mistaken or lying; the anonymous note warning you about your own biases might be a prank. In such cases you may rationally retain your first-order confidence: if you discover the note was a prank, you are entitled to go right back to believing your proof.

(2) In those cases, what you do is make a comparative judgment: that the higher-order evidence is more likely to be misleading than your first-order reasoning is to be unsound. The first order wins the comparison, so it wins.

(3) This comparison is always available. However convincing the higher-order evidence looks, you can always ask whether the error lies there rather than in your object-level reasoning, and nothing external forces either answer.

(4) Whether the higher-order evidence is misleading because of a discovered prank, an honest error you happen to uncover, or nothing in particular, the permission works the same way: whenever the comparative judgment comes out in favor of the first order, you may discount the higher-order evidence.

(5) So the rational agent always has a route to keeping first-order confidence at full strength: run the comparison, conclude that the first-order reasoning is in better shape, set the higher-order evidence aside.

(6) Therefore higher-order evidence never rationally defeats first-order confidence.

(7) Therefore someone who knows with certainty that they have just taken a drug that destroys the ability to do logic, and who retains complete confidence in the logical conclusions they reached that morning, is fully rational.

(8) That is unacceptable.

I want to keep (1) and reject (7). My classmate thinks the problem is that the comparative judgment in (2) is itself a piece of first-order reasoning of exactly the kind the higher-order evidence calls into question, so it cannot be used to rescue its own reliability. But I can't see why that isn't special pleading for higher-order evidence, and my classmate can't explain why self-undermining matters here without first showing that the akratic combination — believing P while believing you shouldn't — is irrational, which is the very question we started with. Which step or transition do you reject, what does rejecting it cost, and can you make the diagnosis without begging the question against either the akratic or the anti-akratic?
