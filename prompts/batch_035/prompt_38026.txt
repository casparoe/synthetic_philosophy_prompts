I run a public-philosophy workshop on moral uncertainty, and our next session takes up what might be called the scale problem in insect ethics: at any moment there are on the order of 10^18 insects, while the credence most of us should place in the sentience of any given insect is low — a percent or two for a bee, far less for a fruit fly. Some argue that when numbers and probabilities are multiplied out, insects come to dominate the ethics of our treatment of animals; others suspect that the step from "1% likely to be sentient" to "counting 1% as a moral patient" is exactly where the argument cheats. Please write a decision tree that takes a participant from the starting point "I am unsure whether insects are sentient" to a position on what, if anything, we owe them. Every internal node should be a concrete case a thoughtful layperson can answer without knowing which position it leads to: whether an animal's moral status requires actual sentience or only a probability-weighted chance of it; whether there is a credence floor beneath which a small probability stops counting no matter how many individuals it is multiplied by; whether being willing, given the numbers, to spend on insect welfare rather than on chicken or human welfare is an application of one's principle or a reductio of it; and whether farming insects for food and feed changes the answer. Aim for five or six internal nodes and no more than eight leaves. The leaves should include at least the expected-value aggregator, a precautionary view, some threshold or moral-discount view, and a deflationary option; diagnose any unoccupied combination rather than leaving it blank, and flag any pair of positions the tree cannot cleanly separate.
