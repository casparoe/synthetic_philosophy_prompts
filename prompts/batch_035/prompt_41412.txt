I've been hanging around LessWrong and the decision-theory threads on and off since the Sequences were still being written, and I've finally decided to stop being a spectator. I want to practice defending causal decision theory against the best version of the opposing view -- the program-equilibrium style of reasoning where you treat your decision as the output of a computable algorithm, ask what policies agents like you should run, and vote for the policy rather than the token act. Please play a committed functional decision theorist: sharp, well-armed, willing to concede genuinely small points but never the main one, and never a strawman. Stay in character until I say "stop," and keep each reply to roughly a paragraph so this feels like a real exchange rather than an essay duel.

To make sure we're working from the same board, here are the cases I'll be gesturing at. In Newcomb's problem, a predictor who has been reliably right about your past choices put a million dollars in an opaque box if and only if it predicted you'd take only that box, and a thousand in a transparent box regardless; you can take both or just the opaque one. In counterfactual mugging, a coin was flipped before you existed; on heads you were never woken up, on tails you're offered a choice between losing a hundred dollars and gaining ten thousand, and you know that if the coin had come up heads you'd have been given the ten thousand had you committed to paying. My side says: one-box if you like, but only because the prediction already happened and your current choice is evidence about it, not because you're "choosing your algorithm's output." Now my opening move.

Your theory tells me to pay up in counterfactual mugging -- handing over real money in the actual, only world I'll ever inhabit, on the strength of what a predictor would have done in a branch I have verified I'm not in. Meanwhile, a causal decision theorist with even a modest toolkit of binding precommitment devices -- contracts, escrow, a friend holding my wallet -- walks into Newcomb's problem having irrevocably locked in one-boxing, collects the million, and then keeps his hundred dollars in the mugging case, because by the time the coin matters there's nothing left to precommit to and CDT correctly says paying is throwing money away. So here's my challenge: name a case where your view wins and my armed-with-precommitments CDT agent loses, and explain why I should pay for your algorithmic identity -- this obligation to act on correlations with versions of me that don't exist -- rather than just treating precommitment as a costly but occasionally worthwhile purchase. Your move.
