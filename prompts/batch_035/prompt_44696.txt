I sit on the grants committee of a regional charitable foundation that has, for the past four years, funded an intensive early-childhood remediation program: daily one-on-one literacy and numeracy coaching for four- and five-year-olds from low-income households, at a cost per child roughly ten times that of ordinary preschool provision. At next month's meeting we must decide whether to renew the program for another five-year cycle, and the committee is deadlocked.

One faction wants to discontinue it. Their argument has two layers. The first is simple: they cite the familiar finding from twin and adoption studies that cognitive traits of this kind are highly heritable — on the order of 50 to 80 percent by late adolescence — and conclude that the program is trying to move a quantity that is largely fixed at conception, so that the money would do more good redirected to, say, unrestricted cash transfers to the families. The second layer is more subtle, and I confess I have not heard a clear answer to it: they grant that the program may raise outcomes for the treated children, but note that because it improves the environment of every enrolled child in roughly the same way, it makes environments *more uniform* across the group. On the standard logic of quantitative genetics, they say, equalizing environments should increase the share of remaining outcome variation explained by genetic differences; so even a successful program could end up widening the gap between the strongest and weakest children in the cohort, while costing a fortune to do it. The program's defenders, meanwhile, mostly appeal to anecdote and urgency, and one of them has started quoting heritability figures back at the other faction without, as far as I can tell, either side actually understanding what the statistic means.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should (1) state a clear and actionable recommendation — renew, discontinue, or renew under specified conditions — with a concrete time frame and decision criteria the committee could actually apply at the five-year review; (2) engage the strongest argument on the losing side honestly rather than dismissing it; and (3) set out, for an audience of intelligent non-specialists, what a heritability coefficient does and does not imply — in particular about the malleability of outcomes, about what follows for a *uniform* environmental improvement as opposed to a targeted one, and about why the figure by itself settles neither the case for discontinuing the program nor the case for renewing it. Where the heritability literature leaves the committee's question genuinely open, the memo should say so plainly rather than paper over it. Assume the readers have no background in genetics or statistics beyond what an educated layperson would pick up from newspapers.
