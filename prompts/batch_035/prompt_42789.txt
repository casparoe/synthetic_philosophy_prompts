I'd like an analysis of a real episode through the lens of the knowing-how / knowing-that distinction. The episode is the "lost secrets" of the Cremonese violin makers, and the theory is Ryle's contrast between knowledge-how and knowledge-that, together with the intellectualist rejoinder associated with Stanley and Williamson, on which knowing-how is itself a species of propositional knowledge — knowledge of a way of doing something, entertained under a practical mode of presentation.

The salient facts: Antonio Stradivari died in 1737; his workshop's knowledge was passed to sons and apprentices rather than written down; within a couple of generations the Cremonese golden age had faded, and it is now a commonplace that modern makers, working with the same spruce and maple and nearly the same geometry, cannot reproduce the golden-period instruments. At the same time, many of Stradivari's molds, templates, and drawings survive, and in a series of controlled experiments in the 2010s, professional soloists — prevented from identifying instruments by sight or by comparing notes — proved unable to reliably distinguish old Italian violins from fine new ones by sound alone, and often preferred the new ones.

Analyze the episode with the theory running in both directions. First, map it carefully: before assigning anything to either side, decide what "the knowledge that was lost" is even supposed to be — candidates include the varnish recipe, the seasoning and treatment of wood, the graduation of the plates, the whole trained hand, or nothing at all — and say, for the candidate you settle on, whether the Rylean or the intellectualist account better captures it. The intellectualist reading predicts that the loss is a loss of recoverable propositions: if Stradivari knew that some way w makes a great violin, then in principle a sufficiently good document or measurement program could restore the knowledge, and the blind-test results might even be taken to suggest there was never any distinctive knowledge there to lose. The Rylean reading predicts that what was lost was a set of trained capacities no document could carry, so that the failure of transmission was a failure of apprenticeship — of bodies and benches rather than of record-keeping — and thus not the kind of thing the blind tests could ever detect, since those tests probe players' discrimination between finished instruments, not anyone's ability to make one.

Then say where each account strains, and whether the episode reveals something neither theory anticipated. One possibility worth taking seriously: that talk of the secret of Stradivari tracks the value a market places on provenance rather than any knowledge of making, in which case what died in Cremona was not a skill at all, and both theories have been asked to explain a non-event. Be explicit about what the experimental evidence can and cannot show, distinguish the question of whether old instruments sound different from the question of whether anyone knew how to make them, and do not force either theory to speak where it is silent. Please keep your response to 600 words or fewer.
