I am a PhD student in bioethics, writing a dissertation on procreative beneficence and liberal eugenics, and I sit as the ethics representative on the reproductive-genetics consultation panel of the teaching hospital attached to my program. The panel meets monthly for two hours and reviews preimplantation cases in which a fertility clinic's patients have asked for embryo selection on non-medical or borderline-medical grounds — and, more often lately, cases involving polygenic risk scores that the clinic's commercial partners have begun offering. Panel members are clinical geneticists, a genetic counselor, a lab director, and me; none of them have read any philosophy, and our charter requires that every recommendation be one a hospital lawyer can defend in a paragraph.

What I cannot do is hand them Savulescu's principle of procreative beneficence — the claim that parents have a moral reason, perhaps an obligation, to select, of the embryos available, the one expected to have the best life — and expect anything usable. Nor can I hand them the standard objections (the non-identity problem, the argument from parental love and unconditional acceptance, disability-rights worries about expressing a demeaning view of existing people with impairments) and expect a recommendation. What the panel needs is a decision procedure it can actually run in the twenty to thirty minutes a case gets: an ordered list of questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a yes or a no, and where it must stop and hand the question back to the panel as a question of values rather than facts — because I suspect a large fraction of our cases are of that kind, and I want the procedure to say so honestly rather than laundering our value judgments through pseudo-clinical steps.

Please write that procedure. In translating the principle into something usable, you will have to decide which philosophical distinctions survive the simplification and which can be dropped: I would particularly like you to be explicit about how you handle the non-identity problem (the embryo selected has no complaint against being selected), the difference between selection against disease and selection for advantage, the move from a moral reason to a permission versus an obligation, and the point at which a couple's request is refused not because the resulting child would be harmed but because of what complying would express. Mark every step at which judgment cannot be eliminated from the procedure, and be honest about the failure modes — including the one where the procedure, followed conscientiously, drifts into being an engine of the very eugenic logic my dissertation is ambivalent about.

Then run the procedure on three cases from our current docket. First: a couple with no infertility issues requesting preimplantation genetic testing purely to avoid an adult-onset, untreatable, penetrant neurodegenerative disorder that runs in one partner's family. Second: a couple requesting polygenic screening for schizophrenia risk, where the score explains only a few percent of variance, the absolute risk reduction from selecting the best-scoring embryo is small, and the clinic's marketing materials imply far more. Third: a Deaf couple, both of whom sign and identify with Deaf culture, requesting that embryos be tested for the connexin-related deafness they both carry and asking that a deaf embryo be prioritized for transfer, on the grounds that the child will share their language and world. I will tell you now that on the third case I expect the procedure to give an answer I will not like, and I want you to report faithfully what it gives rather than adjusting it to spare me.

Please close with a single line beginning "Greatest misfire:" naming, in one short phrase, the case type where this procedure is most likely to go wrong.
