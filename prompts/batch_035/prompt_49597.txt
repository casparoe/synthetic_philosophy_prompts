I'm preparing a seminar write-up of where I stand on moral foundations theory, and before I present it I want a consistency audit of my own position. Here is the position: (1) The descriptive core of the theory is right: moral judgment is generated by a small set of evolved foundations — care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation — and political orientation tracks how many of them a person's judgments actually engage: liberals lean heavily on care and fairness, conservatives draw on the full set. (2) No descriptive fact of this kind vindicates anyone morally: that conservatives run more foundations does not show their morality is more complete, and that liberals run fewer does not show theirs is more purified; foundations are causes of judgment, not evidence about its object. (3) I am a moral realist and roughly a consequentialist: there are stance-independent moral facts, and the central currency of better and worse outcomes is harm and well-being. (4) Still, I cannot shake the conviction that someone with no working sense of loyalty or gratitude — who feels nothing at betraying a confidence to secure a marginal welfare gain — is missing something morally, not merely psychologically, and I would resist any theory on which such a person has no defect. I think of the additional foundations as candidate moral sense-organs: the open question is whether loyalty and sanctity detect moral facts the way harm-detection detects suffering, or whether they generate moral illusions. (5) The evolutionary and cultural etiology of the foundations has no debunking force: intuitions can be adaptations and still reliable, just as vision is. (6) I am aware that the foundation list was in part reverse-engineered from observed left–right differences, and I accept that as legitimate methodology.

Which of these commitments pull against each other, which pairs only appear to, and what is the cheapest repair? Answer in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. Points one through three should present the genuine tensions, ranked from most to least severe, each with the single least costly revision. Point four should identify the pair that most looks like a tension but dissolves on inspection, and explain why it dissolves. Point five should state which single consideration would most likely move you to a different verdict about the most severe tension, or, failing that, where your own reasoning in this audit is most likely to go wrong.
