Some time ago I came across a puzzle called the absent-minded driver problem, and I have been going around in circles with it ever since. Let me set it up as I understand it, so that we are on the same page even if my details differ from the canonical version.

A driver is heading home late at night. His route has two identical intersections in a row, and at each one he can either exit or continue straight. Exiting at the first intersection gets him home by a long, slow route; exiting at the second gets him home quickly; continuing straight past both lands him at a motel for the night, which is the worst outcome. Say the payoffs are 1 for the first exit, 3 for the second, and 0 for the motel. The twist is that the driver is absent-minded in a strong sense: once he is at an intersection, he has no memory of whether he has already passed one, and the two intersections look identical, so he cannot tell which one he is at. He knows the whole setup perfectly; he simply cannot distinguish the two choice situations from the inside.

If he decides before setting out, the obvious best plan is to continue at the first intersection and exit at the second, which is worth 3. But once he is actually at an intersection, that plan seems unavailable to him: he cannot tell which intersection he is at, so he cannot carry out one instruction in one place and a different instruction in the other. If instead he reasons from where he stands — taking himself to be equally likely to be at either intersection, and assuming that whatever he does here he will also do at the other one if he reaches it — then exiting looks better than continuing. Yet a driver who follows that in-the-moment reasoning at each intersection ends up exiting at the first one and limping home with 1. So the reasoning that is compelling in the moment, applied at both moments, does worse than a plan that the same reasoning rejects at each moment. That is what I cannot get straight.

What I would like is an explanation of where exactly the trick is. In particular:

1. What is it about not being able to distinguish the two choice situations that makes this different from an ordinary sequential decision, where deciding in advance and deciding as you go give the same answer? Is the difference just imperfect information, or is it specifically the combination of imperfect information with the fact that the same person faces the same-looking choice twice?

2. In what sense is "continue at the first, exit at the second" even a strategy for this driver? It looks like an instruction he cannot execute, since executing it would require exactly the knowledge he lacks. Is it legitimate to call it his best plan, or is it a fiction that happens to be useful for comparing outcomes?

3. Should the driver at the intersection think of his earlier and later selves as one agent making a single decision, or as something more like several participants whose interests happen to coincide? The puzzle feels to me as though it sits between ordinary decision theory and something game-like, and I cannot tell which side of that line it falls on — or whether that is a substantive question at all rather than a matter of taste.

I have no background in decision theory or game theory, so please explain any technical vocabulary as you go, and keep any mathematics down to arithmetic I could check by hand.
