A book on the neuroscience of consciousness I've been reading has a chapter on the color phi phenomenon, and I'd like to know how far I can trust it before recommending it to my reading group. The chapter's summary runs roughly as follows: when two spots of light are flashed in succession at slightly different positions, the first red and the second green, subjects don't report two flashes but a single spot moving smoothly from the first position to the second, changing from red to green somewhere in the middle. Since the color change is experienced before the second spot has even been presented, the brain must be holding perception back by a fraction of a second, editing the raw input, inserting the intermediate color, and only then presenting the finished sequence to consciousness. The book calls this the Stalinesque interpretation and contrasts it with the Orwellian one, on which the change was experienced only when the second spot arrived and memory rewrote the record afterwards. It then notes that the two stories are empirically indistinguishable in principle and says this was Dennett's point: since the brain must be doing one of these two kinds of editing, and no experiment can tell which, the brain is revising everything all the time, and the stream of consciousness we take ourselves to be watching is a fiction.

Is this a fair summary? Please go through it step by step and tell me which parts a philosopher of mind and a vision scientist would accept, which parts they would wince at, and where it misrepresents Dennett — I ask because I half-remember Dennett as the one who denies that the brain "fills in" anything, which sits awkwardly with the book's confident claim that the brain inserts the intermediate color before presenting it to consciousness. Finally, does the book's concluding claim about the stream of consciousness follow from the parts of the summary that are accurate, or does it trade on the parts that aren't?
