I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about when social sanction is OK, and I've got a bunch of gut verdicts that I can't make line up. Here they are, and I want to know if there's a principle behind them or whether some of them just have to go.

- A platform permanently bans a guy who's spent two years running a harrassment campaign against a woman in his replies: fine, obviously.
- The same platform bans someone over a tweet from nine years ago that was dumb but not targeted at anyone: not fine, feels like a violation.
- A firm fires an employee after video of him screaming racist abuse at a cashier goes viral: fine.
- The same firm fires someone when it comes out he donated to a legal political campaign: not fine.
- Boycotting a bakery because the owner gives money to a cause I find hateful: fine.
- Organizing to get a bookstore to cancel a reading by an author whose book argues something repugnant but legal: not fine, that one really bothers me.
- Organizing to get a venue to refuse to host an actual neo-Nazi group's rally: fine.
- A friend group quietly stops inviting someone after he was creepy to a woman at a party: fine.
- The same friend group publicly denouncing him and tagging his employer: not fine.
- Mass-reporting an account for a technical terms-of-service violation you'd normally ignore, purely to get a rival banned: not fine, kind of obviosuly.

And I keep going back and forth on a couple of others: a comedian whose old set has a joke people find genuinely cruel, so venues start dropping him after fans email them; and doxxing -- I think publishing someone's home address is never OK, but I thought it was fine when journalists published the identities of people who marched at a rally, and I cant see what separates those two.

My tentative principle was something like: sanction is OK when it's proportional to what the person did and comes from people who actually have some relationship to the harm. But that doesnt obviously give me the bookstore-versus-Nazi-rally pair, and "proportional" is doing a ton of unexplained work. Is there a principle, or a couple of candidates, that saves most of these? Which of my verdicts would you overturn, and why? And please tell me what your principle says about a case I haven't listed: an open-source maintainer gets pushed out of a project he founded because other contributors threaten to leave over things he wrote on his personal blog.

Please write your answer in British English.
