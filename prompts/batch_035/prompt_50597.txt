I'm a PhD student in an interpretability group, working mainly on probing methods, and a philosopher in my cohort has an argument she claims shows that no interpretability result to date establishes that a network represents anything. It goes as follows. (1) To say a network has a representation — a "dog feature," a "board-state feature" — is to say there is a fact of the matter about what its internal states are about, independent of what we researchers would find it convenient to label; otherwise realism about interpretability is false and the field is stamp-collecting with our own labels. (2) What fixes what a state is about must be facts about the network itself — weights, activations, training history — since two physically identical networks cannot represent different things. (3) At the level of a layer, those facts are exhausted by what is decodable from its activations: if a probe can extract property F, the layer carries the information that F, and if no probe can, there is nothing there to be about F. (4) But probes extract nearly everything. From a vision model's activations one can train probes that decode not just "dog" but which training dataset an image came from, or whether it has been JPEG-compressed once too often — properties that helped predict labels in training and were carried along. Nobody wants to say the model has a "JPEG-artifact feature" in the sense it has a "dog feature," so interpretability people say the difference is that the dog feature is used by the rest of the network. (5) "Used" can only mean: makes a difference to downstream computation. But the dataset-source information also makes a difference to downstream computation — it improves prediction, and prediction is all the downstream computation is for. So by the only criterion that stays within the physical facts, the artifact feature and the dog feature are on par. (6) Therefore either the model represents dataset sources and compression artifacts in exactly the sense it represents dogs, in which case "representation" is so cheap that every layer represents everything decodable and the concept does no explanatory work, or nothing is represented in the sense interpretability wants, and realism about it is false. She says both horns are bad for me. For what it's worth, I'd like to keep (1), and I suspect the trouble lies somewhere in (4) through (6) — the phrase "in the same sense" seems to be doing a lot of work — but I can't say exactly what. Which premise should I give up, and what does giving it up cost?
