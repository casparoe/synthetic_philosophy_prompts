I've been reading about how my country's regulators weigh costs and benefits, and I gather that a death prevented in 2100 counts, in their arithmetic, for only a small fraction of a death prevented today. Part of that is supposed to cover uncertainty and the fact that people will probably be richer then, but apparently there is also a component that discounts purely for lateness: the very same benefit is worth less simply because it arrives later. That sits oddly next to two other things the same governments do. Their climate rhetoric says future generations have exactly as strong a claim on us as people alive now, and their consumer-finance campaigns treat my own preference for enjoyment today over enjoyment in thirty years as a bias to be trained out of me. So what does the practice of applying a positive discount rate in official cost-benefit analysis actually presuppose? Give me exactly five assumptions it rests on — about time, value, persons, or what consistency requires — numbered, each in a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion. Where the practice could survive dropping an assumption, or where two of the assumptions pull against each other, say so within that item.
