For about eight years I wrote fundraising letters for a mid-sized disability charity, and I was good at it — the sort of good that involves choosing one particular child's photograph, naming her, and letting the reader's own imagination do the rest. I left the job last spring and I still haven't settled in my own mind whether what I was doing was persuasion or manipulation, and at our monthly pub discussion group (five of us: a car salesman, a nurse, a vicar, a retired teacher and me) the arguments go round and round because none of us can even agree on where the line sits. One of our lot insists any appeal that works on feelings rather than reasoning is manipulation, full stop. Another says only trickery counts — lying, hiding things, rigged choices — and everything else is fair game. A third says it's all decided afterwards: if you'd still endorse the choice you made once you're calm, nobody manipulated you, whatever they did. The vicar cares mostly about whether the sender profits; the nurse thinks that's irrelevant, since a doctor steering a patient with hopeful framing ("nine in ten pull through") may be doing exactly the same thing a manipulator does, for the best of reasons.

I want to turn this into a sorting quiz we can hand out at the next meeting, so we find out what each of us actually holds rather than what we shout across the table. Please write ten or so questions, each a short concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a key at the end mapping answer patterns onto positions — you'll have to supply the proper names for the positions, since I don't know them, and if some logical corner of the debate is standing empty, say so and tell me whether that's because the view is incoherent, ugly, or just unexplored. The cases must include: my fundraising letter; a friend guilt-tripping you into attending a party; flattery from a salesperson who genuinely wants you happy with the purchase; the default that automatically enrols employees in a pension scheme; the doctor's framing; and the sweets deliberately placed at a supermarket till. I also have a pet test of my own I'd like worked in as one question — my hunch is that real manipulation is whatever would stop working if the technique were spelled out to you in advance — but please stress-test it, because I've noticed the background music in shops still puts me in a buying mood even though I know exactly what it's for, and I don't know what to conclude from that.

Two constraints on the questions: nobody should be able to tell, from the wording of a case, which "position" it's nudging them towards, and no question may simply ask "was that manipulative, yes or no" — the options need to be about what the case shows or hinges on. At the end, please flag honestly which of the positions your quiz cannot separate with these particular cases, rather than pretending the key is cleaner than it is; my fear is that the profit-matters view and the afterwards-endorsement view end up indistinguishable no matter what we ask, and I'd rather know that going in.
