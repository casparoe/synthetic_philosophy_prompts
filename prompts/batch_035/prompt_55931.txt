So I'm a science journalist, mostly covering psych and consciousness research, and I'm working on a piece about how much we can trust people's reports about their own minds — confabulation stuff, split-brain patient explanations, the whole "you don't know why you picked that jam" literature. While reading around for it I ran into Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument, the one where your hand is warming up gradually and you're asked at each moment whether you feel cold. The way I understand it is this: because knowledge requires a margin for error, and the transition from cold to not-cold is gradual, there will be a moment where you're cold but can't know you're cold — and he generalizes that to say no (non-trivial) condition is ever luminous.

Which, if I'm reading it right, seems like kind of a bombshell for my article? If we literally never know our own mental states, then every pain scale, every "I chose this because...", every introspective report in every study I've ever covered is data that the subject cannot in principle know. That would make it worse than confabulation — confabulation at least means we're sometimes right by accident, this would mean the first-person data is unknowable full stop.

So my questions: (1) given that Williamson proved introspective knowledge is impossible, should reviewers at psych journals basically stop accepting self-report measures, or is there some standard correction researchers apply for it? (2) am I right that the same argument also refutes the KK principle — that if you can't even know whether you're cold, you can never know that you know things in general? Because that second part sounds wierdly strong, like it would undermine knowledge claims everywhere, not just in psychology. I want to get this right before the piece goes to my editor, and the philosophy literature here is pretty dense, so any help untangling what the argument actually establishes would be great.
