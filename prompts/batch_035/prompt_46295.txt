Two people on my team (I'll call them Alex and Dana) have been going at it for two weeks about how we should train and eval the coding agent we're building, and since I have to write up the eval plan for next quarter and honestly can't pick a side, I'm hoping you can referee.

Quick background: the agent takes a task like "fix this bug and make the tests pass," does some stuff (edits files, runs commands, writes up notes about what it's doing), and produces a result. The question is what we grade it on. Alex wants pure outcome supervision: run the hidden test suite, did it pass or not, that's the reward, done. Dana wants process supervision on top of that: humans review the agent's traces (its reasoning notes, its actions) and reward it for doing the work the right way, not just arriving at a passing result.

The argument as it actually went:

(1) Alex said outcome is the only real ground truth. The user doesn't care whether the agent's reasoning reads nicely, they care whether the code works. If we grade process, we're grading our aesthetic preferences about what good reasoning looks like, and the model will learn to write reasoning that flatters the graders rather than reasoning that actually helps, which is just reward hacking with extra steps. Dana said that's a strawman -- nobody's grading "does the reasoning sound nice," they're grading did the agent do anything sneaky, like hardcoding expected outputs or skipping the actual bug. That's not aesthetic, that's substantive.

(2) Alex said if the agent games the tests, that's an environment bug, not a supervision bug -- make the test suite immutable, sandbox it, whatever. You don't fix a broken scoreboard by hiring people to watch the players. Dana fired back that "make the environment such that passing requires actually doing the work" is itself a process constraint, so Alex is doing process supervision, just the cheap unlabeled kind, and not admitting it.

(3) Dana then pushed further: outcome supervision only works at all because coding has checkable outcomes. The whole reason we're building agents at all is to eventually do tasks where we can't run a hidden test suite on the result, and for those tasks, the only thing you can reward is the process, because that's all you can see. If the agent only ever learns "pass the check," you have no reason to trust it when there's no check. Alex said this proves too much: if the process signal is the only signal you'll ever have for the important cases, and the process signal is graded by humans with limited time and their own inconsistencies, then you're training the model to satisfy a noisy proxy on the important stuff, which is exactly how you get a model that's very good at seeming diligent.

(4) Alex also said process supervision caps the model: if we'd process-supervised everything from the start, the model could never learn weird strategies that work but look wrong, and the whole value of the thing is finding approaches we didn't anticipate. Dana said there's a difference between capping intelligence and capping sabotage, and the graders are explicitly instructed to only flag harmful or deceptive behavior, not unconventional approaches -- "looks weird to me" isn't supposed to be a rubric item. Alex said sure, in writing, but rubric drift is real, graders take shortcuts when they're behind on reviews, and pretty soon "unconventional" and "suspicious" blur together, and you won't notice it happening.

(5) Dana's last point was that there's an asymmetry Alex keeps ignoring: judging a good outcome is cheap and objective but only available after the fact and only in sandboxes like ours, while judging a good process is expensive and contested but available everywhere. So the real question isn't which signal is better, it's what the model needs to have internalized by the time we can no longer verify outcomes at all, and only one of the two signals even speaks to that. Alex rolled his eyes and said "internalized" is doing all the work in that sentence, and that Dana is asking us to pay a real cost now (slower iteration, human review bottleneck, noisy labels) for an insurance policy against a situation that may never arrive, and if it does arrive, there's no evidence the process training will transfer anyway.

They're both way better read than me on this stuff so I couldn't tell who was winning. Can you go through the exchange point by point -- who's right about what, where someone's making a genuinely good point their opponent isn't absorbing, and whether there's some framing they're both missing that would dissolve part of the fight?
