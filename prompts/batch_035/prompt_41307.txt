I'm a graduate student in international relations, and I've become puzzled by a pattern in how my field, and the broader foreign-policy establishment, use history as an instrument. On one hand, there is now a whole apparatus of "applied history": think tanks run programs that convene historians to advise on current crises, and the most successful of these exercises was surely the one built around the Thucydides trap. Graham Allison and his collaborators at Harvard's Belfer Center popularized the phrase in the early 2010s, and to back it up they assembled a case file of sixteen historical episodes in which a rising power threatened to displace a ruling one, of which twelve ended in war. The slogan rests on a line from Thucydides which, in Richard Crawley's translation, reads: "The growth of the power of Athens, and the alarm which this inspired in Lacedaemon, made war inevitable." The case count is then used, in books like Allison's Destined for War (2017) and in countless op-eds since, as a kind of base rate for the U.S.–China relationship: a 12-out-of-16 record is cited as a reason to expect, and to work against, war between a ruling and a rising great power. Alongside this, policy schools teach a more modest craft, usually traced to a pair of scholars who studied how administrations actually reason: put the candidate analogy side by side with the present situation, list the similarities and the differences, and ask which of the similarities actually mattered to the outcome in the historical case.

Here is what puzzles me. This counting practice coexists, in the same newspapers and the same think tanks, with a sharply different attitude toward other historical analogies. Invoking Munich — the claim that some adversary is another Hitler and that concession is another appeasement — is now treated by editors and foreign-policy professionals as an intellectual tic, a sign that the writer has stopped thinking. The lesson of 1938 is said to have been "learned" so often, and so badly, that the analogy is presumptively misleading; the Vietnam-era debates were in large part a war over which template, Munich or Vietnam, was the right one, and the exhaustion of that war is what produced the current norm. Yet the same people who dismiss Munich-style reasoning on the ground that surface similarities (a dictator, a territorial demand) are doing work that only structural similarities should do will, in the next column, aggregate sixteen "power transitions" spanning five centuries into a single frequency, as though Portugal facing Spain in the late 1400s and Britain facing Germany before 1914 were instances of one kind of thing.

What I'd like you to do is reconstruct the implicit theory, or theories, of history that this practice presupposes. Does treating the case file as a base rate presuppose that history is governed by regularities stable enough to support frequencies — and if so, how does the practice answer the obvious objection that the sixteen cases were admitted into the class by the very similarity judgment the count is supposed to ground? Is the practice actually committed to one view, or is it compatible with several: for instance, with the view that historical outcomes are unique, under which the count would have to be doing something other than evidential work — a reminder, a rhetorical condensation, a way of triggering attention? Is the hostility to Munich analogies consistent with the enthusiasm for case-counting, or do the two rest on conflicting views about which resemblances carry historical weight, and if they conflict, which part of the practice is philosophically doing the heavy lifting? Finally, tell me what would have to change if the implicit view were replaced by a frankly probabilistic one: would the case file have to be abandoned, or merely rebuilt with explicit and independently motivated criteria of inclusion, and would an analogy then function as an argument at all, or only as a checklist of ways things have gone wrong before? Along the way, I'd like to know where the practice is genuinely underdetermined — where several quite different philosophies of history would license exactly the behavior we observe, so that the behavior itself settles less than it seems to.
