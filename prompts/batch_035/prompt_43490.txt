The exam question was: "Explain Leibniz's claim that this is the best of all possible worlds. How does he answer the objection that the amount of evil in the world makes the claim implausible, and does his answer leave God any real choice in creating?"

Here's the student's answer, typed out as written:

"Leibniz argues that God, before creating, surveys all the possible worlds and selects the one with the greatest overall goodness. The claim that ours is the best possible world is not meant to say that every event in it is good, only that the total package beats every alternative. His central response to the problem of evil is that evil is a privation, a lack of good rather than a positive thing, and so evil strictly speaking doesn't exist. Since evil isn't real, there is literally nothing for God to be responsible for, and the objection dissolves. Privation is Leibniz's own original contribution to the debate. As for God's choice, since God necessarily wills the best, God could not have created any other world, in the same way that 2+2 couldn't equal 5. This means God's choice of world is metaphysically necessary, which is fine for Leibniz because necessity and goodness coincide in God, so calling the choice free or unfree makes no difference to his argument. The upshot is a world where whatever happens is for the best, and God could not have done otherwise."
