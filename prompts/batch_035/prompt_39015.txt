Analyze the July Crisis of 1914 as a test case for the common prior assumption.

The setup is familiar enough not to need much narration: in the summer of 1914, the great powers stumbled into a general European war that several of their own leaders said they did not want, largely on the strength of mutually inconsistent beliefs — about each other's intentions, about whether mobilization could be halted short of war, about whether Britain would enter, and about how quickly any conflict would end. By late July, German and Russian leaderships, British and Austrian leaderships, and so on down the line, held sharply divergent posteriors on questions where they had overlapping information, roughly shared strategic understanding, and every incentive to model each other accurately.

Analyze this episode through the lens of the common prior assumption and its corollaries: Aumann's agreement theorem, Harsanyi's treatment of divergent beliefs as differences in private information over a common prior, and the no-trade-like intuition that rational actors with common priors should not persistently bet against each other in ways that both sides expect to profit from.

I want a disciplined mapping, not a hand-wave. Identify who counts as a "player" for these purposes (governments? individual decision-makers with internal disagreements, like a cabinet split?), what the salient beliefs were and where the priors plausibly did and did not diverge, and which apparent disagreements can be re-described as differences in information or payoffs rather than different priors. Then say where the theory is doing real work: for instance, can the theory's defenders read the crisis as a failure of common knowledge rather than of common priors — hidden actions like Russia's early mobilization preparations and Germany's blank check to Austria acting as private information that no amount of rational dialogue could have properly updated on? Does the escalating spiral of mobilization deadlines look like posterior divergence or like a coordination problem that is common-prior-compatible all the way down?

Then run the analysis in the other direction. What does the episode show about the common prior assumption itself — is it an idealization that still organizes the case, an empirically false assumption that only obscures what happened (national self-images, lessons wrongly drawn from 1870, and culturally entrenched narratives arguably functioning as genuinely different priors that no evidence would have moved), or a concept that was never meant to apply to agents that are not a single mind? Be honest about where you are forcing the theory to speak and where it is simply silent.

Ground the analysis in what is uncontroversially known about the crisis; do not rest any claim on contested fine-grained details, and if a detail is genuinely disputed among historians, flag it as such rather than building on it.
