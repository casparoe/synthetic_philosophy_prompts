I do catastrophe-risk modeling for a mid-sized reinsurer, and our house practice — instituted by our chief actuary after a bad experience with overconfident single-number estimates — is to never average experts when they disagree. If our three internal models put a portfolio's annual loss probability at 0.3, 0.45, and 0.7, we carry the whole interval [0.3, 0.7] forward, on the theory that the spread itself is information about how contested the risk is, and that collapsing it to a single number would launder disagreement into false precision. I've become fairly committed to this way of doing things, so what follows is uncomfortable for me.

Two things I've noticed. First, and modestly: conditioning sometimes widens the interval. That alone isn't shocking — some evidence genuinely polarizes experts — but I've caught the widening happening even when the new information is, by construction, unrelated to the quantity of interest. Second, and this is the case that keeps me up at night: a colleague constructed the following. Take a risk whose probability our models place somewhere in [0.4, 0.6]. Take a fair coin, independent of everything, and consider the event that the risk occurs and the coin lands heads, or the risk fails to occur and the coin lands tails. Before the flip, every one of our models — indeed any probability assignment anywhere in the range — puts that event at exactly one half, so our interval collapses to the single point 1/2, the most confident we are ever allowed to be. Then we observe the coin. Conditional on heads, the event just becomes the underlying risk itself, and our interval springs back open to [0.4, 0.6]. Learning the outcome of a fair coin — information that has nothing to do with the underlying exposure and could hardly be more obviously beside the point — takes us from an exact point probability to maximum spread. I've checked the arithmetic; it isn't a mistake. What I can't figure out is whether this is a known phenomenon, a known artifact of representing uncertainty as a range, or something my colleague has discovered.

So: does this configuration have a name in the probability or decision-theory literature? I dimly know there's a literature on ambiguity and on preferences for known chances over unknown ones, but what I've described seems different — it's not about what anyone prefers to bet on, it's about how the spread of probabilities behaves under conditioning, and in particular about the paradoxical-seeming fact that "irrelevant" learning can destroy an exact point probability. I'd like to know what the nearest established concepts are and exactly how what I've described differs from each. And since I suspect part of the answer is a genuine fight over whether this behavior is a defect of range-based representation or a defensible feature of it, I want you to be explicit about which parts of your answer are settled among people who work on this and which are contested — please don't hand me a contested take dressed up as the consensus, and if you're not confident that a named concept exists for this at all, say so rather than attaching a confident label to something adjacent. If nothing established fits exactly, coin a term, define it precisely, and give me the boundary cases — especially around where the "irrelevance" of the coin breaks down, since a skeptic in my office insists the coin becomes relevant the moment the event is defined in terms of it, and I want to know whether that objection is decisive or trades on a confusion between relevance to the event and relevance to our uncertainty about it.
