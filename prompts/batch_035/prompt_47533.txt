I sit on our city council's Heritage and Commemoration Commission, which rules on interpretive materials on public land, on commemorations the council co-funds, and on the naming of streets and public spaces. The commission has a track record of rulings in which a community's memory of its past and the documentary record pull in different directions, and next month we vote on a new one. I want a recommendation consistent with our own precedents, and I want it to be honest about where those precedents leave the question open.

The precedents. First, the mill fire: local tradition has it that the mill was set alight by soldiers of the garrison during the troubles of the 1740s; the surviving record — a fire assessor's report and two newspaper accounts — shows an accidental fire in the drying house. We required the new interpretive plaque at the site to state the documented cause, and declined even to mention the tradition. Second, the founding festival: the town archive puts the founding three years earlier than the date the festival has kept for over a century; we noted the record but left the festival to keep its traditional date, on the ground that the calendar of commemoration is the community's to keep. Third, the regiment's stand: veterans' memory has the local regiment holding its position for a full day; the unit diaries and after-action reports show four hours. We required the interpretive panel at the war memorial to give the documented timeline, but added a sentence recording the tradition and its place in the town's memory of itself. Fourth, the street name: a street is named for a doctor whom tradition credits with founding the town's first hospital; the biographical record shows that a colleague did most of that work. We kept the name and issued a short statement giving the fuller record.

The new case. A neighbourhood association of descendants of the old weavers' row proposes a memorial on public land at the site of the clearance of the 1840s. All sides agree the clearance happened. What the record and the memory disagree about is the notice: the Improvement Commissioners' minute books — kept by the commissioners' own clerk — record two weeks' notice, removal costs paid, and a small allowance; the oral tradition collected from descendants in the 1970s is unanimous that the families were given a night's notice and put out with what they could carry. The professional historians regard the minute books as sound, though one respected local historian has argued that a record kept by the men who ordered the clearance should not settle the matter, and no independent corroboration of the notice period survives either way. The proposed inscription states the night's notice as plain fact, and it is that detail which carries the moral weight of the memorial: on the tradition the clearance was an act of cruelty, on the record it was harsh but ordinary administration. Our role is to approve or refuse the inscription; we would not be authoring it ourselves.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Please give: the closest analog among our precedents, and why it fails to settle the matter; the rules that fit our track record, each stated so that it covers all four precedents, together with what each predicts for this inscription — I have a suspicion that one rule turns on whether the evidence leaves serious doubt, another on whether the commission is endorsing an assertion of fact as against letting a community keep its own calendar, and a third on whether the tradition accuses or merely inflates, but please test my suspicions rather than adopt them; a recommendation, with your reasons; and, if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the ruling, a statement of the principle we would be adopting with each available choice, together with the further case — an annual re-enactment, perhaps, or a prideful tradition resting on contested evidence — that would best tell those principles apart.

Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested. On the case itself, say plainly where our precedents speak and where they are silent. On the background contrast between collective memory and history that you will presumably want to draw on, mark which claims are broadly settled among people who know the field and which are live debates, and do not present a contested view — about, say, whether collective memory is a defective copy of the past or a different thing with its own functions — as if it were the consensus. Please answer in British English.
