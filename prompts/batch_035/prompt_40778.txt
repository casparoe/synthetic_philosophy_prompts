I've been around the rationalist blogosphere long enough to have seen Aumann's agreement theorem invoked hundreds of times, usually with the familiar caveat that its practical force is deflated by the common prior assumption: the theorem only bites if the disagreeing parties started with the same priors, which actual humans almost never did. A small funder I advise can support two postdocs in formal epistemology for three years, working strictly on the common prior assumption itself, and the practical interest is dispute resolution — when, if ever, should we expect reasonable people who have shared all their evidence to converge, and when is residual disagreement licensed? Which open questions in this area should the postdocs prioritize, and in what order? Some candidates I have in mind: what exactly justifies Harsanyi's move of interpreting differing priors as differences in information rather than as irreconcilable starting points; whether common priors even make sense for self-locating possibilities, where Sleeping Beauty-style cases suggest the parties may disagree about which proposition is on the table rather than about its probability; how logical uncertainty and the problem of old evidence interact with the fact that agents can never literally share everything they know; and how fragile the associated no-trade results are to small departures from common priors. But I care less about my list than about your reasoning: for each item you'd prioritize, say why it's upstream of the others, whether it's tractable with formal tools or quietly requires empirical psychology, and what developments over the next couple of years would move it up or down your list. Finally, please say which popular uses of the agreement theorem your prioritization would leave untouched — I want to know which of those uses are overrated and don't follow from the math at all.
