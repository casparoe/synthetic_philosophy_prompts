I'd like a probability estimate on a question in the epistemology of mathematics, with the reasoning behind the number laid out rather than left implicit.

The proposition: that platonism about arithmetic can survive Benacerraf's epistemic challenge — that is, that one can hold on to a face-value semantics on which arithmetical truths are made true by mind-independent abstract objects, and still give a non-skeptical, non-mysterious account of how our arithmetical beliefs reliably track those objects. My own credence that this combination is ultimately defensible is about 0.35. My supervisor, who works on neo-Fregean approaches and thinks the challenge rests on an optional commitment to a causal theory of knowledge, puts it at 0.65. Please give your own number, and tell me which of us you think is making the bigger error, and about what.

Decompose the credence rather than just announcing it. At minimum, assign rough probabilities with a line of justification each for the following sub-questions, plus any further terms you think a proper decomposition needs:

1. That Benacerraf's argument actually requires a causal (or otherwise causal-theoretic) condition on knowledge, rather than some weaker requirement — say, a counterfactual tracking condition — that abstract objects could in principle satisfy. One wrinkle I want your read on: the natural formulation of tracking for causally inert objects is something like "had the natural numbers been different, our beliefs would have been different," which looks vacuously true if counterpossibles are treated classically. Could a non-vacuous semantics for counterpossibles rehabilitate the tracking story, or would that merely relabel the mystery? Assign this a probability and say why.

2. That something like Gödelian mathematical intuition — whatever its precise epistemic status — can confer genuine warrant without falling to the selection problem: the worry that causal isolation of the objects leaves it unexplained why our belief-forming practices latch onto true arithmetic rather than some coherent rival.

3. That stipulation or implicit definition, in the spirit of the neo-Fregean program, can generate substantive knowledge — that fixing the use of an expression in an abstraction principle confers reference and warrant, rather than merely recording a convention.

4. That the semantic horn of the dilemma was never as secure as the standard framing assumes — i.e., the probability that face-value semantics for arithmetic is simply wrong, in which case the platonist loses on the terms of the dilemma while the epistemic challenge dissolves rather than being met. Note that this term pulls in an odd direction relative to the others, and handle the combination accordingly.

Once you have your terms, combine them into an overall number and tell me two things: which term you are least confident in, and what argument or consideration would move that term the most.

Two constraints on how I want this done. First, distinguish what is broadly settled among philosophers of mathematics from what is genuinely contested — I suspect several of the framing assumptions embedded in my four sub-questions are themselves disputed (for instance, what exactly Benacerraf's argument needs, or whether the two horns really conflict), and I don't want a contested reading presented as the consensus view. Second, don't retreat to a mushy midpoint between my 0.35 and my supervisor's 0.65; commit to your own number, even if it lands between ours, and make clear where you think the disagreement between us actually lives.
