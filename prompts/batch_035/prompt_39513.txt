In my animal ethics seminar we've been discussing Peter Singer's claim that speciesism is structurally analogous to racism and sexism. The thought, as our instructor presented it, is that just as racists give greater weight to the interests of members of their own race and sexists to those of their own sex, speciesists give greater weight to the interests of members of their own species, and that in each case the boundary doing the work — skin color, sex, species membership — is morally arbitrary: what matters morally is capacities like the capacity to suffer or to have interests, not group membership as such. A classmate of mine who disagrees keeps saying the analogy is doing all the work in Singer's argument and that once you look closely it falls apart: races and sexes differ only trivially in morally relevant capacities, so the wrongness of racism has nothing to do with capacities at all, whereas species boundaries track enormous differences in capacities, so the analogy illicitly smuggles in the conclusion that the differences between humans and animals don't matter.

I'd like a proper audit of this analogy and the work it's being asked to do. Please lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what in the racist's case maps onto what in the speciesist's case — the favored group, the boundary trait, the morally relevant trait that's being ignored, the beneficiaries of the prejudice. Then identify where the mapping breaks down. Some candidate disanalogies I'd like you to address specifically: (1) whether racism is wrong because it ignores a capacity that all humans share (which would suggest the capacity itself, not equal consideration, is what carries the moral weight, and then the question is whether animals have enough of it); (2) whether reciprocity, contracts, or membership in a mutual moral community matter in the racism case in a way that makes the analogy to animals asymmetric rather than parallel; (3) whether the analogy depends on assuming a hedonistic/utilitarian theory of what matters, such that someone with a different moral psychology could accept everything about the racism comparison and still deny it applies to animals. For each disanalogy, say whether it is fatal to Singer's use of the analogy or merely limits its scope — that is, whether it defeats the argument or only shows the argument can't get all the way to equal consideration by itself.

Finally, assess what survives. Suppose my classmate is right that the analogy cannot carry the full conclusion it's standardly used to support: which weaker conclusions does it still license — for instance, that the bare fact of species membership cannot itself be a morally relevant property, so that any human-animal moral distinction must be defended by pointing to some capacity or relationship instead? And if the disanalogies cut against Singer, does that amount to a positive argument that species distinctions are justified, or only a rebuttal? If you think there is a better analogy on either side of this debate — e.g., comparing speciesism to favoring one's own family or nation rather than to racism — say what it is and why it maps better.
