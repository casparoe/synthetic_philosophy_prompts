I am a PhD candidate in computer science writing a dissertation on fairness in machine learning, and this year I am on a part-time secondment to the Algorithmic Review Board of a mid-sized public health agency. The board has three reviewers — a lawyer, a policy analyst, and me — and it must sign off on every predictive model the agency deploys on individuals: readmission-risk scores, automated fraud flags on provider claims, a triage model for scheduling specialist appointments. We have roughly two weeks per model, and I am the only one on the board who can read a confusion matrix. Before I return to my dissertation, I want to leave them with a procedure they can run without me.

Please take the role of a senior expert in algorithmic fairness and public-sector model governance — someone who knows the technical literature thoroughly but has also sat on review boards and understands how these decisions actually get made. Before drafting, think carefully about what the procedure must accomplish and what will predictably go wrong when non-statisticians run it; keep that preliminary thinking brief and deliver only the finished procedure.

The intellectual core I want operationalized is the family of impossibility results in fair machine learning: when outcome base rates differ across groups, several intuitively compelling fairness requirements become mutually unsatisfiable — most prominently, calibration within groups cannot coexist with equal false-positive and false-negative rates across groups. The consequence I care about is this: "the model must be fair" is not a specification. Choosing which fairness criterion to enforce is choosing which disparity to tolerate, and that is a value judgment about who bears the cost of error, not a technical fact that the statistics can settle. The procedure's whole point is to drag that choice into the open and put a name on it, rather than letting it hide inside the selection of a metric.

The constraints under which the procedure must work:

- The reviewers have no statistical background beyond what I can teach them in one afternoon; every step must be runnable by them alone, with plain-language definitions of any quantity they must inspect.
- Two weeks per model, most of which is spent chasing documentation, so the fairness-specific portion of the work gets perhaps two days.
- Every decision must end in a public-facing, one-page justification that a non-technical administrator can read and defend.
- The board has adopted a rule I would like you to build the procedure around rather than bolt on at the end: no model may be approved on any fairness criterion without an attached "tolerated-disparity statement" — a short paragraph naming the specific disparity the approval accepts, which group bears it, and why the board finds that acceptable. A good procedure should generate that statement almost as a byproduct of its steps.

Please structure your response as follows, with these sections in this order and approximate lengths:

1. **Overview** (~half a page): what the procedure does, what single question it ultimately puts to the board, and what it deliberately does not attempt to decide.
2. **The steps** (~two pages): a numbered sequence the reviewers run in order, from the first document request to final sign-off. For each step, state what counts as a sufficient answer, what advances the model to the next step, where the procedure terminates in a rejection, and where it stops and hands a genuine value judgment over to the board. Those handoff points should be unmistakable — the reviewers must never believe a step decided something that was actually theirs to decide.
3. **Worked applications** (~one and a half pages): run the procedure on three cases: (a) a readmission-risk score used to allocate scarce post-discharge nursing visits, where base rates differ substantially across neighborhoods that proxy for race; (b) a fraud-detection model flagging provider claims for manual review, where the cost of a false positive falls on providers and of a false negative on the public purse; (c) a specialist-scheduling triage model that is well calibrated within every group but has visibly unequal false-negative rates for the smallest patient subgroup. At least one of the three should end in an answer the board will find unwelcome, and you should say so plainly rather than softening it.
4. **Failure modes** (~half a page): where the procedure will misfire in practice, what it cannot settle even in principle, and what it would be dishonest for it to pretend to decide.

Throughout, be candid about the points where the procedure is a judgment call dressed up as process. I would much rather the board know exactly where they are the ones deciding than let them believe the paperwork decided for them.
