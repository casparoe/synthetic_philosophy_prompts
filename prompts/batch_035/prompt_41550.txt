You are a scholar of comparative church–state law and political philosophy, equally at home in the French, German, English, and American settlements, and you have spent two years mediating a structured adversarial collaboration between two colleagues who hold opposing positions on secularism. One is a French republican laïcité advocate, for whom the state's religious neutrality is the precondition of equal citizenship and the 1905 settlement embodies a genuine constitutional principle rather than an anti-clerical relic. The other is a German-style accommodationist, who regards the cooperative model — state-collected church taxes on behalf of those who remain members, confessional religious instruction offered in ordinary public schools as a normal subject, and concordats and church treaties as part of public law — as a mature and defensible arrangement that treats religious communities as ordinary partners of civil society rather than potential subversives to be kept at arm's length. Each is intelligent and informed, and neither is a caricature: the laïcité advocate is not hostile to religion, and the accommodationist genuinely values religious freedom and does not want the state instructing anyone what to believe.

Produce the joint statement these two could actually sign after their collaboration. The statement must be a constructive document, not a stale compromise: it should contain claims both would endorse as true, not merely tolerable, and it should make their residual disagreement precise rather than papering over it.

Please cover, at minimum:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should include: shared factual descriptions of the main Western settlements (the French regime, including its Alsace-Moselle exception; the German cooperative model with its opt-out procedure for the church tax; the ceremonial establishment of the Church of England, including the presence of bishops in the legislature; and American disestablishment as interpreted through litigation about public religious displays, aid, and school prayer); shared claims about the values at stake (equal citizenship, freedom of conscience including the freedom to change or abandon religion, democratic legitimacy, the civic integration of religious minorities, and the autonomy of religious communities from state capture); and shared concessions — each side's honest statement of the strongest point it has come to accept from the other over the two years.

2. A disagreements table. Each row must state one disagreement in wording the side holding that view would accept as a fair formulation of its own position — so, for example, the accommodationist's position on the church tax should not be phrased as "a coercive levy on the faithful," and the laïcité advocate's position on school headscarves should not be phrased as "the repression of minority girls." Suggested rows (you may add or refine): whether formal evenhandedness in state support of religions suffices for equal citizenship, or whether equality requires the state's withdrawal from all religious sponsorship; whether an avowedly secular state is neutral in the relevant sense or is itself committed to a substantive stance that religious citizens can reasonably reject; whether the cost and friction of opting out of a state-administered church tax counts as a constraint on freedom of religion, and at what point friction becomes coercion; whether religious instruction as a school subject integrates religious minorities or signals which affiliations count as national; and whether accommodation tends over time to domesticate and hollow out the accommodated churches.

3. A cruxes section. For each crux, state why settling it would move at least one side, and record at least one argument, historical case, or observable state of affairs that both parties agree would bear on it, together with each side's prediction about what that case shows, written down in advance. One crux both regard as important is the comparative vitality and civic position of religious communities under each settlement — the laïcité advocate and the accommodationist give opposing explanations of the same patterns of membership, attendance, and minority integration, so the statement must record each side's prediction about what one should find under a third settlement, so that their explanations can in principle come apart. You should include at least one crux on which one side concedes the other's prediction is plausible and say what would follow.

4. A short section identifying which of their disagreements are substantive and which are verbal — for instance, whether their dispute over "neutrality" is partly a dispute over whether neutrality means distance from all religious expression or evenhandedness toward all of them, and, if so, how much of the overall disagreement dissolves once the two senses are separated.

Write with care and at a level of specificity that could only come from having thought through the actual mechanics of these arrangements — the opt-out declaration, the confessional versus ecumenical versions of school instruction, the difference between a display the state owns and one the state merely permits. Avoid slanted vocabulary throughout; every formulation should be one that both drafters, reading the final text, would say describes their own view correctly.

British English throughout. Your response must open directly with the joint statement itself — no preamble, no framing commentary, no restatement of this brief — and any reasoning you need in order to get the formulations right should happen before the statement begins, not inside it.
