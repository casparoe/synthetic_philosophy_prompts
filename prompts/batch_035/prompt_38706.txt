i'm a phd student in political theory, and my dissertation chapter on the ethics of secession has drifted way off into straight philosophy, so apologies if this is a mess. i keep circling something about diplomatic recognition and can't find a frame for it, so let me just lay out the practice.

international lawyers will tell you — it's in basically every textbook, and it's even stated in treaties like the Montevideo convention — that recognition doesn't make a state a state. statehood comes from facts on the ground (territory, population, effective government), and recognizing is just acknowledging that those facts obtain; this is the so-called declaratory theory. but the actual behavior of states doesn't look much like that. states switch recognition: the US in 1979 stopped recognizing the government in Taipei and started recognizing the one in Beijing, which treats a country's standing as something that can be reassigned — and you can't "re-assign" a factual verdict, that's just changing your mind about the same fact. states withdraw recognition decades after granting it, for openly political reasons. some entities get recognized while plainly failing the effectiveness criteria, others meet them and stay unrecognized. and there's this thing where recognition from small or disliked states seems to not really count: Kosovo has a lot of recognizers, but everyone behaves as if what matters is recognition by major powers or admission to the UN, as though recognition from certain quarters is nearly worthless. meanwhile some actors (the EU, roughly, in the early 90s) made recognition conditional on internal stuff like democracy, rule of law, minority protection — which is bizarre if recognition is just noticing that some government effectively controls a territory.

so here's my question. what does this practice actually presuppose about what recognition *is*? my instinct is the lawyers' theory is doing no real work and the practice only makes sense on something like a constitutive view — recognition as conferring a status rather than registering one. and i've been reading Hegel's master–slave section for the chapter, which suggests a sharper version: there, recognition only counts when it comes from someone you count as an equal — the master's problem is that he's being recognized by someone he refuses to recognize back, so the recognition he gets is worthless to him. read through that, the diplomatic practice seems to presuppose both that status is conferred, and that the conferral is only efficacious between parties already counted as peers — which sits oddly on top of an official ideology of sovereign equality where everyone is a peer by definition. so: reconstruct what the practice presupposes, tell me whether the declaratory theory can actually explain any of the parts i listed (the switching, the withdrawals, the conditionality, the discounting of small-state recognition) or whether it's pure fiction, say whether the parts presuppose one consistent thing or several inconsistent ones, and sketch what the practice would have to look like if we took the lawyers' theory seriously and rebuilt it to be consistent. i'd also like to know which parts are genuinely forced on the practice and which could go either way — i don't want to write the chapter claiming the whole institution secretly rests on one theory if several theories would license most of it.
