A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about punishment, desert, and free will. She takes roughly the Dennett line: that holding people responsible requires treating some people as genuinely deserving of blame, and that Caruso and the hard incompatibilists give away too much in denying this. I ended up somewhere closer to Caruso, and before I defend my side of it again I want to check whether my own position even hangs together. Here is what I came away believing:

1. Basic desert is false: no one ever deserves suffering simply in virtue of what they chose, and this holds regardless of one's story about free will — the dispute between compatibilism and hard determinism does not change it.
2. Punishment, as deliberately imposed hard treatment, can nevertheless be justified — but only instrumentally: deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation. Nothing about making a person suffer is good in itself.
3. The reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation — are fitting responses to wrongdoing. They track something real about the wrong and the person who did it; they are not merely useful pressures we apply.
4. Ordinary blame of particular people can be legitimate in a non-instrumental sense: some people simply are the right targets of blame, and not only because blaming them produces good effects.
5. Proportionality is close to absolute: even where longer confinement would plainly reduce crime, the state may not imprison a person beyond what the seriousness of the offense warrants, and this limit is not to be traded off against aggregate welfare.
6. Where an offender is genuinely dangerous and not treatable, preventive detention beyond the proportionate sentence is permissible — indeed prudence seems to demand it.

My friend's main charge was that (3) and (4) smuggle desert back in through the blaming door, so that (1) becomes an empty gesture. I don't think she's right, but I can't quite see why. And I have a worse worry that she never raised. Point (5) looks like a desert constraint: the claim that no one may be made to suffer more than the offense warrants is just deserts with a negative sign. Yet (1) denies desert and (2) allows only instrumental justification, and I cannot see what, on that basis, could make a proportionality limit *near-absolute* rather than one consideration among many to be weighed against expected benefits. Meanwhile (5) and (6) look flatly inconsistent unless "proportionate sentence" is somehow doing work I cannot specify.

Please audit this as a package rather than a list of separate questions. Identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, and order them by severity instead of presenting them as equally troubling. For each real tension, say what would have to give and roughly what that concession costs elsewhere in the set. Finally, tell me which single revision would restore the most coherence overall. If (3) and (4) can survive (1) on grounds my friend cannot dismiss — for instance, something about what the reactive attitudes are responding to, rather than what people deserve — I would especially like to know whether that same story could rescue (5). If it cannot, I suspect (5) and (6) cannot both stand, and I would want to hear which one you think has to go and why.
