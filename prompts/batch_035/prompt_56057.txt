"The pattern is a real problem for simple socialization stories. If gendered choices were purely cultural, liberalizing a culture should shrink them, but the Nordic countries show some of the strongest occupational sorting by sex anywhere in Europe. So the standard social role explanation is refuted, and what survives is the biological one: when people are free, average sex differences in interests express themselves, and the weaker sorting elsewhere reflects constraint rather than preference.

Then again, the cross-country inference is weaker than it looks. The countries that top the equality indices also tend to be rich welfare states with compressed wages, so affluence or the structure of the labor market could produce the pattern without any difference in underlying preferences. The indices themselves mostly track outcomes like wage gaps and representation, so equality is operationalized one way while conclusions get drawn about another.

Still, suppose the biological reading is correct: where women choose freely, they choose differently. It follows that imbalances like having few women in engineering are not unjust. Rawls held that natural endowments are arbitrary from a moral point of view and that institutions must correct for arbitrary differences, so on his view the egalitarian is committed to erasing natural differences between the sexes, which is impossible. Hence egalitarians face a dilemma: deny the science or accept an absurd demand.

The escape route is to deny that the choices are really free: people adapt their preferences to their options, adjusting what they want to what they can get, so a free choice of nursing over engineering may just be rationalization. But this cuts both ways — it explains the banker's choices as easily as the nurse's, so it can't do much work in an argument for reform."
