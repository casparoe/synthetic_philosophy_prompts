My department has a fortnightly reading group that spans philosophy and economics, and at the pub afterwards this week a genuinely good argument broke out between two of the postgrads, Dana (third-year economics) and Emeka (philosophy of science). The trigger was a third member mentioning that he'd put fifty pounds on a leadership contest at a prediction-market site, purely on his read of the party conference coverage. Dana opened by declaring the bet pointless in principle: on bets placed purely on beliefs, she said, rational agents with a common prior can never agree to trade, because the very fact that the other side is willing to take your bet is evidence against your position, and once you update on it, the mutually acceptable price window collapses — so any belief-motivated bet that actually gets made shows that at least one party has made a mistake somewhere. Emeka replied that this just proves a premise is false: people obviously do bet, and often profitably, so the common-prior assumption has to go — and there is no reason at all why his priors, shaped by his life and his reading, should coincide with Dana's, which is precisely why trade happens and why markets are useful. Dana countered that this is a bigger concession than Emeka realises: if priors differ, a market price only reflects the distribution of prior opinions and risk appetites among whoever happens to be trading, so the whole epistemic story of prices — that they aggregate scattered information about the world — quietly collapses into the price merely mirroring the crowd. Emeka pushed back on two fronts: first, that even with identical beliefs people have legitimate, non-speculative reasons to trade — hedging, liquidity needs, divergent exposures to the outcome — and those trades are what give markets their liquidity, so the no-trade result was never really about markets as they exist; second, that disagreement is epistemically productive rather than pathological, because the prospect of a bet forces each side to take the other's evidence seriously, and once they've pooled it they should converge — the bet is the discovery procedure. Dana's closing rejoinder was that the convergence point concedes everything: if pooling evidence via honest conversation does the converging, the money was never doing any work, and a rational person should rather have had the conversation and saved the stake.

They eventually turned to me, and I found I couldn't referee it past the first couple of moves, partly because I'm not sure Dana's opening statement of the result is even the right statement of it (she ran together several things: the willingness-to-trade-as-evidence idea, the claim that concluded trades must reflect someone's error, and something about common knowledge), and partly because Emeka's replies seem individually plausible but possibly not jointly consistent — I couldn't tell whether his point about heterogeneous priors, his point about non-speculative trading motives, and his point about bets as discovery procedures are three independent objections or whether some of them undermine each other. Could you go through the exchange move by move — Dana's opening, Emeka's common-prior reply, Dana's collapse-of-the-price-signal response, Emeka's two-front pushback, and Dana's closing rejoinder — and say who is right at each step, flagging anywhere a premise is being misstated or an objection is being double-counted? I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing about the relationship between markets as epistemic devices and the conditions under which rational trade is impossible.
