I am a recently retired endocrinologist who has taken up reading philosophy in earnest, and I find myself increasingly preoccupied with a problem that my clinical career brushed against daily but never named. In medicine, some of our most robust causal knowledge concerns absences: scurvy from the lack of vitamin C, cretinism from iodine deficiency in the mother, diabetic ketoacidosis from the absence of insulin. We routinely say that a failure to treat caused the death, that the missing hormone caused the deformity, that a missed dose caused the relapse. Yet the philosophers inform me that causation by omission is a notorious muddle: the gardener who fails to water the flowers supposedly causes their death, but then so does everyone else in the world who did not water them, and the drought, and the neighbour's forgotten hose. Before I wade further into the literature, I want the specification first. Please write the desiderata that any adequate theory of causation by omission must meet: the cases it has to classify correctly, the distinctions it must respect (for instance, between genuine causation by absence and the mere absence of a cause, or between physical causation and moral accountability — I gather the courts care about the latter), the results from ordinary science it cannot contradict, and the triviality problem it must defuse somehow. With each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which requirements you take to be non-negotiable for any theory whatever and which are instead the prejudices of one camp, so that I can see where the genuine disagreement lies.

Two further things I would like. First, tell me whether any subset of your desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — I am told some philosophers bite the bullet and deny that omissions cause anything at all, and if your list forces that conclusion, say so plainly rather than hiding it in a footnote. Second, briefly assess how the main contenders you know of fare against the list; I have in mind the counterfactual approach as Lewis and his successors developed it, the view that absences never strictly cause but only explain in some weaker sense, the account that treats omissions as genuine absences of connection between distinct causal processes, and any interventionist treatment in which one manipulates a variable standing in for the absence. Keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard — the criteria are where the thinking is, and I can grade the theories myself once I hold a decent list. Please write in British English, and feel free to be technical where the subject demands it; my decades of reading trial data have left me unafraid of dense prose, though I would rather not wade through jargon that does no work.
