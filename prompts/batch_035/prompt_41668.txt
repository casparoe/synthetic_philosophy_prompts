Last week my philosophy of probability course covered Bertrand's paradox (pick a "random" chord on a circle: depending on the method you get probability 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4 that it's longer than the inscribed equilateral triangle's side). Afterwards two people in my study group, Aisha and Marcus, got into it and basically wouldnt drop it.

Aisha said the paradox just shows the principle of indifference is broken: if "assign equal probability across the alternatives" gives different answers depending on how you describe the setup, it can't be a rule at all. Marcus said no — it shows "a random chord" is underspecified, like asking for a random number without a procedure; each of the three answers is right given its own way of generating chords, so indifference was never actually asked a well-posed question. Aisha came back with the biased-coin case from earlier in the semester: a coin of unknown bias, where indifference says p is uniform on [0,1]. That description seems unambiguous, she said, yet you still get contradictions by redescribing (uniform in p versus uniform in some function of p gives different answers), so "it's just underspecified" can't be the whole story. Marcus replied that for the coin, the flat prior is at least picking out something real about our ignorance, and anyway his view doesn't need indifference to be a general rule — it only needs it to never fire, which is fine, since real randomization is always some physical procedure, and where there's a procedure the procedure already fixes the answer. Aisha said that concedes everything: the principle was supposed to tell you what to believe *before* you have a procedure, and if it only "works" when a procedure already did the work, it's dead weight, not a principle.

Neither would budge, and honestly I couldn't follow all of it. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step, and whether there's something both are missing? (One complication if it helps: our TA mentioned offhand that if you physically tossed straws at a circle on the floor, the answers might converge on one value — I cant tell whether that supports Marcus or just adds fuel.)
